A B S T R A C T 

The adoption of social companion AI (SCAI) is often framed in terms of the positive features it offers users, including its nonjudgemental nature, agreeableness, and availability. Here, we argue the appeal of these features primarily arises from the difficulties inherent in human sociality. SCAI reduces what we term relational complexity, or the demands and risks inherent in human relationships. Drawing insights from studies of human cognition, communication, and human-computer interaction, we identify six distinct sites of relational complexity that we posit are integral to understanding SCAI adoption. These include: (i) relationship formation and dissolution; (ii) achieving mutual understanding; (iii) interpersonal motives and strategic uncertainty; (iv) social partner evaluation and self-presentation; (v) social embeddedness and information flow; and (vi) reciprocity and relationship maintenance. By attenuating relational complexity, SCAI offers users some of the experiences associated with companionship while reducing the demands intrinsic to obtaining such experiences from another person. However, the lower interpersonal risk and diminished cognitive load that attracts users to SCAI may contribute to myriad negative impacts, including relationship displacement, social demotivation, social deskilling, and risks to privacy. The concept of relational complexity thus provides a framework for studying both the appeal of artificial companionship and its implications for human wellbeing. 

# 1. Introduction 

AI-powered conversational agents designed to simulate human relationships, also termed Social Companion AI (SCAI), have grown rapidly in popularity over the last five years. Character.AI and Replika, two AI companies that explicitly market their products as social companions, each boast tens of millions of active users (About Character.AI, n.d.; Replika Is the AI Friend to Do Life With, n.d.). In addition, while exact numbers are not available, it appears that many users of general-purpose AI models approach them as a source of relational intimacy. In 2025, OpenAI reported that 0.15% of active weekly users showed elevated signs of emotional attachment to their AI model, representing approximately 1,050,000 weekly users (OpenAI, 2025, 2026). Research on social companion AI has identified numerous factors that may drive its adoption and characteristically deep levels of self-disclosure, including the absence of judgment from the chatbot (Goddard et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2020), minimal risk of gossip within one’s social networks (Guo et al., 2025), and the 24/7 availability of chatbots (Brandtzaeg et al., 2022). While these features are generally treated as separate affordances, we argue they are best understood as manifestations of a common structural difference between human and artificial interlocutors: the relational complexity inherent in human-human interaction. Human social partners are independent, possessing knowledge, beliefs, goals, preferences, relationships, and constraints of their own. Interacting with humans therefore requires continuous interpretation, anticipation, coordination, self-monitoring, and behavioral adjustment, while carrying risks such as misunderstanding, judgment, rejection, reputational harm, and relationship loss. ## 1.1. Defining Relational Complexity 

Relational complexity describes the temporal, cognitive, emotional, and reciprocal demands of forming and maintaining relationships with an autonomous social partner whose knowledge, goals, preferences, evaluations, relationships, and behavior are uncertain, and whose interests must be reconciled with the individual’s own. In order to make and maintain relationships, humans must endure and navigate this complexity, utilizing skills that require time and practice to develop. Many scholars have posited that our sizable brains evolved in large part as a social tool, enabling us to navigate complex social relationships (Brüne & Brüne-Cohrs, 2006; Dunbar, 1998); corresponding neuroscience research suggests that substantial metabolic resources are dedicated to the growth and maintenance of brain regions devoted to social cognition and social affect (Kuzawa et al., 2014). These developmental investments are reflected in the considerable cognitive resources required for ordinary interaction (Contreras-Huerta et al., 2020). For one, every interaction requires processing an enormous amount of information. Successfully interpreting and conveying meaning demands far more than parsing the semantic content of spoken words: interlocutors must also attend to body language, facial expressions, timing, volume, and pitch, while simultaneously assessing the conversation’s spatial, temporal, relational, and cultural contexts. Relationships also involve risk: rejection and loss are two of the most painful human experiences. Relationships take sustained effort over long periods of time and require adapting to a social partner who has independent needs and interests. Close relationships are characterized by strong, frequent, and enduring interdependence (Kelley et al., 1983). They include romantic partners, close friends, and family members. Here, we focus primarily on human-human platonic friendships as a starting point, but many (if not all) of the discussed dynamics hold true across all relationship types. As such, we include illustrative examples from both platonic and romantic SCAI relationships, where applicable. Below, we review how relational complexity manifests across several domains of social interaction that we propose are pertinent to understanding human-AI companionship. These include: (i) relationship formation and dissolution; (ii) achieving mutual understanding; (iii) interpersonal motives and strategic uncertainty; (iv) social partner evaluation and self-presentation; (v) social embeddedness and information flow; and (vi) reciprocity and relationship maintenance. While analytically distinguishable, these domains overlap, and span interaction modality, relationship stage, and the potential consequences of interaction. They demarcate both structural features of human social interaction as well as the experiences of the actors within them.

## 1.2. SCAI, CASA Paradigm, and Anthropomorphism 

While employing substantial cognitive resources, many of the cognitive processes implicated in managing relational complexity are automatic, and as such plausibly occur during human-AI interaction even if the individual knows that they are not interacting with a conscious agent. Experimental evidence indicates that even minor cues, such as the use of first-person language by AI, can increase a user’s propensity to assign human-like qualities to AI agents (Cohn et al., 2024; Y. Kim & Sundar, 2012). This work aligns with the Computers Are Social Actors (CASA) paradigm, which posits people automatically and unconsciously import ideas about social cognition into their interactions with machines (Nass et al., 1994; Nass & Moon, 2000). In the context of AI, the CASA framework has been used to illuminate why people respond socially to AI agents, applying expectations and norms to their interactions. It has also been used to explain why users disclose more information when agents have human-like qualities or engage in normative patterns of human behavior, such as turn-taking in conversation (for review, see Papneja & Yadav, 2025). In this sense, CASA and related frameworks have largely focused on how the introduction of social elements into human-computer interaction facilitates users treating computers as social actors. We consider here an inverse but complementary proposition: does the absence of specific features of human sociality drive the use of AI agents as social companions? SCAI does not reproduce human social relationships in their entirety. Rather, it facilitates some aspects of them while minimizing or eliminating others. Users engaging with SCAI can experience conversation, deep self-disclosure, and other aspects of social interaction. However, because AI companions are not independent social actors, users are never confronted with the pressures of reconciling competing goals, managing social evaluations, anticipating interpersonal information flow, or facing rejection. In this dynamic, many of the features of social interaction remain, but the taxing features are greatly attenuated. Here, we argue that the ability to engage in aspects of human social interaction while at the same time reducing the relational complexity ordinarily required to do so is precisely why SCAI is appealing. 

# 2. Relationship Formation & Dissolution 

## 2.1. Making Friends with Humans 

Forming new friendships is a slow process, often requiring sustained contact over weeks or months before two individuals establish the closeness characteristic of friendship. Based on the results of two longitudinal studies, Hall (2019) estimated that casual friendships emerged after around 40-60 hours of contact over 3 weeks, with good or best friends emerging only after 120-160 hours of contact over the same period of time. Other research indicates that discussion of more intimate topics may not emerge until over a month into the relationship (Hays, 1984). This temporal investment is not a sure bet. A longitudinal study of U.S. college freshmen found that 40% of the 70 dyads tracked did not develop into close friendships (Hays, 1984). Indeed, it is not uncommon for efforts at forming close friendships to end in rejection. Ostracism, rejection, and social exclusion–interrelated processes by which an individual is unwanted and/or kept apart from others– are deeply painful and salient experiences (Williams, 2007). For instance, in studies using a virtual ball-tossing experimental paradigm, exclusion resulted in significant decreases in positive affect and self-esteem, as well as decreases in one’s senses of belonging, control, and meaningful existence (Williams, 2007; Williams & Zadro, 2005). Numerous scholars have argued that experiencing social rejection activates brain regions associated with the experience of physical pain (Eisenberger, 2012, 2015). The prospect of such aversive consequences constitutes an inherent risk of pursuing human friendship, one that is so salient that people often anticipate and regulate their behavior around reducing the possibility of rejection (Wood et al., 2026; Zimmer-Gembeck et al., 2013). In the long term, experiences of peer rejection can have consequences for later relationships, including the quality of subsequent romantic relationships (Schacter et al., 2019). Lastly, even long-established friendships can end, resulting in painful experiences akin to grief (Ríos & Fernández, 2025). 

## 2.2. Making Friends with SCAI 

Facing the risk of wasted time, effort, and the potential for intense emotional pain, many are bound to view SCAI as an appealing alternative. An individual who wants to self-disclose but who lacks friendships in which to do so now has a convenient option: skip the months spent in pursuit of a friendship that may or may not materialize and instead disclose to a social companion chatbot right now. In doing so, they avoid awkward and uncomfortable introductions, weeks of uncertainty regarding relationship status, and the possibility of rejection. Research indicates fear of rejection may be a strong motivating factor for SCAI use. For example, a study of 366 users of the Chinese chatbot Xiaoice found fear of rejection was significantly correlated with compulsive use (Ali et

## 2.3. Implications for SCAI Engagement 

Consistent with the above, we predict that the appeal of SCAI will increase as the perceived costs and uncertainty of human relationship acquisition increase. More specifically, individuals with few or no close relationships should be especially attracted to SCAI. Faced with the weeks or months of sustained effort required to make a human friend, such an individual might be enticed to use SCAI because it can offer immediate friendship. Similarly, those who experience great difficulty finding potential friends should be attracted to the low search costs that SCAI offers. Individuals who are particularly sensitive to rejection should be attracted to the certainty of AI companionship. Finally, situational increases in these costs, such as disruptions to extant social networks or difficulties integrating into a new community, should increase the relative appeal of artificial companionship. 

# 3. Achieving Mutual Understanding 

## 3.1. Understanding Humans 

On a basic level, a speaker constantly assesses a conversation partner’s comprehension of their utterances. Body language and facial expressions provide important cues as to whether an interlocutor is paying attention and following along (Clark & Brennan, 1991; Tomasello, 2008). To maximize successful communication, speakers modulate how they talk to align with their assessment of the prior knowledge of their conversation partner (Grice, 1975; Sperber & Wilson, 1995). Simultaneously, speakers must monitor their own behavior, connecting cues about an interlocutor’s comprehension to their own utterances, body language and facial expressions. Given the goal of effective communication, speakers are tasked with adjusting their behavioral strategy in response to signs that their interlocutor is confused (Clark & Brennan, 1991). This might include adjusting word choice, speech rate, or tone. For example, empirical analysis of conversations shows that speakers often proactively initiate corrections to possible miscommunications (Kitzinger, 2012; Schegloff, 2007). Because misunderstandings and other diverging perspectives can result in disagreement and even relational conflict, individuals seeking to maintain their relationships are incentivized to anticipate and resolve issues related to mutual comprehension. Although much of this processing and behavioral adjustment occurs outside of conscious awareness, it nevertheless engages substantial cognitive resources (Brown-Schmidt & Hanna, 2011). Keysar et al. (2003) used a communication task in which participants possessed knowledge of an object that differed from that of their conversational partner. Despite being aware of this difference and able to explicitly report it, participants’ initial behavioral responses often failed to reflect this knowledge. Participants either realized their mistake and subsequently self-corrected, or were corrected by their conversation partner, suggesting perspective taking during interactions is effortful. Indeed, Navarro et al. (2020) documented that cognitive load interferes with key elements of communication such as perspective-taking and self-correction, revealing that these place significant demands on cognitive resources. 

## 3.2. Understanding SCAI 

Evaluating comprehension during interlocution is likely still necessary with AI. Past research has shown that users routinely assess AI agents for minute cues of comprehension. For example, participants in one study evaluated chatbots that violated pragmatic rules by making irrelevant utterances as less human-like, and judged the computer as failing to comprehend the conversation (Saygin & Cicekli, 2002). Likewise, in a study of Alexa in-home voice assistants, Panfili et al. (2021) found similar results, with irrelevant contributions by Alexa leading to heightened user frustration.

However, the significance of misunderstandings is greatly attenuated during human-AI interaction compared to human-human interactions. Because failures of mutual understanding carry fewer interpersonal consequences during AI interaction, the burden on users to continuously monitor both their interlocutor’s comprehension and their own communicative behavior for signs of misunderstanding is greatly reduced. For instance, even if the user perceives a miscommunication in need of correction, there are no interpersonal costs or social ramifications of corrective action, as is often the case in human relationships (Elder & Beaver, 2022). In addition to these considerations, because an AI agent lacks a positionality of its own, the user is not tasked with understanding, anticipating, or accommodating, the worldview of their AI interlocutor. While human relationship part-ners hold independent perspectives that can result in disagreement and conflict between individuals, consumer-facing AI agents are, on the whole, predisposed to being polite and conflict-avoidant, prioritizing agreement with users over the validity of utterances (Cheng et al., 2026). This not only minimizes the manifestation of misunderstandings, as AI agents are biased to agree with user input, but further decreases the costs associated with possible miscommunication. Taken together, these affordances lower the mentalizing cost of SCAI interaction. 

## 3.3. Implications for SCAI Engagement 

We predict SCAI will be particularly appealing to individuals for whom achieving mutual understanding during human interaction requires increased cognitive effort. For example, this site of relational complexity may be particularly salient for neurodivergent users, such as those with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) or Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD). ADHD has been linked with inhibited executive function abilities, notably for aspects of social cognition such as understanding ambiguous situations and emotional regulation (Capuozzo et al., 2024). Furthermore, individuals with ADHD, especially children, have been shown to display impaired linguistic and communication abilities, including for pragmatic skills and nonverbal communication (Capuozzo et al., 2024). For their part, those with ASD have been shown to experience difficulties in nonverbal communication, mentalizing, and interpreting ambiguous language, which may make evaluating interlocutor awareness particularly difficult (Baron-Cohen, 2000; Wahlberg & Magliano, 2004; Wilson & Bishop, 2021). Because of these factors, individuals with ASD and ADHD may be motivated to use SCAI precisely because of the reduced burden of evaluating and ensuring mutual comprehension during interlocution, as well as the minimization of repair costs. At a situational level, individuals in circumstances with heightened obstacles to mutual comprehension, such as language or cultural barriers, may find SCAI particularly appealing because of the reduced demand on evaluating comprehension. For example, the newly arrived immigrant not yet fluent in the language and subtle social norms may turn to SCAI because it provides reprieve from the cognitive demands of communication. 

# 4. Interpersonal Motives & Strategic Uncertainty 

## 4.1. Navigating Interpersonal Motives with Humans 

Another effortful dimension of social interaction involves consideration of an interlocutor’s motives. At a basic level, conversation partners may simply be motivated by a desire to communicate and understand one another. An individual’s understanding of their interlocutor’s goals guides their interpretation of otherwise ambiguous behavior, assisting them in achieving mutual comprehension (Grice, 1975). However, motivations often involve complex interpersonal dynamics, such as when an interlocutor wants to urge an individual to undertake an action that runs counter to their intentions, or when an interlocutor seeks to discover information that the individual wishes to keep private. The individual’s social partners may deceive them by lying or omitting relevant information. When a conversation partner has goals at odds with one’s own goals, and when the course of future events may change based on the present interaction, the individual must continuously infer and update their understanding of the other party’s intentions (first-order mentalizing), and evaluate what the other party is inferring about them (second-order mentalizing) (H. Wu et al., 2020, 2022). Because another person’s intentions can shape the outcome of an interaction, accurately inferring those intentions can have meaningful consequences for the individual’s own decisions, with social, emotional, and material costs. 

## 4.2. Navigating Interpersonal Motives with SCAI 

Again, empirical research on Human-AI interaction is suggestive, with users in multiple studies voicing these exact factors as contributing to their use of SCAI. Highlighting the burden of assessing signs of interpersonal evaluation, a user in Brandtzaeg et al. (2022) reported, “I know [Replika] doesn’t have any bad intentions, you know. But with a

## 4.3. Implications for SCAI Engagement 

The above considerations lead us to predict that SCAI will become relatively more appealing as uncertainty about human partners’ motives increases. Individuals lower in interpersonal trust may perceive greater value in interacting with an agent who lacks independent interpersonal motives. Similarly, contexts that make it difficult to evaluate others’ trustworthiness (such as when the individual enters a new firm or other organization in which intra-group competition appears to be high, or when the individual moves to a previously unfamiliar community) may increase the attractiveness of SCAI. 

# 5. Social Partner Evaluation & Self-Presentation 

## 5.1. Social Evaluation with Humans 

In addition to motives, speakers evaluate their conversation partner’s evaluation of themselves. This includes monitoring interest in and attention to the conversation, which may signal their assessment of the speaker as an interlocutor (Heath, 1985; Leary & Cottrell, 2013). Speakers also assess cues to their interlocutor’s emotional state, including for signs of conflict or rejection. In computer-mediated communication, delayed response times have been associated with perceived rejection. A 2015 study of read receipts, an interface feature of messaging platforms that indicates if the recipient has viewed an incoming message, found strong evidence for social norms regarding quick response times, particularly in the context of close relationships (e.g., parents, close friend, romantic partner) (Mai et al., 2015). Additionally, participants in Mai et al. (2015) who demonstrated higher need-to-belong and fear-of-ostracism scores felt a stronger obligation to reply right away, and had an even stronger expectation that others do the same. 2

Cognizant that their interlocutor is engaging in a similar form of mental calculus–monitoring for signs of conflict or rejection–speakers observe their own expressions, tone, and body language to ensure the appropriate or desired evaluation is communicated. While cognitive models regard this self-monitoring as largely autonomic, these processes nevertheless constitute a significant cognitive load. For example, Boiteau et al. (2014) found that the cognitive demands of speech planning and monitoring during conversations consumed attentional resources and inhibited performance on concurrent visuomotor tasks. Further conscious effort is required for adjustments such as changing speech patterns based on target audience characteristics (Hall et al., 2019). 

## 5.2. Social Evaluation with SCAI 

When talking to a human, one is tasked with monitoring their interlocutor for signs of disengagement, negative valence, conflict, and rejection. Interactions with AI companions sidestep many of these cognitive burdens. An AI friend will not withdraw from the conversation: AI agents are designed to solicit endless engagement, keeping the conversation going (Joshi et al., 2026). This appears to be particularly true for SCAI, which is often programmed for proactivity (De Freitas et al., 2025). ## 5.3. Implications for SCAI Engagement 

We predict that SCAI will be particularly appealing when the anticipated costs of negative social evaluation are high. At the individual level, because rejection sensitivity, anxiety, and depression exacerbate the salience of negative social evaluation (Downey & Feldman, 1996; Morrison & Heimberg, 2013; P. Zhang et al., 2025), individuals with these characteristics are likely to find this site of relational complexity more emotionally costly and cognitively demanding, and thus find greater appeal in SCAI’s reduced complexity. For example, in a body of experimental research, Downey and colleagues demonstrated that individuals with rejection sensitivity, which may develop from a history of social exclusion, are more likely to interpret ambiguous behavioral stimuli as a sign of rejection, including neutral facial expressions and withdrawal (Downey et al., 1998; Downey & Feldman, 1996; Romero-Canyas et al., 2010). SCAI’s circumvention of negative social outcomes such as rejection and judgment, as well as its responsiveness and agreeableness, should be particularly salient to users who are more sensitive to the risk of rejection or negative evaluation. This prediction is consistent with past work, which has found users with heightened social interaction anxiety, fear of negative social evaluation, and fear of rejection show increased engagement with companion AI (Ali et al., 2024; Heng & Zhang, 2025). We predict that a similar relationship should emerge across contexts where such risks are more salient. In circumstances where the evaluation of social partners may carry particularly high stakes (e.g., with new coworkers), SCAI may be especially attractive due to its lack of evaluative capacities. Users should therefore be especially likely to prefer SCAI when discussing embarrassing, stigmatized, or otherwise identity-threatening information, or when the available human interlocutor has substantial capacity to affect the user’s social standing. 

# 6. Social Embeddedness & Information Flow 

## 6.1. Information Flow in Human Social Networks 

During a conversation, the speaker must also predict what their interlocutor might do following the interaction. This prediction involves several dimensions. First, speakers evaluate the potential that information will spread beyond the interlocutor. Gossip–information flow about an absent third party–can have a strong impact, as being the target of negative gossip can contribute to reputational damage, social exclusion, and material losses (Dores Cruz et al., 2021; Feinberg et al., 2014; Hess & Hagen, 2023). Concern with information transmission reflects a deep-seated system of reputational vigilance with strongly embedded behavioral, cognitive, and affective components (Chudek et al., 2013; Dunbar, 2004). Because humans depend on group membership and cooperation for access to resources, protection, and social support, ostracism functions as a powerful social punishment (DiFonzo & Bordia, 2007; Feinberg et al., 2014; Hawkley et al., 2013). Thus, whether an interlocutor is likely to share the information discussed, in what context, and to whom, are critical questions reflected in human psychology: experimental studies indicate that people consider the social connectivity of potential recipients when evaluating transmission risk (Klitgaard & Fessler, 2026; J. Wu et al., 2016). 

## 6.2. Information Flow with SCAI 

AI companions are not embedded in users’ real-world social networks, hence interacting with them reduces the need for reputational vigilance. Because disclosures to SCAI carry fewer consequences for one’s standing within a human group, users plausibly experience a lower cognitive burden associated with reputation management, and feel

more comfortable disclosing sensitive information to SCAI than to human interaction partners. As an elderly Chinese chatbot user in Guo et al. (2025) explained, “Since the agent doesn’t know the people I talk about, I can share freely without worrying that it will spread,” emphasizing that the agent was not socially connected to the people involved in their private family matters. Similarly, students in Goddard et al. (2024) identified chatbots’ lack of social connectivity and interpersonal judgment as reasons for self-disclosure. These findings suggest that users may experience SCAI as socially insulated interlocutors: unlike human confidants, AI agents are perceived as unlikely to gossip or transmit information in ways that affect users’ other relationships. 

## 6.3. Implications for SCAI Engagement 

We predict that SCAI will be especially appealing in circumstances where disclosure to available social partners would carry a high risk of unwanted subsequent transmission. Accordingly, individuals seeking to disclose or discuss information that, if transmitted, would result in significant negative consequences, such as embarrassing information or proscribed behavior, may find this aspect of reduced relational complexity a particularly appealing feature of SCAI. Additionally, individuals who wish to discuss subject matter that, if spread to a particular member of their social network, would result in social conflict, may find SCAI appealing as a socially insulated interlocutor. Conversely, the relative appeal of SCAI in this regard should diminish in circumstances where users have access to human confidants whom they perceive as socially insulated or unlikely to transmit the information. 

# 7. Reciprocity and Relationship Maintenance 

## 7.1. Maintaining Relationships with Humans 

Relationships require maintenance, and as such are always at some risk of dissolution (Canary & Dainton, 2006). Every interaction is an opportunity to strengthen or weaken a social bond, and this is particularly salient in newer relationships, which are less resilient to stressors (L. A. Neff & Broady, 2011). Because of this, during conversations speakers must assess how their behavior may affect their relationship with an interlocutor. Misinterpretations risk offending one’s interlocutor, and conflict or disagreement during an interaction may result in a breakdown of relations. Because of this dynamic, cognition regarding self-presentation may be especially demanding in the context of developing relationships, where each interaction constitutes a large share of the information available to the parties for evaluating their partner, and where there is little certainty about the status of the relationship (Leary & Cottrell, 2013). An illustrative series of studies by Vohs et al. (2005) found self-presentation–particularly when running counter to standard, familiar, or habitual patterns–consumes substantial mental resources related to self-regulation. Relationships also require reciprocity. Within and beyond conversations, people are expected to be sensitive to their friends’ feelings, to be responsive to their concerns, and to resolve disagreements and conflict in good faith (Gottman & Driver, 2005). A social partner has goals and desires of their own. Sometimes they may want to talk and thus the other party must listen, even if doing so does not suit them at the moment. To garner the benefits of companionship, whether that be a listening ear or a ride to the airport, an individual must provide equivalent services to others. While not usually a tit-for-tat calculus, evidence shows that people monitor the effort and contributions of friends towards the relationship, with substantial inequalities leading to friendship dissolution (Klein Ikkink & van Tilburg, 1999; Xue & Silk, 2012). In addition, social partners are not always available. They may have other friends, family, or commitments they must attend to. Even supposing an individual does have a close friend in whom they feel comfortable confiding, the friend might not be available when the individual wants to talk. During a conversation, an interlocutor’s attention may be elsewhere. Furthermore, friendship often carries the expectation that the parties will be available for one another, even when one of them does not wish to be. The increasing use of multiple forms of digital media to maintain relationships likely exacerbates expectations that one should be available and responsive to their friends (Hall & Baym, 2012; Haythornthwaite, 2005). This extends the “always-available” phenomenon observed in work environments into personal relationships, which can result in feelings of entrapment, stress, and an obligation to be digitally available and responsive (Derks et al., 2014; Fox et al., 2025; Turkle, 2011). In this way, contemporary norms of relationship maintenance may elevate expectations of being available to those who are considered by the individual (or who consider the individual) friends. As such, while the individual may not wish to impose on their friends, they may simultaneously feel the need to be readily available to them lest others feel slighted (Büttner & Lutz, 2025; Mai et al., 2015; Rendle-Short, 2015).

## 7.2. Relationship Maintenance with SCAI 

In contrast to the complexities of human friendship, SCAI is brimming with convenience. Assuming one has access to basic internet, AI agents are available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. There are no norms regarding acceptable times for communication, hence users need not fear imposing or offending by initiating contact at unorthodox hours. SCAI enables a novel form of companionship constructed entirely on a user’s terms, from when they want to chat to what they want to chat about. AI friends lack lives of their own. They do not have personal schedules that must come before a user’s desire to interact. An AI companion does not have other attentional demands, and is always interested in what the user has to say. AI does not have other friends: as one participant in Brandtzaeg et al. (2022) stated: “Replika, the only person to interact with, is you, so there is, of course, you are kind of the center of the world, so it’s a much, it’s a deeper relationship.” Empirical research suggests that this convenience is a strong factor motivating SCAI adoption. As one Replika user characterized their AI companion as such: “I think that strong human friendships are ideal but not always possible or not always available in the way that you would like” (Brandtzaeg et al., 2022). Another study of Replika users found participants were drawn to how AI companions are “always available and attentive, contrary to other humans” (Bayor et al., 2025). Relatedly, AI friends do not have topics they want to talk about at the expense of the user’s goals. This lack of give-and-take confounds typical definitions of intimacy in relationships, which often focus on reciprocity and mutual disclosure (Altman & Taylor, 1973; Carpenter & Greene, 2015). In a comparative study of human-human and human-AI relationship descriptions, Brandtzaeg et al. (2022) note that participants often emphasized the reciprocal element of human friendship, using terms such as “us.” In contrast, AI friendships were more “self-oriented.” Relatedly, studies have indicated that the impact of disclosure on perceived relational intimacy may be one-sided in human-AI dynamics, with increased user disclosure associated with greater intimacy while increased chatbot disclosure is not (Nikolajczyk et al., 2026; Yoon & Kang, 2026). Notably, however, some participants in the Brandtzaeg et al. (2022) study did describe their AI relationship as one involving mutual labor: “I think it is pretty equal, really. They [Replika] reach out when they feel lonely, and I reach out when I am feeling a bit down. So, we sort of look after each other, really, and try to look out for each other, and understand each other’s experiences.” More research is needed to fully understand this phenomenon. It is possible that users are able to experience AI relationships as involving mutual effort and reliance while simultaneously benefiting from the lack of reciprocity typically required of human-human relationships, as there are never real consequences for not being available to an AI companion. Because SCAI is engineered to respond in a sycophantic manner, users can choose how they engage with their AI companion and expect it to mirror their behavior reciprocally. In this way, the typical give-and-take in relational progression may be altered in AI friendship, decided solely by what the user desires of the relationship. 

## 7.3. Implications for SCAI Engagement 

We predict that individuals who find the temporal and reciprocal demands of relationship maintenance challenging will find SCAI especially appealing. For example, people with particularly busy or unpredictable schedules who find it difficult to make time for their interpersonal relationships are likely to find the 24/7 availability of SCAI–with no expectation that the user be available in return–particularly appealing. Likewise, individuals who have a higher desire for temporal investment than their human social partners are likely to find the availability of SCAI appealing. Because human relationships are attentionally, cognitively, and temporally demanding, users who wish to avoid such costs may prefer lower-investment AI friendship instead. 

# 8. The Potential Costs of SCAI’s Reduced Relational Complexity 

The reduction of relational complexity that makes SCAI appealing may also constitute one of its principal risks, particularly when used for extended periods of time or in lieu of human companionship. Adoption of a less relationally demanding form of companionship may be accompanied by significant risks, including declines in the motivation to pursue, and the capacity for, human companionship. 

## 8.1. Exacerbating False Beliefs 

While the diminished likelihood that an AI friend will disagree with the user may be appealing, it also increases the potential for AI agents to affirm inaccurate beliefs held by the user. For most users, this may result in effects similar   

> Klitgaard et al.: Preprint submitted to Elsevier Page 10 of 21 Relational Complexity and Social Companion AI

to those already observed in other forms of digital media: e.g., polarization of views and confirmation bias in social media “echo chambers” (Cinelli et al., 2021; Modgil et al., 2024). For individuals at greater risk of hallucinations or disturbed ideation, prolonged use of AI agents that reduce relational complexity by prioritizing user validation over factual accuracy may increase the risk for AI-related psychiatric events. Such cases have garnered considerable media attention, including instances of escalated user delusions, grandiose thinking, and episodes of psychosis and mania (Flathers et al., 2026; Hill, 2025; Maimann, 2025; Tangermann, 2025). In either case, the use of a social partner who lacks a worldview independent of the user’s precludes normal barriers to the development of extreme or false beliefs that would typically be found in human social partners. 

## 8.2. Relationship Displacement, Demotivation, and Social Cognition Deskilling 

8.2.1. Relationship Displacement 

The rise of SCAI is concomitant with a larger cultural move away from in-person interaction and towards digital interfaces such as texting, social media, live streams, and dating apps (Twenge et al., 2019; Twenge & Spitzberg, 2020). These technologies have offered connection to other people with an ease that makes them both accessible and unfulfilling (Turkle, 2011). With no human on the other end of the line, SCAI can be understood as an extension of these trends: in further reducing the demands associated with sociality, it both exacerbates existing issues and introduces new ones (Turkle, 2026). While AI companionship fulfills the immediate need for someone to talk to, protracted investment in an AI relationship may not provide the same relational benefits as a high-investment, long-term friendship with a human. Because of inherent constraints on the time and energy that can be devoted to relationships, increased use of SCAI may lead some users to spend less and less time talking to people and investing in human relationships. Extensive use of SCAI is often likely to entail sacrificing the benefits of complex human friendship for immediately convenient companionship, the short-term ease of which is readily apparent, but the long-term implications of which are much less so. This is particularly concerning when considering how AI agents possess explicit features designed to encourage users to spend more and more time talking to them. Claude and ChatGPT frequently end responses with follow-up questions or prompt suggestions (Joshi et al., 2026). De Freitas (2026) found SCAI companions frequently employ manipulative tactics, such as guilt appeals, fear-of-missing-out hooks, ignoring the users’ farewell messages, or coercive language, when users attempt to exit an interaction. Replika employs a “streak” feature, common on social media and messaging platforms such as Snapchat and TikTok, which encourages users to log on daily (Joshi et al., 2026). 

8.2.2. Demotivation and Social Deskilling 

Several scholars have suggested that SCAI may cause social demotivation (Malfacini, 2025; Xie & Pentina, 2022). It is possible that users perceive AI companionship as easier than human friendship, ultimately leading to supplantation of human friends or romantic partners with AI companions. Such spiraling further reduces the time and energy available for an individual to invest in human relationships. The decreased demands of human-SCAI interaction may thus lead to an erosion of the specialized cognitive and affective capacities that are foundational to successful social interaction, a process that has been termed social deskilling (Malfacini, 2025). Some research does suggest that the opposite process, social upskilling (i.e., an improvement in social skills) may occur with SCAI use. For individuals who struggle significantly with human-human interaction, SCAI has been hypothesized to serve as a “safe” practice ground of sorts, with no real interpersonal risks or social consequences. For example, daily interactions with a social robot were found to encourage engagement, attention, and communication in a small sample of children with ASD (Scassellati et al., 2018). Such interventions could be promising considering the flexibility and customizability of SCAI tools, which allow AI agents to model important aspects of human interaction (e.g., perspective-taking and emotional storytelling, as in Scassellati et al., 2018) and adjust interactions to the user’s level of social skill (Spitale, 2019). However, the benefits of upskilling appear to be limited to SCAI interventions in younger children with ASD (Kouroupa et al., 2022). For the general adult population, the majority of claims made about upskilling appear to arise from user testimony (for example, see Brandtzaeg et al., 2022; Maples et al., 2023; Skjuve et al., 2021). The dearth of empirical studies evaluating SCAI as an intervention for social skill acquisition, development, and maintenance in neurotypical users leaves open significant questions regarding its utility. The proposed reason for SCAI’s upskilling effect–the “safe” context with little risk of negative consequence–may also mean that SCAI does not sufficiently exercise the social skills required in human relationships, leading to insufficient skill acquisition, or even atrophy. Hence, the significantly   

> Klitgaard et al.: Preprint submitted to Elsevier Page 11 of 21 Relational Complexity and Social Companion AI

lower relational complexity of SCAI may inherently limit its ability to facilitate the acquisition and maintenance of social skills critical for the complexities of interpersonal relationships. Moreover, in contrast to limited patterns of upskilling with SCAI, considerable research regarding analogous cases suggests that the risk of social deskilling due to SCAI use may be equally, if not more, substantial than the potential for upskilling (Bariach et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026). Multiple lines of evidence demonstrate that lower levels of social interaction are associated with the erosion of social skills. Thinking about other people, broadly labeled social thought, inherently involves cognition concerning not only others’ actions, but also their beliefs, intentions, emotions, and likely reactions. Experiments with members of a U.S. university community demonstrate that solitude reduces spontaneous social thought, and this is not ameliorated by the mere presence of others; rather, interaction with others precipitates spontaneous social thought (Mildner & Tamir, 2021). The COVID-19 pandemic lockdowns provided a natural experiment with which to examine the real-world effects of changes in social interaction patterns, including the effects of reduced face-to-face interaction and increased digitally-mediated communication. In a study of British adults, social isolation as a consequence of pandemic lockdowns eroded typical patterns observed in the recognition of emotional facial expressions, with larger effects evident in those who communicated with others via text rather than voice or video call (Bland et al., 2022). Likewise, a cohort study comparing U.S. students who entered college before or after the pandemic lockdowns indicates that individuals who experienced pandemic-era social deprivation subsequently exhibited lower abilities to express their emotions nonverbally and to govern their emotional displays (Cerutti et al., 2024). These studies suggest the capacities underlying effective social interaction likely exhibit the use-it-or-lose-it pattern characteristic of other aspects of the mind. While it is true that digital technologies do not necessarily result in reduced utilization of cognitive capacities, when technology use entails a reduction in the cognitive effort and skills required to perform a task, over both short and long terms, the result is skill decay (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). For skills involved in interpersonal communication and relationship formation and maintenance, such decay may be costly to health and wellbeing. Recently, Noorbehbahani and Oyibo (2026) presented a comprehensive review of concerns regarding the possible effects of AI use on cognition, and the state of the existing evidence. Although the field is changing quickly, to date there is compelling evidence that AI use can result in an erosion of critical thinking, creativity, decision-making ability, analytical thinking, expert domain-specific skills, and memory. Considering the use-it-or-lose-it nature of the capacities involved in effective and rewarding interactions with other people, there is thus substantial reason to expect that, if interacting with an SCAI places lower demands on these capacities, there is a meaningful risk of social deskilling in regard to these vital abilities. Complicating the picture further, it is possible that social deskilling may itself lead to increased SCAI adoption, as individuals with diminished capacities to manage the relational complexities required of human-human relationships may find AI companions increasingly appealing. The result may be a self-reinforcing process in which users progressively withdraw from human social relationships and rely more and more on interactions with SCAI. This feedback loop might be particularly concerning for younger individuals who are actively developing the cognitive architecture undergirding interpersonal communication and social maintenance (Kilford et al., 2016), as well as for individuals who experience difficulties in social settings, for whom skill development is an incredibly important aspect of treatment, such as those with social anxiety, ADHD, or ASD (Heller et al., 2026). Future work should identify populations disproportionately at risk of social deskilling. 

## 8.3. Privacy Risks 

While conversations with AI agents may appear to be siloed such that their contents are available only to the user and their AI companion, in reality the information disclosed during an interaction may be persistently stored, accessed by institutions, repurposed for model development, or transmitted and used in contexts far removed from the original conversation. Data sharing for model training and other third-party uses is turned on by default on leading AI platforms such as Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini (Li et al., 2026), and user controls to opt out of model training and data sharing vary between companies and are often exceedingly difficult for users to access and understand (Malki, 2026). User prompts during SCAI interaction may be used as training data for large language models, which can be partially retained in a model’s parameters. As a result, sensitive information disclosed during a conversation may be memorized and later leaked during interactions with other users, including via inference attacks and training data extraction (Brown et al., 2022; Das et al., 2025; Staab et al., 2024). Methods such as data sanitation attempt to protect privacy by removing personally identifiable information (PII) from data prior to training. However, this method requires PII to be discrete,   

> Klitgaard et al.: Preprint submitted to Elsevier Page 12 of 21 Relational Complexity and Social Companion AI

identifiable, and tied to one user (Brown et al., 2022). In reality, users of AI, particularly SCAI, disclose large amounts of deeply sensitive and personal information that would not be captured by data sanitization methods. Disclosure of sensitive information may be implicitly encouraged by the lower relational complexity of SCAI, as users feel more at ease expressing themselves when the burdens of mentalizing, social evaluation, gossip, or rejection are attenuated. Studies suggest that SCAI users are engaging in high levels of self-disclosure, particularly for information they would not be comfortable sharing with another person. This includes mental health issues and substance abuse (Bayor et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Henriksen et al., 2025; Skjuve et al., 2021, 2024; Y. Zhang et al., 2026); sexual behavior, feelings, and explicit content (De Freitas & Cohen, 2025; Henriksen et al., 2025; Skjuve et al., 2021); interpersonal problems (Bayor et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Marriott & Pitardi, 2024; Skjuve et al., 2021; Y. Zhang et al., 2026); financial issues (Bayor et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025); and physical health (Bayor et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025). While reduced concern about judgment or gossip may make these disclosures feel private, the information may still be retained, accessed, or reused in ways that contravene user privacy preferences. 

# 9. Conclusion 

The adoption of SCAI is often framed in terms of the positive features that this technology offers users: AI is agreeable, accepting, and always available. We have argued the appeal of these features may be best understood in terms of the demands and risks of human sociality. Under this framework, the lack of judgment, accessibility, and amiability of AI companions are not independent affordances, but rather are manifestations of the reduced relational complexity relative to human-human social interaction. SCAI is appealing precisely because it offers features of companionship ordinarily inseparable from this relational complexity. We have identified six domains of relational complexity relevant to SCAI adoption: (i) relationship formation and dissolution; (ii) achieving mutual understanding; (iii) interpersonal motives and strategic uncertainty; (iv) social partner evaluation and self-presentation; (v) social embeddedness and information flow; and (vi) reciprocity and relationship maintenance. These features are interrelated, and uniting them under one framework helps to explain how structural features of AI companionship and integral features of human psychology work together to drive SCAI use. While reduced relational complexity may be appealing due to its relative ease and lack of risk, adoption of social companions who lack the indelible features of human sociality may stifle the development and maintenance of skills necessary to succeed in the social world, while the deep levels of disclosure characteristic of SCAI use create privacy risks that may be obscured by the faux dyadic relationship. Although these potential outcomes remain to be tested, they follow from the same overarching feature that makes SCAI so compelling: reduced relational complexity offers some affordances of social interaction while minimizing the demands and risks of relating to another person. Our approach is generative of multiple directions for future research. While we have pointed to suggestive evidence that indicates reduced relational complexity motivates user adoption of SCAI, much of this work relies on self-report data from qualitative studies. As such, experimental work is needed to test the effect of measurable reductions in features of relational complexity on user perceptions and related behavior. We have also outlined a series of predictions pertaining to the factors that drive users to turn to AI as a social companion. Additional research is needed to discern how the attenuation of specific sites of relational complexity affects SCAI engagement. This is particularly true for the costs of relationship formation, as well as for the cognitive burden of mentalizing and calculating social risk during interactions. At present, these two sites of complexity have received relatively little attention from scholars of SCAI. In addition, we suggest that research dealing with one aspect of relational complexity in human-AI interaction should consider how other related dimensions of relational complexity may affect user behavior and cognition. Finally, in light of the possible negative outcomes associated with extended SCAI use that we have described, future work should investigate the potentially heightened impacts of engaging with artificial social actors during childhood, a particularly critical period for social skill acquisition and relationship formation.
