# When Do Humans and Machines Truly Complement Each Other? Formal Conditions and Empirical Evidence for Human-AI Synergy 

Andrea Campagner, Federico Cabitza, Vincenzo Crupi 

Abstract —Can humans and artificial intelligence cooperate such that their combination yields greater accuracy than either agent alone? This result, termed synergy or complementarity , is notoriously difficult to achieve, even when both agents possess significant independent accuracy. In this paper, we present a formal framework to model human-AI interaction through the lens of reliance patterns, which characterize the joint correct-ness structure of human and algorithmic decisions. We derive mathematically necessary and sufficient conditions for synergy, demonstrating that its emergence depends on the ability of either agent to identify and correct the errors of the other, what we call Human and Algorithmic Correction. Beyond its theoretical contribution, the proposed framework provides a foundation for the design and evaluation of knowledge-based decision support systems, where interaction policies must dynamically integrate heterogeneous sources of expertise. We demonstrate this through the analysis of seven clinical case studies. Our empirical results reveal a “dominance trap,” where a strong algorithmic advantage can paradoxically inhibit synergy by eroding human corrective intervention. We also show that synergy can persist even in the presence of cognitive biases, such as automation bias, provided there is functional de-correlation between human and AI errors. These results provide a formal foundation for key concepts in the human-AI systems literature and offer actionable insights for designing interaction-centric AI systems that foster comple-mentarity. 

Index Terms —Human-AI Synergy, Human-AI Complemen-tarity, Human-AI Interaction, Reliance Patterns, Metacognition, Dominance Trap 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# ARTIFICIAL Intelligence is being increasingly advocated and employed as a tool to support judgment and decision making in an impressive variety of domains. The breakthrough of contemporary AI has revitalized and reshaped two long-standing inquiries: first, how human judgment compares with algorithmic outcomes across diverse contexts; and second, under what conditions the integration of these two assessments can reliably yield superior accuracy. Current theoretical understanding appears to be rather strong for the first issue, but still limited for the second [1, 2]. Crucially, a recent comprehensive review [3] notes that we still lack a broad conceptual understanding of when human-AI teams outperform their individual components (“when a combination of humans and AI are better than either alone”). From a philosophy-of-technology perspective, recent work [6] has argued that the explanatory gap surrounding human–AI synergy is partly conceptual: synergy is not merely a stand-alone, post hoc indicator of predictive performance, but rather a form of defeasible evidence that a given human–AI interac-tion constitutes a reliable epistemic process. Complementing this view, recent contributions in the human–machine systems literature [5] emphasize that synergy is not merely an outcome, but a design-dependent property emerging from the interaction between human and algorithmic components: differences in information, capabilities, and error structures between humans and AI—-rather than overall accuracy alone—-constitute the primary sources of complementarity and must be explicitly leveraged through appropriate interaction mechanisms. Taken together, these perspectives suggest that understanding synergy requires moving beyond purely outcome-based definitions toward a unified account that integrates interaction design and the structure of human–AI error complementarities. From a more technical perspective, the scientific community has only recently begun to address this issue by investigating theoretical formulations of synergy, and three main strands of research can be identified, which, however, have been relatively isolated from each other. Most of the work in this setting has been developed in the theoretical machine learning literature and has focused on the study and formal analysis of the deferral paradigm, that is, developing AI systems that can defer their judgment to a human. One of the seminal contributions in this field is the work of Madras et al. [7] who proposed and first analyzed the effects of deferral on human-AI collaboration relying on the framework of selective prediction in machine learning literature. Developing this con-tribution, Charusaie et al. [8] studied the theoretical properties of deferral learning systems trained to be complementary to humans; Straitouri et al. [9] similarly considered how synergy could be achieved through conformal prediction (a family of statistical techniques to create deferral AI systems); De Toni et al. [10] studied the computational complexity of achieving complementary through deferral, showing that in general the problem is computationally infeasible. Although this body of work characterizes conditions for synergy, it remains largely inapplicable to traditional AI systems that provide judgments without deferral. Furthermore, these studies emphasize the algorithmic perspective, leaving the more interactional dimen-sions of the collaboration relatively unexplored. Crucially, the deferral paradigm focuses on a logic of delegation , where the final judgment is produced by only one of the two agents. arXiv:2610.27965v1 [cs.AI] 20 Oct 2026 IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON HUMAN-MACHINE SYSTEMS, VOL. In contrast, our work addresses judgment integration (or co-agency), where both human and AI assessments are available and must be combined. A different stream of work, more focused on the interac-tional aspects, has aimed at explicitly studying combination mechanisms to model the interaction between AI and human agents. In this light, the Bayesian paradigm, which models both human and AI reasoning as well as their combination as parametric probabilistic models and employs empirical interaction data to fit their parameters, has been dominant, partly due to the already rich literature on probabilistic models of human cognition [11]. Steyvers et al. [12] proposed a Bayesian framework to model and combine the predictions of human and AI agents; Westby et al. [13] studied a Bayesian theory of mind framework for modeling collective intelligence (as a special form of synergy) in human-AI teams. While these studies include a representation of the cognitive processes underlying human judgment, the framework we adopt in this work does not involve such commitment. This approach allows us to directly address the constructs relevant to human-AI in-teraction as they emerge from formal probabilistic constraints. An additional limitation of Bayesian approaches is their focus on modeling joint performance rather than identifying the foundational conditions for synergy; consequently, the require-ments for synergy emerge only as indirect byproducts of their parametric fits, rather than as explicit theoretical constraints. Moreover, they often rest on strong assumptions (e.g., normal-ity, or specific constraints on combination functions), which limit their ability to yield actionable insights, particularly when such assumptions are violated. To address these gaps and more in line with the approach we take in this paper, a recent strand of work in the study of human-AI interaction mechanisms has aimed at characterizing conditions under which synergy can (or cannot) emerge by adopting a more general (model-free) probabilistic framework. Donahue et al. [14] studied equivalent definitions of synergy when the combination of AI and human agents is realized by linear combination rules, also providing characterizations of cases in which synergy is impossible to achieve. The study analyzed synergy solely within the constraints of linear combination rules, which assume fixed weights for each agent. Such models may fail to capture the non-linear and context-dependent interactions typical of work practices, where a human expert evaluates the quality of an AI signal on a case-by-case basis. More recently, Peng et al. [15] studied no-free lunch theorems for human-AI synergy, showing that it is generally impossible to achieve synergy if the combination rule only takes into account the overall accuracy of the human and AI agents. By shifting the focus from global perfor-mance metrics to the granular dynamics of reliance patterns (specifically Human Correction and Algorithmic Correction, as defined below), we demonstrate that synergy is not only theoretically possible but also empirically predictable when the interaction logic exploits and fosters the mutual reduction of errors on a case-by-case basis. The goal of this contribution is to advance insight on these matters: to do so, we adopt a probabilistic approach, similar to those previously considered in [14, 15], which aims at address-ing the limitations of previous analytical studies on human-AI synergy. In contrast to prior research, our framework avoids parametric assumptions regarding both how agents formulate decisions and how their judgments are combined, and instead adopts a non-parametric and model-free approach. Thus, we make two main contributions.

II. T HE FORMAL FRAMEWORK 

In this section, we introduce a formal framework for modeling human–AI interaction. This framework is intention-ally model-agnostic and non-parametric: rather than assuming specific cognitive or algorithmic processes, the framework captures interaction in terms of observable variables—namely, the correctness of human judgments, AI outputs, and final decisions. This abstraction enables the framework to be ap-plied across different domains and system implementations, supporting both theoretical analysis and empirical evaluation. Formally, let X ∈ { x, ¬x} be a binary variable representing the ground-truth state of a situation of interest S. For instance, 

x may denote the presence of a specific pathology in a patient’s tissue (where the patient case constitutes S), while 

¬x denotes its absence. A human user (e.g., a clinician) typically assesses the validity of x against ¬x based on a complex and often heuristic array of information indicators. Let H ∈ {h, ¬h} include the possible outcomes of such human expert’s judgment process about X. For simplicity, we write H = X to mean that either (x ∧ h) or (¬x ∧ ¬ h)

holds. Consider also an artificial system designed to similarly make a decision about the situation of interest (e.g., in our recurring example about clinical-decision making, detecting specific pathologies) by processing fine-grained data extracted from each case. For our current purposes, we can think of an algorithm devised through machine learning to deliver an outcome in the form of a further binary variable A ∈ { a, ¬a}.Finally, when both H and A are available, a system (or human agent) integrates them into an overall decision F ∈{f, ¬f }. B EYOND AUTOMATION : A CONDITION FOR SYNERGISTIC INTERACTION 

While the conditions studied in the previous section are sufficient to ensure that simple combination rules achieve non-trivial levels of accuracy, they do not, in general, guarantee that the resulting human–AI system outperforms its individual components. In this section, to address this gap, we move beyond this default setting to identify specific conditions under which synergy can emerge. In particular, we shift the focus from general accuracy properties to the structure of disagreements and error correction mechanisms between human and AI agents. Bridging these perspectives is important for both theoretical and practical reasons. V. C OGNITIVE AND METACOGNITIVE CORRELATES OF 

HUMAN CORRECTION 

The notion of Human Correction (HC), introduced in Sec-tion 4, captures a key aspect of the human contribution to Synergy: the ability of a human decision-maker to correctly override an algorithmic output when the latter is wrong. Formally, HC is satisfied when, conditional on a disagreement between human and algorithmic assessments, the final decision 

F follows the human judgment H more often when the AI is wrong, than when it is correct. In other words, HC expresses a systematic capability to detect and correct algorithmic errors. Beyond its formal definition, this property has a precise cognitive interpretation. HC can be viewed as the behavioral manifestation of a second-order competence, namely metacog-nitive sensitivity — the capacity to monitor and evaluate one’s own confidence and the reliability of external sources. In the context of human–AI interaction, metacognitive sensitivity supports an adaptive balance between reliance and autonomy: it allows the human agent to infer, from contextual cues and subjective uncertainty, when the algorithm is likely to be wrong and when it can be trusted. This self-monitoring faculty is pivotal to Synergy, empowering humans to provide informed interventions instead of arbitrary rejection. In the literature on metacognition, such sensitivity is often quantified through Type 2 receiver operating characteristic 

(Type 2 ROC) analysis, which assesses the correspondence between confidence judgments and actual correctness [24]. A high area under the Type 2 ROC curve indicates that con-fidence reliably tracks accuracy, implying that the agent can discriminate between correct and incorrect decisions. Trans-lating this into our probabilistic framework, a high metacog-nitive sensitivity corresponds to a high conditional probability 

Pr( H = X | high confidence ) and, consequently, can increase the likelihood that the HC condition holds in practice, insofar as it enables selective overriding when contextual cues indicate that the algorithm may be wrong. However, we note that HC is a behavioral property of the interaction protocol and cannot be reduced to confidence discrimination alone. This interpretation also connects HC to the notion of 

confidence calibration , a classical measure of the alignment between subjective confidence and objective accuracy. Poor calibration can lead either to automation bias , when over-confidence in the AI diminishes corrective intervention, or to self-anchoring bias (also called algorithmic aversion [25] or conservatism bias [16]), when underconfidence prevents beneficial reliance. By contrast, well-calibrated metacognitive monitoring fosters appropriate reliance which, under certain conditions (as shown in Theorem 3), can lead to synergistic ef-fects. Thus, while Algorithmic Correction (AC) depends on the statistical superiority of the model ( Algorithmic Advantage ), Human Correction (HC) depends on the human’s ability to track and regulate the reliability of both agents in interaction. This perspective underscores that synergy is not solely a product of informational overlap, but also of the hu-man’s metacognitive competence to exploit that complemen-tarity [12]. This competence is part and parcel of the cognitive capability that has been also denoted as teaming intelligence ,that is the kind of intelligence and competence needed to effectively work with AI systems [26], and theory of ma-chine [27, 28], that is a practical understanding of how the AI system behaves, including its strengths, limitations, and typical failure modes (i.e., a form of Theory of Mind directed toward machines). These “theories”, while not necessarily technically accurate, are pragmatically valuable for interpreting the sys-tem’s behavior, evaluating the reliability of its outputs, and determining when to rely on or disregard its suggestions [29]. Accordingly, the empirical detection of Synergy should include measures of metacognitive sensitivity (e.g., confidence discrimination, Type 2 ROC area, appropriate reliance score), human-AI agreement (e.g., influence index), and calibration (ECE, ECI), alongside standard accuracy metrics. Integrat-ing these psychometric and behavioral constructs into the probabilistic framework developed in this paper provides a bridge between formal models of judgment combination and cognitive theories of self-monitoring, offering a richer account of when and why genuine Human–AI Synergy can emerge. VII. D ISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS FOR THE DESIGN OF 

AI-BASED DECISION SUPPORT 

Our theoretical and empirical findings support a central con-clusion: synergy is not primarily determined by the standalone accuracy of human or AI agents, but is instead an emergent property of the interaction policy that governs how their judgments are combined. This perspective shifts the focus of system design from improving individual models in isolation to engineering interaction mechanisms that enable effective collaboration between humans and AI systems. A key implication of our results is that increasing the per-formance of an AI system alone is not sufficient to guarantee improved system-level outcomes. In fact, the prevalence of the “dominance trap” in several of the clinical case studies, sug-gests that strong AI performance can inadvertently suppress human involvement in the decision process, in particular when the human user owns the final process of decision combination. When users become overly reliant on the AI, opportunities for human correction diminish, and the system as a whole may fail to exploit complementary strengths. This highlights the importance of designing interaction protocols that preserve an active and meaningful role for human judgment, even when AI performance is high. A practical line of intervention would consist of avoiding creating black-box systems that merely provide a final label. Instead, AI tools should be designed to enhance users’ understanding of their outputs. However, this does not entail aligning AI system design uncritically with XAI research, which maintains that it is important for AI systems to “explain” what they do. A growing body of literature (e.g., [34, 35, 36]) has already warned about the risks of machine explanations increasing deference and automation bias, precisely because of their persuasive power. In this sense, our framework suggests treating explanations as reliability metadata that inform users at the level of individual classifications and local predictions [37], or as alternative hypotheses [38, 39] to consider in order to gain a better under-standing of the matter at hand. In doing so, AI systems would promote users’ metacognitive sensitivity [40]—for instance, by highlighting areas of high uncertainty or providing cues that enable humans to effectively perform Human Correction. Furthermore, while we note that enforcing the agreement condition is trivial from a systems-design perspective 5, the evidence that Synergy can emerge even in the absence of such conditions suggests that the goal of a hybrid system should be “functional de-correlation”, as this latter can enforce even

larger gains in performance than merely relying on interaction policy that are solely relegated to cases of disagreement. This insight has different implications depending on whether the combination rule is realized by human or, rather, through computational means. In the first case, human training can be easily seen to be a fundamental dimension for enabling synergy: users of AI-based decision support systems should develop a properly calibrated theory of machine. Training programs that expose users to examples of AI errors, or that simulate disagreement scenarios, can help improve calibration and support more effective reliance behavior. Such training can enhance the ability of users to detect when the AI is likely to be correct and when it should be overridden. By contrast, in the second case, our results imply that AI systems should be optimized not to simulate human reasoning—whatever this may mean—but rather to develop a distinct perspective on the phenomena of interest [41]. In this sense the conditions of Human and Algorithmic Correction, as well as the margin con-ditions given in Theorem 4, provide natural learning objectives to be used to to tailor a policy implementing the combination rule. More in general, and in both cases, our results also imply that designers should focus on identifying and supporting the human’s ability to act as a diagnostic filter, directing attention to cases in which the AI is most likely to fail (the “broken leg” cases) and protecting expertise from skill atrophy. This latter concern suggests designing and implementing human–AI interaction protocols aimed at “slowing down” users and making interaction with AI systems more effortful [42]—so-called inhibition protocols, that are, protocols that deliberately introduce a form of cognitive friction [43], such as requiring users to provide their judgment before being exposed to the machine’s advice [32], or having the AI system abstain from providing any advice at all when the risk of broken leg cases is too high [44], with goal of making engagement with AI a more deliberative and reflective (rather than automatic) process. At a broader level, governance and deployment strategies should take into account not only the accuracy of individual components but also the quality of their interaction. Monitor-ing system performance should include metrics derived from reliance patterns, disagreement rates, and correction frequen-cies. These indicators provide insight into whether the system operates in a regime conducive to synergy, and can reveal whether interaction policies are functioning as intended. Over time, such monitoring can support continuous improvement of both the AI system and the surrounding interaction protocols. In summary, achieving synergy requires a coordinated ap-proach that integrates model development, interface design, IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON HUMAN-MACHINE SYSTEMS, VOL. X, NO. X, MARCH 2026 9

interaction protocols, and user training. Rather than focusing solely on maximizing standalone performance, system de-signers should aim to create environments in which human and AI agents can effectively complement each other. This involves promoting structured interaction, designing princi-pled disagreement-resolution policies, supporting metacogni-tive awareness, and encouraging functional differences that allow each agent to correct the other. Under these conditions, synergy emerges not as an accidental outcome but as a design-enabled property of the human-AI system. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

This paper has developed a probabilistic framework to for-malize the elusive notion of human-AI synergy. Our analysis demonstrates that while Algorithmic Advantage and Human Augmentation are frequent benchmarks in the literature, true Synergy is a more rigorous condition, mathematically tied to the existence of effective Human Correction (HC) and Algorithmic Correction (AC) mechanisms. By grounding these concepts in the formal calculus of reliance patterns, we have provided a principled basis to move beyond simple accuracy metrics toward a deeper understanding of hybrid decisional systems, as well as highlighted significant implications for the design of hybrid human-AI systems. Our empirical investigation across seven clinical datasets confirms that Synergy is not a guaranteed outcome of AI in-tegration. The observed “dominance trap” in high-performing AI settings and the compensatory role of HC and AC high-light that the path to Synergy is mediated by the human’s metacognitive sensitivity and the team’s ability to functionally de-correlate errors. These findings suggest that the future of AI design should prioritize the calibration of the human-AI interaction over the mere maximization of model performance. In conclusion, the pursuit of Synergy, although more chal-lenging than plain automation, is shown to be a mathematically sound and empirically attainable goal. The formal conditions identified here provide researchers and practitioners with a roadmap for designing and evaluating hybrid systems that do not merely supplement human judgment but transcend the limitations of both human and artificial intelligence, to realize the potential of a hybrid intelligence .REFERENCES
