Abstract 

Depression assessment from counseling speech requires integrating textual and acoustic psy-chological evidence across turns, yet existing end-to-end classification approaches lack ex-plicit multimodal evidence interpretation and context-aware reasoning. We propose AudiPsy, an evidence-grounded omni-LLM for depres-sion assessment over spoken counseling con-versations. AudiPsy employs an Observation– Assessment–Summary (OAS) paradigm to in-tegrate dialogue transcripts, textual and acous-tic emotions, and textual–acoustic emotion in-congruence into structured reasoning. OAS incrementally organizes multimodal observa-tions, performs contextualized assessment, and maintains an evolving dialogue summary for subsequent turns. To support this framework, we construct AudiPsy-Counsel, a synthetic spo-ken counseling conversation corpus with mul-timodal context augmentation, and develop a unified three-stage curriculum for depression-oriented reasoning. Experiments on DAIC-WOZ demonstrate superior depression assess-ment performance over specialized depres-sion detection methods and general-purpose speech/omni-LLMs, along with improved rea-soning quality, while supplementary evaluation on EATD-Corpus supports AudiPsy’s applica-bility to naturalistic conversations. 

1 Introduction 

Depression affects more than 322 million people worldwide and remains one of the leading causes of disability (World Health Organization, 2026). De-pression assessment is typically conducted through counseling conversations, where human counselors observe seekers’ behaviors, interpret cues, accu-mulate evidence and reach a judgment (Mao et al., 2023). Recent advances in AI for medical diagno-sis have shifted from prediction toward evidence-based reasoning (Wang et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 

> *Corresponding authors

2026). In the domain of mental health, automated depression assessment similarly calls for evidence-grounded multimodal reasoning across evolving spoken conversations. Existing depression detection systems predom-inantly formulate the assessment as a multimodal classification problem, directly mapping linguis-tic and acoustic representations to diagnostic la-bels (Wu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024b, 2025). Although effective on benchmark datasets, this paradigm suffers from two limitations. First, at the representation level , most multimodal methods rely on low-level acoustic features or latent repre-sentations whose psychological meaning remains largely implicit. Second, at the process level , ex-isting approaches typically aggregate multimodal representations into a holistic prediction without interpreting them under the conversational context. These two limitations are coupled: the model can-not state what it observes if it relies on implicit representations; the model cannot explain how the observed evidence supports its assessment without an interpretive process. We therefore address them jointly. For what the model reasons over, we ground the assessment in affective evidence: textual emo-tion, acoustic emotion, and the incongruence be-tween the two, expressed as affective concepts in natural language so that an LLM can reason over them. Psychological studies have shown that in-dividuals with depression often exhibit atypical emotional patterns (Clark and Watson, 1991), and inconsistency between semantic and acoustic emo-tion, as described by the emotion context insensi-tivity (ECI) theory (Bylsma et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2024). Recent studies have observed that modeling this emotional incongruence improves depression detection performance (Su et al., 2026). This evi-dence is psychologically interpretable and naturally amenable to language-model reasoning. lowing the structure of clinical progress notes (Weed, 1968), we introduce the Ob-servation–Assessment–Summary (OAS) paradigm for contextual reasoning over multi-turn spoken counseling conversations. At each conversa-tional turn, OAS extracts structured semantic and acoustic observations (O), interprets them in light of accumulated dialogue context and textual–acoustic emotion incongruence (A), and updates a compact dialogue summary (S) to guide subsequent assessment. This incremental process preserves the contextual meaning of psychological evidence while reducing the burden of long-context reasoning for current omni-LLMs (Chen et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). To this end, we propose AudiPsy , an omni-LLM for depression assessment, which explic-itly reasons over spoken counseling conversa-tions in terms of multimodal affective evidence based on the proposed Observation–Assessment– Summary (OAS) reasoning paradigm. To over-come the scarcity of real-world counseling speech corpus, we further develop a counseling context augmentation pipeline and a unified three-stage curriculum for depression-oriented reasoning. Ex-periments on DAIC-WOZ (Gratch et al., 2014), fea-turing structured spoken counseling conversations, demonstrate superior depression assessment and reasoning performance. Additional evaluation on EATD-Corpus (Shen et al., 2022a), comprising nat-uralistic conversations, further examines AudiPsy’s applicability across conversational settings. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose AudiPsy , an evidence-grounded omni-LLM for depression assessment through structured OAS reasoning. • We construct AudiPsy-Counsel , a bilingual synthetic counseling speech corpus with OAS annotations and controlled emotion-incongruence cases. • We develop a unified three-stage curriculum for Depression-oriented Reasoning , integrat-ing affective perception, structured reasoning, and preference alignment. • Experiments demonstrate improved depres-sion assessment and reasoning quality, while analyses reveal how AudiPsy perceive, utilize, and interpret affective evidence. 

2 Related Work 

Automated depression assessment over spoken counseling conversations leverages linguistic, acoustic, and multimodal signals to identify men-tal health markers. Existing methods can be broadly categorized into three areas: (1) Text-based approaches utilize transcripts to capture depres-sive states. Early methods rely on affective lex-icons or neural representations (Mallol-Ragolta et al., 2019; Xezonaki et al., 2020), while re-cent frameworks employ large language models (LLMs) through prompting and instruction tun-ing (Xu et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). (2) Speech-based approaches identify depression-related cues from acoustic characteristics such as prosody and spectral features (Sardari et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2023), with recent methods incorporating data aug-mentation and retrieval-based reasoning to improve robustness (Liang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). (3) Multimodal approaches integrate linguistic and acoustic signals through feature fusion (Shen et al., 2022a; Chen et al., 2024) or LLM-based mod-eling of textual and speech-derived representa-tions (Zhang et al., 2024b, 2025). Despite this progress, existing methods predominantly formu-late depression assessment as end-to-end classifi-cation, leaving the psychological meaning of mul-timodal representations largely implicit and lack-ing contextualized interpretation of evidence as the conversation unfolds. 

3 Methodology 

As illustrated in Figure 1, AudiPsy combines the OAS reasoning paradigm with counseling context augmentation and a three-stage training curriculum for depression-oriented reasoning. Together, these components enable interpretable, context-aware multimodal reasoning for depression assessment.

3.2 Counseling Context Augmentation 

Real-world speech-based counseling data is ex-tremely scarce due to privacy concerns and acqui-sition costs, while text-only counseling resources are considerably abundant, but lack the affective context and its acoustic realization required for mul-timodal depression reasoning. To bridge this gap, we formulate data construction as a context aug-mentation problem. Starting from publicly avail-able text-only counseling records, we progressively enrich them along three orthogonal dimensions: 

situational, affective, and acoustic , so that each resulting sample supplies the contextual evidence consumed by the OAS paradigm, as shown in Fig-ure 1b. Although synthetic, the resulting dataset, AudiPsy-Counsel, provides structured supervision for learning how to perceive and interpret multi-modal evidence to support depression assessment across dialogue turns. 

Situational augmentation. The seed corpus is an union of two public text counseling cor-pora, CounselChat (Bertagnolli, 2020) and CPsy-CounR (Zhang et al., 2024a). We employ GPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025) as a situational re-writer which maps each counseling record into a bilingual di-alogue transcript Odt between a counselor and a seeker. The rewriting preserves the original clini-cal issue while diversifying emotional conditions and interaction trajectories, resulting in richer situ-ational variations while preserving the natural dis-tribution of counseling topics, including depres-sion, relationships, self-development, and more (Figure 2). 

Affective annotation. For each utterance, GPT-5 also acts as an affective annotator to produce textual and acoustic emotion annotations Ote

Together, these an-notations provide explicit ground-truth multimodal evidence including textual–acoustic emotion incon-gruence, which is rarely available and difficult to verify in organic data. Conditioned on the annotated Ot, Aei t , and St−1,together with the original counseling record, the LLM generates the depression assessment Adep t fol-lowing a reasoning skill informed by the ECI theory and PHQ-8 diagnostic algorithm (Kroenke et al., 2009). Since each assessment is conditioned on ex-plicit observations and guided by these principles, the resulting reasoning is grounded in multimodal evidence. Consequently, each dialogue turn is an-notated under the complete OAS paradigm. 

Acoustic realization. The annotated scripts are rendered into emotional speech with the prompt-controlled text-to-speech synthesis model Qwen3-TTS (Hu et al., 2026), where dialogue transcripts 

Odt t serve as synthesis inputs, the acoustic emotion descriptions Oae t serve as speech-style prompts, and the speaker gender g serves as the speaker selection reference. Consequently, the pipeline yields AudiPsy-Counsel, denoted as Dapc , a synthetic bilingual speech-counseling dataset with 2,400 conversations for downstream model training. 3.3 Depression-oriented Reasoning 

To equip Fθ with the capability of evidence-grounded multimodal reasoning for depression as-sessment, we optimize it through a three-stage train-ing curriculum, as illustrated in Figure 1c. The curriculum progressively develops affective percep-tion, structured counseling reasoning, and correc-tive preference alignment. 

Stage-1: Affective perception. We first train 

Fθ to learn the observation field Ot from emo-tional speech to enable robust multimodal affec-tive perception under both emotion-congruent and emotion-incongruent conditions. Specifically, we use IEMOCAP (Busso et al., 2008) as the sponta-neous corpus, where we annotate the textual polar-ity of each utterance and construct a balanced train-ing set by randomly downsampling each textual– acoustic emotion label combination. We further construct a Synthetic Emotion-Incongruence (SynEI) dataset, a depression-related emotional speech corpus derived from the train-ing split of DAIC-WOZ and EATD-Corpus, by altering the speech emotion through speech re-synthesis (Zhou et al., 2026) while preserving the original text and speaker characteristics. Construc-tion and dataset details are provided in Appen-dices C and D. Accordingly, the Stage-1 objective is defined as: 

LS1 = −EDemo log pθ(Ot | xt). (3) 

Stage-2: Structured counseling reasoning. We then continue supervised fine-tuning on AudiPsy-Counsel Dapc to adapt the model to depression-oriented counseling reasoning. Fθ learns the com-plete OAS reasoning process with the Stage-2 ob-jective defined as: 

LS2 = − EDapc log pθ (Ot, A t, S t | xt, S t−1) .

(4) Stage-3: Corrective preference alignment. Fi-nally, we align Fθ on real depression-assessment data (DAIC-WOZ; EATD-Corpus), denoted as 

Dreal . Considering its limited scale, we adopt the lightweight ORPO objective (Hong et al., 2024), which jointly performs supervised learning and preference optimization in a single stage. For EATD-Corpus, the counselor speech signals ct are reconstructed using Qwen-TTS, as they are unavail-able in the original dataset. During ORPO train-ing, the Stage-2 output is treated as the rejected response, while a preferred response is regener-ated using a pretrained speech emotion recognition (SER) model (Sailor et al., 2025) together with the AudiPsy-Counsel affective annotation pipeline conditioned on the ground-truth depression label. The model is optimized using the standard ORPO objective: 

LORPO = EDreal [LSFT + λLOR ] . (5) 

4 Experiments 

We evaluate AudiPsy from three perspectives: (1) depression assessment and human reasoning qual-ity evaluation on DAIC-WOZ; (2) supplementary depression assessment on EATD-Corpus; and (3) multimodal evidence perception, utilization, and interpretation through speech emotion recognition, evidence utilization analysis, and reasoning case studies. 

Training AudiPsy is initialized from Qwen2.5-Omni-7B (Xu et al., 2025a) and trained with the proposed three-stage curriculum using the ms-swift framework (Zhao et al., 2024) on 4 NVIDIA H800 GPUs. Stage-1 and -2 perform LoRA-based su-pervised fine-tuning on the affective perception corpus Demo (2,109 samples) and the AudiPsy-Counsel corpus Dapc (2,400 samples), respectively. Stage 3 further aligns the model using ORPO on the depression-assessment corpus Dreal (280 con-versations after conversation-level resampling to mitigate class imbalance). 

Inference During inference, AudiPsy performs turn-by-turn reasoning, conditioning each turn on the previous summary. Dialogue-level predictions are obtained by majority voting, with ties resolved as yes . Greedy decoding (temperature = 0) en-sures deterministic evaluation. Following prior work (Wu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024b, 2025), we evaluate on the DAIC-WOZ development set (33 conversations: 12 depressed, 21 non-depressed) for direct comparability. Separately, we evaluate on the EATD-Corpus validation set (79 conversations: 11 depressed, 68 non-depressed) to assess perfor-mance across conversational settings. Further im-plementation details and prompts are provided in Appendix E. 

4.1 Depression Assessment Performance Baselines We compare AudiPsy against repre-sentative speech-based binary depression classifi-cation methods, including BiLSTM+GRU (Shen et al., 2022b) and WavLM Layer 10 (Wu et al., 2023), as well as multi-stage LLM pipelines, in-cluding Speech Landmarks+Llama2 (Zhang et al., 2024b) and SpeechT-RAG (Zhang et al., 2025). We reproduce BiLSTM+GRU using its official imple-mentation and test set, while reporting published single-model F1 results for the other methods. For speech LLMs, we select models of compa-rable scale to AudiPsy’s Qwen2.5-Omni-7B back-bone (Ding et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026). For omni-LLMs, we include the Gemini 2.5 series (Co-manici et al., 2025) as representative proprietary models, together with Qwen2.5-Omni-7B (Xu et al., 2025a) and the larger Qwen3-Omni-30B se-ries (Xu et al., 2025b) from the same Omni family. A subset of these models is additionally evaluated under the OAS paradigm without task-specific fine-tuning. All evaluated models are listed in Table 1. 

Metrics We evaluate AudiPsy from two perspec-tives: depression assessment performance and rea-soning quality. For depression assessment, we report Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), binary F1, recall, precision, and specificity. In ad-dition to standard binary classification metrics, we include MCC and specificity to provide a balanced measure under class imbalance, accounting for per-formance on both depressed and non-depressed individuals (Chicco and Jurman, 2020). For reasoning quality, four practicing psycho-logical counselors evaluate 5 methods on 10 class-balanced conversations from DAIC-WOZ, exam-ining 250 LLM outputs per evaluator. Reason-ing quality is rated along four dimensions: Evi-dence Grounding (EG), Clinical Plausibility (CP), Multi-Modal Integration (MMI), and Contextual Evidence Integration (CEI). Evaluators assigned integer scores on a 1–5 scale. To accommo-date dimension-specific rating baselines, we re-port mean-centered scores and win–tie–loss results across methods for each dimension. Main results As shown in Table 1, AudiPsy at-tains the highest MCC, F1, and precision, out-performing specialized speech-based methods and general-purpose speech and omni-LLMs on DAIC-WOZ. Notably, Kimi-Audio fails to generate out-puts for long audio inputs, while Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Thinking frequently declines to make depression predictions. Simply applying OAS to off-the-shelf models does not consistently improve performance. Al-though OAS explicitly structures multimodal evi-dence, Qwen2.5-Omni-7B often produces inaccu-rate observations and assessments, while Gemini 2.5 Pro and Qwen3-Omni-Instruct exhibit high re-call but limited MCC, suggesting a tendency to-ward positive depression predictions. These results reinforce the coupling between evidence represen-tation and contextual interpretation: structured rea-soning alone is insufficient without reliable affec-tive perception and task-specific training. 

Ablation study We conduct six ablation experi-ments to examine the contributions of the training curriculum, emotion incongruence, and the struc-tured OAS representation. Removing individual training objectives or their combinations generally degrades performance, showing the necessity of the full three-stage curriculum. In particular, removing 

LS1 leads to a clear performance drop, highlighting the importance of affective perception for subse-quent depression reasoning. Removing emotion incongruence also substantially degrades perfor-mance, demonstrating its importance as a source of multimodal evidence. We further analyze their effects on affective per-ception and evidence utilization, respectively, in Section 4.4. We also rewrite the structured OAS fields into an unstructured natural-language ratio-nale while preserving the same evidence content and passing it across dialogue turns during training. The resulting performance degradation shows that the benefit of OAS goes beyond rationale content it-self, with its explicit structure facilitating evidence organization and information maintenance across dialogue turns.

5 Conclusion 

We presented AudiPsy, an omni-LLM for de-pression assessment based on the Observation– Assessment–Summary (OAS) reasoning paradigm. By progressively accumulating multimodal evi-dence and modeling textual–acoustic emotion in-congruence, AudiPsy demonstrates the potential of evidence-grounded multimodal reasoning for interpretable depression assessment. Limitations 

Evaluation Data and Protocols. Our evalua-tion is confined to DAIC-WOZ and EATD-Corpus, which are currently the only publicly available speech datasets for depression assessment. Large-scale speech counseling datasets with depression labels remain scarce due to privacy concerns and high acquisition costs, limiting our ability to further evaluate the generalizability of our findings. Nev-ertheless, evaluating on these widely used bench-marks aligns with established practices in the field. We follow the official train/test splits of both datasets, resulting in unavoidable class imbalance in testing. Besides, some prior studies on EATD-Corpus adopt random three-fold evaluation (Shen et al., 2022a), this setting is not readily applicable to our multi-stage training and alignment pipeline. Consequently, metrics reported under these differ-ent evaluation protocols could not be directly com-pared numerically. 

Synthetic Counseling Data. AudiPsy-Counsel is constructed by augmenting real-world textual counseling records with affective annotations and synthesized speech. Its acoustic realizations may therefore differ from naturally occurring counsel-ing speech in acoustic variability and emotional expression. AudiPsy-Counsel is primarily intended to provide controlled supervision for learning struc-tured reasoning and interpreting multimodal affec-tive evidence, rather than to approximate the distri-bution of real clinical speech. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work is intended solely for research and ex-perimental evaluation and should not be used for real-world clinical diagnosis or treatment decisions. Although AudiPsy is designed to reason over multi-modal evidence for depression assessment, its pre-dictions and generated rationales may contain clin-ically inappropriate interpretations. The reported performance on benchmark datasets should there-fore not be interpreted as evidence of clinical va-lidity or readiness for deployment. Depression as-sessment is a sensitive clinical task that requires qualified professional judgment, and outputs from our system should not replace evaluation by mental health professionals. Our experiments use publicly available research and synthetic datasets, which do not involve the collection of new patient data.

A Details of OAS Reasoning Paradigm 

A.1 OAS Template 

We introduce the Observation–Assessment– Summary (OAS) structured reasoning paradigm. The paradigm organizes the model output into three structured fields: Observation , Assessment , and 

Summary . This design encourages the model to ex-plicitly separate evidence extraction, cross-modal emotion incongruence analysis and depression as-sessment, along with updated conversation sum-mary during multimodal depression reasoning. The Observation records turn-level multimodal evidence, including the dialogue transcript, textual emotion and polarity, speech acoustic emotion and category label, and discretized valence–arousal– dominance (VAD) attributes for acoustic emotion. The Assessment performs higher-level reason-ing based on the extracted evidence. Specifically, the model evaluates cross-channel emotional incon-gruence between linguistic and vocal expressions, and assesses depressive tendency by considering both current-turn observations and accumulated di-alogue context. The Summary block maintains a compact conversation-level representation consisting of a natural-language conversation summary and cumu-lative affective statistics (e.g., counts of negative-polarity turns, low-valence speech turns, and emo-tionally incongruent turns). This summary is recur-sively updated after each dialogue turn and serves as the conversation context for subsequent reason-ing steps. OAS’s fields and their value are summarized in Table 5. The complete OAS template is shown below.

> [OBSERVATION] *Turn Context* Transcript: (Counselor) {question} (Seeker) {answer} *Seeker Analysis* Text Emotion={description} Text Polarity={polarity_label} Speech Emotion={description} Speech Emotion Category={category_label} VAD=({V},{A},{D}) [/OBSERVATION] [ASSESSMENT] Emotion Incongruence={Yes|No}{rationale} Depression={Yes|No}{rationale} [/ASSESSMENT] [SUMMARY_UPDATE] Turns={n} Conversation Summary={summary} Stats: Negative transcript={x1}/{n} Low valence={x2}/{n} Low arousal={x3}/{n} Low dominance={x4}/{n} Emotion incongruence={x5}/{n} [/SUMMARY_UPDATE]

A.2 Observation Field Annotation 

The Observation field contains six attributes: Di-algoue Transcript , Textual Emotion , Text Polarity ,

Acoustic Emotion , Acoustic Emotion Category , and 

VAD . Their annotation procedures are described below. 

Dialogue Transcript. Each dialogue is repre-sented as an ordered sequence of alternating utter-ances between the Counselor and the Seeker . The original speaker order is preserved. The ground truth is from the original dataset. 

Textual Emotion. The Text Emotion attribute is generated using GPT-OSS-120B (Agarwal et al., 2025) based solely on the dialogue transcript using the prompt presented in Appendix G.3 to produce a natural-language description of the textual emo-tional state. 

Text Polarity. The Text Polarity attribute classi-fies the transcript into one of three sentiment polar-ity categories: Positive , Neutral , or Negative . The annotation is performed using GPT-OSS-120B, us-ing the prompt presented in Appendix G.4. 

Acoustic Emotion. The annotation procedure de-pends on the source dataset. For AudiPsy-Counsel, the acoustic emotion description is directly ob-tained from the synthesis prompt used to generate the speech. For DAIC-WOZ and EATD-Corpus, acoustic emotion is predicted using a speech emo-tion recognition (SER) model (Sailor et al., 2025) and hold the same value as Speech Emotion Cate-gory. 

Acoustic Emotion Category. The categori-cal acoustic emotion is predicted by the SER model (Sailor et al., 2025), which outputs one of seven categories: Anger , Happy , Neutral , Sad , Sur-prise , Disgust , and Fear .

VAD. The valence, arousal, and dominance (VAD) scores are predicted as continuous values in the range [0 , 1] using the SER model (Sailor et al., 2025). Each dimension is then discretized into five equal-width intervals: [0 , 0.2) (Very Low), 

[0 .2, 0.4) (Low), [0 .4, 0.6) (Medium), [0 .6, 0.8) 

(High), and [0 .8, 1] (Very High). 

A.3 Assessment Field Annotation 

The Assessment field contains two attributes: Emo-tion Incongruence and Depression . Their annota-tion procedures are described below. 

Emotion Incongruence. The Emotion Incongru-ence attribute indicates whether an inconsistency exists between the textual and acoustic emotional expressions of the speaker. The annotation proce-dure depends on the source dataset. For AudiPsy-Counsel, the ground-truth incongruence labels are obtained during dataset construction, and GPT-5 is employed to generate a natural-language ratio-nale explaining the label using the system and user prompts in Appendix G.5. For DAIC-WOZ and EATD-Corpus, the dataset does not provide emotion incongruence labels. Therefore, GPT-5 is first required to determine whether emotion incongruence is present based on the transcript and acoustic emotion annotations, and then generate a brief rationale for its decision. We employ the system prompt described in Ap-pendix G.6, together with the same user prompt used for AudiPsy-Counsel in Appendix G.5. 

Depression. The Depression attribute indicates whether the speaker exhibits depressive symptoms and provides a corresponding explanation. For AudiPsy-Counsel, the dialogue topic serves as the depression label. For DAIC-WOZ and EATD-Corpus, the depression label is derived from the dataset questionnaire results. A skill informed by PHQ-8/SDS and the ECI theory is combined with GPT-5 to produce a natural-language explanation, with the prompts presented in Appendix G.7. 

A.4 Summary Field Annotation 

The summary field provides a compact overview of each dialogue and its overall emotional characteris-tics. The conversation summary is generated based on the complete dialogue till the current turn, using GPT-OSS-120B and the prompt presented in Ap-pendix G.8. The statistical attributes are computed by aggregating annotations across the conversation. For each emotional attribute, the corresponding statistic is reported as the ratio of utterances satis-fying the criterion (e.g., negative sentiment or low valence) to the total number of utterances, yielding a set of conversation-level summaries that can be used for efficient filtering and retrieval. B AudiPsy-Counsel Dataset Counstruction 

B.1 Situational Augmentation and Affective Annotation 

To construct the textual component of AudiPsy-Counsel, we collect source materials from two psychological counseling datasets: 

CounselChat (Bertagnolli, 2020) and CPsy-CounR (Zhang et al., 2024a). These datasets contain authentic mental health discussions and counseling reports, providing realistic scenarios for depression-oriented dialogue generation. Given each source instance, we prompt an LLM to rewrite it into a counseling dialogue between a seeker and a counselor in both English and Chinese. This process transforms static counseling records into interactive conversations suitable for subsequent speech synthesis and multimodal training. In addition to the dialogue content, the LLM generates several affective annotations required by AudiPsy. For each seeker utterance, it produces a acoustic emotion description and an emotion incon-gruence label indicating whether the vocal emotion is consistent with the semantic content. To ensure sufficient supervision for emotion incongruence reasoning, each generated dialogue contains at least one incongruent seeker utterance. The LLM also in-fers the seeker’s gender ( male , female , or unknown ), which is later used for speaker assignment during speech synthesis. The resulting dataset therefore provides counseling conversations with rich affec-tive annotations that support emotion perception, incongruence detection, and depression-oriented reasoning. We use a unified prompt (Appendices G.1 and G.2) to convert each counseling record into a three-turn dialogue with basic affective annotation. For other affective attribute annotation, please refer to Appendix A. 

B.2 Acoustic Realization 

We convert the generated counseling conversations into speech using the prompt-controlled text-to-speech system Qwen3-TTS (Hu et al., 2026). For each utterance, the dialogue text is used as the syn-thesis input, while the corresponding emotion de-scription generated during dialogue construction is provided as an instruction to control the emotional speaking style. Since Qwen3-TTS provides a fixed set of pre-defined voice identities, we assign speakers before speech synthesis. For seeker utterances, a voice is randomly sampled from the predefined speaker pool according to the predicted gender. Specifically, voices are sampled from the corresponding male or female speaker pool when the predicted gender is Male or Female , respectively, while utterances labeled as Unknown are assigned a random voice from the entire speaker pool. Counselor voices are sampled independently from the predefined speaker set. Once assigned, the same voice identity is used consistently for all utterances of the corresponding speaker throughout the dialogue. Through this process, each bilingual counsel-ing dialogue is transformed into a multi-speaker emotional speech interaction. Combined with the dialogue content, emotion descriptions, emotion incongruence labels, and speaker profiles, the re-sulting AudiPsy-Counsel dataset provides rich mul-timodal supervision for emotion perception, incon-gruence detection, and depression-oriented coun-seling analysis.

Happy , Sad , Angry , Surprised , Disgust , and Fear .We then apply a speech emotion recognition (SER) model (Sailor et al., 2025) to the synthesized au-dio. If the emotion predicted by the SER model is inconsistent with the category derived from the emotion description, the audio is discarded and re-generated. B.4 Dialogue Augmentation 

The generated counseling conversations are ini-tially constructed as three-turn conversations for efficient and consistent data generation. However, real-world counseling sessions, such as those in DAIC-WOZ, typically involve substantially longer interactions. To reduce this discrepancy, we further extend the conversations using two simple augmen-tation strategies. First, we prepend several short rapport-building exchanges (e.g., greetings and opening questions) before the original dialogue to simulate the natu-ral opening phase of counseling sessions. Second, we concatenate multiple generated conversations sharing the same counseling topic into a longer conversation. The transitions between adjacent dia-logue segments are rewritten by the LLM to ensure supportive coherence and conversational continu-ity. These dialogue augmentation strategies increase the diversity of dialogue lengths while preserving the original counseling content and affective anno-tations, enabling the synthesized corpus to better resemble real-world multi-turn counseling conver-sations.

C IEMOCAP Annotation and Filtering 

IEMOCAP (Busso et al., 2008) is a multimodal conversational emotion dataset containing natu-ral spoken interactions with manually annotated acoustic emotions, making it well suited for Stage-1 affective perception. F Human Evaluation of Reasoning 

F.1 Evaluation Protocol 

We recruited four practicing psychological coun-selors from established mental health counseling institutions as human experts to evaluate the rea-soning generated by five methods. The experts were recruited through personal referrals and were compensated based on their standard hourly con-sultation rates. The compensation was aligned with their usual professional fees to ensure fair payment for their time and expertise. All experts were in-formed of the evaluation task and compensation arrangements in advance and voluntarily agreed to participate. We collected only their evaluation scores and did not collect any personal or sensitive information. The evaluation did not involve clinical interventions or the collection of patient data. The evaluation set consists of 10 conversations randomly sampled from DAIC-WOZ, balanced be-tween depressed and non-depressed cases. Among the five methods, three follow the OAS paradigm and produce turn-level reasoning, while the other two generate a single dialogue-level reasoning out-put. We randomly sample five turn-level outputs from each OAS-based method, totaling 250 model outputs. Evaluators consider the sampled turn-level outputs jointly and assign one dialogue-level score to each method under each criterion. Thus, each evaluator provides scores for 50 method–dialogue pairs while examining 140 individual model out-puts. For each criterion, evaluators assessed dialogue-level reasoning using integer scores on a 1–5 Likert scale. They were instructed to make comparative judgments among the methods, assigning higher scores to stronger methods and lower scores to weaker ones. As a reference, a method judged slightly above the average quality of the compared methods received a 3, while one judged slightly below average received a 2. We use numerical ratings rather than rankings to capture both the rel-ative ordering of methods and the perceived mag-nitude of their differences. Accordingly, we report mean-centered scores that reflect each method’s performance relative to the average of the com-pared methods. We evaluate reasoning quality along four com-plementary dimensions. Evaluators assign higher scores when the generated reasoning better satis-fies the corresponding criterion and lower scores otherwise. 

Evidence Grounding (EG) This criterion eval-uates whether the reasoning identifies depression-relevant evidence from the input speech, such as linguistic content, emotional expression, and vo-cal affect, and grounds its assessment in observ-able evidence rather than unsupported speculation. Stronger reasoning identifies relevant evidence and clearly connects it to the assessment. 

Clinical Plausibility (CP) This criterion evalu-ates whether the reasoning is consistent with es-tablished psychological and clinical knowledge of depression. Stronger reasoning provides clinically plausible interpretations of observed symptoms, behaviors, and risk factors while avoiding unsup-ported or unreasonable clinical inferences. 

Multi-Modal Integration (MMI) This criterion evaluates whether the reasoning integrates informa-tion across modalities, particularly linguistic con-tent and vocal characteristics, rather than relying predominantly on a single modality. Stronger rea-soning considers the consistency, complementarity, or discrepancy between modalities and incorpo-rates these relationships into the assessment. 

Contextual Evidence Integration (CEI) This criterion evaluates whether the reasoning integrates evidence across the dialogue context rather than drawing conclusions from isolated observations. Stronger reasoning connects information across dialogue turns and identifies cumulative evidence, recurring patterns, or changes over the course of the interaction.

The original instruc-tion slides and criteria are included in the data sup-plementary materials to ensure transparency and reproducibility.
