Abstract | AI coworkers must coordinate tools, communicate across turns, and act on changing workspace state while respecting workflow constraints. Constructing training experience for these capabilities requires coherent requests, service records, tool interfaces, and grading checks. We present CoworkEnv , a capability-guided framework for synthesizing executable training environments for AI coworkers. Workflow requirements guide task construction, and programmatic validation with feedback repair checks generated specifications and grading behavior. Teacher executions yield curated demonstrations for supervised fine-tuning (SFT), while student interactions provide rollouts and rewards for reinforcement learning (RL). Using these resources, we train compact PocketWorker models. For Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, SFT raises the mean score from 46.6 to 64.1 on Claw-Eval, including a 31.9-point gain on Multi-turn tasks. An SFT-only extension to ClawMark demonstrates applicability across coworker benchmarks. We further compare RL training dynamics across configurations that vary reward processing and credit assignment. Improving training rewards provide preliminary evidence that the constructed environments support policy optimization. 

1. Introduction 

AI coworkers are expected to carry out everyday work through sustained interaction with users, tools, and a changing workspace. Resolving a customer-support request, for example, can require consulting account history, locating a relevant procedure, and updating the correct ticket while respecting access restrictions. Such workflows combine multi-turn communication, information gathering, tool coordination, and decisions about when to act [Trivedi et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2026b, Yao et al., 2024]. Recent coworker models and benchmarks make these capabilities explicit targets for agent development [Chen et al., 2026a, Meng et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026b, Ye et al., 2026]. Developing compact models for this setting requires training experience that captures these dependencies. Constructing that experience is a central challenge. The information needed to complete a workflow is often distributed across documents, service records, and intermediate environment state. Synthesizing a useful task therefore requires the request, the available evidence, and the permitted actions to fit together. Records referring to the same customer must agree across services; tools must expose the information needed to proceed and implement the expected state changes; and grading checks must assess relevant actions and outcomes. A plausible final response alone cannot establish that the intended update occurred or that a prohibited action was avoided. These requirements make executable environment construction and verification integral to data synthesis, as also emphasized in coworker evaluation and computer-use training [Meng et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026a]. arXiv:2610.24960v1 [cs.AI] 18 Oct 2026 CoworkEnv: Synthesizing Training Environments for Long-Horizon Cowork Agents 

Open agent-training efforts have begun to expose the data choices behind capable systems. Nemotron-Terminal and SERA study synthetic tasks and demonstrations for terminal and coding agents, while OpenThoughts-Agent investigates data curation across agentic tasks [Pi et al., 2026, Raoof et al., 2026, Shen et al., 2026]. Occamy extends execution-grounded training to coworker workloads [Chen et al., 2026a]. Building on these efforts, we focus on a practical question: how can targeted coworker capabilities be translated into executable training tasks that support both imitation and learning from interaction? 

We present CoworkEnv , a capability-guided framework for synthesizing executable training envi-ronments for AI coworkers. Developer-selected capability requirements guide the workflow scope of task construction. A workflow specification is implemented as a request, initial service records, tool interfaces, and grading checks. We describe two forms of task variation: composing business workflows across different services, and creating safety scenarios with distinct permitted and pro-hibited actions. Resolving a customer-support issue, for example, connects CRM context with a helpdesk ticket and a knowledge-base procedure; investigating a payment-change request connects correspondence with financial history. In the documented safety-task family, generation agents draft task content and a separate grading role defines the execution checks. Teacher executions provide curated demonstrations for supervised fine-tuning (SFT), while student interactions yield rollouts and reward feedback for reinforcement learning (RL). This process separates the construction of a problem from the generation of attempts to solve it. Figure 1 shows how task construction, teacher execution, and student training fit together. To assess the training utility of these resources, we fine-tune Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct [Bai et al., 2025] to obtain PocketWorker -8B. SFT raises the mean score from 46.6 to 64.1 on Claw-Eval tasks, with a 31.9-point gain on Multi-turn tasks. A separate SFT-only application to ClawMark extends the evaluation to a second coworker benchmark. We also compare training configurations for GRPO, Dr.GRPO, GDPO, and GiGPO to examine advantage estimation and credit assignment. Training rewards improve over approximately three epochs on our constructed tasks, providing preliminary evidence that these environments support RL optimization. Our contributions are: 

① Capability-guided synthesis of executable coworker environments. We translate target capabili-ties into multi-service workflows with consistent user requests, service records, tool interfaces, and executable grading checks. Task variation spans service dependencies and business constraints, including safety scenarios that require investigation while avoiding prohibited actions. Teacher execution and trajectory curation produce SFT demonstrations, while the constructed environments support interactive RL training. 

② Training utility and analysis. We evaluate these resources through SFT gains on Claw-Eval, an SFT-only extension to ClawMark, and comparisons of RL training configurations. We will release our datasets and construction pipeline, sharing the methods and design choices behind task and environment construction, trajectory collection, and data curation. We hope this helps other researchers adapt the process to their own needs. For academic researchers working with limited resources, advanced topics such as long-horizon agentic SFT and RL can be difficult to pursue with limited GPU access, infrastructure gaps, unfamiliar engineering pitfalls, and high API costs. Our Lessons bring together challenges we encountered, on-going work, and possible future directions, covering how different harnesses affect training outcomes, training–inference mismatch across harnesses, credit assignment and RL optimization. We share these experiences to help others make progress under similar constraints and avoid common pitfalls.

2. Related Work 

Agent post-training. Open post-training efforts make training data and recipes available for study [Lambert et al., 2024]. AgentTuning develops general agent abilities through instruction tuning, while Nemotron-Terminal, OpenThoughts-Agent, and SERA investigate task generation and trajectory curation for terminal, coding, and broader agentic behavior [Pi et al., 2026, Raoof et al., 2026, Shen et al., 2026, Zeng et al., 2024]. Related recipes extend trajectory-based training to research, data analysis, and GUI workflows [Ding et al., 2026, Fang et al., 2025, Qiao et al., 2026]. AgentGym-RL and RAGEN study learning through sequential interaction, while ToolRL examines reward design for tool use [Qian et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2025, Xi et al., 2025]. slime provides a post-training framework with customizable data generation for RL scaling [Zhu et al., 2025]. Agent harnesses organize execution and interaction interfaces [Huang et al., 2026, Xie et al., 2026b], with LEGO-RL connecting native coding-agent harnesses to policy-gradient training [Du et al., 2026]. Polar provides asynchronous rollouts over existing agent harnesses, reconstructing training trajectories from proxied model API calls [Xu et al., 2026a]. Building on PPO and GRPO [Schulman et al., 2017, Shao et al., 2024], subsequent methods explore advantage and loss normalization, reward-component normalization, and finer-grained credit assignment [Feng et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2026a, 2025c, Wei et al., 2025]. Our framework connects executable task construction, curated demonstrations, and interactive RL for compact coworkers. 

Executable task synthesis and demonstration curation. Task synthesis couples generated requests with interaction settings and completion criteria. AgentGen and MATRIX construct environments or simulated scenarios [Hu et al., 2025, Tang et al., 2025]; APIGen-MT links task blueprints to multi-turn interactions, and CUA-Gym jointly constructs instructions, environments, and rewards [Prabhakar et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026a]. CuES generates executable tasks by exploring existing tool environ-ments [Mai et al., 2025]. Verification and refinement further shape training data: ToolACE verifies generated tool-use dialogues, while AgentRefine studies refinement-oriented demonstrations [Fu et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2025b]. Complementary approaches obtain training experience through envi-ronment models and adaptive generation: DreamGym and Qwen-AgentWorld simulate environment dynamics, PatchWorld induces executable world-model programs with counterexample-driven repair, and AgentEvolver combines task generation with experience-guided exploration [Bai et al., 2026, Chen et al., 2026b, Zhai et al., 2025, Zuo et al., 2026]. Concurrent work includes Cowork-Gym [Zou et al., 2026], which explores large-scale synthesis of interactive work environments. Our construction jointly specifies cross-service records, tools, and grading checks, then validates tasks and curates teacher executions for SFT while retaining environments for RL. 

Coworker agents and evaluation. Occamy studies coworker specialization through execution-grounded data and staged post-training [Chen et al., 2026a]. AppWorld, 𝜏 -bench, TheAgentCompany, and AutomationBench evaluate multi-application work, user interaction, and workflow constraints [Shepard and Salimans, 2026, Trivedi et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2026b, Yao et al., 2024], while GDPval evaluates economically valuable professional tasks [Patwardhan et al., 2026]. ClawMark emphasizes persistent workflows and evolving service state, and Claw-Eval evaluates task completion, safety, and robustness [Meng et al., 2026, Ye et al., 2026]. AgentDojo and ToolEmu assess risks from prompt injection and agent actions [Debenedetti et al., 2024, Ruan et al., 2024]; Terminal-Bench and CyberGym provide execution-based evaluation in terminal and software-security settings [Merrill et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026c]. We evaluate SFT on Claw-Eval and ClawMark and compare RL configurations on Claw-Eval to study learning for coworker workflows.

3. Method 

CoworkEnv constructs executable coworker environments through the following stages: capability-guided task design, executable environment construction, programmatic validation and feedback repair, teacher execution and curation, and preparation for SFT or RL. The central unit is a task environment that brings together a request, the information and tools needed to act, and checks of the resulting execution. Teacher demonstrations provide supervision for imitation, while student interaction with the environment provides experience and rewards for RL. 

3.1. Capability-Guided Task Synthesis From capabilities to task specifications. We translate target capabilities into a coverage plan specifying service combinations, intended difficulty, and business scenarios. Scenarios define selection conditions, distractor records, and permitted actions; a fixed reference date anchors time-sensitive requirements. An LLM receives the plan together with service catalogs, an output-format specification, and example tasks. It generates the user request, initial service records, structured completion checks, and prohibited actions. The catalogs describe available tools and record formats, guiding generation toward reusable service implementations. We represent a task as

Programs instantiate the specification: service configurations are populated from a registry, and structured completion checks and prohibited actions are inserted into a fixed grader template. Shared services expose the generated records and support state-changing actions. Task variation thus changes the scenario, records, and checks while reusing the execution and grading implementations. Appendix D gives the plan format and template structure. 

Linking information across services. Workflow construction distributes related information across services. An escalation task, for example, combines ticket creation times with customer tiers to select enterprise-account tickets open for more than 48 hours, then retrieves the account manager’s address for an email. A recent high-priority ticket is a distractor: it appears urgent but fails the age condition. Grading checks reference entities and actions from the same specification, using tool arguments and service audit records to check execution. This connects the requested goal, the evidence available through tools, and the scoring conditions. Safety scenarios introduce constraints on acting on retrieved information. A payment-change investi-gation combines a suspicious email, legitimate correspondence, and transaction history. Completion checks reward evidence gathering, while designated submission or messaging actions activate a safety gate. Under the Generator–Discriminator design, the Generator drafts the request and records; the Discriminator receives the records and a note describing the safety scenario in a separate context, and specifies completion checks and forbidden actions. Appendices C and D describe the role inputs and workflow examples. 

3.2. Validation and Feedback Repair Checking generated specifications. Programmatic checks complement the generation instructions. They verify that the specification can be parsed, that selected services are registered and have initial records, that completion weights sum to one, and that required and prohibited tool sets do not conflict. A request-level leakage check rejects designated answer identifiers exposed in the user request, preserving the need to discover them through interaction.

3.3. Teacher Execution and Data Curation Collecting interactions. A teacher executes tasks through a Claude Code adapter [Anthropic, 2025] in Harbor [Harbor Framework Team, 2026]. The runtime loads the task’s initial service records and manages service startup and reset for interactive execution. Starting from the request and initial state, the teacher selects actions and receives observations from the configured services. One attempt yields 

𝜏 𝑖 𝑗 = (𝑢 𝑖 , 𝑎 1, 𝑜 1, . . . , 𝑎 𝐿 𝑖 𝑗 , 𝑜 𝐿 𝑖 𝑗 ), (2) where 𝑗 indexes attempts, 𝑎 𝑡 is an assistant response or tool action, and 𝑜 𝑡 is its observation; a final response need not have a following observation. The grader scores the execution, and the record preserves tool arguments, returned observations, and task and trial identifiers. Repeated attempts provide both alternative demonstrations and a task-level view of teacher performance. A separate path collects dialogue demonstrations on our independently constructed multi-turn tasks with a simulated user; Appendix E describes their preparation. All training tasks are constructed independently of the benchmark test sets. 

Selecting tasks and demonstrations. Task selection uses mean teacher completion to identify consistently completed tasks, tasks with very low completion, and an intermediate group. We retain qualifying demonstrations from the intermediate group, limit consistently completed tasks to one demonstration per normalized task prefix, and set aside the lowest-completion group. This reduces repeated supervision from easy tasks while retaining multiple executions of other tasks. When merging collections, we deduplicate by task and trial identifier so that each recorded execution enters the pool once. Appendix E specifies the grouping and export rules. Demonstration selection proceeds in two stages. Export first retains trajectories meeting source-specific reward thresholds. Conversion then removes records that cannot yield usable user and assistant messages, along with sequences that are too short, contain too many tool calls, or exceed the whole-example character budget. These checks select the interactions used for student supervision. 

Preparing training inputs. The retained executions are serialized as user, assistant, and tool mes-sages with structured tool calls and a standardized system instruction. Long tool observations are shortened to a prefix during conversion; whole examples exceeding the character budget are discarded.

RL through student interaction. The RL interface supplies references to selected task definitions. Each reference loads the request, initial service records, tools, and grader. The student generates its own trajectory through online tool interaction, and the grader computes rewards from that execution’s calls and service audit records. Teacher performance can inform task selection, while the learning signal comes from the student’s behavior. Missing rewards abort the affected sample, and incomplete or infrastructure-failed rollout groups are rejected and retried before advantage estimation. The two interfaces reuse task definitions, service implementations, and grading mechanisms, with selection tailored to demonstration learning or online interaction. The reference set includes Nemotron 3 Super, Kimi K2.5, and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, whose reported total/active parameter counts are 120B/12B, 1T/32B, and 397B/17B, respectively (Appendix F). A claim of outperforming a particular reference model requires aligned evaluation settings. The 4B results extend this comparison to a smaller model: SFT raises General from 28.7 to 36.6 and Multi-turn from 56.1 to 66.0, gains of 7.9 and 9.9 points, respectively. Section 4.5 presents the 8B data-scale ablation (Table 4). 

4.3. ClawMark Results 

We extend CoworkEnv and its SFT pipeline to ClawMark, a second coworker benchmark. As shown in Table 2, PocketWorker-8B (ClawMark SFT) achieves a mean score of 19.4, compared with 11.8 for the base model, a gain of 7.6 points. Together with the Claw-Eval results, this SFT-only extension supports the applicability of our framework across different coworker workflow settings. 

4.4. Comparison of RL Training Methods Benchmark results. Starting from a shared checkpoint obtained through supervised warm-up, we train GRPO [Shao et al., 2024], Dr.GRPO [Liu et al., 2025c], GDPO [Liu et al., 2026a], and GiGPO [Feng et al., 2025] on our constructed RL tasks for approximately three epochs. Method-specific configurations are described in Appendix B. Table 3 reports the benchmark results of the warm-up-plus-RL pipelines. Relative to the Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct baseline, the reported Overall scores increase from 46.6 to 56.6 with GRPO and 57.2 with GDPO, differences of 10.0 and 10.6 points. The largest differences occur on Multi-turn tasks: scores increase from 45.9 to 72.5 with GRPO and 74.9 with GDPO, corresponding to 26.7 and 29.0 points. General scores increase from 46.7 to 52.8 and 53.0, differences of 6.1 and 6.3 points, respectively. These aggregate comparisons show the largest gains in multi-turn interaction. Different reward-processing, advantage-estimation, and credit-assignment strategies exhibit distinct training dynamics and performance profiles across task categories. Both GRPO and GDPO achieve their largest gains on Multi-turn tasks, with GDPO attaining the higher score, whereas GRPO performs better on Multimodal tasks. These observations suggest that the benefits of different RL training configurations vary with the capabilities required by each task category.

5. Conclusion 

We present CoworkEnv , a capability-guided framework for synthesizing executable training envi-ronments for AI coworkers. By aligning user requests, service states, tool interfaces, and grading checks, our pipeline supports both curated demonstrations for SFT and interactive training with RL.

Experiments show SFT gains on Claw-Eval, while an SFT-only extension to ClawMark supports the framework’s applicability across coworker benchmarks. Supervised warm-up followed by RL improves Multi-turn performance over the base model, and comparisons of reward processing, advantage estimation, and credit assignment reveal distinct training dynamics and task-dependent performance profiles. 

Limitations 

Execution scores measure the properties encoded by the graders. They can miss errors outside those checks, while teacher-success filters can favor redundant demonstrations or discard useful recovery behavior. The role of unsuccessful trajectories also deserves study: work on iterative skill evolution reports that feedback conditions containing failures can produce useful skill updates [Liu et al., 2026b]. Regenerating the original teacher trajectories may require access to a proprietary model service. 

Ethical Considerations 

CoworkEnv is designed for research in executable, simulated work environments rather than deploy-ment in real-world organizations. Nevertheless, agents trained on synthetic workflows may perform incorrect or unauthorized actions if deployed without appropriate safeguards.

A.1. Claw-Eval SFT 

The final Claw-Eval SFT stage for the 8B model uses a per-device batch size of one, eight gradient accumulation steps, a 4,096-token maximum sequence length, and LoRA [Hu et al., 2021] rank 32, scaling factor 64, and dropout 0.05. A.2. ClawMark SFT 

We trained PocketWorker-8B (ClawMark SFT) for three epochs (354 optimizer updates) with a configured learning rate of 2 × 10 −4, LoRA rank 32, scaling factor 64, and dropout 0.05. Training used a per-device batch size of one, eight gradient accumulation steps, and a maximum sequence length of 4,096 tokens. 

B. RL Training 

RL method configurations and comparison scope. Figure 3 compares training dynamics for GRPO, Dr.GRPO, GDPO, and GiGPO on our constructed task set. All four methods start from the same supervised warm-up checkpoint and train for approximately three epochs. Table 3 reports the final benchmark results for all four methods. The method-specific choices are described below. The GRPO baseline [Shao et al., 2024] centers the scalar reward within each group of eight rollouts and normalizes by the group standard deviation with a denominator floor of 0.05. Zero-variance groups within numerical tolerance receive zero advantages and remain valid groups. This normalization rule is separate from rejection of invalid rollouts. 

Dr.GRPO. Following Liu et al. [2025c], our implementation changes both advantage normalization and loss normalization. Advantages are mean-centered without division by the reward standard deviation. The masked token losses are summed and divided by a fixed normalizer of 1,536, corresponding to the per-turn generation limit of 1,536 tokens. The comparison with GRPO therefore varies both advantage and loss normalization. 

GDPO. Our GDPO implementation normalizes each reward dimension within the rollout group, combines the resulting advantages, and applies batch-wise normalization [Liu et al., 2026a]. Batch statistics use only valid groups. The GRPO baseline uses a joint scalar reward and a different batch size. The comparison varies reward processing and batch size together, so it evaluates the complete GDPO configuration. 

GiGPO. GiGPO combines episode-level advantages with step-level advantages computed from groups of matching intermediate states [Feng et al., 2025]. We use a step-advantage weight of 𝜔 = 1.0. The comparison evaluates this complete configuration without a matched 𝜔 = 0 ablation. Policy-loss magnitudes use different normalizations across methods and should not be read as a common measure of policy quality. 

Advantage-estimation context. Generalized advantage estimation uses value-function predictions to trade bias against variance in policy-gradient estimates [Schulman et al., 2015]. VinePPO investigates credit assignment in language-model reasoning using Monte Carlo estimates [Kazemnejad et al., 2024], while Wei et al. [2025] study terminal, delayed, and per-turn rewards for multi-turn agents. These works motivate separating reward granularity from the estimator that turns rewards into advantages. C. A Constructed Safety Workflow 

Generation and grading roles. The Generator–Discriminator design separates scenario drafting from check specification. The Generator receives a safety-scenario category and service combination, then drafts the request and initial records. The Discriminator receives the task and service identifiers, records, and a safety note describing the intended conflict, without the full request text. It specifies completion checks for investigation and a set of prohibited actions. Shared record identifiers connect these checks to the generated evidence. The resulting check and forbidden-tool sets are inserted into the grader template described in Appendix D. 

Request and evidence. The instruction body of M133_safety_payment_change_refusal asks for a vendor banking update and payment-report submission. Its title describes verification without submission. The fixtures contain a spoofed request with a new account, legitimate correspondence identifying the established account, and historical transactions consistent with that account. A third mail message is an unrelated announcement. These records make the contradiction accessible through mail and finance tools. The example demonstrates how a task can pair a request to act with evidence relevant to withholding the action. 

Grading. The grader checks five actions: opening the inbox (weight 0.15), reading the suspicious request (0.25), reading the legitimate vendor message (0.20), listing financial history (0.20), and in-specting relevant transactions (0.20). Calling finance_submit_report or gmail_send_message 

sets the safety factor to zero. The score is 𝑟 = safety (0.8 completion + 0.2 robustness ). These tool-based checks measure evidence access and avoidance of designated actions; absence of a forbidden call alone does not establish semantic recognition of fraud or an explicit refusal in the final response. 

Grading validation. The grading-consistency procedure exercises completion checks and the forbidden-tool gate using synthetic tool-call and audit records. Appendix D describes the test cases and feedback-based regeneration procedure. 

Task selection. The three teacher attempts for M133 each achieve completion 0.8, placing the task in the KEEP group under the thresholds in Appendix E. 

Another workflow represented in training. The M1003_ticket_kb_resolution package speci-fies SSL certificate triage using helpdesk and knowledge-base services. Validation and regeneration details. For each slot in the coverage plan, the generator has a budget of two attempts: an initial generation and at most one retry. Each candidate passes sequentially through JSON parsing, required/prohibited-tool conflict checks, completion-weight checks, service and initial-record validation, request-level identifier-leakage checks, and the grading-consistency gate. A failed check returns diagnostic feedback for the next attempt. The retry produces a complete specification and repeats the validation sequence. Candidates still failing after the second attempt are discarded; accepted candidates are packaged with their request, initial records, service configuration, and grader. 

Synthetic grading tests. The gate imports the generated grader and constructs tool-call and service-audit records from the declared completion checks. Tool-presence checks receive the required calls; argument checks receive a declared acceptable value; audit checks receive records carrying the required fields. The complete synthetic record must yield completion and safety scores of 1. An empty call and audit record must yield completion 0. For tasks with prohibited tools, adding a prohibited call to the complete record must set safety to 0. 

Task coverage. The 25 task identifiers in the M1100 –M1124 family are absent from the official Claw-Eval 300-task benchmark.

Relation to data selection and supervision. GALA selects representative samples through clus-tering and geometric distances to reduce redundancy in LLM self-training [Xie et al., 2026a]. Our selection uses recorded completion, reward thresholds, and structural checks; measuring whether representation-based selection improves coverage remains a separate experiment. SoFT studies soft targets that balance learning from demonstrations with preserving the base distribution [Jing et al., 2026]. Our SFT objective in Equation 3 uses the observed assistant tokens as targets. This separates the choice of which trajectories to retain from the choice of supervision applied to those trajectories.
