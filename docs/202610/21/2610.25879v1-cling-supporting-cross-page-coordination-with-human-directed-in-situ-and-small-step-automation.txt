## 1 Introduction 

Web-based information work often spans many pages [33]. When searching for an apartment before relocating, for example, a person may examine a rental listing, view its location on a map, browse its reviews on a community website, compare alternatives, and record selected details in a personal shortlist. In this process, the user needs to frequently transfer information across pages, cross-examine content on different pages, and integrate details from different pages. Such cross-page coordination is a recurring pattern of everyday information work, yet it is often tedious and disruptive: users must mentally keep track of numerous disconnected pages while manually performing low-level navigation and information-transfer operations. arXiv:2610.25879v1 [cs.HC] 19 Oct 2026 Conference’17, July 2017, Washington, DC, USA Huang, et al. 

Supporting cross-page coordination, however, presents both structural and interaction challenges. Structurally, webpages are independently developed and generally lack incentives for inter-operability. Shared data structures and protocols can support co-ordination, but typically only within the boundaries of integrated enterprise applications or ecosystems [29, 58, 66]. Meanwhile, co-ordination often emerges opportunistically as work unfolds, and therefore, cannot be anticipated and supported with system fea-tures [55]. A detail encountered on one page may prompt a new search, introduce a new comparison criterion, or shift the user’s priorities. Cross-page coordination can therefore range from a one-off lookup to repeated information transfers, and from a step in a predefined workflow to part of an exploratory activity whose goals and constraints evolve throughout the process. Client-side web automation tools [16, 17, 72, 73, 89] and recent computer-use agents [8, 67, 68] offer ways to execute operations across page boundaries. As these capabilities advance, an important interaction question is how to make desired automation available within ongoing, human-directed work. Specifying a workflow or delegating a task can be useful when the goals and constrains are already clear. In other situations, users discover what they want to do through inspecting information and making situated [80] and contextual judgments [42, 53, 79, 85, 87]. They may need assistance with an individual lookup or compare corresponding fields while continuing to determine the next step themselves. Supporting this process requires making automation accessible at the moment a need becomes clear, while allowing users to continue directing the activity. Otherwise, users are left to either specify each operation for automation, interrupting their engagement with the participating pages and opportunities to discover their next steps, or to carry them out manually through repeated low-level actions. We investigate how cross-page coordination can be supported by incrementally surfacing in-situ automation opportunities that users can selectively invoke as needs arise. Our approach uses information correspondences across pages as the basis for identi-fying potential coordination actions. For example, an address in a listing and a search field on a map page suggest a possible value transfer, while corresponding attributes across listings (e.g., square footage and price) suggest opportunities for cross-referencing or comparison. Concretely, we model webpage content as a set of entities, each represented by entity schema, runtime values, and references to its original DOM elements. Under this representation, cross-page coordination can be expressed as operations for selecting, filter-ing, navigating, and transferring information among corresponding entities. Based on this formalization, we define five coordination patterns that capture distinct forms of cross-page correspondence. We use LLMs to construct the schematic representation of each webpage, while deliberately relying on rule-based correspondence detection to surface small-step automation opportunities in real time. As people interact with live webpages, the system dynami-cally surfaces coordination opportunities derived from cross-page entity correspondences. Depending on their current goals, people can then selectively apply any automation opportunity in-situ to speed though tedious low-level manual operations while retaining control over the direction and progression of the broader task. We instantiate this approach in a protoype system, Cling. We assess our approach through both technical and user eval-uations. In the technical evaluation, we evaluated our technical pipeline (Section 5) across 48 webpages spanning 3 common web task domains. Two human coders annotated all 220 coordination opportunities surfaced by the pipeline, and rated them 92.5% accu-rate with a mean usefulness of 4.48/5. On average, the page-model extraction took 5.82s across three independent runs. We further conducted a two-part user study with 12 participants, examine Cling in both structured cross-page coordination and broader exploratory tasks. In structured tasks, Cling combined the efficiency of end-to-end automation (compared with Manual, it re-quired 61.1% fewer clicks, 99.6% fewer copy/paste actions, and 80.2% fewer tab switches, while completing tasks 2.07 × as fast as Manual and 2.14 × as End-to-End Agent). At the same time, participants reported substantially greater control with Cling than with End-to-End Agent (2.83 points higher on a 5-point scale, 95% CI [2.33, 3.33], 𝑝 < 0.001), while reporting a level of control comparable to, and slightly higher than, Manual. Moreover, Cling’s in-situ and small-step automation allowed participants to inspect and correct mistakes as automation unfold, much as they did during manual work, while accommodating dynamic user intents that emerged and evolved over the course of the task. In summary, this paper makes the following contributions: 

• The formalization of a class of interaction, which we call cross-page coordination as a set of 5 coordination patterns. 

• A technical pipeline that constructs reusable entity-based page models and identifies coordination opportunities through page-model correspondence. 

• User interfaces and interaction techniques for representing, sur-facing, and invoking in-situ, small-step automation. 

• Cling, a prototype system that instantiates these technical and interaction approaches. 

• Technical and user evaluations that demonstrate the feasibility, effectiveness, and values of supporting cross-page coordination with human-directed, in-situ, and small-step automation 

## 2 Related Work 

Information tasks spanning multiple materials and systems often require context switching, information transfer, and coordination across interfaces. Prior work has addressed these demands through integrated environments, interoperability, task automation, and correspondence-driven interaction. We review these approaches to situate Cling’s support for human-directed, in situ, small-step coordination across webpages. 

## 2.1 Integrated Workspaces for Distributed Information Tasks 

A common strategy for reducing coordination overhead is to build integrated environments for tasks that span multiple materials or processing steps, such as content creation [2, 10, 12], data analysis [24, 59, 71, 81], programming [54, 56], and task management [29, 56, 58, 83]. Although these systems promise an “all-in-one” experience, they typically demand substantial engineering effort and often depend on complex organizational infrastructure. Integrated environments have also been used to support human– AI collaboration: Buschek et al. integrate diverse AI-powered tools Cling: Supporting Cross-Page Coordination with Human-Directed, In-Situ, and Small-Step Automation Conference’17, July 2017, Washington, DC, USA 

into a unified writing environment [9], and Cao et al. combine core video-editing structures—such as canvas, document, grid, and timeline—within a single environment to enable human–AI co-creation [12]. In such systems, coordination is often delegated to AI through predefined rules. However, building these environ-ments has traditionally required developers to predefine informa-tion sources, structures, and tools. Cao et al. recently reduced this burden with an approach that uses AI to dynamically generate environments in response to evolving user criteria [13]. Browser-based workspaces address similar organizational needs across web materials. Prior work has explored augmenting brows-ing environments with in-situ support. For example, systems have incorporated query suggestions [69] and note-taking [21, 48, 84] into browsing workflows, transformed tabs into spatial or canvas-based workspaces to better scale browsing activity [37], and exter-nalized users’ evolving interests through compositional filters and lenses [15]. Other systems help users externalize mental models such as comparing across collected information [14, 43, 49, 50], and triage during sensemaking [51]. These approaches provide representations for organizing and reasoning across distributed materials. Cling complements this direction by supporting operations between the materials them-selves: users can navigate corresponding entities, filter options, and transfer information across the live webpages involved in their work. 

## 2.2 Interoperability and End-User Composition 

Information materials and systems that support interoperability generally incur lower correspondence costs, as shared representa-tions mitigate the need for manual transformation and coordination. Nelson’s concept of transclusion enables portions of one document to be embedded within another, facilitating the flexible composition of both information and functionality [65]. This idea has been fur-ther extended in systems such as Webstrates [39] and subsequent work [40, 41, 74], which support the sharing and synchronization of information and functionality across heterogeneous environ-ments (e.g., web pages and devices). Beaudouin-Lafon et al. pro-poses a specification language to reason about structural analogies of interfaces [5]. Interoperability has also been pursued through system-level mechanisms, including proprietary approaches such as Microsoft’s Object Linking and Embedding (OLE) [57] and Apple’s OpenDoc [3, 78]. End-user composition and customization allow people to adapt existing resources to their own needs. Hartmann et al.’s d.mix ex-plores programming by sampling existing websites to create new web applications [32]. Wildcard exposes application data through a spreadsheet interface, enabling users to customize website be-havior [47]. Together, these approaches show how existing data, functionality, and interface structures can become resources for composition. Min et al. explore malleable overview-detail interfaces that let users customize content, composition, and layout, includ-ing revealing attributes across views and using AI to transform or generate attributes [63]. These approaches make existing data, functionality, and interface structures available for user-directed adaptation and composition. However, achieving interoperability typically requires the adop-tion of shared protocols, data schemas, and frameworks, which introduces substantial development overhead and limits practical adoption. In many cases, such efforts are further constrained within platform-specific ecosystems, resulting in “walled gardens” that hinder integration across heterogeneous systems. More fundamen-tally, supporting interoperability remains intrinsically challenging, as developers cannot realistically anticipate the full diversity of user needs and usage contexts to define the scope of interoperability [38]. Rather than relying on developers to conform to a single, glob-ally agreed-upon standard, an assumption that is often untenable, we explore an alternative approach: leveraging generative AI to dynamically parse diverse web pages into shared, structured rep-resentations, thereby enabling on-demand interoperability across otherwise incompatible systems. 

## 2.3 Task Automation and In-Situ AI Assistance 

Traditional web automation systems, including web-scraping scripts [1, 22, 23, 28] and programming-by-demonstration approaches [4, 16, 17, 25, 72, 73], offer reliable, performant ways to navigate web-sites and collect information at scale. Yet they generally assume well-defined environments and well-specified success criteria, as-sumptions that often fail in exploratory, open-ended, and uncertain tasks [79, 85–87]. Recent LLM-based agents [8, 52, 67, 68] optimize for task completion by committing to browsing trajectories shaped by model priors and interface cues [27, 77]. In contrast, many in-formation tasks evolve incrementally, motivating in-situ assistance that users can invoke in small steps while deciding how to proceed. Context-sensitive assistance has a long history in interface agents and mixed-initiative interaction, including anticipating browsing interests, inferring assistance needs, and deciding when to intervene [34, 35, 46]. Recent work revisits these questions: Fairy Cursor explores a cursor-based agent for in-the-flow assistance [11], while Just-In-Time Objectives infers immediate goals from observed activity to specialize AI-generated tools and responses [44]. Cling builds on this direction by surfacing individual coordination actions as small-step automation that users can selectively execute within ongoing browsing. Action generalization provides a related precedent for this gran-ularity of support. Prior techniques allow selection, text entry, and clicking to be applied across multiple structurally similar items [45, 60–62]. Zhang et al. recently brought this idea to the web, allowing users to perform the same action simultaneously on multiple selected targets [89]. However, such techniques rely on the involved pages having similar structures, such as in their CSS selectors or DOM placements, an assumption that breaks down for heterogeneous pages. Our approach addresses this limitation by lifting heterogeneous pages to their schemas, where cross-page coordination can be generalized despite structural differences. 

## 2.4 Correspondence-Driven Interaction 

A substantial body of research in HCI and information visualization has investigated how semantic correspondences within content can be leveraged to enable coordinated and automated interaction. For example, systems built on time-aligned transcripts—such as Quick-Cut [82] and CrossCast [88]—exploit temporal alignment between Conference’17, July 2017, Washington, DC, USA Huang, et al. 

text and video to support script-based editing workflows. Simi-larly, Data Particles [10] and CrossData [18] utilize the semantic correspondence between natural language, data operations, and visual encodings to facilitate the authoring of data-driven interac-tive articles with rich visualizations. In visualization, prior work has also explored leveraging shared data dimensions to support coordinated view management across multiple plots [7, 75]. For web browsing specifically, Contextual Facets transforms webpage elements into controls for filtering and retrieving related pages [55]. Dontcheva et al. enable users to establish relationships between websites, collect related content into personalized cards, and reuse these relationships through search templates [26]. By operationalizing these correspondences, such systems can au-tomate a range of otherwise tedious low-level operations, including navigation, view coordination, content transfer, and transformation. Our work aligns with this line of research in leveraging inherent correspondences within information artifacts. However, rather than relying on developer-specified mappings, we target open-ended web environments where such correspondences are not predefined. We introduce an approach that leverages generative AI to parse heterogeneous web pages into structured schemas and employs heuristic strategies to infer correspondences at interaction time, enabling real-time support for navigation and information trans-formation across heterogeneous pages. 

## 3 Example Use Case 

Polly, staff at a university, is co-organizing a panel on AI’s impact on education. She is responsible for identifying potential participants. The organizing team maintains a shared spreadsheet listing candi-date invitees, including their name, affiliation, contact information, and a brief justification for inclusion. To populate this spreadsheet, Polly needs to visit faculty directo-ries from different departments, looks up their profiles on Google Scholar, reviews their publications, and decides whether they are a good fit. In a typical workflow, for each faculty member, she first checks whether they are already in the spreadsheet by copying their name and searching for it (e.g., using cmd+F ) in another tab. If not found, she needs to then switch to Google Scholar, copies the name into the search box, submits the query, and reviews the profile. When appropriate, Polly needs to manually copy the name, affiliation, and email back into the spreadsheet, carefully aligning each field with the correct column in the spreadsheet. If the faculty member is already listed, Polly still needs to verify whether the contact information is up to date, requiring her to locate the cor-responding row and cross-reference multiple sources. She repeats this process for each faculty member. With Cling, Polly first places the faculty directory, Google Scholar, and the spreadsheet side by side. When Polly selects a faculty name on the directory page, Cling indicates whether the person is already in the spreadsheet. If not, Cling suggests trans-ferring the selected name into the Google Scholar search field and submitting the query. After reviewing the publications, Polly de-cides to include the faculty member and selects “Add to spreadsheet.” Cling then aggregates and reformats the relevant information into a new row matching the schema. After previewing the entry, Polly confirms the insertion. If the faculty member is already in the spreadsheet, Cling surfaces a “Go to” action that navigates directly to the corresponding row. Polly can then quickly verify and update contact information by cross-referencing the directory and Google Scholar pages, without manually searching or aligning fields. Using Cling’s navigation widget, Polly can step to the next researcher while preserving the coordination context across the pages. At each step, Cling updates the relevant actions—for example, whether the researcher is already in the spreadsheet, where their row is, and by issuing the next Google Scholar search automatically. Polly can thus move continuously through researchers without repeating the same search, tab-switching, and viewport-alignment work for each person, allowing her to stay focused on evaluating and selecting participants for the panel. 

## 4 Cross-Page Coordination Patterns 

Supporting cross-page coordination requires identifying how infor-mation on one page relates to information and actions on another. We use these correspondences as a basis for a catalog of five coor-dination patterns, each mapping a configuration of page models and their correspondences to applicable coordination actions. We first define the page model, then describe each pattern. 

## 4.1 Pages Viewed as Databases 

We conceptualize each page as a structured representation that en-codes the underlying data with a specific schema. While not all web pages are driven by relational databases, most could be viewed from a database perspective, where each page contains entities E

(i.e., rows), such as products and restaurants; and attributes A (i.e., columns), such as price and location. With this perspective, we can describe cross-page coordination with the following formalization: 

• Source Page: a dataset 𝐷 𝑠 ⊆ E 𝑠 × A 𝑠 under schema 𝑆 𝑠 

• Target Page: a dataset 𝐷 𝑡 ⊆ E 𝑡 × A 𝑡 under schema 𝑆 𝑡 

• Cross-page Coordination: an operation mapping a user’s ex-pressed interest on the source page to the target page. 

## 4.2 Coordinations as Database Operations 

Taking this database-oriented view, we can comprehensively model cross-page coordination by examining the possible operations across two databases. This leads to five primitive coordination patterns. More complex coordination behaviors can be understood as compositions of these operations. In practice, a single task often involves multiple patterns simultaneously or sequentially. 

C1. Entity Cross-Reference ( E𝑠 ↔ E 𝑡 ). The user’s interest is at the entity level, analogous to selecting a row in a database table. Coordination establishes a correspondence between entities in D𝑠 and D𝑡 via primary-key-based joins. This includes one-to-one mappings (e.g., selecting a specific flight in a search result highlights the same flight in a booking tab) and one-to-many relationships (e.g., selecting a faculty member in a directory navigates to a list of all their publications in a proceedings). At the interface level, this appears as selecting an entity in one page to focus, highlight, or navigate to related instances in another. 

C2. Attribute Cross-Reference ( A𝑠 ↔ A 𝑡 ). In this case, the user’s interest is not in a particular entity, but in a specific attribute dimension, analogous to selecting a particular property in a dataset. ## 5 Technical Pipeline 

Building on the coordination patterns, we implement a technical pipeline to construct schema-based page representations and de-rives coordination opportunities from them, as illustrated in Fig. 3. For each page, the system captures and cleans its DOM to con-struct a page model comprising entities and their relationships. HTML structures varies substantially independently developer sites: generic tags such as <div> are often repurposed for arbitrary in-terface semantics, accessibility annotations are inconsistent, and markup conventions differ widely across organizations. This hetero-geneity makes it brittle to construct page models with rule-based approaches. We therefore use LLM to extract coordination-salient semantics from the underlying markup, transforming heterogenous webpages into consistent page models. ## 5.1 Per-Page Representation Construction 

We represent each page as a set of entities : units of information that users interpret and operate on. 

5.1.1 Entity model. Each entity consists of a schema , a runtime 

value , and reference to the page’s DOM. 

Schema: Each entity’s schema specifies its data structure and coordination-relevant interaction properties. – Data. The schema specifies the entity’s type . Types may be primitive (e.g., string , date ) or composite (e.g., object, list); we refer to named fields of a composite entity as attributes (e.g., price within a product entity). – Interaction. The schema encodes properties and roles that deter-mine how an entity can participate in coordination. isEditable 

and isExtendable indicate whether values can be modified or added through the interface, isIdentifier marks attributes that uniquely identify their enclosing entities, and role distinguishes data-bearing entities from action entities (e.g., submit buttons). 

Runtime Value: The runtime value contains the data currently associated with the entity. For lists, the system infers one shared schema and instantiates it with the values extracted from each list item. 

Reference: Each entity retains one or more handles to the interface objects through which it is presented or manipulated. For webpages, these handles are references to DOM elements. Reference allows the system to map user interactions on the live page to entities and to execute coordinations back to their corresponding interface objects. 

5.1.2 Page Model Extraction. Once a page loads, the system scans its live DOM and CSS selectors to identify salient regions (e.g., lists of similarly-structured elements, standalone elements with many nested child elements, forms, and submit buttons), while removing irrelevant elements (e.g., non-content <script> and decorative 

style tags). It characterizes each salient regions and selects representative items from each list based on how common and structurally rich they are (i.e., a typical item with the most common structure and examples of other variants that contain substantial text, fields, or links). An LLM reasons over such regions to infer the page’s purpose and determine whether each region has potential to participate in cross-page coordination. For each retained list, the LLM infers a shared item schema that captures its common attributes and meaningful structural varia-tions. For each list of elements, the LLM derives a schema that unionizes common attributes with optional attributes that are only seen over some list elements. The system then maps each schema attribute to the corresponding DOM element within every list item. It uses these mappings to extract the item’s runtime values and retains references to the matched DOM elements. Standalone enti-ties, forms, and actions are interpreted separately and incorporated into the same page model with their runtime values and DOM references. Page-model extraction is performed independently for each page, allowing pages to be added to or removed from a cross-page coordi-nation without reprocessing other pages. Completed page models are cached by URL. For paginated pages that share the same list structure, the system can reuse the inferred list schema while ex-tracting the runtime values and DOM references of each page anew.

## 5.3 Coordination Generalization 

Coordination opportunities are first identified between a source-target entity pair. The system then generalizes the pairwise coordi-nation to analogous entities in the source and target schemas via structural relationships such as array membership, entity contain-ment, and relative paths within composite entities, enabling reuse of the established coordination for other suitable pairs. 

## 5.4 Incremental Entity Updates 

After constructing a page model, the system keeps it synchronized with the live page by monitoring the DOM elements that ground its entities. When the DOM changes, these references identify the affected portion of the page model, allowing the system to update it without re-extracting the page. For an updated list item, the system reapplies the list’s existing schema to the affected DOM subtree. The item’s identifier attribute determines whether the result updates an existing entity or creates a new one, and removing the subtree removes the corresponding entity. For an updated object attribute, the system parses the up-dated content using its existing type and updates only that attribute. If its referent is replaced, the system reconnects it to the replace-ment element. If the updated content cannot be interpreted as the attribute’s existing type, parses it as a new entity and removes the old entity. 

## 5.5 Implementation 

The pipeline is implemented in TypeScript as part of an Electron ap-plication. The current implementation uses gemini-3.5-flash-lite 

by default. Semantic comparisons between entities are also per-formed locally using all-MiniLM-L6-v2 with cosine similarity. 

## 6 Cling: User Interfaces and Interactions 

We designed Cling to reify cross-page coordination by making implicit relationships between heterogeneous pages explicit. Fig. 4 illustrates the end-to-end flow from intent specification to how coordination opportunities are surfaced and executed. 

## 6.1 Specifying Coordination Intent and Surfacing Coordination Opportunities 

Cling allows users to place two pages into side-by-side views to indicate interest in coordinating between them. For any pair of pages with extracted page models, Cling does not speculatively correspond compatible entities to compute all coordination oppor-tunities because it would warrant exhaustively exploring a large correspondence space. Instead, it waits for users to specify their coordination intent by cmd+clicking a DOM element on the page. Cling uses the bidirectional reference to the page model to identify its corresponding entity as the source entity. Then, Cling runs the correspondence heuristics to identify all possible coordination opportunities involving the specified source entity. Once coordination opportunities are surfaced following a user specification, Cling presents them in a popover menu next to the selected DOM element (Fig. 4a), for users to select the intended coordination. When proposed, each coordination pattern in Fig. 2 deterministically maps to one option on the popover menu. Some coordination patterns allow the user to further refine the coordina-tion requirement on the popover. For example, when attribute filter (C3) is proposed (Fig. 4b), the user can specify the constraints with more concrete criterion (e.g., “total price below 500") in natural language.

## 6.3 Extending the Scope of Coordination 

Cling extends coordination support through action reuse across additional pages and retrieval of attributes beyond the initially extracted page models. 

6.3.1 Reusing Actions Across Pages. Cross-page coordination in web tasks often involves more than two pages. To extend coor-dination beyond two pages arranged side by side, Cling allows users to save pairwise coordination actions and reuse them across additional pages. Saved actions, such as those applying a shared budget or spatial constraints from a floor plan, appear in a side panel (Fig. 4d), where users can selectively apply them to new page pairs. This provides a visual way to reuse actions across pages and incrementally apply multiple constraints. For example, when select-ing furniture under budget and spatial constraints, users can save actions that check prices against a shared budget and dimensions against available space in a floor plan. As they browse additional listings, they can reapply these actions to assess each option against both constraints without repeatedly establishing the same cross-page relationships. 

6.3.2 Retrieving External Attributes. Coordination actions may de-pend on attributes that are not available on the existing extraction of the page model. For example, when selecting furniture, the di-mensions the user looks for may be nested in the product detail pages. Likewise, when filtering a houseplant catalog, a user may specify a “cat-safe” criterion that requires searching for general knowledge beyond the catalog. To retrieve these missing attributes, Cling uses the Gemini API with Google Search grounding [30] to obtain the requested attribute values for relevant entities on the page (Fig. 4e).

## 7 Technical Evaluation 

We conducted a technical evaluation of Cling’s end-to-end pipeline by first extracting page schemas and then applying schema corre-spondence to surface cross-page coordination opportunities. Al-though the evaluation focuses on surfaced opportunities, it also reflects schema extraction quality, as downstream coordination depends on it. Our evaluation was guided by the following research questions: 

RQ1 : How accurate are the coordination opportunities surfaced by the pipeline? 

RQ2 : How useful are the surfaced coordination opportunities in the context of the pages being coordinated? 

## 7.1 Methodology 

We constructed an evaluation dataset spanning three task domains commonly used in prior evaluations of web-based systems: travel planning [15, 36, 64, 70], shopping [14, 43, 51], and information work [17, 43, 48, 50, 76]. Within each domain, we identified five representative tasks. For each task, we selected three web pages that were central to the completion of the task and that exhibited diverse interface structures. We then combined these pages into pairs, yielding three pairs per task. In total, this produced 45 page pairs. To ensure stable evaluation conditions, we archived each page by downloading its HTML and associated assets and serving them locally, thereby avoiding nondeterministic changes in live web content. We then ran the schema extraction pipeline for each archived page using gemini-2.5-flash-lite [20]. For each page pair, we sampled representative source entities from the extracted schemas by selecting one instance of each dis-tinct entity type on the page. For each sampled source entity, we ran the pipeline to surface all possible coordination opportunities with the paired page. When appropriate, we manually added reasonable task-level constraints, such as a shared budget across two shop-ping pages, to reflect realistic coordination scenarios. This process yielded 220 coordination opportunities. Across the 5 patterns, the dataset includeds 17 Entity Cross-reference (C1), 87 Attribute Cross-reference (C2), 30 Attribute Filter (C3), 71 Attribute Transfer (C4), and 15 Entity Transfer (C5). The large numbers of C2 and C4, com-pared to the others, are expected, as entities often contain multiple attributes. Two external human coders independently annotated each surfaced coordination opportunity for accuracy , whether the pipeline correctly identified the opportunity, and usefulness , how helpful the opportunity would be in the context of the paired pages and task scenario. Usefulness was rated on a 5-point ordinal scale. Our accuracy measure evaluates the correctness of surfaced opportunities, rather than recall over all potentially useful coordi-nations. Measuring recall would require a ground truth of all salient coordination opportunities across all possible tasks involving a page pair, which is difficult to define because relevance depends on users’ intents, constraints, and workflows. To assess whether the technical pipeline supports interactive use during browsing, we measured entity model extraction time for each of the 48 webpages in the evaluation suite over three independent runs. 

## 7.2 Results 

For accuracy, the two coders achieved high inter-rater reliability across the 220 evaluated coordination opportunities. We report Gwet’s AC1 [31], as very high raw agreement in several categories can make measures such as Cohen’s 𝜅 unstable [19]. Overall, coders achieved Gwet’s AC1 = 0.910 with 92.3% observed agreement, indi-cating substantial agreement. We did not seek consensus on use-fulness because, unlike correctness, it was intentionally rated with respect to each coder’s own lived experience. 

Accuracy of Surfaced Opportunities . Overall, the pipeline sur-faced coordination opportunities with high accuracy, achieving an average accuracy of 92.5% across all 220 opportunities. Broken down by coordination pattern type, the average accuracies were 94.1%, 92.5%, 85.0%, 94.4%, and 96.7% for C1 through C5, respec-tively. 

Usefulness of Surfaced Opportunities . Surfaced opportunities were generally rated as useful, with a mean score of 4.48 /5. Mean ratings for C1–C5 were 4.94, 4.08, 4.57, 4.72, and 4.93, respectively. 

Entity Model Extraction Latency . Across individual pages, the mean extraction time was 5.82 seconds (95% CI: 5.31–6.33 seconds), with a median of 5.02 seconds and a 95th percentile of 11.46 seconds. Conference’17, July 2017, Washington, DC, USA Huang, et al. 

## 7.3 Takeaways 

The results suggest that the pipeline can reliably surface coordi-nation opportunities that are both accurate and useful across co-ordination patterns. Accuracy was high overall and strongest for C1 and C5, indicating that the pipeline performs especially well when coordinating at the entity-level. Usefulness was also high overall, but varied more across coders and coordination patterns, suggesting that perceived value depends more on task context and prior habits than correctness does. Most inaccuracies stemmed from attribute misalignment. Schema extraction sometimes retained under-specified labels such as name ,even when multiple entity types on the same page (e.g., foods and restaurants) exposed that label, causing incorrect matches. Repeated entities on a page also did not always share identical attribute struc-tures: some items included additional or variant attributes, such as 

discounted to alongside price . This could cause the pipeline to miss the intended correspondence or coordinate on an available but less relevant attribute (e.g., using the listed rather than dis-counted price when applying a budget constraint during shopping). C3 was further affected by our use of a single semantic similarity threshold, which could be either too strict (e.g., failing to match product titles when one page omitted a brand name) or too permis-sive (e.g., matching address strings that shared city, state, and ZIP code despite referring to different locations). Variation in usefulness ratings reinforces our decision to present coordination opportunities in a lightweight, non-distracting man-ner rather than as a persistent or intrusive feature. This is especially important for C2, which is inexpensive to propose and thus easy to surface frequently, but may not always be useful for the user. 

## 8 User Study 

We conducted a two-part user study of Cling, examining its effec-tiveness in structured cross-page coordination tasks and its role in broader exploratory information activities. We sought to answer the following research questions: 

RQ1 : How effectively and efficiently does Cling support cross-page coordination? 

RQ2 : What are the values and limitations do people experi-ence with Cling’s in-situ, small-step automation during cross-page tasks? 

RQ3 : How do people incorporate cross-page coordination sup-port into broader exploratory tasks? 

## 8.1 Participants 

We recruited 12 participants (6 female, 6 male, aged 20–28) through internal communication channels within a large public university. Participants included undergraduate and Ph.D. students. All par-ticipants use browsers on a daily bases and find themselves using multiple pages to complete a single task many times a day. All sessions were conducted in person and lasted approximately 100 minutes. Each session was screen- and audio-recorded. Participants received a 35 USD Amazon gift card as compensation. 

## 8.2 Prototype Conditions 

All three conditions used the same underlying Electron/Chromium browser shell, which supported side-by-side page views and pro-vided lightweight spreadsheet, calculator, and rich-text editor tools. The conditions differed only in the coordination and automation support layered on top of this common environment. 

Manual. The manual condition used the base browser shell with-out any Cling-specific cross-page coordination or AI automation support. 

End-to-End Agent. The End-to-End Agent agentic condition augmented the same browser shell with an AI assistant imple-mented using Browser Use v0.13.8 1, an open-source framework for connecting LLMs to web browsers, with GPT-5.6 Sol as the underlying model. To provide a strong browser-automation base-line, we enabled DOM-based page inspection, bounded JavaScript execution for batched edits, and a scratchpad for tracking multi-step tasks. The agent was instructed to ground task-specific claims in evidence observed during the task rather than rely solely on pretrained knowledge. 

Cling. The Cling condition augmented the same browser shell with the technical backend described in Section 5 and the cross-page coordination interactions described in Section 6. 

## 8.3 Procedure 

The study consisted of the following phases. 

Introduction (5 mins) . Participants were introduced to study context and procedure; informed consent was obtained. 

Tutorial (10 min) . Participants went through structured tutorial for all three prototypes. For each tutorial, the experimenter first demonstrated the relevant interactions using a separate example task, after which participants completed a brief hands-on practice task and could ask clarification questions. Practice materials were not reused in the measured tasks.

The tasks were designed around representative cross-page coordination episodes spanning all five coordination patterns introduced in Sec-tion 4, while retaining objectively specified outcomes that allowed us to measure task completion, correctness and completeness, and completion time. In the End-to-End Agent condition, participants were instructed to use the AI assistant as the primary means of in-teracting with webpages, asking the agent to carry out page actions on their behalf whenever possible. They remained free to inspect the pages themselves to understand the information or verify the agent’s work.

We counted criteria articulated during think-aloud or externalized in the artifact, including filtering constraints reused in Cling and from prompts sent to the AI assistant in the baseline. The combined count of criterion additions and updates was higher with Cling than with the baseline ( 𝑀 = 6.58 vs. 3 .83; Holm-adjusted 𝑝 = 0.41). Criteria updates specifically were also more frequent with Cling (𝑀 = 1.50 vs. 0 .08; two-sided exact paired sign-flip test, Holm-adjusted 𝑝 = 0.008). Although updates were modest in absolute number, eleven of twelve participants updated at least one criterion with Cling, compared with only one participant in the baseline. 

## 9 Discussion 9.1 Situated Automation vs. End-to-End Delegation 

Cling makes automation available as coordination needs arise dur-ing ongoing exploration. This is particularly beneficial when tasks involve tacit, dynamic, or compounded constraints that emerge through exploration. For example, when deciding which furniture to buy, P7 reported that only after extended comparison did a small set of options begin to “make sense.” What makes an option de-sirable may depend on many interdependent considerations and be burdensome to articulate. The situated automation supported by Cling does not need to know why an option is desirable; it only needs enough information to carry out the coordination that follows from the user’s choice. This contrasts with task-level delega-tion in end-to-end automation, which pressures users to translate contextual and still-evolving intent into language. P1 described the “inertia to put things into words,” while P10 preferred directly changing an explicit filter rather than explaining through chat that a previous constraint no longer applied. Participants also reported that Cling allowed them to remain actively involved for enjoyment, learning, or a sense of responsi-bility, without being burdened by manual work they considered “noise” (P5). For example, P7 explained that when exploring an un-familiar space, they might work more manually or “offload a lot” depending on “how much I want to learn.” P2 chose to rely on their own judgment when selecting furniture simply because doing so was “fun.” P4 connected the cross-page transfer task to a recurring work activity in which they would shortlist papers from a large pool for a mentee. They felt Cling would be a good fit because Cling: Supporting Cross-Page Coordination with Human-Directed, In-Situ, and Small-Step Automation Conference’17, July 2017, Washington, DC, USA 

it could offload the manual foraging of papers while leaving the substantive judgment of suitability to her. More broadly, this suggests an automate-as-you-go model in which exploration, automation, and assessment remain tightly cou-pled. Rather than requiring users to specify a complete task and then inspect its outcome, automation can be invoked incrementally as new needs emerge. Instead of merely remaining present to ap-prove or correct automated actions, the user would exert his or her own judgements and redirect the task as their intent develops. 

## 9.2 Schema Correspondence as a Basis for Controllable Automation 

In this work, we formalize cross-page coordination as a pervasive interaction pattern in everyday information work and introduce a schema-correspondence perspective that provides a concrete vocab-ulary for a previously fragmented set of low-level tasks, including copying and pasting, information retrieval, content reformatting, and transfer. Rather than treating these as separate interaction tech-niques, schema correspondence characterizes them in terms of how entities and attributes on one page relate to those on another. This abstraction also provides a basis for more controllable au-tomation. LLMs can recover schemas and semantic correspondences from heterogeneous webpages, while explicit rules deterministically specify how information should be selected, aligned, transformed, and transferred. This separation confines probabilistic reasoning to interpreting heterogeneous interfaces while keeping execution predictable and inspectable. Because the resulting rules operate over semantic structures rather than page-specific DOM elements, they can also be inspected, modified, reused, and composed as coordination needs evolve. 

## 9.3 Toward a Semantically Structured Web 

In the current Web ecosystem and the design of Cling, the schema of each page must often be reconstructed on the client side at use time, causing different users to repeatedly infer similar semantic structures from the same webpages. A more efficient architecture would allow webpages to expose their entities, attributes, and rela-tionships directly as machine-readable schemas, bringing the Web closer to the Semantic Web vision [6]. The historical difficulty of this vision was partly practical: author-ing and maintaining semantic representations imposed additional work on webpage developers and required agreement on represen-tation conventions. LLMs substantially lower this barrier by making it possible to generate and maintain such representations from ex-isting content and interfaces during authoring time. Importantly, this need not require every webpage to share a universal schema. Individual pages could expose their own local semantic structures, while systems such as Cling dynamically infer correspondences between them as coordination needs arise. This suggests a path toward semantic interoperability that combines authoring-time semantic structure with use-time semantic alignment. 

## 10 Conclusion 

To summarize, we conceptualize cross-page coordination as a dis-tinct class of interaction and characterize it through five recurring coordination patterns. We then introduce a technical pipeline that reconstructs reusable webpage schemas and identifies coordination opportunities through schema correspondence, operationalized in our prototype, Cling. Our technical evaluations show that this ap-proach can surface accurate and useful coordination opportunities, while findings from the user study show that in-situ, small-step automation enables users to perform coordination tasks efficiently while remaining in control. More broadly, this work points toward an information environment in which independently developed webpages become interoperable at the point of use, allowing users and AI to collaboratively compose information and functionality across page boundaries to accomplish complex information tasks more effectively.
