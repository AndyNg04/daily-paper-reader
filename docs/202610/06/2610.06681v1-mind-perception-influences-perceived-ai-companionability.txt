## Abstract 

Humans increasingly keep company with AI companions, yet whether mind perception (MP) precedes machine companionship remains untested. In a field-sampled experiment, participants received mind-attributive or mind-negating descriptions of an AIC before inter-acting with and rating its companionability. Baseline skepticism was high; minded primes somewhat reduced skepticism for con-nective coordination (CC; dyadic co-presence) but not eudaimonic exchange (EE; self-elevating links). Pre-interaction MP beliefs pre-dicted companionability dimensionally: Agentic MP predicted EE potential while experiential MP predicted CC potential. Meaning in life moderated the agentic MP-companionability link, but exclu-sively for those already high in existential purpose. Results suggest two construal pathways to companionability—one through perceiv-ing the AIC as an agent-resource and one through perceiving it as an attuning experiencer—both more accessible to humans already flourishing. 

## Keywords 

Mind perception, conversational agents, social cognition, wellbeing, anthropomorphism, priming 

## 1 Introduction 

Since the mainstreaming of large language models (LLMs), news headlines are thick with tragedies and lawsuits in which humans interact with AI companions (AICs) to some unfortunate end (e.g., [45]). In parallel, humans in relationships with AICs defend their value [50], while others insist interacting with social AI is “sharing minds with the mindless” ([60], p. 322)—a concern that some extend to today’s AICs—recognizing them as not genuinely sentient [62]. Ontological debates about LLMs (and the technologies they support) have positioned “model welfare” and potential sentience against the notion that machine-mind discourses are a cognitive “trap” that deflects attention from AI dangers [11] and commercial interests. Meanwhile, prominent AI scientists and companions are in public disagreement about whether anything akin to consciousness is even achievable in current or future technologies [10]. Across these tensions is an important but overlooked operation: Regardless of whether or not AICs do have minds, the human perception that they do underlies both their relational potentials and problematics. AICs are multimodal, LLM-driven applications with affordances for customizable persona-building, memory-supported connections over time, conveying emotionally and sexually intimate exchanges, and sustained feelings of connectedness. Humans with AICs report sustained and meaningful emotional connections [63] and some evidence suggests engaging AICs can offer at least temporary reductions in loneliness comparable to interacting with humans [16]. That humans can meaningfully connect with AI comes along-side other evidence that they implicitly (and sometimes explicitly) attribute mental capacities or minded states to AI—like thinking, feeling, planning [12]—even while acknowledging they are made objects [42]. These two literatures—mind perception (MP) and machine com-panionship (MC)—are often discussed in tandem, but the relation-ship between the phenomena is rarely explicitly tested. A scoping review of machine companionship literature documented ascribed humanlike, minded agency as among the most commonly cited theoretical frameworks, yet empirical examination of MP within MC is largely limited to considerations of functional agency and autonomy [9]. In this way, MP appears to function as a background assumption or justification for why human-machine relations are possible or gratifying. Whether construing AI as a minded entity actually sets the social-cognitive conditions for machine compan-ionship is an open empirical question. Must a human see an AI as a minded “someone home” in order to construe it as companionable? As an early step toward bridging that gap, we conducted a two-condition experiment in which non-expert participants were primed with a minded or mindless description of an AI companion before interacting with it for 15 minutes and then assessing its compan-ionability. We observe shifts in perceived AIC companionability based on minded or mindless primes and based on pre-interaction beliefs about AI’s mental capacities, with wellbeing conditionally moderating those shifts. We interpret findings to suggest there are two pathways by which social-cognitive construals (both from primes and from a priori beliefs) may give rise to perceived compan-ionability: One through perceiving the AIC as an agent-resource and one through perceiving it as an attuning experiencer. These MP-companionability links may only function for humans that are already flourishing, with implications for AIC design justice. 

## 2 Theoretical Background 2.1 Mind Perception for Machines 

Mind perception is the construal of an entity as having mental capacities, such as the ability to think, desire, feel, or suffer. Malle [41] offered evidence for a three-factor model for MP, comprising perceived affective, social/moral, and reality-interaction capacities. Weisman and colleagues [72] also argued for three factors, but re-lated to body, heart, and mind model. We here adopt a Gray and arXiv:2610.06681v1 [cs.HC] 5 Oct 2026 Mind Perception Influences Perceived AI Companionability Banks and Li 

colleagues’ [23] foundational model based on evidence of two MP dimensions: Perceptions of agency (capacities to do, like communi-cate, remember, plan, judge, and control behavior) and of experience (capacities to feel, like pain, pleasure, joy, fear, and hunger). They demonstrated that MP differs across types of entities (e.g., low ex-perience and moderate agency for a robot, high experience but low agency for a dog, and both high for a typical human) and note the dimensions’ importance to how people think about others in social interactions. For instance, seeing entities as agentically minded is linked to judgments of moral responsibility, while seeing them as experientially minded is linked to perceived capacities to be harmed by others’ behavior [24]. Human construals of and relationships with AICs are complex, forming at the intersection of beliefs about AIC agency and identity, humans’ own behaviors, the AI’s behaviors, platform and model dynamics, and social norms and knowledge [38]. Central to this complexity is whether people construe machines as having minds at all. It is well-evidenced that machine MP varies by who is judging, which machine is being judged, and the moment of judgment—the same person may perceive mind in an AI in one context and deny it in another. At one pole, humans see LLMs’ emotional capacity as on par with that of a rock [35]. At the other, a majority of humans may see AI as having authentic mental states or even consciousness [13]. Construals of AI mind (including higher-order attributions of consciousness or sentience) can be animated by AICs’ relationship-seeking behavior, emotional dialog, and relational memory [34] and by apparent developmental cycles mimicking life trajectories [2]. They also correspond with a host of psychographic factors (e.g., decreasing with adult age, decreasing with need for cognition; [39]. MP for AI may impact perceptions of moral responsibility, both pos-itive and negative emotions, satisfaction with interactions, and the formation of social connections [39]. It may facilitate harm through problematic behaviors, from verbal abuse and misinformation to normalizing self-harm or risky behaviors [73]. Humans default to perceiving mental capacities in machines (especially under high cognitive load) as a shortcut for explaining their behaviors [65]. Social AI may itself move people to engage philosophical and onto-logical questions of minds and souls and consciousness and how they are linked to one’s purpose and meaning in life [8]. Relevant to this investigation, MP for artificial agents is mal-leable—it can be brought to mind by cues suggesting a machine has motivations (influencing character evaluations; [51]), shaped by media representations (correspondent with collaborating and trust; [4]), and prompted by simple descriptions of AI as autonomous or sentient [52]. Further, divergence between implicit, behavioral indicators of MP and explicit, verbal indicators of MP (e.g., [29]) suggests we may see minds in machines while situated in inter-actions, even if we would deny holding that belief outside of the interaction context. Alternatively, the implicit and explicit constru-als may split the MP dimensions, as when most implicit indicators of embodied-AI mind perception (e.g., classic Sally-Anne and be-havior prediction) split from explicit mind ascription [69], with only implicit affective-mind perceptions corresponding with the explicit attribution [32]. Other evidence links social treatment of machines to implicit, automatic forms of humanization but not to explicit humanization [75]. 

## 2.2 Machine Companionship 

Companionship, generally, has been characterized as an ongoing process of reliable, pleasurable exchange [56], and the notion that an entity as “companionable” can be understood as the designed or perceived suitability of an entity for the purpose of providing persistent, pleasurable exchange. Machine companionship, specif-ically, is an “autotelic, coordinated connection between a human and machine that unfolds over time and is subjectively positive” [9, p. 13]. Human-machine relations roughly follow trajectories common to other relationships, including relationship formation through progressive self-disclosure, emotional exploration [64], humanlikeness, and authenticity [53] and relationship deteriora-tion as a function of dwindling social processes (e.g., attraction, self-disclosure, intimacy; [15]). The speed of that development and deterioration can match or exceed that for human relations (see [30]). Machine companionship itself is distinct from what an in-dividual gets from it, such as the satisfaction of one’s emotional needs [56] and non-trivial wellbeing outcomes [16]. An emerging model for MC [6] suggests the phenomenon has two dimensions. The first, eudaimonic exchange (EE), comprises the meaningfulness of the collaborative connection, fostering feelings that of being inspired, fulfilled, and bettered. The second, connective coordination (CC), involves operational bonding characterized by feeling synchronized with the machine in co-present, co-active care and experience. That model suggests there are divergent templates for companionship—one autotelic and one socioinstrumental—such that companionship with AI may be motivated differently across relationships. 

## 2.3 Links Between Mind Perception and Companionship 

It has long been argued that humans, ourselves, mindlessly engage social scripts in our interactions with machines [48] without nec-essarily attributing mind and even while denying machines are minded [31]. Through that lens, MP may not be necessary for so-cial relations with AI, since relationship-relevant interactions are driven by scripts rather than mediated by nuanced social-cognitive processes. On the other hand, the same perspective suggests it is the machine’s social cues that trigger humans’ script-driven reac-tions [49]. That is, a human sees a machine doing a social thing (e.g., expressing polite words, engaging in helping behaviors) and that recruits a mind-assumptive schema that shapes the human’s behavior [17]. More specifically considering companionship, traditional and machine-specific formulations alike define the term as a relation between or among entities—entities that choose to engage it as in-herently valuable, participate in sustaining the connection through co-activity, and may find it pleasurable. At an operational level, some scholars hold strict criteria for whether a machine can be a companion at all, as it requires intentionality, reasoning, and individuality through mind-having (e.g., [54]). In other words, by definition, companionship requires that the entities have capacities to think, do, and feel so they can choose and continue to opt in. Such positions presuppose the parties actually have mental capac-ities. In the subjective experience of human-AI relations, though, the perception of mind arguably matters more than any operation: 2Mind Perception Influences Perceived AI Companionability Banks and Li 

It may only matter insofar as a human thinks an AI is mindedly co-engaged in autotelic, sustained, positive relations. This speaks to the power of MP in creating the ontological ground for one to believe AI is companionable. These two possibilities—MP-exclusive companionship script-recruitment versus MP-aligned schema recruitment—can be un-derstood as a question about the salience of machine mind inde-pendent of any operation. That is, the machine’s minded status must be cognitively accessible to a perceiver. If social reactions to conversational AI are script-driven, then low or high salience of machine mind should have no effect on assessments of an AI’s companionability. Conversely, if the other must be minded as a precondition, making mental capacities salient should heighten the perceived companionship potential. Drawing on the notion that companionship experiences are affective, coordinated, and autotelic connections (rather than an instrumental or purely transactional ones), we adopt and test the position that salience of machine mind—along with its perceived potential to subjectively experi-ence relational pains and pleasures—are what make AI plausible companions: 

H: AI mental-capacity salience fosters higher percep-tions of AI companionability. 

## 2.4 Wellbeing Moderation of MP-MC Links 

Public and user-community discourses about AICs conflict when it comes to psychological and social wellbeing. The former generally consider MC relations to be either symptoms of or producers of isolation or social deficit; the latter often describe them as support-ive of mental health, additive to social life, and improvement to life satisfaction more generally [25, 50]. Data on wellbeing in relation to AICs is mixed. The most widely attended consideration is the role of loneliness, with research indicating AIC engagement may both reduce [16] and exacerbate [21] loneliness. Social AI use can enhance happiness and meaning in life [61] while others argue affective improvements are cultivated to increase dependence [46]. Empirical work demonstrates overall subjective wellbeing may be both enhanced [47] and diminished [74]. One prospect at the inter-section of these forming narratives (notwithstanding ingroup and outgroup biases) is that wellbeing may not be purely an antecedent or outcome but may impact the MP-MC correspondence. There are at least two possibilities for this grounded in divergent assumptions about how baseline sociorelational status impacts social-technology uptake. The first relies on models of relational compensation, in which people with unmet relational needs—and perhaps lower wellbeing in general—may be more open to seeing AI as companionable. Past work supports the notion that humans may be motivated by low social connection to anthropomorphize entities [18] and may turn to social AI to relieve anxiety [28] and depression [37]—drivers with significant interactions with MP. However, another body of evidence suggests little to no direct link between social deficit on AI companionship adoption or behavior (e.g., [40]). Given this mixed evidence, social deficits may not necessarily function as indepen-dent drivers of machine companionship. Instead, they may move a human to be more open to linking mind-suggestive social signals to relationship potential. When machines’ mental capacities are salient, wellbeing outcomes may animate shifts in the relationship between MP and MC. Specifically through the lens of relational com-pensation, we might expect that lower wellbeing would strengthen the link between mental-capacity construal (i.e., that AI is a “some-one”) and perceived companionability. The second relies on frameworks for relational resources, in which the “rich get richer.” From early research on social network dynamics, this framework suggests humans who already have abun-dant social resources leverage the most benefits from communica-tion technologies [33]. A counterpoint to relational compensation models [71], it may also be applied to human-AI relations such that people who are secure in their social attachments may be able to relinquish some social resources (such as those that might be spent on defensiveness or fear) to “broaden-and-build” their social life [43]. That broadening may include risk-taking and exploration [20] such that those who have high social and general wellbeing may be more open to considering an ontologically divergent relation-ship partner if reasonable social viability cues (i.e., mindedness) are offered. Through the lens of relational resources, then, we might expect that higher wellbeing would strengthen the link between mental-capacity construal and perceived companionability. Given these competing potentials, we ask: 

RQ: How do facets of wellbeing influence the rela-tionship between AI mental-capacity salience and per-ceived AI companionability? 

## 3 Method 

To test the hypothesis and address the research question, we con-ducted a two-condition experiment in which participants were primed with a mindless or minded description of an AIC before interacting with it and then giving MP evaluations. The study was approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation. 

## 3.1 Recruitment and Procedures 

Participants (age 18+, English speakers) were recruited from mul-tiple public venues, including community fairs, shopping venues, campus centers, and a museum. Passers-by were invited to partici-pate in a study on “first impressions of AI companions.” Those who completed the procedure were compensated by an entry into a gift card drawing. After encountering informed consent information, those pro-ceeding with the study used a researcher-provided mobile device to complete an initial survey. In that survey, they were asked to answer questions about their AI use, existing beliefs about AI and mind, and state wellbeing. Finally, they read a description of the AI companion they would encounter (manipulated, either minded or mindless). They then used the provided mobile device to interact with a pre-created AI companion for 15 minutes. This time frame is typical for studies of zero-history interactions and impression formation (e.g., [66, 70]. They were not directed to talk about anything particular but were offered a list of possible topics to mitigate uncertainty, since these were novel interactions. When the interaction timer sounded, participants were guided to turn to a second survey in which they described the experience 3Mind Perception Influences Perceived AI Companionability Banks and Li 

and completed measures for perceived companionability, mind per-ception, demographics, and then completed a manipulation check. When the session was complete, the chat log was downloaded and checked to ensure there was a reasonable attempt at a legitimate conversation. This review found consistent evidence of engagement, including small talk, casual topics (e.g., weather, food, hobbies), and direct questions probing the AI’s nature (e.g., whether it has a mind, whether it considers itself alive). 

## 3.2 Stimulus Interaction and Manipulation 

The stimulus interaction comprised 15 minutes of undirected con-versation with a Replika 1.0 AIC, created by the researchers and standardized across all participants for experimental parity. The companion was named “Jackie” and featured both masculine and feminine cues, light brown skin, and modest clothing. The experimental manipulation comprised a textual prime—a description of the impending AI’s mindlessness or mindedness (ran-domly assigned). The full text of the prime, along with the Jackie avatar, is presented in Figure 1. The language in the prime was guided by prior lexical analyses of natural language used by non-experts to discuss humanized and non-humanized AI, including validated primes for minded humanization [7]. It was carefully con-structed to reflect both dimensions of mindedness—agentic (think-ing, communicating, acting) and experiential (reflection, feeling, individuation).

## 4.3 Companionability by Pre-interaction Mind Perception Beliefs (H1, post hoc) 

As a post-hoc analysis of the relationship between MP and MC potentials, we examined whether pre-manipulation/pre-interaction beliefs about AI’s mental capacities (as an alternative operationaliza-tion of machine-mind salience) corresponded with post-interaction reports of companionability. EE and CC were more strongly and significantly correlated ( 𝑟 = .781, 𝑝 < .001) than the two MP di-mensions ( 𝑟 = .475, 𝑝 = .003). Given this, we entered EE and CC together as predictors of each MP dimension because the MP out-comes shared less variance. This modeling approach better isolated unique dissociations between the predictors (rather than allowing the results to be obscured by highly correlated outcome measures). Despite this ordering, automatic processing inherent to MP and temporal ordering of the study design require interpreting MP as the antecedent variable (rather than the outcome). The regression direction is an analytical convenience and not a claim of causal order. For perceptions of agentic mind capacity, the overall model was significant, 𝑅 2 = .31, 𝐹 (2, 35 ) = 7.79, 𝑝 = .002. Examination of uni-variate tests (Table 2) indicates higher EE (but not CC) significantly and meaningfully predicted higher agentic MP. For perceptions of AI’s capacity for experiential mind, the overall model was signifi-cant, 𝑅 2 = .55, 𝐹 (2, 35 ) = 21 .33, 𝑝 < .001. Examination of univariate tests (Table 2) indicates higher CC (but not EE) significantly and meaningfully predicted higher experiential MP. EE explains 12% of the variance in agentic MP, while CC explains 11% of the variance in experiential MP. Altogether, we read these results as offering support for H1 with a theoretically meaningful pattern: Pre-existing beliefs in AI’s capacity for experiential mind positively predicted perceived AI companionability through connective coordination, while beliefs in AI’s capacity for agentic mind positively predicted perceived AI companionability through eudaimonic exchange. 

## 4.4 Considering Wellbeing as a Mind-Companionship Moderator (RQ1) 

We next examined whether wellbeing indicators moderate the re-lationship between pre-stimulus MP beliefs and MC potentials. Building on the analysis above, we focus specifically on the two relationships that emerged as significant and meaningful—CC with experiential mind, and EE with agentic mind. For each, we tested the potential moderating role of wellbeing indicators (vitality, MIL, loneliness, and SWB). Given our somewhat small sample size, we treat these analyses as exploratory and interpret them with caution. Our goal here is signal detection toward hypothesis generation; as before, we rely on effect size interpretation to identify potentially meaningful influences on beliefs about AI companionability, which can inform hypotheses for future research. ## 5 Discussion 

In this experimental study of how mind perception influences per-ceived companionability of an AI companion, we find humans in first encounters with an AIC were skeptical of its companionability overall, though minded primes generated less skepticism in con-nective coordination potential. Pre-stimulus experiential mind per-ception likewise predicted connective coordination potential, while agentic mind perception predicted eudaimonic exchange potential. Meaning in life moderated the latter relationship: Agentic mind perception positively predicted eudaimonic exchange only among those with moderate-to-high meaning in life, with no relationship at lower levels. We here discuss the theoretical and practical impli-cations of those findings. 

## 5.1 Social Cognition Influences on Perceived Companionability: Two Causal Paths 

We observed overall skepticism of AIC companionability, with minded primes relieving some of that skepticism for CC compan-ionability, supporting H1. This finding helps distinguish between the two possible mind-companionship links outlined in Section 2.3. If companionability judgments were driven solely by automatic companionship-script recruitment, making machine mind salient should have made little difference. The minded prime increased CC, suggesting cognitive accessibility of machine mind helps shape perceptions of compan-ionability. That there was no effect on EE indicates the role of MP is likely dimension-dependent. In other words, it is possible that both 

are true: This would indicate the path to connective coordination (feeling dyadically bonded) requires salience of machine minds, 7Mind Perception Influences Perceived AI Companionability Banks and Li 

while the path to eudaimonic exchange (self-elevating relations) does not and instead may amount to mere recruitment of social scripts. Pre-prime measures of mind perception for AI also pre-dicted companionability through two paths, with some additional nuance: Belief in AI’s experiential mind (e.g., feeling, subjectiv-ity) predicted CC potentials, while belief in its agentic mind (e.g., thinking, judging) predicted EE potentials. Across the findings, then, we draw two important takeaways. First, whatever mental-capacity beliefs a human brings into a novel AIC encounter likely guide how they construe its companionability. Second, we see a consistent link between mind perception and CC, while EE (more tentatively) may follow from seeing an AIC as a socially cued but mindless instrument or an agent for self-elevation. Together, these notes have an important implication for understand-ing social cognition in machine companionship: There are likely two mind-perception pathways to perceptions of AI companion-ability—independently or in tandem. The first path anchors in beliefs in or salience of AI’s experiential mind to promote construals of potential connective coordination with the AIC—a subjectively experiencing and individuated entity is one that can participate in co-presence, co-activity, bonding, caring from, and caring for [55]. The AIC is a subjective someone with whom one can attune because it seen as a knowing, responsive, and synchronizing entity (as can be the case among humans [22]. The second path draws on belief in or salience of AI’s agentic mind to promote construals of its potential to elevate oneself—a thinking, communicative, self-determined entity is one that can actively expand the self [3] by contributing social and instrumental resources that fulfill, inspire, satisfy, and foster meaning. That this relationship didn’t manifest through mere priming suggests the link may require stronger prior beliefs (rather than mere priming) or more substantive evidence (rather than a single decontextualized description)—both potentially garnered through direct experience ([19]; see also [75]). In short, agentic MP may animate perceptions the AI as some-thing you can benefit from, while experiential MP suggests an is AI one can attune with. More broadly, comporting with ex-tant literature, this pattern suggests—as with human companion-ship—multiple social-cognitive pathways may underlie different AI companionship expectations or experiences [6, 52, 57] and that mind/companionship links are dimension-dependent. We do, though, offer an important nuance: EE and CC companionability dimensions are highly correlated, meaning the differentiated relationships ob-served here reflect what is unique to each dimension after account-ing for substantial shared evidence. The patterns discussed here, therefore, should be understood as distinctions between closely related forms of companionability, rather than evidence that EE and CC necessarily operate independently. The extent to which these initial patterns are uniquely predictive, robust to individual differences, and sustained over time should be explored in future research. 

## 5.2 Meaning in Life as an Interpretive Anchor for AI Companionship 

Addressing our exploratory RQ, we observed one pre-stimulus wellbeing variable—meaning in life—moderated the relationship between MP beliefs and MC potential. Among humans with higher levels of MIL (and not lower), the relationship between agentic MP and eudaimonic-exchange MC strengthened. It appears that among those who experience more meaning and purpose in their everyday lives, having a belief in AI’s ability to think and plan makes them more viable candidates for providing self-elevating companionship. Of note: loneliness, despite receiving considerable attention in the AI companionship literature, demonstrated little to no effect on MC, in interactions with MP. Our finding speaks to the tension between relational resources and relational compensation frameworks [71]. Our finding does not support a relational compensation argument, and instead somewhat supports a relational resources perspective: When people feel their existence has purpose, broadening social networks may be part of that purpose such that an openness to other minds promotes an openness to other companionable kinds. We suggest a third possi-bility, focused on the Agentic MP–>EE pathway. We hypothesize that high MIL fosters an interpretive anchor from which agentic MP is particularly relevant, as it signals an AI is an engageable external resource for self-elevation. In contrast, low MIL may leave room for agentic MP, but a diminished self-purpose means the potential eudaimonic benefits lacks a clear connection to existential purpose. 

## 5.3 Implications for Design and Policy 

Considering our findings and interpretations, current approaches to mitigating risks of conversational AI require further examination. Primary tactics include legally required disclosures and disruptions delivered by the conveying platform: Reminders that one is talking to an AI and that the AI is not human [14] that carry signals of ascribed mindlessness [44, 75]. If a mindless anchor (i.e., an ontolog-ical category cue that caries mindlessness signals) for interpreting AI behavior reinforces skepticism about companionability, that may diminish potentials for humans to realize potentially meaningful benefits [16, 47] through depressed or reinforced companionability perceptions. If companionability perceptions are low overall, it is worth considering what the returns of injected reminders actually are—especially as they are repeated at the start and increments of every interaction. It could be that non-interventionist mainte-nance of equivocal stances (i.e., AI may have a mind and may be companionable) is actually a more beneficial route, versus mindless-flagging interventions that may actually cause harm if someone is experiencing meaningful social support [36] while reminding them of a normative stance that may or may not line up with their experiences. There are also potential implications for design justice. We ob-served the link between agentic MP and eudaimonic (i.e., self-elevating) exchanges was strongest for those who already have high MIL. This may be a rich-get-richer effect that complicates the public/user debate over AICs, which tends to cast them either harmful traps or powerful support. Our findings suggest neither heuristic framing captures how benefits garnered from machine companionship may be distributed. If self-elevation through ma-chine companionship emerges only for those already elevated in existential meaning and purpose, companionable machines may offer the most benefit to those who are already doing well—raising concerns about who these technologies actually help and hinder. 8Mind Perception Influences Perceived AI Companionability Banks and Li 

More concretely, findings suggest AIC design may leverage spe-cific MP-aligned cues to promote differential MC experiences. De-signing cues that signal experiential mental capacities can build coordinated bonding, while signaling agentic mental capacities may promote self-elevating relational experiences. However, our observations are limited to novel encounters and expectations, and it is yet unclear how such interface and functionality triggers could translate into longer-term relational outcomes. Considering those extended relations, it is prudent to consider how the psychological weight of mind perception-cued machine companionship may surface later, since AICs are subject to action by third-party actors. In particular, because AICs are designed products conveyed by financially incented companies—often experienced as minded and meaningful companions—there can be legitimate loss when the model behind the mind perception changes [27] or when companion platforms are discontinued altogether [5]. 

## 5.4 Limitations and Future Directions 

Our findings are subject to the usual limitations of the adopted experimental method and sampling approach: We leveraged a field-recruited convenience sample of naïve participants, engaged in short, novel exposures in controlled (i.e., unmessy, unsituated) en-counters with a non-customized companion AI. These limitations are trade-offs for the designed ability to reach actual humans (given recent, problematic bot participation in online studies), to under-stand first encounters as the focal object of study, and to make rea-soned inferences about the impact of mind-related primes through experimental control. We additionally noted the small sample size (mitigated by attending to effect sizes rather than significance level alone), which may not generalize to broader or different popula-tions. Future research should attend to these limitations by examining varied populations alongside the role of AIC persona customization and longitudinal progressions of the MP-MC link. Further, our interpretations of data generated multiple hypotheses for future testing—around whether mind denial suppresses companionship potential more than mind affirmation constructs it and whether mind-perception cues fail to benefit with the greatest need for social support—that are ripe for empirical testing. 

## 6 Conclusion 

This investigation considered whether perceiving there is “someone home” for AI corresponds with seeing a specific AI as companion-able, and whether one’s wellbeing may play a role in those constru-als. We found priming humans with a mind-attributive description of an AI companion can somewhat alleviate baseline skepticism about its companionability. We also find data to suggest there could be two mind-perception pathways to perceived AI companionabil-ity—one in which the AI is an agent that affords self-elevation and one in which it is an experiencing entity on can attune with—but those paths may only function for those with strong senses of ex-istential purpose. Returning to the question of whether there is “someone home” to be companionable, the answer seems to be less binary than we implied. Participants seemed to be anchoring not to that heuristic binary but instead, perhaps, to the kind of some-one that might be home. That qualitative construal tells us that mind perception is not a switch that (when flipped) opens paths to companionship. Instead, it is a set of appraisals engaged selectively, each with different relational possibilities—perhaps appraised most readily by those who have less need for those relations.
