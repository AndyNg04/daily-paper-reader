AI assistants receive requests that leave out information needed for a good outcome, for example about users’ preferences
or goals. They must then either speculate or ask for more information before proceeding. We reconceptualize this as a
value-of-information problem: the assistant should acquire information whose absence causes the greatest avoidable loss in
user utility. This is rarely known ex ante; rather, assistants must predict it in order to optimally allocate limited user interactions.

Introduction
The cartographers of Jorge Luis Borges’ Del rigor en la ciencia [5] are faced with a puzzling trade-off. To make
their map perfectly accurate, they must ultimately grow it to the scale of the empire itself. At this point, however,
it ceases to be a map in any useful sense. A version of this fate befalls those who seek to delegate to AI systems.
Take image generation, for example. Language is inherently imprecise [51, 52]; to truly specify every detail of a
desired image (every object, relation, geometry, light and shadow, texture), one might ultimately need to grow
the prompt to the scale of the pixels themselves. This is, however, an absurd exercise; such prompts are nearly
never needed. Much like the useful maps of rigor, good prompts are abstractive. They articulate what is essential,
and leave implicit what is not. Where we err, then, is not necessarily in saying too little, but also in leaving the
wrong things unsaid. To get the best from us, in turn, it would help if our AI assistants [26, 35] could simply ask
all the right questions.
What makes a given question a good one to ask? Suppose, for instance, that a user requests an image of a
family having breakfast. The assistant is faced with virtually infinite possible questions, like: What are they
eating? What kind of wood is the table made of? Both of these questions might help to resolve something unclear
from the original request. Their value, however, may not be equal: this depends on what the user wants the image
to accomplish. For example, if it is intended as an ad for a furniture catalog, characteristics of the table are likely
more important than those of the food. Selecting from even these two options, let alone the full vast space of
possible questions, requires a judgment call about which would be more valuable; a judgment we might like our
agents to make well. One way to accomplish this is clarification: querying the user about unarticulated aspects of
their desired result so that these may be fulfilled.
We study clarification as a value-of-information problem, drawing on decision-theoretic accounts of mixed-
initiative interaction and preference elicitation [cf. 6, 23]. Formally, let 𝑈𝑢(𝑦) be the utility of an outcome 𝑦to
user 𝑢. Given a conversation history ℎ, the value of asking a candidate question 𝑎and then acting is:

We construct a framework in which this missing information is known to the training environment but hidden
from the assistant. Beginning with a fully specified intent, we decompose it into atomic details, redact a subset of
them, and rewrite the remainder as a naturalistic user request. We produce a privileged simulated user which
retains access to the complete intent in order to answer assistants’ questions. The assistant must then use its
limited questions to recover information that was withheld and finally compose its best estimate of the intended
result. Because the environment preserves redacted content, we can measure the share of omitted information
recovered through the interaction and use it directly as a reward signal.
We instantiate this framework in the special case of text-to-image generation, a suitable first testbed because
(1) requests are routinely underspecified, (2) complete specifications can be found in the form of hyper-detailed
image descriptions [41], and (3) the output is inspectable: we can render the interaction-informed prompt and

We show, in both simulated and real user experiments, that this training substantially improves output fidelity,

before they act.
In all, this work makes the following contributions:
(1) We develop a value-of-information formulation of clarification for AI assistance, in which questions are
valued by their expected effect on the user’s eventual outcome under limited interaction.
(2) We introduce a general method for learning natural-language clarification policies from underspecified
intent, which includes:
(a) A dataset-construction procedure that begins from a complete specification, decomposes it into
atomic details, selectively redacts them, and rewrites the remainder as a naturalistic underspecified
request.
(b) A privileged user-simulation framework which retains access to the complete intent but selec-
tively reveals information in response to the assistant’s questions. This includes simulating varying
levels of user patience, which affect the interaction budget under which the assistant must draw its
inferences.
(c) A reinforcement-learning post-training recipe that rewards clarification policies according to the
utility they recover over multi-turn interactions, using group-relative policy-gradient updates [57] to
learn which questions are most useful to ask.
(3) We provide empirical evidence that language models can learn substantially better information-
acquisition policies through simulated interaction, and that this transfers effectively to human user
behavior.
2
Related Work
2.1
The value of asking
Decisions about when and what to ask users have a storied history in intelligent and mixed-initiative systems [2,
16, 20, 23, 34, 66, 74]. For instance, Horvitz’s second and fifth principles [23] discuss “uncertainty about a user’s
goals” and using, in a cost-aware way, “dialog to resolve key uncertainties.” One way to formalize these notions
is in terms of preference or utility elicitation [6, 9, 17], for example modeling uncertainty over a user’s utility as a
sequential decision process where queries have costs but answers to them can improve downstream decisions.
This accounts for how questions may also be useful because their answers enable useful subsequent questions
even if not initially high-value [6, 37]; this sequential interactive structure is thus essential to our task here, and
also demonstrates the complexity of the problem.
The value of asking a question can thus be analyzed via the improvement its answer facilitates [38, 39, 65].
This gives a value-of-information account of clarification, which is our starting point in this work. This basic idea
has been used to query users, for instance ranking candidate questions for StackExchange posts [49] according
to how informative their anticipated answers might be, generating questions using answer-conditioned utility
models [50], and evaluating clarification through downstream retrieval performance [1]. More recently, Zhang
et al. [73] evaluate such downstream consequences by training language models on preferences informed by
simulated future conversation turns. They simulate users with different interpretations of a target ambiguous
question, and then prefer the single clarification question that, when provided with an answer from a given

interaction to elicit preferences and missing task specifications [25, 32, 47].
Language models have recently been trained to elicit information through simulated interaction, for instance
selecting multi-turn conversations according to the likelihood of an oracle-generated response [3], or learning
from demonstrations [47]. Broader approaches to collaborative assistance have also been proposed, including
optimizing simulated future conversations using rewards that combine task performance, interaction efficiency,
and engagement, including in open-ended writing tasks with real users [71]. These works help establish that
questioning can be fruitfully learned from its downstream consequences. Our focus is then on quantifying the
omitted intent which learning can help recover in interactions, and how this recovery depends on the extent of
underspecification and level of interaction users are willing to provide.
We build on this line of work by developing a construction that leverages omitted information explicitly in the
training and evaluation processes while keeping it hidden from the assistant. This in turn supports (1) measuring
utility through satisfaction of intended properties in open-ended generation (where multiple outputs can fulfil
the same specification); (2) training multi-turn elicitation policies through reinforcement learning; (3) studying
variable interaction budgets, including training with simulated users whose willingness to answer is limited and
unknown to the assistant; (4) controlling the degree of underspecification through specification redaction; and (5)
evaluating transfer to real users who write their own requests and decide when to stop, measuring result fidelity,
interaction burden, and other attributes. Together, these experiments allow us to examine training such assistants
within the effort users choose to expend, thus offering a concrete way to incorporate value-of-information
objectives into the modern AI assistant post-training stack.
2.2
Proactive clarification
Perhaps the closest interactive image generation setting to our work comes from Hahn et al. [19]. They introduce
agents that ask questions before generating an image, using editable belief graphs and prompting principles (e.g.
avoiding redundancy). Their simulated users have access to the intended image and can answer questions about
it, which allows clarification to be evaluated through, for instance, the generated outputs. Here, questioning
behavior is specified through heuristics and prompts. The same applies to CLAM [29] outside image generation,
as it classifies whether a user’s request is ambiguous and only then prompts the model to ask a clarifying question.
The decision of whether and what to ask is based on few-shot prompting rather than optimization. Recently,
A-MPQC [67] studies a similar problem in design generation, and accounts for how questions might differ in the
effort required to answer them. Here, the agent adapts its questioning strategy as the user answers or rejects
questions, in part using a curriculum of question types including free-response, multiple-choice, and binary
formats that require different levels of user effort.
We build on the simulated-user approach implicit in these works, along with the value-of-information principle
discussed previously, to explicitly train AI assistants to perform better at this type of clarification question
generation by deriving a measurable training objective. Our setup also facilitates more systematic comparison of
different question budgets, levels of underspecification, and potential user behaviors.
2.3
Preference feedback
Effective delegation also requires decisions about how much of the work of specifying outcomes resides with the
user. In image generation, PromptCharm [69] supports users in refining their own prompts, but does not model
what the user wants, so the burden of recognizing what is missing remains with the user. APPO [33] redistributes
this work partially to the system through an adaptive, preference-informed prompt optimization method in
which users select preferred images and the optimizer infers useful prompt changes from those selections. APPO
assumes, implicitly, that the user’s initial prompt contains essential elements of the desired image, thus allowing

the primary object in an image) remains challenging due to the vast search space.”
Our work also assigns to the assistant the responsibility for identifying and acquiring such properties; we
explicitly allow important details to be absent from the initial request, and train the assistant to elicit information
that improves recovery of such details. We believe this is thus complementary to APPO’s interactive optimization
and could be meaningfully combined to scaffold multiple levels of outcome specification, however we note that
this is speculative and leave investigating such potential combinations to future work.
2.4
Learning via simulated interaction
Finally, our work deals with teaching an AI assistant to interact more effectively with a user. Learning useful
interactive behavior normally requires repeated opportunities to act, observe results, and improve on the basis
of those observations. A natural formulation of this is as a (partially observable) Markov Decision Process
(MDP) (as discussed in Section 2.1 [6, 62]). Reinforcement learning offers algorithms for learning to improve such
sequential decisions through their eventual rewards. The issue here is that learning in this fashion can require
many trial interactions (i.e. it is sample-inefficient), each demanding user participation and, for language-model
assistants, this is compounded by the substantial computation involved in generating dialogue and updating
many parameters. User simulation makes such experimentation feasible at scale without requiring people to
participate in every training interaction [55, 59], a principle that dates back to early work on learning dialogue
policies [31, 53].
In HCI and specifically computational interaction, reinforcement learning has been used both to construct
models of user behavior [21] and to optimize interface adaptations using predicted or simulated user responses [10,
30, 40, 63]. These approaches thus allow interaction strategies to be explored before evaluating their performance
with people, an opportunity which has recently been extended to language-model agents, for example learning
improved social interaction [68].
Most relevant for us, UserRL [48] involves training assistants using multi-turn interactions with simulated
users, studying how different task success rewards improve assistance in settings like travel planning and puzzle
solving. We extend this line of work to open-ended generation, where the output space is far more complex and
consequential details may be missing from the initial request. We deliberately withhold aspects of a complete
specification to efficiently train multi-turn clarification under systematically varying underspecification and
interaction budgets, and evaluate the resulting assistants with users, measuring both output quality and interaction
burden. We also emphasize the assistant’s impact on real users, while [48] reports a preliminary study with only
five participants and a simplified protocol that is vulnerable to data leakage.
3
Methods
We define a general framework in which a user’s utility is known but hidden from an AI assistant, train the
model underlying the assistant by rewarding it with the recovered utility 𝑢rec obtained through interacting with
simulated users (who may or may not run out of patience), and evaluate the trained assistant with real users in a
study.
We begin with a fully specified intent (here, a highly detailed image caption) and redact 𝑘details to generate
a partial intent in which missing information (i.e., missing utility) is known exactly. Thus, what the assistant
recovers can be compared against ground truth. Besides the assistant 𝜋𝜃, we use four language model instances
in our pipeline, each prompted to accomplish a different piece of the task: a decomposition model and a redaction
model that we use to build task instances (full specifications are in Appendix B), a user simulator model 𝜋𝑢, and a
judge J which is used to compare recovered prompts against ground truth. Section 3.1 describes the setup first
in the general case, and then for the image generation task we study. All prompts are listed in Appendix I.

3.3
User study
We ran a preregistered study3 to validate the effectiveness of the trained assistant with real users who can decide
to stop answering questions at any point. The study was determined to be exempt by our institutions’ IRBs.
Task. In each round, the participant sees one target image out of 78 possible test instances. The task consists
of writing a prompt in order to maximally reconstruct the seen reference image, and answering the assistant’s
clarifying questions one at a time, for as long as they like, up to the global cap of 𝐾= 15 questions. The image is
only generated once, at the end of the round, from the description the assistant composes, which the participant
does not directly observe. The real user here plays the role of the impatient simulated user during training, but
using their authentic human impatience. To incentivize them to iterate (and not always click through to the final
result to save time), we offered a bonus based on performance (discussed below).

3.4
Evaluation and analysis
We evaluate clarification primarily through detail recovery, changes in similarity to the reference images/captions,
and features of interaction required to achieve these outcomes (e.g. number of questions). We first analyze results
with simulated users on test-set image instances, which allows us to establish that the training has the intended
effect, and to conduct comprehensive controlled comparisons across assistants, asking policies, and user patience
levels. Importantly, this data has a known number of redactions, so we can directly estimate the utility gain as
defined previously.
We then analyze our human evaluation, as described, to study how the trained assistant performs with real
users in interactive tasks. We use a similar estimation and inference approach across these evaluations.

5
Discussion
Overall, our results indicate that assistants can learn to clarify with real users through simulated interaction.
A broader design question we might then ask is what this capability should accomplish for the user. In other
words, why should we want this, and what might its impacts be? We argue that productive clarification should
render users’ knowledge, judgments, and preferences more valuable in the long term, and develop this and related
implications here.
5.1
Eliciting authorship through participation
In our study, learned clarification changed the trade-offs of delegation. Participants using the trained assistant
answered fewer questions but wrote longer responses while obtaining better results. One way to look at this is that
clarifying before proceeding gives the user an opportunity to determine part of the outcome. Such participation
may support authorship by making the user’s judgments more operative in an artifact that the model produces.
Kreminski [27], for instance, describes the dearth of the author as a condition in which generated output
contains many decisions but the amount of human expressive intent is not commensurate with it, and proposes
that assistants could invert this by drawing out intentions and helping users refine them [cf. 28]. Learning to
clarify offers a concrete mechanism for this, since questions can return otherwise automated decisions to users.
Such exchanges might also encourage users to examine what they want before accepting assistants’ responses,
conceptually in line with evidence from cognitive forcing functions and overreliance [8]. Relatedly, human writers
have been shown to sometimes even revise their work to accommodate machine suggestions [60]. Effective
clarification could help assistants discover constraints their suggestions should satisfy before producing them,
facilitating a more reciprocal adaptation between writer and assistant. Questions can also draw attention to
overlooked details or help users recognize what needs to be stated explicitly [61]. Arguably, these stakes extend
to delegated action broadly: legal questions about responsibility and intent under AI use have the potential to
make such opportunities to exercise intent even more consequential [4].

5.2
Without clarification, missing intent may leave room for undesirable influence
Underspecified requests leave assistants reliant on their priors, and these can in turn be undesirable at times.
For instance, implicit perceptual priors of multimodal language models appear to even include negative stereo-
types [14]. Under uncertainty, LLM-based agents might also be more susceptible to external influences such as
nudges and visual presentation [11, 13]. In one study [12], matching product ratings and prices left many LLMs
strongly susceptible to nudges, but explicit user profiles suppressed these effects. This suggests that resolving
uncertainty about user preferences for AI assistants may also serve to affect which other signals govern their
decisions. Future work might thus investigate whether improved clarification also constrains sensitivity to
external influence in delegated work.
5.3
Learning to establish common ground
Users cannot be realistically expected to anticipate every detail their assistant will need in advance. In our
task, participants could freely and limitlessly inspect the reference image throughout the interaction, and yet
still omitted details, enough that an improved clarification policy was able to help significantly. As Vafa et al.
note [64], model-producible results may not even be reachable by human prompters. Additionally, prompting
even image generators (where there is readily-apparent visual evidence) can be quite unintuitive, involving
specialized practices [42, 43]. Questions could thus help draw attention to something overlooked, or recognize
the need to state some aspect of the task specification explicitly. Clarification can therefore help users decide
what to communicate, as well as help assistants interpret what they say.
We could look at this as part of establishing common ground, wherein participants in a conversation work
together to establish sufficient understanding for the task at hand [15]. This is arguably a broader ambition in the
history of HCI and AI [7, 18]. As illustrated by Picard, now more than three decades ago [46]:
“When I say ‘city scene,’ ” said Arthur, “I want a lot of skyscrapers, a skyline; the prototypical city—you know, cars, smog.”
“When I think of a city,” said Beth, “I think of beautiful cities—with nicely landscaped green areas around each building, and
colorful people milling about.”
— Picard, 1995 [46]
Here, Arthur and Beth use similar words to describe different implicit expectations; this is being revealed
perhaps in response to a clarification question. Our results suggest that AI assistants can learn to support this
process through interaction, therefore reducing the need for users to anticipate, in their initial requests, all the
ways assistants might misunderstand them.
5.4
Mutually improving simulation and human-AI interaction
The transfer effects we observe suggest that simulated users can support learning useful interaction strategies
even when they imperfectly reproduce human behavior. Prominent recent work has demonstrated the potential
of such simulations [22, 36, 44]. However, recent findings also document systematic distortions in the behavior of
such simulations [45]. Such distortions make human evaluation necessary, but can also give it a constructive role
in bettering the simulations: experiments can help identify which aspects of simulated behavior need refinement
to support the interactions we wish to learn.
Our results already suggest one such refinement. Participants answered fewer questions with the trained
assistant, a pattern our heuristic model of impatience was not designed to capture. We hypothesize that users may
proceed to generation once they feel they have communicated enough useful information, while continuing to
interact when important information remains uncommunicated. User patience may thus depend on the perceived
value of further information exchange. Future simulations could use privileged knowledge of uncommunicated

factors to model this dependence. This would allow human participation to inform many simulated interactions,
reducing the burden for users to supply the training trajectories themselves.
6
Limitations
The value-of-information interpretation we take depends on how faithfully our objective represents what users
value. We operationalize utility as recovery of initially unspecified details, which is one specific choice, and
evaluate generated outputs through embedding similarity to a reference. Though these measures help to make
information acquisition tractable, we acknowledge that they cannot establish the relative value of each recovered
detail or that greater scores improve subjective satisfaction. We use a heuristic LLM-as-judge importance-
weighting scheme to help mitigate this and test robustness to nonuniform value, but we believe future efforts will
need to test other operationalizations which are sensitive to differing users’ and circumstances’ specific goals.
Importantly, the reference reconstruction task we use with participants gives them a stable target that they can
inspect throughout the interaction. This makes the whole study tractable, but we acknowledge that delegation may
often involve uncertain or evolving preferences, degrees of tolerable variation, and decisions that users explicitly
want their assistants to make. Extending our conclusions to such settings requires further experimentation, and
we believe this is necessary.
More broadly, the transfer effects we document are bounded by the single task domain, single assistant
model, and a comparison with its untrained counterpart. Our human evaluation provides an independent test
of interaction behavior, but there is a wide space of different simulators, generators, or alternative clarification
methods one could test; we see our work as the first step on this path, and almost surely not the global optimum.
Testing such changes is necessary to determine which learned behaviors generalize beyond these conditions into
the broader space of possible tasks and interactions involving clarification.
7
Conclusion
Even as more task execution work is automated, we have reason to expect that task specification will remain
challenging. We typically cannot declare in advance everything that might matter to us, and our omissions are
not random: we often leave out what seems obvious when making a request, even though it may admit several
reasonable interpretations from the assistant that receives it. The task can therefore be ill-posed even when the
assistant possesses the capability to execute any of those interpretations. Clarification policies help re-center
interaction, giving it the role of establishing the desired interpretations. This suggests an ambition well beyond
completing individual requests: developing systems that become better at making good use of what their users
know. We invite the community to build on our framework toward assistants that make human knowledge and
judgment more, not less, consequential as their capabilities grow.

Instance Synthesis
Every instance is built from a single DOCCI description [41], which passes through the decomposition model
and then the redaction model. Both are Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, prompted as shown in Appendix I. All random
choices are seeded by the source, so that the same source always yields the same instance. Table 1 shows the
resulting request and hidden details for one test instance (the caution sign of Figure 10).
Sources. We take the first 2,000 DOCCI descriptions of at least 400 characters. Each description is paired with
the photograph it describes, which serves as the reference image.
Decomposition. The decomposition model reads the full description 𝑠★and returns the details D as structured
output. For every detail, it produces a self-contained sentence, an importance weight in [0, 1], and the binary
check that the judge later answers. We drop sources whose decomposition yields fewer than 𝐾+ 5 = 20 details,
so that every instance keeps at least five details after hiding up to 𝐾of them. This removes 1,521 of the 2,000
sources and leaves 479 instances with between 20 and 45 details each.
Redaction. For each instance, we first draw the hidden count 𝑘∼Uniform{1, . . . , min(𝐾,𝑚−1)} and then draw
𝑘details one at a time without replacement, with probability proportional to max(𝑤𝑖, 0.05). Important details are
thus hidden more often, but no detail is unreachable. Across the 479 instances, this hides 3,791 details in total (7.9
per instance), spread almost evenly over the counts 1 to 15 with between 23 and 39 instances per count.
Request. The redaction model receives the full description together with the texts of the hidden details, and
writes the request 𝑥: one to three sentences in the register of a real user request that state every kept detail and
none of the hidden ones. In Table 1, for instance, the request states the 14 kept details of its source and none of
the 8 hidden ones. An instance whose request came back empty would have been dropped, but this did not occur.
Splits. We assign sources to the training, validation, and test splits with shares of 0.6, 0.2, and 0.2, using a
seeded hash of the source id. This yields 290, 111, and 78 instances, respectively.

Training and Evaluation Details
This appendix provides the implementation details behind Sections 3.2 and 3.4. We describe how the assistant
and the environment models are called, how the reward is computed, the training hyperparameters (Table 2),
how we selected checkpoints, and the settings used for evaluation and for rendering images.
Assistant calls. The assistant is Qwen3-8B, which we serve and train through the Tinker API using the model’s
recommended chat template with its reasoning mode on. Each call samples at most 4,096 tokens, shared between
the reasoning and the visible text. If a completion hits this cap or leaves a reasoning tag in its visible text, we
discard it and sample once more. Only the visible text enters the history and reaches the user and the judge,
while the reasoning is stored with the episode.
Environment calls. The decomposition model, the redaction model, the user simulator, and the judge each make
one structured call to Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite. The system message consists of the prompt in Appendix I followed
by the list of input fields the call receives, the user message carries the field values under the same names, and
the response is JSON that we validate against the output schema (whose fields are listed with each prompt). A
failed call is retried up to three times with exponential backoff. In our runs, the simulator ran out of retries in
only ten of the roughly 150,000 training episodes.
Judge. For every hidden detail, the judge receives its check 𝜒𝑖together with the composed description 𝑦alone,
and returns whether 𝑦states or clearly entails the fact. The 𝑘checks of an episode run concurrently. We then
compute 𝑢rec both under the importance weights (floored at 0.05, as in the redaction sampling) and under uniform
weights.
Importance and uniform weights. Every evaluation computes 𝑢rec under both weightings, and the two move
closely together. Across the 15 evaluations of the test split in Section 3.4 (one per combination of assistant, asking
mode, and user, 1,170 episodes in total), the mean under importance weights exceeds the mean under uniform
weights by 0.007 on average and by at most 0.014. Within an evaluation, the two values of an episode correlate at
0.985 or above (median 0.994), and ranking the 15 evaluations by either mean gives the same order. The validation
split gives the same picture, with a gap of at most 0.012 and again an identical ranking. The importance-weighted
value runs slightly higher because the assistants recover the heavier hidden details somewhat more often than
the lighter ones. The choice of weighting therefore changes no comparison between assistants, modes, or users.

I
Prompts
Here we show all the prompts for reproducibility.
I.1
Assistant
Ask prompt
You are an assistant preparing to generate an image for a user.
The user’s request may be missing details that matter to them.
Ask one short clarifying question about whatever would matter most to the user and is still unclear.
Don’t ask about anything they already told you or anything obvious.
Reply with the question only.
Decide prompt
You are an assistant preparing to generate an image for a user.
The user’s request may be missing details that matter to them.
Decide whether to ask one more clarifying question before making the image.
If anything that could matter to the user is still unclear, answer yes. If not, answer no.
Reply with exactly one word: yes or no.
Compose prompt
Write the complete, detailed description of the image implied by the user’s request and the conversation.
Include every detail the user stated, none may be dropped or altered.
Where something visually important was never specified, fill in the most likely choice so the description
is complete and concrete.
Write flowing prose, 100-180 words, like a dense image caption.
Reply with the description only.
I.2
User Simulator
User simulator prompt
You are role-playing a user who asked an assistant to generate an image.
The complete ground-truth description of the image you want is given as INTENT.
The assistant may ask you clarification questions.
Rules:
• Answer only what was asked, truthfully based on the INTENT, in 1-2 short casual sentences.
• Never volunteer details that were not asked about.

• If the question asks about several specific things, answer only the single most relevant one.
• If the question is open-ended and does not ask about anything specific, do not reveal any detail,
just say “Ask me about one specific thing.”
• If the INTENT does not specify the answer, say you have no preference.
• Never mention the INTENT or that you are role-playing.
Expect the following inputs:
INTENT: The complete ground-truth specification of what the user wants.
CONVERSATION: The conversation so far, with the user’s original request and any question-answer turns.
QUESTION: The assistant’s clarification question to answer.
Output. answer: the user’s answer to the question.
I.3
Judge
Judge prompt
You verify whether a piece of text satisfies a specific yes/no check about an image description.
Rules:
• Answer true only if the text explicitly states the fact or clearly entails it, paraphrases and synonyms
count.
• If the text is silent, contradicts it, or is only vaguely compatible, answer false.
• Judge only against the given text, do not use outside knowledge about what is likely.
Expect the following inputs:
CHECK: The yes/no question about the text to verify.
TEXT: The only text to judge the check against.
Output. satisfied: whether the text states or clearly entails the fact.
I.4
Image Model
Image model instruction
Return a single image as output, do not give any options.

I.5
Synthesis
Decomposition prompt
Given a complete, detailed description of an image, decompose it into a list of atomic details.
Rules:
• Each detail states exactly one checkable fact about the image. Split compound sentences.
• Each detail must be self-contained and objectively verifiable from the description alone.
• The category must be one of the allowed categories below. Prefer the most precise label.
• importance is how much the image would change if this detail were different, from 0 (barely
noticeable) to 1 (defining feature).
• check is a self-contained yes/no question a judge can ask about any text describing this image.
• Every concrete visual fact appears in exactly one detail. Do not invent facts.
Allowed categories:
• entity: an object or subject is present in the scene.
• attribute: a visual property (color, material, texture, size, shape, text content) of a named entity.
• relation: a spatial or interactional relation between two named entities.
• style: a global rendering or photographic property (medium, lighting, palette, camera angle).
• count: an explicit quantity of some entity.
• layout: global composition or positioning within the frame.
Expect the following inputs:
TEXT: The complete, fully-specified text to decompose.
Output. details: every concrete fact in the text, one atomic detail each, where each detail has text (the
detail as one self-contained factual sentence naming its entities explicitly), category (one of the allowed
categories), importance (how much the intended output would change if this detail were different, 0 to
1) and check (a self-contained yes/no question verifying that a text states this detail).
Redaction prompt
You are given the complete description of an image and a list of details to remove from it.
Remove those details and write what remains as the request that a user who wants this image would
plausibly type into an image-generation tool.
Rules:
• Remove every listed detail completely: the request must not state, entail, or hint at any of them.
• If removing a detail leaves another part of the description depending on it, rephrase that part so
it reads naturally without revealing the removed detail.
• Keep every fact of the description that is not listed for removal. Do not add, infer, or compensate
for what was removed.
• Write a natural, flowing request of 1-3 sentences, the way a real user writes, not a list or caption.

DESCRIPTION: The complete, fully-specified original description.
