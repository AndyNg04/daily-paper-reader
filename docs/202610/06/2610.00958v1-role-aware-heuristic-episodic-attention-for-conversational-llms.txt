Wanyang Hong Zhaoning Zhang * Yi Chen Libo Zhang Baihui Liu Linbo Qiao Zhiliang Tian Dongsheng Li

Abstract 

Large language models often lose track of per-sistent instructions and relevant information as multi-turn conversations grow. We study this cumulative contextual decay through three related failure modes: attention pollution, di-lution, and drift. We propose REA 1 (Role-aware Heuristic Episodic Attention), a context-management framework that assigns different persistence and representation policies to in-structions and episodic interactions. Instruc-tional Memory retains identified global con-straints in a dedicated prefix. Episodic Memory preserves user inputs and compresses model replies, while heuristic retrieval selects raw text, compressed representations, or omission for each historical turn. On Long-MT-Bench+, REA improves the judge score from 6.32 to 7.36 on a 10-point scale, a 16.5% relative gain over the Vanilla baseline, and reduces average latency by 2.91 ×. Additional evaluations show aggregate gains on three backbones spanning 1.7B–7B parameters and on Chinese and En-glish role-playing tasks. These results support role-aware context management as a practical approach to maintaining conversational conti-nuity and instruction adherence. 

1 Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have revolu-tionized natural language processing, exhibiting human-level or even superhuman abilities in single-turn tasks such as question answering, summa-rization, and code generation (Brown et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022; Achiam et al., 2023). How-ever, when the interaction shifts from isolated queries to multi-turn conversations, model perfor-mance often degrades substantially, even within the first few turns (Shuster et al., 2022; Kwan et al., 2024; Laban et al., 2025). Along-side position-dependent effects such as “lost in the middle” (Liu et al., 2023), multi-turn interaction introduces several related challenges: attention pol-lution , where earlier errors or distracting content interfere with subsequent responses; attention di-lution , where useful information is surrounded by growing amounts of less relevant history; and at-tention drift , where persistent constraints lose influ-arXiv:2610.00958v1 [cs.CL] 1 Oct 2026 ence as the local topic changes. Figure 1 illustrates the resulting disconnect: a model can answer the immediate question while losing an instruction es-tablished earlier. Maintaining a conversation there-fore requires both recovering relevant past content and preserving constraints that remain applicable across topic changes. Our working hypothesis is that context construc-tion contributes to these difficulties. A full-history prompt preserves the conversational sequence but does not explicitly assign different persistence poli-cies to global instructions and episodic content. The central distinction is between information that governs future responses and information that helps answer a particular query. For example, “start all responses with O” remains applicable when the user moves from a factual question to a writing task. Its relevance comes from its instructional role, even when its words have little semantic overlap with either query. A previous factual answer, in contrast, may be useful for a follow-up question but unnecessary after a topic change. Longer context windows (Beltagy et al., 2020; Zaheer et al., 2020; Press et al., 2021) increase available capacity, while compression and re-trieval (Jiang et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023; Lewis et al., 2020) reduce the history presented to the generator. These approaches motivate a comple-mentary question: which conversational content should persist independently of topical relevance, and which content should be recalled selectively? Increasing capacity leaves this selection problem open. A fixed recent window can remove an early constraint together with outdated exchanges, while query-based retrieval favors topical matches. Com-pressing history reduces its size, but still requires a decision about which instructions must remain explicit. These considerations motivate separate persistence and representation policies for different conversational roles. REA addresses this question through role-aware memory management . Instructional Memory (IM) retains identified global instructions in a ded-icated prefix, independent of episodic similarity scores. Episodic Memory (EM) stores user–model exchanges and compresses model replies into latent representations. Heuristic Context Retrieval (HCR) chooses a raw or compressed representation for rel-evant turns and omits other turns from the current input. The contribution lies in organizing these established memory, compression, and retrieval primitives around different functional roles. This division gives each component a specific responsi-bility: IM carries persistent constraints across topic changes, EM provides compact representations of past interactions, and HCR adapts the episodic con-text to the current request. The generator receives these complementary sources in a single hybrid input. Experiments show improved long-horizon qual-ity and instruction adherence, with a 2.91 × latency reduction on Long-MT-Bench+. A same-backbone comparison with MemoChat and evaluations across three backbones and two languages broaden the em-pirical support. Removing IM from the EM+HCR configuration reduces instruction adherence from 8.18 to 1.95, supporting the importance of retain-ing persistent constraints separately from topical retrieval. 

Contributions. Our contributions are threefold: • We introduce a role-aware context-management design that retains persistent functional instructions separately from episodic history. • We combine asymmetric reply compression with variable-resolution retrieval, preserving user text in selected turns while adapting the representation of model replies. • We demonstrate gains in long-horizon qual-ity, instruction adherence, and latency, and re-port additional evidence across 1.7B–7B back-bones and Chinese and English role-playing evaluations.

This complements instruction tuning by preserving the instructions that the model is expected to follow throughout the conversation. 

3 Methodology 

3.1 Architecture Overview 

Let Ht = {(u1, b 1), . . . , (ut, b t)} denote com-pleted exchanges. Figure 2 organizes REA into three stages. First, role-aware memory decou-pling separates recognized persistent instructions in MIM from episodic exchanges in MEM . Sec-ond, HCR compares the current query ut+1 with stored episodic representations and selects a reso-lution for each recalled exchange. Third, hybrid context reconstruction combines the instruction pre-fix, selected history, and current query for response generation. The two memories have distinct update sched-ules. Before answering, REA recognizes any global instruction in ut+1 and updates IM so that the instruction can affect the current response. Af-ter generating bt+1 , it compresses the reply and stores the completed exchange in EM for subse-quent queries. This separates persistent instruction access from the query-dependent use of conversa-tion history.

EM applies asymmetric compression: user inputs remain in their original textual form, while model replies have both raw and latent representations. User inputs specify the requests, corrections, and constraints that define an interaction; preserving their wording maintains these signals in recalled exchanges. 3.3 Instructional Memory and Recognizer 

IM stores constraints whose scope extends across subsequent responses. For example, “Keep all fu-ture answers under 200 words” specifies a persis-tent constraint, whereas “Summarize this article in 200 words” applies to the current task. This scope distinction determines whether an input belongs in the persistent instruction prefix or is accessed as part of episodic history. The Instruction Recognizer (IR) combines rule-based filtering with a lightweight Qwen3-0.6B clas-sifier to identify user inputs that specify persistent constraints. Each new query is scored against stored memory again, allowing an exchange omitted on one turn to be recalled on a later turn.

4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup Benchmarks and Metrics We evaluate conver-sational quality on MT-B ENCH (2 turns), MT-EVAL (5–12 turns), and L ONG -MT-B ENCH + (60+ turns). We report benchmark-prompted GPT-4 judge scores, denoted Acc on a 0–10 scale. Rule-based Instruction Adherence Rate (IAR) evalu-ates persistent constraints in the instruction task, and Joint Goal Accuracy (JGA) measures exact-match success in state tracking. Additional eval-uations use Chinese and English C HARACTER -BENCH (Zhou et al., 2025), with its five-point role-playing protocol. Appendix C.3 separates the evaluation procedures. The dialogue benchmarks assess response quality, the instruction task tests persistence of global constraints, and state track-ing tests the integration of information distributed across turns. 

Baselines and Implementation We compare Vanilla full history, Recent-k (k = 5 ), rolling sum-maries, BM25 ( k = 5 ), LLMLingua2, LongAl-paca (Chen et al., 2023), MemoChat (Lu et al., 2023), MemGAS (Xu et al., 2025), and Reply-Soft-Compress (reply compression without IM/HCR). The primary backbone is Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2; Table 1 identifies the different-backbone baselines. Generation uses greedy decoding. REA uses Qwen3-0.6B for IR and the memory configuration in Section 3.4. Training and evaluation use a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. Appendix C collects the available configurations.

4.5 Ablation and Component Analysis 

We examine instruction retention, recognition er-rors, and episodic representation policies through the following ablations and component analyses. 

Role-Aware Memory Table 4 compares the eval-uated memory configurations. EM alone improves IAR from 5.76 to 7.37. Adding HCR without IM yields 1.95, whereas the full EM+HCR+IM system reaches 8.18. Removing IM from the retrieval-based configuration therefore sharply reduces ad-herence in this setting. A persistent instruction can be semantically distant from a later content query; retaining it outside topical retrieval addresses this mismatch. The ablation supports separate persis-tence policies for instructions and episodic content. In particular, selection that is useful for topical con-tent must be paired with a separate path for global constraints. The full system retains this path while still applying HCR to episodic exchanges, yielding the strongest adherence among the tested configu-rations.

5 Conclusion 

REA organizes conversational history according to functional role: recognized global instructions per-sist in IM, while episodic exchanges are recalled at variable resolution. On Long-MT-Bench+, it achieves a 16.5% relative judge-score gain and a 2.91 × latency reduction over Vanilla. Same-backbone, cross-backbone, and bilingual evalua-tions provide additional evidence within the tested settings. Together with the instruction ablation, these results support explicit persistence policies and selective episodic context as practical tools for multi-turn LLM interaction. 

Limitations 

Our evaluation covers 1.7B–7B backbones and Chi-nese and English conversations; broader model and language coverage remains future work. Turn-level similarity can miss cross-turn references, motivat-ing retrieval that explicitly models dependencies between exchanges. Response-quality evaluation relies on automated judges and would benefit from complementary human assessment.

A Architecture Details and Training Protocol 

This appendix provides a comprehensive technical specification of the REA framework, covering the dual-LoRA implementation, training data pipelines, and the configuration of the Instruction Recognizer. 

A.1 Dual-LoRA Implementation and Initialization 

The core of REA’s Episodic Memory (EM) is a dual-LoRA architecture integrated into the Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 backbone. • Module Configurations: We apply Low-Rank Adapters (Hu et al., 2021) to all linear projections, including query, key, value, and MLP layers. The rank is set to r = 16 with a scaling factor α = 32 .• Latent Embedding (mem) Tokens: We in-troduce n = 8 special soft compression to-kens per turn. To ensure stable gradient flow and rapid alignment during the initial training phase, these tokens are initialized using the mean embedding of the base model’s vocabu-lary. • Asymmetric Compression Logic: For EM storage, LoRA cmp encodes model replies bi

into latent vectors Vi, while user inputs remain in raw text. The same compressor separately encodes the current query for retrieval scoring. 

A.2 Training Protocol and Optimization 

REA is trained end-to-end to synchronize the com-pression and generation objectives. • Dataset Statistics: We curate a high-quality subset of 40,000 multi-turn sessions from Top-iOCQA, UltraChat and ShareGPT (Adlakha et al., 2022; Ding et al., 2023; ShareAI Lab, 2023). Each sample is structured as a triplet 

(H<t , U t, R t), where H<t is the history to be compressed, Ut is the current query, and Rt is the target response. • Optimization Hyperparameters: We use the AdamW optimizer with a shared learning rate of 1e − 5 for both LoRA modules. We use an effective batch size of 128. • Hardware and Epochs: Training was con-ducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU for 2 epochs. 

A.3 Training Data Curation 

The 40,000 sessions are selected from ShareGPT, UltraChat, and TopiOCQA through four stages. First, we select conversational data from these sources. Second, a lightweight classifier filters sam-ples containing large code blocks or non-natural-language special formats. Third, semantic-density filtering removes simple interactions shorter than a 10-token threshold, such as brief acknowledge-ments. Fourth, contextual-horizon filtering retains dialogues spanning 3–15 turns. Each training instance supplies conversation history, a current query, and its target reply; the response-generation loss trains the two adapters jointly.

C Experimental Setup Details 

This appendix provides detailed information on the benchmark datasets, baseline model implementa-tions, and evaluation protocols used in our experi-ments. 

C.1 Benchmark Dataset Details 

We evaluated REA on three multi-turn conversa-tional benchmarks of gradually increasing length and complexity to comprehensively test its ability to mitigate cumulative contextual decay in various scenarios. 

MT-Bench We employed MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023), a widely-used multi-turn evaluation benchmark. It comprises 80 high-quality, two-turn open-ended questions designed to assess a model’s conversational abilities. In our experiments, it represents the short-turn (2-turn) conversation sce-nario. 

MT-Eval To assess medium-length conversa-tions, we used MT-Eval (Kwan et al., 2024), a com-prehensive benchmark for evaluating the multi-turn capabilities of LLMs. It contains 168 samples with an average of 6.96 turns and classifies interactions into four types (Recollection, Expansion, Refine-ment, and Follow-up). The MT-Eval-recollection 

task used in Section 4.4 is a specific subset, serving as a key diagnostic tool for evaluating attention drift. 

Long-MT-Bench+. For long-horizon stress test-ing (60+ turns avg.), we utilized Long-MT-Bench+ (Pan et al., 2025a). It is designed to rigorously test a model’s robustness and consistency in extended conversational scenarios. 

State Tracking Task. As a diagnostic for atten-tion pollution (Section 4.4), we used the diagnostic task from Laban et al. (2025). This task employs a sharded simulation framework, distributing key instructions across multiple turns to evaluate the model’s state-tracking capabilities.
