## Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) often perform intermediate cogni-tive work while carrying out users’ requests, yet it remains unclear which parts users intended to delegate and how they wanted to remain involved. This matters because consequential choices may go unnoticed, limiting users’ ability to steer the process, while reviewing every step would make delegation burdensome. We ex-amined this with 24 LLM users across three knowledge-work tasks, collecting 992 retrospective annotations of reasoning steps. From this, we developed taxonomies of LLM cognitive work, delegation enactment, and desired delegation protocols at the reasoning-step level. Our analysis revealed that participants viewed about half of all steps (48.6%) as AI-initiated, meaning the AI took on work they had not requested. Desired involvement varied with cognitive work and delegation enactment, even when contributions matched par-ticipants’ intent. We propose design implications and sketches for supporting more deliberate cognitive delegation through flexible protocols and inspectable, revisable AI-initiated decisions.

## 1 Introduction 

People often delegate a task to an LLM through a prompt with-out specifying every piece of cognitive work required to carry it out [32, 47]. Reasoning-capable LLM systems can turn a task re-quest into a sequence of intermediate reasoning steps and tool actions, forming and revising an approach as they work toward an output [34, 53]. In doing so, they may resolve choices the user left unstated—what assumptions to make, which possibilities to pursue, and how much detail to develop—by inferring defaults for unspecified requirements [41, 52]. Such an AI initiative is part of what makes delegation useful. However, asking an LLM to com-plete a task does not necessarily specify how the user wants every intermediate decision to be handled. This raises the question of which decisions users want to inspect or revise, and when they want to be involved. Prior systems address this by giving users oversight of AI outputs and how they are produced, for example by exposing plans, rea-soning steps, or contribution provenance [12, 17, 34, 50]. Yet such oversight does not by itself indicate which parts of AI-initiated work require users’ attention. Inspecting or confirming every step would make delegation burdensome [7, 34]. Studies of delegation further show that desired involvement varies across tasks and con-texts, but have examined these preferences at the level of tasks, workflows, or interaction modes [14, 19, 45]. What remains unclear is a user-grounded understanding of how delegation unfolds across the intermediate work of producing a single response: what users intended the AI to take on and how they wanted to be involved. We studied this at the level of reasoning steps and tool actions in a study with 24 frequent LLM users. Each participant completed one of three predefined tasks, each representing a major category arXiv:2610.06328v1 [cs.HC] 5 Oct 2026 Conference acronym ’XX, June 03–05, 2018, Woodstock, NY Yoonsu Kim et al. 

of LLM-assisted knowledge work identified in prior work: Creation, Information, or Advice [6, 25]. Using a custom interface for task completion and retrospective review, we collected 992 step-level annotations in which participants judged, looking back at each step, whether they had asked for it or the AI had taken it on by itself, how critical it was, whether its contribution matched their intent, and how they would have wanted to be involved. From these data, we developed three taxonomies at the level of LLM reasoning steps, characterizing the cognitive work each step performed, how participants perceived that work to have been delegated, and how they wanted the delegation to proceed. Because all three apply to the same steps, we can examine how the kind of work relates to participants’ interpretation of its delegation and their preferred coordination. Our step-level analysis revealed patterns obscured at the task level. Participants judged 48.6% of reasoning steps as AI-initiated, meaning they saw the AI as taking on work they had not asked it to perform. This share rose from 31.1% in the first fifth of a turn to 61.4% in the final fifth. Yet AI initiative was not necessarily unwanted: 62.7% of AI-initiated steps contributed as participants wanted. Even so, unwanted and minor steps were more often AI-initiated than wanted and critical steps (70.0% vs. 41.1%; 75.8% vs. 44.4%). Participants did not want oversight of everything, preferring full delegation for 63.9% of all steps. However, they wanted con-sultation when the AI took on critical work they had not intended to delegate, and less AI involvement when it performed unneces-sary minor work. They also wanted involvement in 28% of critical AI-initiated steps that contributed as they wanted, including most requests for after-the-fact disclosure. Desired involvement also varied by cognitive work: consultation for Generating , calibration toward doing less for Repeating , and relatively little involvement for Understanding , Planning , and Recalling . Together, these findings suggest supporting selective involvement in individual decisions throughout a response, including when AI initiative yields contri-butions that participants value. Grounded in these findings, we propose two design implications for deliberate cognitive delegation and explore them through low-fidelity sketches. First, interfaces should support multiple delegation protocols within a response to accommodate different involvement preferences across cognitive work. Second, they should make conse-quential AI-initiated decisions inspectable and revisable, addressing the desire for involvement even when contributions are satisfactory. We make the following contributions: 

• Three complementary, user-grounded taxonomies that char-acterize the cognitive work performed in LLM reasoning, how its delegation is enacted, and how users want that dele-gation to proceed. 

• An empirical analysis of 992 annotated step-level instances from 24 participants across three knowledge-work tasks, revealing patterns in perceived AI initiative, alignment with user intentions, and desired involvement. 

• Two design implications, explored through low-fidelity sketches, for tailoring delegation protocols and inspecting and revising AI-initiated decisions. 

## 2 Related Work 

We position our work at the level of the intermediate cognitive work an LLM performs within a single response, extending prior research on AI delegation, reliance, and the division of work in human–AI collaboration. 

## 2.1 Delegation, Reliance, and User Oversight in Human–AI Collaboration 

AI systems can support a wide range of knowledge work, including information seeking, analysis, and artifact creation [6]. Delegat-ing this work can reduce users’ workload [33], but extensive or unreflective reliance may weaken their oversight of how a task is carried out. Prior work also finds that extensive AI assistance can reduce users’ persistence and cognitive effort and hinder skill for-mation [9, 25, 27, 43]. When users delegate tasks end-to-end, they may fail to notice when outcomes degrade as work unfolds [22]. At the extreme, this can become “cognitive surrender,” in which users relinquish responsibility for consequential cognitive work [42]. Research on AI delegation has examined how users decide whether and what to delegate to AI [46]. Related work on appropriate re-liance examines whether users appropriately accept or reject AI recommendations [37]. Prior systems have explored how interfaces can help users assess these recommendations and calibrate their re-liance [36]. More broadly, this literature distinguishes reliance from decision quality, where following or rejecting AI advice can each be appropriate or inappropriate depending on its correctness [40]. However, it remains unclear how delegation unfolds across the intermediate work an LLM performs while generating a single response to a user’s request. A task request does not show which work users intended the AI to take on or how they wanted to be involved. We address this gap by linking step-level cognitive work with users’ interpretations of its delegation and their preferred involvement. 

## 2.2 Division of Cognitive Work in Human–AI Collaboration 

Characterizing delegation within a single LLM response requires distinguishing what cognitive work is performed from how that work is divided between users and AI. Function-allocation research provides a basis for this distinction by separating the type of func-tion being automated, such as information acquisition, analysis, decision selection, or action, from its level of automation [35]. Re-search on adaptive autonomy further shows that no single, fixed level of AI control is optimal across situations [39]. Together, this work frames delegation as a coordination problem involving differ-ent functions and shifting degrees of initiative, rather than a binary choice between manual work and full automation. Research on generative AI has extended these ideas to knowledge-work domains. Studies of LLM-supported learning, writing, design ideation, and professional work show that users delegate differ-ent parts of a task and that control can shift between users and AI as work progresses [6, 29, 31, 44, 45, 49]. Broader taxonomies likewise describe human-AI interaction in terms of stages such as planning, facilitating, iterating, and testing [11], or dimensions such as agency, interaction, and adaptation [16]. Finer-grained studies examine how programmers respond to code suggestions or shift between acceleration and exploration, but still focus on work visible in the interaction between the user and the system [3, 30]. Contemporary reasoning models can carry out multiple reason-ing steps and tool actions in response to a single prompt, making consequential decisions without a corresponding user-visible ex-change. Existing task- and interaction-level accounts therefore offer limited insight into how cognitive work is divided within a single response. We address this gap by relating the cognitive work per-formed at each reasoning step to how users perceived its delegation. 

## 2.3 Supporting User Involvement of AI-Initiated Cognitive Work 

When an LLM can make consequential decisions within a task with-out a user-visible exchange, an important design question is how users can become aware of and retain control over this AI-initiated cognitive work. Prior research has proposed several approaches to help users evaluate, inspect, and intervene in AI assistance. One line of work supports more deliberate evaluation of AI recommendations through calibrated uncertainty [8], sources and inconsistencies in LLM explanations [20], or cognitive forcing func-tions [7]. These mechanisms help users decide whether to accept an output but offer limited visibility into the intermediate work that produced it. A second line of work gives users visibility into and influence over how AI carries out a task. Before execution, AI-generated plans allow users to review and revise the proposed course of ac-tion [12], while interactive controls surface underspecified choices in a request [54]. Guided copilots instead give users opportuni-ties to participate as the task progresses, rather than delegating it end-to-end [19]. Other systems support inspection during or af-ter execution. Editable workflows expose intermediate steps and results that users can modify [50], while hierarchical representa-tions help users navigate and revise model-produced reasoning [34]. Provenance views show how human and AI contributions combine to shape a final artifact [17]. These systems provide different points of intervention but offer limited guidance on how appropriate involvement depends on the cognitive work performed or how users perceive its delegation. We address this by relating users’ desired involvement to both dimen-sions at the reasoning-step level, informing design implications for deliberate cognitive delegation. 

## 3 Study Design 

We designed a user study to characterize how cognitive work is distributed between users and LLMs during knowledge-work tasks. In particular, we focused on the individual reasoning steps through which an LLM carries out a task that was delegated by the user, how delegation unfolds within those steps, and how users would prefer that delegation to proceed.

## 3.2 LLM Reasoning-Trace Inspection Interface (Figure 1) 

We developed a custom interface for two purposes: LLM-assisted task completion and retrospective inspection and annotation of the reasoning steps and tool actions underlying each response. The interface integrated a reasoning-capable LLM ( gemini-3.5-flash )with web search and a PowerPoint-generation tool implemented through the Model Context Protocol (MCP). For each turn, the sys-tem logged the user’s prompt, the model’s reasoning steps and tool actions, and its final output. After completing the task, participants revisited this interaction history through the same interface. They could inspect the reasoning steps and tool actions underlying each AI output, select specific spans, and annotate them using structured labels and free-form comments. To reduce the burden of entering detailed qualitative feedback, the interface also supported speech-to-text input. Participants could verbalize their explanations while annotating a reasoning step, and the system transcribed them into the free-text annotation field. Participants could review and edit each transcription before submitting it.

## 3.4 Procedure 

Each study session lasted approximately two hours and consisted of three stages: (1) a 10-minute introduction covering informed consent, task instructions, and the LLM interface; (2) a 20-minute 

task phase , in which participants completed their assigned task with the LLM; and (3) a 90-minute retrospective annotation and interview phase , in which participants reviewed the recorded trace of how the LLM carried out their prompts, including its rea-soning steps and tool-use actions, annotated selected parts of the trace, and participated in a semi-structured interview. The study was approved by our institution’s IRB, and all participants provided informed consent before participating in the study. 

3.4.1 Task Phase. After the introduction to their assigned task and the LLM interface, participants were instructed to complete the task using the study system as they normally would. We provided no guidance on how to formulate prompts, which parts of the task to ask the AI to handle, or how to structure their interaction with it. Participants were therefore free to decide what to ask the AI to do, what to retain for themselves, and how much autonomy to allow the AI during the task. The complete interaction history, including user prompts, AI reasoning traces, tool calls, and outputs, was recorded for the subsequent retrospective annotation phase. 

3.4.2 Retrospective Reasoning Annotation and Interview Phase. Af-ter completing the task, participants revisited their recorded in-teraction turn by turn. Before inspecting the AI’s reasoning and tool-use traces, they described how they understood their own and the AI’s roles in completing the task. For each turn, participants first recalled their intent and reviewed the corresponding AI out-put, marking notable good or problematic parts. This output-level review provided context for subsequently inspecting the associated AI reasoning and tool-use trace, but was not itself a primary unit of analysis. Participants then inspected each reasoning step and tool-use action, providing at least one annotation per step or ac-tion. They could add further annotations to notable spans and were encouraged to connect parts of the output to the work that may have produced them. For each annotation, participants made three structured judgments: initiative , criticality , and impact . We selected these dimensions to distinguish whether each step reflected work the user had intended to delegate or work the AI took on without being asked, how consequential that work was, and whether its contribution ultimately aligned with the user’s desired outcome. Treating these as separate judgments allowed us to distinguish how delegation was enacted from whether it was successful [15, 47]. 

• Initiative: who set this work in motion? Participants judged whether the work was something they had prompted the AI to do as (“I prompted it” – User-initiated ) or something the AI had done on its own (“AI did it” – AI-initiated ). 

• Criticality: how consequential was this work? Partici-pants judged whether the work was Critical or Minor to the overall task and its outcome. 

• Impact: did this work contribute to an outcome aligned with what users wanted? Participants judged whether its contribution was Wanted or Unwanted , relating the step or action to the output they had reviewed. For every annotation, participants explained each judgment in their own words. These free-form comments provided qualitative evi-dence for understanding why participants made each judgment. Participants typically verbalized their explanations while anno-tating; the speech-recognition system transcribed them into text, which participants could review and edit before submission. In a subsequent semi-structured interview, participants discussed how they perceived work the AI had taken on without being asked, how they wished to be involved in such work, and how they would prefer to recover when it produced unwanted outcomes.

## 4 Taxonomies of LLM Cognitive Work and Delegation 

Through iterative analysis of participants’ annotations and com-ments, we developed three taxonomies that describe each reasoning step or tool action along three dimensions: (1) what cognitive work the LLM performed, (2) how delegation unfolded—whether the user had asked for the work or the AI took it on, and whether the con-tribution was what the user wanted, and (3) how the user wanted the AI to proceed. Together, the taxonomies let us examine what work the AI performed within a response, whether users had asked for it, and when and how they wanted to be involved. 

## 4.1 Taxonomy Development 

We developed the taxonomies through an iterative qualitative anal-ysis of the annotated reasoning steps and participants’ accompany-ing free-form explanations. The structured annotations captured participants’ retrospective judgments of each step’s initiative, criti-cality, and impact, while their free-form comments clarified these judgments and how they thought the AI should have proceeded. Two authors first independently open-coded [13] 247 data points (24.9% of the full dataset) from six participants, balanced across the three tasks. For each instance, we developed initial codes around three questions: (1) what kind of cognitive work the AI was per-forming, (2) how that work came to be delegated to the AI and whether this aligned with the participant’s intent, and (3) how the participant wanted the delegation to proceed. We then brought the independently developed codes together through iterative affin-ity diagramming, grouping similar concepts, merging overlapping codes, and refining their definitions and boundaries through dis-cussion. Through this process, we developed an initial set of codes that captured distinct patterns observed across the data. We next applied these initial codes to an additional 320 data points from six other participants, again with two participants from each task. The two authors independently coded these data and refined the code set when new cases were not adequately captured. This iter-ative process continued until the codes were stabilized across the 567 development data points. To provide an independent review of the resulting structure, two additional authors joined the anal-ysis to review the resulting codes and conduct axial coding [13], organizing related codes into higher-level categories and clarifying their relationships. This process resulted in three taxonomies: (1) 

LLM’s Cognitive Work , capturing the types of cognitive work performed within LLM reasoning steps and tool-use actions; (2) 

Delegation Enactment , capturing how that work came to be del-egated to the AI and whether it aligned with the user’s intent; and (3) Desired Delegation Protocol , capturing how users wanted the AI to communicate and proceed when performing that work. ## 4.2 Taxonomy of Cognitive Work in LLM Reasoning Steps 

Our first taxonomy characterizes what kind of cognitive work the LLM performs within each reasoning step (Table 2). Like prior func-tional taxonomies of reasoning steps, but extending beyond their focus on benchmark problem solving [4, 5], ours captures user-facing knowledge work across Creation, Information, and Advice. We identified five higher-level categories: Framing , Sourcing , Devel-oping , Reviewing , and Reprocessing . The first four characterize the primary object and function of a reasoning step. Reprocessing , in contrast, captures cases in which the model revisits and further pro-cesses work from an earlier reasoning step. We set it apart because participants perceived these steps differently. When looking at what cognitive work a reasoning step performed, they interpreted a step that returns to earlier work in relation to that earlier step rather than on its own. We coded the five categories as mutually exclusive. For Reprocessing steps, we additionally recorded which earlier step they returned to and which category that step had received. 

Framing. It captures work that establishes how the task will be understood and approached based on the user’s prompt. It includes 

Understanding , in which the model interprets the user’s intent, task goal, and relevant context, and Planning , in which it formulates a strategy or sequence of actions for carrying out the task. 

Sourcing. It encompasses work that brings information needed for the task into the current reasoning process. Gathering obtains information from external sources, such as through web search or tool use, whereas Recalling retrieves relevant information from the preceding conversational context. 

Developing. It covers work that directly advances the substantive content of the task. It includes Brainstorming alternative possibil-ities, Analyzing information or decomposing it into meaningful elements, Narrowing among alternatives or emphasizing selected dimensions, and Generating new task artifacts or substantive con-tent. 

Reviewing. It captures meta-level work in which the model exam-ines its own preceding reasoning. Evaluating assesses prior reason-ing steps against relevant criteria, while Synthesizing integrates multiple prior reasoning steps into a consolidated understanding or conclusion. 

Reprocessing. It characterizes cases in which the model returns to content from a specific earlier reasoning step. Elaborating devel-ops that earlier work with additional detail or specificity, whereas 

Repeating restates or reproduces it without substantial new devel-opment. 

## 4.3 Taxonomy of Delegation Enactment in LLM Reasoning Steps 

Delegation begins with the user’s prompt: by asking the LLM to complete a task, the user delegates that task at a high level. However, the user’s prompt does not specify every piece of work required to carry it out [23], and the model can introduce additional opera-tions, judgments, or decisions as it reasons toward the requested outcome. We therefore introduce delegation enactment to describe how a high-level delegation is concretely carried out at the level of individual reasoning steps. We characterize delegation enactment along two user-annotated dimensions: initiative and impact . Initia-tive distinguishes work that the user explicitly prompted, which we refer to as user-initiated , from work that the AI independently took on while carrying out the user’s prompt, which we refer to as AI-initiated . Impact concerns what a step contributed to the resulting output: participants judged whether that contribution was wanted 

or unwanted . Crossing these dimensions yields four different forms of delegation enactment (Table 3). 

Intended Delegation. It refers to work that the user explicitly asked the AI to perform and that the AI handled in accordance with the user’s intent. It represents cases in which both the allocation of the work and its execution matched what the user intended to delegate. 

Regretted Delegation. It describes cases in which the work was explicitly requested by the user, but its interpretation or execution ultimately did not match what the user wanted. Participants often located the problem in their own prompting rather than in the AI, reflecting that they should not have delegated the work in that way. In retrospect, they recognized that although they had asked the AI to perform the work, doing so had not actually reflected how they wanted the task to be handled. 

Serendipitous Delegation. It encompasses work that the user had neither explicitly prompted nor necessarily anticipated, but that the AI autonomously took on in a way the user found aligned with their intent or unexpectedly valuable. These cases show that autonomous initiative reveals useful possibilities the user had not considered or relieves users of work they were comfortable leaving to the AI. 

Unintended Delegation. It captures work that the user had not explicitly prompted and that the AI autonomously took on in a way that was not what the user wanted. Here, the AI extended the high-level delegation into work that the user did not intend it to handle in that manner. 

Criticality of AI-initiated work. Criticality further differenti-ated the meaning of delegation primarily for AI-initiated work. When users had explicitly prompted the work, they almost al-ways regarded it as important, and the few instances labeled as minor did not form a substantively distinct pattern from critical user-prompted work. In contrast, for AI-initiated work, criticality marked a meaningful difference in how participants interpreted the AI’s autonomy. We therefore used criticality to further distinguish AI-initiated forms of delegation. Within Serendipitous Delegation ,we termed critical work Beneficial Autonomy , in which the AI took on work that shaped the outcome and contributed something the user valued despite not having asked for it, such as relating material the user had not thought to connect. Minor work was termed Routine Autonomy , capturing low-stakes initiative that users were comfortable allowing the AI to handle on its own, such as organizing an output into a table without being asked. ## 4.4 Taxonomy of Desired Delegation Protocols 

Delegation enactment describes what actually occurred, but partici-pants’ free-form comments additionally revealed how they believed the AI should have proceeded. Rather than simply indicating whether participants wanted more or less AI autonomy, these protocols capture where user involvement should occur relative to the AI’s action and how much of the work the AI should take on. Full Delegation. This protocol describes cases where users wanted the AI to perform the work autonomously without additional con-sultation or notification. The relevant reasoning step could remain effectively invisible to the user as long as the AI handled it appro-priately. 

Disclosed Delegation. This mode reflects a preference for the AI to act independently while keeping the user informed afterward about what it did or decided. The AI could retain execution authority, while the user retained visibility and an opportunity to review its actions. 

Calibrated Delegation. This protocol refers to cases where users were comfortable with the AI taking on the work, but wanted to adjust the extent of its involvement as the work progressed. Consultative Delegation. Consultation was desired when users wanted the AI to pause before proceeding with the work or com-mitting to a consequential decision, align with them on how to continue, and obtain their input or confirmation before acting. This need for consultation did not simply arise because the user’s prompt was ambiguous or required clarification. Rather, it reflected cases in which users wanted to retain authority over how the work should proceed, rather than have the AI proceed autonomously. The protocols are ordered by the relative autonomy afforded to the AI, from Full Delegation , where the AI can proceed inde-pendently, to Consultative Delegation , where user input is required before proceeding. They also differ in whether and when the user is brought into the delegation process: not at all ( Full Delegation ), after the AI acts ( Disclosed Delegation ), as the work unfolds ( Cali-brated Delegation ), or before the AI acts ( Consultative Delegation ). We collectively refer to the latter three as protocols that called for user involvement .

## 4.5 Taxonomy Application and Dataset Construction 

We applied the finalized taxonomies to the remaining 425 data points from the 12 participants not used during taxonomy devel-opment, with four participants from each task. To scale the coding while assessing whether the taxonomies could be applied consis-tently to unseen data, we used two LLMs from different providers, GPT-5.6 1 and Claude Opus 4.8 2, as annotators. Each model re-ceived the finalized taxonomy definitions and independently as-signed codes to all 425 data points. The annotation prompts are provided in the Supplementary Materials, and procedural details are reported in Appendix A. Each model annotated Cognitive Work 

and Desired Delegation Protocol in separate annotation passes, with each taxonomy coded independently of the other. Delega-tion Enactment labels were derived directly from participants’ structured annotations. We assessed agreement at two levels. First, we measured agree-ment between the two LLM annotators across all 425 held-out data points. We then evaluated their coding against human judgments using a participant-balanced sample of 60 instances (five randomly sampled data points per held-out participant), which one author independently labeled without access to the models’ predictions. For Desired Delegation Protocol , the two LLM annotators agreed on 94.1% (Cohen’s 𝜅 = .876), with similarly high agreement across all annotators in the validation sample (Fleiss’ 𝜅 = .853). For Cog-nitive Work , the two LLM annotators agreed on 72.7% (Cohen’s 

𝜅 = .686), with a Fleiss’ 𝜅 of .656 across all annotators. These results indicate that the finalized taxonomy could be applied consistently across model annotators and showed substantial agreement with human judgments [1, 24]. Disagreements between the two models were subsequently adjudicated by two authors through discussion and resolved by consensus. We then combined the resulting la-bels with the 567 data points used during taxonomy development, constructing a fully coded dataset of 992 reasoning steps for our empirical analyses.

## 5 Empirical Analysis 

Based on the taxonomies we built in Section 4, we analyze how delegation unfolds within LLM reasoning—what cognitive work it involves and when it extends beyond users’ prompts—through three research questions. 

RQ1. What cognitive work do LLMs perform in their reasoning, and how is it organized—across tasks and within a turn? 

RQ2. How is delegation actually enacted inside reasoning—who initiates each step, on what cognitive work does it fall, and where does it depart from what users wanted? 

RQ3. How do users want delegation to proceed, and how do their preferred protocols vary across different types of cognitive work? Analyses cover the 992 annotated reasoning steps of Section 4.5. Because multiple reasoning steps came from the same participant, we accounted for within-participant dependence in all significance tests. We used permutation tests based on the 𝜒 2 statistic while preserving within-participant dependence. For step-level tests, la-bels were shuffled within participants; for position tests control-ling for cognitive-work composition, labels were shuffled within participant-by-category cells; and for participant-level tests, partic-ipants were permuted across conditions. 

## 5.1 RQ1: What Cognitive Work LLMs Perform in Reasoning 

5.1.1 Cognitive Work Is Unevenly Distributed. Cognitive work was unevenly distributed across the reasoning traces (Figure 2). Gener-ating was the most frequent (21.7%), followed by Analyzing (14.2%), 

Planning (12.4%), and Understanding (12.1%). The prominence of 

Generating partly reflects how tool use surfaces in reasoning traces: each artifact-producing tool call, such as generating one slide of a pitch deck, appears as its own step, and 78% of the 167 tool-call steps were coded Generating . These are genuine acts of production delegated to the AI, but a single user request can fan out into many of them. 

5.1.2 Cognitive Work Differs Across Tasks. The composition of cog-nitive work depended on the task ( 𝜒 2 = 320 .8, Cramér’s 𝑉 = .402, 

𝑝 < .001; Figure 3). Each task had a distinct dominant code: Gen-erating defined Creation (47.5% of its steps, 2 .2× its overall share), 

Gathering was concentrated in Information (1 .8×), and Analyzing 

dominated Advice (22.7%, 1 .6×). At the same time, a core of work stayed nearly constant across tasks: Understanding , Planning , Recall-ing , Elaborating , and Narrowing varied by at most a few percentage points. Task-specific differences were therefore concentrated in par-ticular forms of cognitive work, while several other types remained relatively stable across tasks. 

5.1.3 Cognitive Work Shows Positional Tendencies Within a Turn. 

Different types of cognitive work had different median positions within a turn, although the distributions overlapped substantially (Figure 4). Understanding , Planning , and Recalling had earlier me-dian positions (0 .00, 0 .25, and 0 .40, respectively). Generating had a later median position (0 .61), while Evaluating (0 .80) and Syn-thesizing (0 .86) had the latest medians. Overall, these suggest a progression from framing and sourcing toward developing and reviewing. 

## 5.2 RQ2: How Delegation Is Enacted in LLM Reasoning 

5.2.1 Nearly Half of Steps Were Judged AI-Initiated. Users dele-gated a task through their prompt, but this did not mean that they had asked the AI to take on every reasoning step involved in com-pleting it. Participants judged 48.6% of all steps as AI-initiated, meaning that they saw the AI as taking on work they had not re-quested (Figure 5). Across the four forms of delegation enactment, Intended Delegation accounted for 43.6% of steps, Serendipi-tous Delegation for 30.4%, Unintended Delegation for 18.1%, and Regretted Delegation for 7.8%. 

5.2.2 Unwanted and Minor Steps Were More Often AI-Initiated. AI-initiated work was not necessarily unwanted. Of the AI-initiated steps, 62.7% contributed as users wanted (Serendipitous Delega-tion), while 37.3% did not (Unintended Delegation). However, AI initiation was more common among unwanted and minor steps. 70.0% of unwanted steps were AI-initiated, compared with 41.1% of wanted steps, and 75.8% of minor steps were AI-initiated, compared with 44.4% of critical steps (both 𝑝 < .001). 

5.2.3 Some Kinds of Cognitive Work Invite Unwanted Initiative. 

Delegation enactment varied across cognitive work (Figure 7). Un-derstanding and Planning were most often explicitly prompted and completed as participants wanted, with 72% and 54% classified as Intended Delegation. AI-initiated work was often welcomed in 

Synthesizing , Brainstorming , and Analyzing , whereas Unintended Delegation was most common in Repeating (44%), Narrowing 

(38%), Generating (28%), and Gathering (25%). These differences did not simply reflect how often the AI took ini-tiative. Analyzing and Gathering had nearly identical AI-initiation rates (51% and 50%), but 26% of AI-initiated Analyzing steps were unwanted, compared with 51% of Gathering steps. Generating con-tributed the most unintended cases by volume: 61 steps, or 34% of all Unintended Delegation. 

5.2.4 Later Reasoning Steps Were More Often Judged AI-Initiated. 

Participants judged 31.1% of steps in the first fifth of a turn as AI-initiated, compared with 61.4% in the final fifth ( 𝑝 < .001; Figure 8). Part of this increase coincided with a shift from Framing toward 

Developing , Reviewing , and Reprocessing , which were more often judged AI-initiated. However, this change in cognitive-work com-position predicted an increase only from 38.0% to 52.6%, below the observed 61.4% (Figure 8c). The increase therefore reflected both changing cognitive work and greater AI initiative within the same kinds of work later in the turn ( 𝑝 = .008). Unintended Delegation also became more common later in the turn. However, an AI-initiated step was not significantly more likely to be unwanted later, suggesting that the increase primarily resulted from more steps being attributed to AI initiative. ## 6 Design Exploration: Toward Deliberate Delegation 

Our analysis shows that a single task request can encompass mul-tiple kinds of cognitive work that users may want delegated in different ways. The three taxonomies in Section 4 help translate this finding into design by identifying what work the AI performed, how it was delegated, and how users wanted the AI to proceed. Drawing on the taxonomies and empirical findings, we develop two design implications for supporting more deliberate delegation— enabling users to decide more selectively what work the AI takes on and when and how they remain involved. We illustrate these implications with five low-fidelity design sketches that make con-crete how interfaces could represent delegated work and let users shape how it proceeds. 

## 6.1 DI1: Support Multiple Delegation Protocols Within a Single Response 

A single LLM response involves multiple kinds of cognitive work, and users did not want to delegate all of it the same way: users wanted consultation before some AI-initiated generation, calibra-tion for repetitive work, disclosure of work they wanted to see, and full delegation for work they were comfortable leaving to the AI. These patterns can indicate where coordination may be needed, but should not prescribe fixed protocols. Building on these patterns, an interface could translate the relevant cognitive work into concrete task decisions and let the user choose how the AI should proceed. We explore how this involvement could be configured before the task, connected to the current request while prompting, and enacted during and after execution. 

Before the task. Users could review a delegation plan specifying which cognitive work the AI should carry out independently and where they want to be involved (Figure 11a). The plan could start from protocols the user selected in previous tasks and show a past episode to help them decide what to change. For laptop selection, 

Gathering specifications and prices might remain Silent , while a previous incident in which the AI applied an unwanted price cap leads the user to set Narrowing to Ask . This gives users a preview of how the AI will carry out the task and lets them decide which work to delegate or handle together. 

While prompting. As users compose a prompt, the interface could show what cognitive work it may lead the AI to perform and how the delegation plan would apply (Figure 11b). In “find me a good laptop I can travel with—nothing too expensive,” Under-standing concerns interpreting travel needs, Narrowing concerns applying a price limit, and Generating concerns producing the rec-ommendation. Because Narrowing is set to Ask , the interface opens a question about the price limit. This could help users notice, before sending the prompt, that the AI may otherwise choose a budget on their behalf. They can then set that budget or leave it to the AI, making their intended division of work clearer without specifying every intermediate step. 

During and after execution. The interface could let users ask the AI to pause, review a choice it has made, or adjust work in progress (Figure 12). Under Consultative Delegation, Ask pauses before shortlist selection and lets the user decide whether they or the AI should choose the laptops. Under Disclosed Delegation, 

Tell identifies a choice within the answer: the AI interpreted “noth-ing too expensive” as a $3,000 cap, excluding four laptops. Showing the criterion and the affected options could help users decide how to steer the next interaction. ## 6.2 DI2: Make Fine-Grained Delegation Visible as Interaction Unfolds 

Delegation continues to take shape after the user sends a prompt, as the AI takes on different kinds of cognitive work while producing a response. Our findings also showed that users sometimes wanted visibility into AI-initiated work even when its contribution was wanted. Interfaces could therefore make it easier to see what work the AI took on, how it approached that work, and where the user may want to intervene. Such visibility could support deliberate delegation by helping users connect the AI’s actions to their own intentions and decide how to proceed together. 

Within a response. Users could inspect reasoning steps labeled with their cognitive-work type and attributed initiative (Figure 13a). A summary reports how many steps were attributed to AI initia-tive, while the underlying steps remain accessible. In the example, opening “Reclaim” on a Narrowing step reveals that the AI applied a $3,000 price cap and excluded four laptops. Users can change the criterion, regenerate the shortlist, or request consultation before similar decisions. The same interaction could support involvement without rejecting the result: users might keep a satisfactory short-list while reviewing its selection rule or requesting consultation next time. 

Across turns. A conversation overview could show how many of the attributed steps in each reply were AI-initiated (e.g., 5 of 8), while retaining access to the underlying work (Figure 13b). Opening a reply would reveal its cognitive work, allowing users to inspect a decision and choose how similar work should proceed later without reviewing every step or restarting the task. 

Across tasks. A personal dashboard could help users review how they have delegated different kinds of cognitive work across interactions (Figure 14). Its overview places each work type by the number of AI-initiated decisions and the user’s current involvement setting. Inspecting a point reveals the underlying counts, protocol selections, and a past episode. The review panel could help users consider those choices in context: they may have asked for consul-tation after an inferred laptop price cap while allowing the AI to shortlist hotels silently in another task. When a choice recurs, the interface could offer to save it as a default and let the user select the task types to which it applies. This could help users carry useful ways of working with the AI into future tasks while retaining the ability to choose differently in other contexts. Overall, these sketches show how interfaces could support delib-erate delegation: users could choose how different cognitive work should proceed, inspect and revise work the AI takes on, and carry those choices into later interactions. ## 7.3 From Task-Level Collaboration to Deliberate Delegation 

We argue for deliberate delegation in human–AI collaboration: mak-ing allocations of cognitive work visible and revisable without requiring user intervention at every step. Existing accounts often describe collaboration through tasks, workflow stages, interaction modes, or conversational turns [11, 16, 45]. Yet a single response may contain multiple kinds of cognitive work, including work the user expected to retain. A step-level view reveals how this work is allocated and where users may want different forms of involvement. DI1 treats involvement as a choice among coordination protocols, rather than a level of automation. Users may prefer guided partici-pation to full automation for exploratory or creative work [19, 45]. By supporting consultation, adjustment, disclosure, or full delega-tion for different cognitive work, interfaces could preserve useful AI initiative without requiring involvement at every step. DI2 concerns what an interface should make visible and revisable. Decomposing AI work can support steering and verification [18], while provenance can help users retain control and ownership [17]. Rather than expose every step, interfaces could surface AI-initiated work that may warrant attention, let users inspect and revise spe-cific decisions, and carry reviewed choices into later interactions. Deliberate delegation thus combines selective involvement with visibility into how cognitive work is allocated, allowing users to retain a role without giving up useful AI initiative. ## 7.4 Limitations and Future Work 

Our findings come from 24 frequent LLM users and three predefined tasks. Each of the Creation, Information, and Advice categories was represented by one task, so differences between categories may also reflect differences between the specific tasks. The tasks were short and carried fewer consequences and organizational constraints than many forms of knowledge work. Participants also used one model, one interface, and a limited set of tools. Model behavior, system prompts, trace format, and available tools can all change which steps appear. For example, the PowerPoint tool represented separate artifact-producing calls as separate steps, increasing the share of Generating in the Creation task. Future work should apply the taxonomies across populations, domains, tasks, models, and agent architectures before treating the distributions reported here as general patterns. Participants evaluated each step retrospectively after seeing the output. This context helped them connect intermediate work to its effects, but it may also have introduced hindsight and outcome biases. Reviewing every step after a task also differs from being interrupted during ongoing work. Stated preferences for consulta-tion, adjustment, or disclosure may change when those mechanisms impose real costs. The design sketches in Section 6 are therefore proposals rather than evaluated interfaces. Future studies should implement these protocols and measure task performance, inter-ruption, workload, control, ownership, and the retention of useful AI initiative over time. The taxonomies also simplify work that can serve more than one function. We assigned one primary cognitive-work code to each step, and agreement for Cognitive Work was lower than for Desired Delegation Protocol. The categories may need to be extended or represented hierarchically in specialized domains. Further human validation and cross-domain use could clarify their boundaries. The post-training and adaptive-policy directions also require larger preference datasets and tests of whether learned policies generalize across contexts. Such systems should avoid treating group-level patterns as user-specific preferences and consider the privacy and agency implications of maintaining a history of what each user chooses to delegate. 

## 8 Conclusion 

We examined how users perceived delegation within LLM reasoning traces. Drawing on 992 retrospective step-level annotations from 24 participants, we developed three taxonomies characterizing what cognitive work each reasoning step performed, how that work was delegated, and how users wanted the AI to proceed. Participants viewed nearly half of the steps as AI-initiated, with this share increasing as reasoning progressed. Desired involvement varied across cognitive work and delegation enactment, including when contributions were wanted. These findings highlight the importance of understanding delegation at the reasoning-step level, where users may want different roles across the work involved in a single task. Our design exploration illustrates how this understanding could support deliberate delegation by helping users inspect AI-initiated work and choose when and how to participate.

## A LLM-Based Taxonomy Application Procedure 

After developing the taxonomies through qualitative analysis, we used LLMs to code the held-out portion of the dataset for two fi-nalized taxonomies: Cognitive Work and Desired Delegation Protocol . This appendix provides the detailed procedure for this LLM-assisted coding process, including input construction, prompt-ing, reliability assessment, and adjudication. The full prompt tem-plates used for LLM annotation are provided in the supplementary materials: one for Cognitive Work classification and one for Desired Delegation Protocol classification. 

## A.1 Annotation Scope 

The unit of annotation was an individual reasoning step or tool-use action produced by the LLM during a participant’s task session. Although labels were assigned at the step level, each step was classi-fied in context rather than as an isolated text span. This context was important because several labels depended on relationships among steps. For example, Elaborating and Repeating required identifying whether a step returned to content from a prior reasoning step, while Synthesizing required recognizing when the model integrated multiple earlier reasoning steps into a consolidated conclusion. We also provided preceding turns from the same session when they were needed to interpret the target turn. This was especially important for Recalling , which required determining whether the model was bringing forward information from earlier conversation history. We used LLM annotators for two taxonomies: Cognitive Work 

and Desired Delegation Protocol . These were classified in sep-arate annotation passes using separate prompts. Delegation En-actment was not classified by the LLM annotators; it was derived directly from participants’ structured annotations of initiative, crit-icality, and impact. 

## A.2 Input Construction 

For each target turn, we constructed an annotation packet contain-ing the interaction context needed to classify its reasoning steps. Each packet included the user’s prompt, the AI’s final response, the ordered reasoning steps and tool-use actions in that turn, and the participant’s retrospective annotation and free-form explana-tion for each step. The packet also included preceding turns from the same session to provide conversational context. The prompt included the finalized taxonomy definitions and coding rules for the taxonomy being applied. For Cognitive Work classification, each LLM annotator selected the type of work performed by each reasoning step, such as Un-derstanding , Planning , Gathering , Generating , or Elaborating . For Desired Delegation Protocol classification, each LLM annotator se-lected how the participant wanted the AI to proceed with that work, such as Full Delegation , Disclosed Delegation , Calibrated Delegation ,or Consultative Delegation . When classifying Desired Delegation Protocol, the participant’s free-form explanation was treated as the primary evidence because the taxonomy captures the participant’s preferred form of involvement. 

## A.3 Prompting Procedure 

We prompted two LLMs from different providers, GPT-5.6-sol and Claude-opus-4.8, as independent annotators. The two models re-ceived the same annotation packets and taxonomy definitions, but their labels were generated independently. We used one prompt template for Cognitive Work classification and a separate prompt template for Desired Delegation Protocol classification. In the Cognitive Work pass, the model was instructed to as-sign exactly one cognitive-work code to each reasoning step and to provide a brief rationale grounded in the supplied context. For steps labeled as Elaborating or Repeating , the model was addition-ally instructed to identify which prior reasoning step the current step elaborated on or repeated. This allowed us to preserve the relational structure of Reprocessing , whose meaning depends on a step’s connection to earlier reasoning. In the Desired Delegation Protocol pass, the model was instructed to assign exactly one desired-protocol label to each reasoning step and to provide a brief rationale grounded in the participant’s ret-rospective explanation and the surrounding turn context. Because this taxonomy captures how participants wanted the AI to pro-ceed, the model was instructed to prioritize participants’ free-form explanations when they clarified the desired form of involvement. 

## A.4 Reliability Assessment and Adjudication 

We assessed the consistency of LLM-based taxonomy application in two ways. First, we measured agreement between the two LLM annotators across the 425 held-out data points. For Cognitive Work, they agreed on 72.7% of labels, with Cohen’s 𝜅 = .686. For Desired Delegation Protocol, the two models agreed on 94.1% of labels, with Cohen’s 𝜅 = .876. Second, we compared the model labels against human judgments using a participant-balanced validation sample of 60 instances, con-sisting of five randomly sampled data points from each held-out participant. One author independently labeled this sample without access to the models’ predictions. Agreement across annotators was substantial for Cognitive Work, with Fleiss’ 𝜅 = .656, and high for Desired Delegation Protocol, with Fleiss’ 𝜅 = .853. After reliability assessment, disagreements between the two LLM annotators were adjudicated by two authors through discussion and resolved by consensus. The adjudicated labels for the 425 held-out data points were then combined with the 567 data points used during taxonomy development, resulting in a fully coded dataset of 992 reasoning steps for the analyses reported in Section 5.
