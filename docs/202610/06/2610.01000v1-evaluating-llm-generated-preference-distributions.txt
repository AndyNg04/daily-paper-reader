Abstract 

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increas-ingly used as probabilistic generators for simu-lation, synthetic data generation, and decision support in settings where real-world data are unavailable. Yet, the structure and reliability of the distributions they produce remain under-studied. Here, we systematically analyze LLM-generated distributions of preferences for air travel, restaurants, and consumer products. En-couragingly, all models considered in our analy-sis exhibit self-coherence, with the most proba-ble outcomes stabilizing rapidly under repeated sampling. At the same time, we observe sub-stantial discordance across both model families and scales, with little consensus even among their most probable outcomes. These patterns hold across nine open-weight models, three choice domains, and show robustness under temperature changes, greedy decoding, and per-turbations of prompt and ordering. Our findings indicate that outcomes are influenced more by the choice of model than by the wording of the prompt, challenging the common assumption that sufficiently capable LLMs produce similar preference distributions when used as stand-ins for survey respondents. 

1 Introduction 

There is a growing trend in relying on Large Lan-guage Models (LLMs) as probabilistic generators for synthetic data creation (Argyle et al., 2023), agent-based simulation (Park et al., 2023), opin-ion modeling (Santurkar et al., 2023), and survey simulation (Tjuatja et al., 2024). In a typical use case, an LLM is queried repeatedly under struc-tured prompts, and its output is interpreted as a draw from some underlying, meaningful distribu-tion. This latent distribution being sampled is often referred as an implicit world prior , that is, a repro-ducible mapping from a natural-language scenario description to a probability distribution over a dis-crete outcome set, induced by pre-training rather than by explicit calibration. Unlike a full “world model” (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018), which im-plies an internal simulator for planning, an implicit world prior is defined operationally through ex-ternally observable distributional behavior. These theorized distributions would only be useful for downstream simulation when they are stable under repeated sampling, internally structured rather than degenerate, and responsive in a bounded way to controlled prompt perturbations. Recent work supports the plausibility of such priors: LLMs encode geographic and temporal reg-ularities (Gurnee and Tegmark, 2024), can be repur-posed as world models that supply state predictions to an external planning algorithm (Hao et al., 2023), and can be characterized as models of the agents that produced their training data (Andreas, 2022). Building on this, a growing body of work treats LLMs as probabilistic stand-ins for human popula-tions and as agents in social simulation, while also documenting their sensitivity to prompt design; we review this literature in Section 2. Despite this growing reliance on LLM-derived distributions, their structural properties remain un-derexplored. Existing evaluations largely target the factual accuracy (Lin et al., 2022), confidence calibration, or aggregate benchmark performance (Hendrycks et al., 2021) of single outputs, rather than the entire output distribution under repeated sampling, the differential effect of model scale on the latent prior, or the magnitude of prompt-induced shifts in distributional terms. Recent work names prompt sensitivity and the confounded state of LLM evaluation among the foundational open challenges in assuring LLM behavior (Anwar et al., 2024). In this paper, we focus our attention on LLM-generated preference distributions for air travel, restaurants, and consumer products. We ask four 1arXiv:2610.01000v1 [cs.AI] 1 Oct 2026 questions: (i) Under repeated sampling at a fixed prompt, do LLM distributions converge, and, if so, which parts (high-probability modes or low-probability tails) stabilize first? (ii) Do different model sizes within the same family encode the same underlying prior? (iii) How sensitive are these distributions to demographic, contextual, and seasonal prompt perturbations? and (iv) Do the ob-served patterns generalize across choice domains and model families? To answer these questions, we introduce a frame-work for evaluating LLMs’ implicit world priors by prompting them to output explicit probability dis-tributions over discrete choice sets, applied to nine open-weight models across three families (Llama, Qwen, Mistral) and three domains (travel, restau-rants, products). As a desired outcome, we observe, irrespective of the domain of application and the LLM’s model family considered, a consistent be-havior characterized by a relatively quick consol-idation of the modes of the distributions. On the other hand, we also find that models from differ-ent families generate rather different distributions, and that even within the same family, there is a strong scale discordance: for Llama models (8B to 405B), different scales encode structurally differ-ent priors over the same choice set, with pairwise Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence in the range 0.36 to 0.50 and top-5 agreement below 0.13, larger than any bounded shift induced by prompt perturbations (JS in the range 0.13 to 0.33); this discordance per-sists, though more mildly, across the Qwen and Mistral families. All findings are validated under temperature ablation, greedy decoding, paraphrase and permutation robustness, and choice-set size variation; code and processed data will be made publicly available. Our results question the implicit assumption in silicon-sampling work (Argyle et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023) that a single fixed prior can be elicited from any LLM. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 LLMs for Simulation and Prediction 

Recent work has explored LLMs as simulators of human behavior (Park et al., 2023). Argyle et al. (2023) show that conditioning on socio-demographic backstories yields response distribu-tions that correlate with those of the matching hu-man subgroups, whereas Santurkar et al. (2023) report substantial misalignment between the opin-ion distributions of current LLMs and those of US demographic groups, a gap that persists even when the models are explicitly steered toward a group. Tjuatja et al. (2024) similarly found that LLMs generally fail to reflect human-like response biases under question modifications known to elicit those biases in humans, and Anwar et al. (2024) iden-tify prompt sensitivity and confounded evaluation among the foundational open challenges in assess-ing LLM behavior. However, the distributional properties of LLM outputs themselves (stability, convergence, and model sensitivity) remain largely uncharacterized at the level of full output distribu-tions. 

2.2 Probabilistic Calibration and Uncertainty 

Research on neural network calibration has long examined whether model confidence aligns with accuracy (Guo et al., 2017). In the context of LLMs, Jiang et al. (2021) investigated calibra-tion for question answering, while Kadavath et al. (2022) showed that language models can often assess whether they know the answer to a ques-tion. More recent work has developed methods for quantifying semantic uncertainty in generated text (Kuhn et al., 2023) and explored whether LLMs can verbally express their uncertainty (Xiong et al., 2024). While these studies focus on calibration of single-point predictions or confidence scores, our work analyzes full probability distributions over outcome spaces, providing a more complete char-acterization of LLM distributional behavior. 

2.3 Prompt Sensitivity and Robustness 

Studies have documented that LLM outputs can be highly sensitive to prompt phrasing, including the order of few-shot examples (Lu et al., 2022) and biases toward majority labels or recent ex-amples (Zhao et al., 2021). Perez et al. (2021) demonstrated that prompt selection can substan-tially affect few-shot performance, while Webson and Pavlick (2022) questioned whether models truly understand prompt semantics or rely on super-ficial patterns. Prompting strategies such as chain-of-thought (Wei et al., 2022) have been shown to elicit more reliable reasoning, yet the sensitivity of outputs to prompt variations remains a fundamental challenge. 

2.4 Mode Collapse and Numeric Calibration 

A related line of work examines mode collapse ,where generation diversity degrades during fine-tuning (Janus, 2022; O’Mahony et al., 2024). Zhou 2et al. (2024) showed that LLMs are reluctant to express uncertainty, relevant to our verbalized elic-itation design. Lovering et al. (2025) demon-strated poor numeric calibration in language mod-els. Lum et al. (2025) advocated evaluation beyond isolated test cases, a perspective aligned with our distribution-level framework. 

3 Methods 

We prompt LLMs to generate explicit probability distributions over large discrete choice sets, en-abling direct comparison across repeated queries. Throughout the paper, our primary domain is air-travel destination choice : each query elicits a distri-bution over a fixed choice set of 300 contiguous-US commercial airports, adopted from prior work on the US air transportation network (Kim et al., 2025). The framework itself is domain-agnostic; we addi-tionally study restaurant choice (Yelp) and product purchase (Amazon), but use these two domains only to cross-validate that our findings generalize beyond air travel (Section 5.5). 

3.1 Problem Formulation 

We treat LLMs as implicit world priors that man-ifest as probability distributions over discrete out-come spaces conditioned on natural language prompts. Formally, given a prompt x describing a contextual scenario (e.g., departure airport, traveler persona), an LLM induces a probability distribution over outcomes y ∈ Y : p(y | x). In our setting, Y

corresponds to a finite set of candidate destinations. Rather than treating LLM outputs as single-point predictions, we explicitly analyze the structure, sta-bility, and sensitivity of p(y | x) under repeated sampling and controlled prompt perturbations. 

3.2 Elicitation Method Validation 

We adopt verbalized distribution elicitation :prompting the LLM to output an explicit proba-bility distribution over all options in a single query. Hu and Levy (2023) argue that direct probability readout via logits is more faithful than metalinguis-tic prompting; however, both they and Lovering et al. (2025) examine calibration-to-ground-truth ,whereas our analysis targets reproducibility and 

structural consistency , properties for which ver-balized elicitation is sufficient. The two natural alternatives, logit inspection and repeated outcome sampling, are both infeasible at our experimental scale (see Appendix A for the full comparison and pilot study).

3.4 Mode and Tail Regions 

To characterize internal distributional dynamics, we partition each distribution’s outcomes into high-probability modes and low-probability tails. For a fixed non-negative integer k, order the outcomes by decreasing probability, p(y(1) | x) ≥ p(y(2) | x) ≥· · · ≥ p(y(|Y| ) | x) (ties broken arbitrarily), and de-fine the mode set as the k most probable outcomes, 

Mk(p) = {y(1) , . . . , y (k)}. The tail is defined as the complement Tk(p) = Y \ M k(p). We note that this mode–tail distinction is temperature de-pendent. Our use of low temperature ( T = 0 .2) is designed to minimize this effect; see Section 5.6 for a systematic analysis of temperature’s influence on mode–tail separation. This partition enables us to separately analyze the stability of dominant se-mantic beliefs (modes) and exploratory variability in low-probability outcomes (tails). In our experiments, we set k = 5 , which is em-pirically grounded: across 300 departure contexts, the natural mode–tail boundary (largest consec-utive probability gap) falls at rank ≤ 5 in 99% of distributions. 4 Experiments 

Our experimental approach is described in terms of our primary domain of air-travel destination choice for clarity, but additional experiments were car-ried out for cross-domain validation on restaurants (Yelp) and product choice (Amazon) separately, with results presented in Section 5.5. For these airline queries, we employ structured prompts pro-viding LLMs with a traveler persona, a departure airport, 300 possible destination airports, and in-structions to output preferences as probabilities. 5.1 Rapid Stabilization of Modes of the Distributions 

Our primary finding is that LLM-generated dis-tributions exhibit a consistent modes-first conver-gence pattern: high-probability outcomes are con-sistent across independent samples, while variabil-ity is concentrated in low-probability alternatives. Across contexts and model sizes, the cumulative destination support stabilizes within a few sam-ples (Table 1). The dominant modes show high pairwise agreement: across all sample pairs, mean Jaccard@5 is 0.72 and 36 of the 74 sample pairs share an identical top-5 set (Jaccard@5 = 1 .0), with Spearman rank correlation exceeding 0.95 at stabilization points. The per-model variation in stabilization rate and unique-destination count, par-ticularly the much later stabilization (sample 17) and higher diversity (100 vs. 32–54 destinations) of the 8B model, is discussed under scale discor-dance in Section 5.3. Full iteration heatmaps and per-context stability metrics are provided in Ap-pendix G.

5.4 Prompt Perturbations are Bounded and Structured 

We next examine the sensitivity of LLM-generated distributions to controlled prompt perturbations. Table 5 reports JS divergence with bootstrap confi-dence intervals for different perturbation types. Per-sona conditioning induces bounded shifts 3 (JS = 0.13 ± 0.01 ), while demographic attributes pro-duce larger effects, including age ( 0.33 ± 0.05 )and education ( 0.26 ± 0.03 ). Context specification (regional vs. hub) also substantially reshapes distri-butions ( 0.31 ±0.04 ). The persona-conditioned dis-tributions in Appendix J (Figure 12) illustrate how persona information systematically redistributes probability mass across outcome types. 5.5 Cross-domain generalization 

To assess whether our findings generalize beyond the primary domain, we replicate our conver-gence analysis in two additional domains: restau-rant choice (300 restaurants from the Yelp Open Dataset (Yelp, n.d.), Indianapolis) and online prod-uct shopping (300 Home & Kitchen products from Amazon metadata (Ni et al., 2019), sampled via stratified embedding-based selection). Dataset con-struction is detailed in Appendix D. Across all three domains, the modes-first, tails-later pattern holds. The cumulative support keeps expanding as new tail items continue to appear, while the JS distance to the converged distribution decreases steadily, fully converging by iteration 8 for travel, 11 for Yelp, and 17 for Amazon. The three domains differ in single-context final support size (29 destinations for travel from Indianapolis vs. 103 restaurants and 51 products for Yelp and Ama-zon; across all 300 departure contexts the travel experiment reaches 185 unique destinations, Ap-pendix F) and in top-10 mass: Amazon (79% of probability mass in the top-10) > travel (75%) >

Yelp (49%), consistent with differences in utility structure. The Amazon modes are a few specific best-selling appliances (e.g., a KitchenAid stand mixer, a Mr. Coffee espresso maker, and a Hamilton Beach slow cooker), whereas the Yelp mass spreads thinly across many interchangeable national chains (e.g., Starbucks, Chipotle, and Subway), indicating that product choices exhibit clearer utility hierar-chies than restaurant preferences. Because Yelp and Amazon datasets are real-world choice sets whose specific item lists are unlikely to be mem-orized verbatim, and because the same dynamics appear across three model families trained on dif-ferent corpora, this pattern is not attributable to single-corpus memorization. 

5.6 Robustness of Findings 

We validate the central findings under four comple-mentary robustness checks. 

Temperature ablation. Systematic ablation across 10 temperature values ( T ∈ { 0.1, . . . , 1.0})and 6 settings confirms temperature effects are significant for maximum probability, entropy, and top-5 mass (6/6 settings, all p < 0.001 , Kruskal-Wallis) and directionally consistent in 17 of 18 metric-by-setting comparisons (Appendix L). Low temperatures yield substantially greater ranking stability (Jaccard@5 = 0.93 at T =0 .2 vs. 0.81 at 8T =1 .0), validating T =0 .2 as a principled choice; on those same three metrics the low-versus-high temperature contrast is large in 13 of 18 compar-isons (Cohen’s |d| = 0 .98 –3.50 ). Further, full greedy-decoding ( T =0 ) replication preserves all main findings (Appendix M). 

Multi-source variability. Across three orthogo-nal sources of variability, input-ordering permuta-tions ( N =15 ), prompt paraphrasing (5 variants), and multiple origin airports (IND, LAX, BOS), all metrics exhibit CV below 21%, with the frame-work most robust to input ordering (mean CV = 9.6%) and prompt paraphrasing (mean CV = 10.5%). Core destinations emerge consistently re-gardless of ordering (e.g., ORD appears in top-5 across 100% of permutations). Full per-source co-efficients of variation (Table 15) are reported in Appendix N. 

Variance decomposition. An intraclass-correlation analysis (ICC(1,1); Appendix N, Table 16) finds that departure airport and persona contribute little systematic between-condition variance (airport ICC ≤ 0.08 on every metric; persona ≤ 0 apart from a modest 0.16 on peak probability), whereas model scale is the only factor with a non-trivial positive component, capturing the largest share of variance in distributional spread (support ICC = 0.46 ) and the largest ICC on every metric except top-5 mass. This corroborates scale discordance through a variance-based lens, independent of the pairwise divergences in Section 5.3. 

6 Discussion and Limitations 

Our results support viewing LLMs as structured stochastic generators of categorical distributions. The principal practical implication is that model choice, not prompt design, is the dominant lever shaping the elicited distribution: across three fam-ilies and three domains, different models encode structurally different priors rather than refinements of one another, both between families and between scales within a family. Several scope conditions limit these conclusions. The analysis is restricted to discrete choice set-tings with enumerable outcome spaces, specifi-cally on the order of hundreds of outcomes, and the patterns may not transfer to open-ended gen-eration. We evaluate open-weight models only, since closed-source APIs apply provider-side post-processing that confounds distributional measure-ment; whether the same dynamics hold in closed-source systems is an open question. All prompts are in English, and we do not benchmark against ground-truth human preferences, so the correspon-dence between characterized priors and real-world choice behavior is unstudied here. We emphasize, however, that in many settings where LLM-derived priors are most useful (small or hypothetical pop-ulations, future or counterfactual scenarios, and choices for which no survey or behavioral data exist), a ground-truth distribution is unobservable in principle rather than merely unavailable to us. Eliciting an LLM’s implicit world prior is appeal-ing precisely in such cases, where the operative question becomes the prior’s internal consistency and structure (which we characterize) rather than its fidelity to a reference that does not exist. We also do not claim the observed regularities identify a specific internal mechanism: multiple internal processes may produce similar external behavior, and our contribution is to characterize that behavior systematically as consistent with implicit world pri-ors. The margin by which model choice outweighs prompt design further depends on how far apart the two models are in scale: the divergence between the 8B model and either larger checkpoint ( 0.474 

and 0.501 ) separates from every prompt perturba-tion we measure with non-overlapping confidence intervals, whereas the 70B-versus-405B divergence (0.365 ) and the largest prompt effects (age, 0.330 ;context, 0.310 ) have intervals that overlap and are not resolved at our persona sample size. We read this as sharpening the claim rather than qualifying it: discordance scales with the size gap, so the prac-tical guidance is strongest precisely where practi-tioners are most likely to substitute one model for another, and it identifies adjacent large checkpoints as the regime in which prompt design and model choice carry comparable weight. 

7 Conclusion and Future Work 

Treating LLMs as implicit world priors (eliciting full probability distributions over discrete choice sets and analyzing them as repeated samples) re-veals a consistent distributional anatomy across nine open-weight models, three families, and three choice preference domains. To this end, all models appear to be solid generators of distributions: distri-butions converge modes-first , with top-k outcomes stabilizing within a few samples while tail vari-9ability persists; for Llama, prompt perturbations induce systematic but bounded shifts below the ef-fect of changing scale; and the modes-first dynam-ics replicate across all three choice domains and, in the travel domain, across all three model fami-lies. Decoding temperature amplifies or dampens this tail variability, but it does not create it: most of the tail is still present under greedy decoding (T =0 ), where sampling randomness is switched off entirely. On the other hand, there is a strong discordance between the outcome of the various models, both across families and scales. Two practical implications follow. For down-stream applications that treat LLM outputs as sam-ples from a meaningful prior, model selection should be treated as a primary experimental vari-able, not as a cost–performance trade-off; and a small number of independent samples is sufficient for identifying dominant outcomes but not for char-acterizing tail uncertainty. Future work includes validating the elicited priors against real-world hu-man preference data, developing theoretical ac-counts that link distributional dynamics to training objectives and data, and extending the framework to closed-source models, alternative architectures, and non-English settings. Until elicited priors can be checked against human preference data, we rec-ommend reporting any LLM-derived distribution together with the model that produced it, since the distribution is as much a property of that model as of the question it was asked. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work analyzes the distributional properties of LLM-generated outputs under controlled prompt variations, including demographic and contextual cues. While these analyses can reveal systematic patterns in model behavior, they are not intended to support normative or policy decisions. Any down-stream use of LLM-generated distributions, partic-ularly in decision-support or simulation settings, should be accompanied by validation against real-world data and appropriate domain expertise. Our findings further underline that model choice sub-stantially influences the generated distribution, so transparency about model selection and sensitiv-ity analyses is important whenever LLM outputs inform applied decisions. The experiments rely on synthetic data and do not involve personal in-formation, but care should still be taken to avoid misinterpretation of demographically conditioned outputs. Large-scale LLM inference also incurs nontrivial computational and environmental costs, which we encourage practitioners to consider when planning experiments.

Logit Inspection. Next-token logits provide probabilities over vocabulary tokens, not over multi-token outcomes such as three-letter IATA airport codes. B.6 Justification for k=5 Mode–Tail Threshold 

We adopt a fixed-k definition rather than acumulative-probability threshold (top-p) because fixed set sizes are essential for direct cross-model and cross-condition comparisons via Jaccard over-lap and rank correlation, the central metrics in our scale discordance and prompt sensitivity analyses. A top-p criterion would produce variable-size mode sets (e.g., 3 destinations for a peaked distribution vs. 50 for a flat one), confounding set-size differences with genuine distributional disagreement. The choice of k=5 is empirically grounded. We identify the natural mode–tail boundary by computing the probability gap between each con-secutively ranked outcome (i.e., the difference 

p(y(i)) − p(y(i+1) ) where outcomes are sorted by decreasing probability); the rank at which this gap is largest indicates where the distribution transi-tions from concentrated modes to a diffuse tail. Across 300 departure contexts, this largest gap falls at rank ≤ 5 in 99% of distributions (median at rank 1). Table 8 repeats the mode–tail analysis of Table 2 for k ∈ { 3, 5, 7, 10 , 15 } across the four primary contexts. Two of the three k-dependent quantities are insensitive to the cutoff: tail divergence ex-ceeds mode divergence in both post-convergence phases at every k ≥ 5, with the margin widen-ing as k grows (in the 10–20 phase, from 2.3× at 

k=5 to 3.7× at k=15 ) because outcomes beyond the natural boundary are then counted as modes; at k=3 the gap survives only in the later phase, as expected when the cutoff sits below the natu-ral elbow; and post-convergence mode agreement is nearly flat (mean Jaccard@ k between 0.72 and 

0.77 ). The stabilization point R∗ is the exception, drifting later as k grows ( 1–7 at k=3 versus 10 –18 

at k=15 ). This drift is mechanical rather than sub-stantive: a single swap in a top-k set pushes the Jaccard index below the detection threshold for any 

k (Appendix B.7), and larger mode sets offer more boundary outcomes to swap. It is a further reason to fix k at the natural elbow rather than above it. The sweep above is computed at the primary set-ting T =0 .2, but the mode–tail structure is not an artifact of that choice. Across the ten-value temper-ature ablation of Appendix L, tail divergence ex-ceeds mode divergence at every temperature tested (10 of 10 values in T ∈ [0 .1, 1.0] ), and mode-set stability is preserved at both cutoffs: Jaccard@10 stays between 0.81 and 0.91 while Jaccard@5 de-cays from 0.93 to 0.81 , and top-5 mass remains between 0.49 and 0.57 (Table 13). Raising the tem-perature therefore blurs the mode–tail separation gradually rather than relocating the boundary. 

B.7 Threshold-Insensitivity of the Stabilization Point 

The stabilization point R∗ (Section 3.5) is detected by thresholding the top-k Jaccard index between successive cumulative top-k sets, and the specific value of this threshold is immaterial.
