ABSTRACT 

Large language models (LLMs) increasingly mediate human communication, from drafting emails to summarizing scientific reports, yet whether they faithfully preserve a speaker’s position remains largely untested. We model AI-mediated communication as a two-step generation-extraction pipeline: one LLM produces an argument from a specified stance, and a second LLM extracts the stance from that argument. We represent the pipeline as a probabilistic state transition over five Likert-type stance categories and define the stance preservation rate (SPR) as the average probability that the extracted stance matches the initial stance. Across 112 debate propositions, none of the nine LLMs tested exceeded an SPR of 0.7 under the default configuration. Three drift patterns accounted for most of the drift: po-larization, deviation from neutrality, and flipping. Among the mitigation strategies tested, including in-context learning, multiple extraction with shuffled options, as-sertion, and reflection, only adding medium reasoning effort to a reflection prompt for GPT-5.4 substantially improved the SPR, to 0.775 , yet polarization remained the largest pattern, with 0.119 of the transition mass. An exploratory comparison with human extraction on a single proposition suggests that drift arises at both the generation and the extraction stage. These results point to a fidelity gap in AI-mediated communication, with implications for journalism, policy deliberation, scientific communication, and other domains where opinion-laden messages pass through language models. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

History shows that small failures in message fidelity can have outsized consequences: the Berlin Wall fell hours after a confused spokesperson mistakenly announced that the borders were opening ‘immediately, without delay’ (Hertle, 2001). Today, this fragility of transmission is no longer limited to human intermediaries but is now embedded in the algorithms that are increasingly integrated into human communication across domains including decision making (Chen et al., 2023; Brand et al., 2023), scientific research (Horton, 2023; Qin et al., 2024), and communications (Bick et al., 2024;

In one illustrative example from our framework, an AI-mediated pipeline can invert an initial “Agree” stance on the proposition “The use of AI should be abandoned” into “Dis-agree” and “Disagree strongly”, effectively reversing the speaker’s position (Fig. 2B). The inversion evokes familiar science-fiction tropes about AI resisting shutdown, but the scientific issue here is more basic: whether large language models (LLMs) preserve a communicator’s intent as messages pass through them. This question is increasingly important because LLMs are now routine intermediaries in written communication. From July 2024 to June 2025, writing-related activities (including text generation, editing, critiques, summarization, and translation) made up more than 40% of ChatGPT’s work-related use (Chatterji et al., 2025). Beyond the well-studied issues with LLM-generated content, such as inherent biases and hallucinations (Acerbi & Stubbersfield, 2023; Spitale et al., 2023; Piao et al., 2025; R¨ ottger et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), an equally important but less explored challenge is the preservation of stance , the attitude or position toward a given proposition, during AI-mediated communication. Classic stance detection frames this task as classifying a writer’s position toward a target us-ing discrete categories (e.g., in favor, against, or neutral), leading to benchmarks such as 

SemEval-2016 (Mohammad et al., 2016) and surveys that consolidate conventions across plat-forms and domains (K¨ uc ¸ ¨ uk & Can, 2020; AlDayel & Magdy, 2021). More recent work on subjective global opinions shows that LLM interactions often involve value-laden judgments, with model re-sponses reflecting specific human opinion distributions (Durmus et al., 2023; Santurkar et al., 2023), and examines LLM response behavior in surveys (Tjuatja et al., 2024), self-preference (Panickssery et al., 2024), and sycophancy (Sharma et al., 2024). At the communication level, Hancock et al. (2020) propose studying how AI intermediaries affect human communication, and related research studies whether transformations preserve perspective: co-writing with an opinionated model shifts writers’ views (Jakesch et al., 2023), and in news summarization, Liu et al. (2024) find that sum-marization systems frequently alter an article’s political stance. Beyond single-step transformations, transmission-chain (“telephone game”) studies with LLMs reveal cumulative, directional changes as messages are iteratively regenerated (Acerbi & Stubbersfield, 2023; Perez et al., 2024). While valuable, these strands (i) do not capture the full opinion-communication process from initial mes-sage generation to downstream stance extraction, (ii) focus on specific tasks or settings rather than fundamental mechanisms of communication, and (iii) often lack a general, quantitative statistic for end-to-end stance fidelity. We present a systematic framework for quantifying stance preservation in complete AI-mediated human-to-human communication processes by adopting a simple, task-agnostic decomposi-tion— proposition (what is asserted) plus stance (position toward it)—and purposefully using dis-crete categories to match social-science measurement practices (Likert-type elicitation) (Likert, 1932; Croasmun & Ostrom, 2011). As LLM-generated text is repeatedly reused, real-world in-formation flow naturally forms a generation-extraction pipeline (Fig. 1): a sender prompts a model to produce an argument from a given stance, and a receiver later extracts the stance from that text with LLM assistance. We therefore study whether the sender’s stance is faithfully conveyed through AI mediation. We call this stance preservation (the receiver recovers the same discrete stance as the sender intended) and refer to any AI-induced change as stance drift . We model the end-to-end process as a probabilistic state transition, introducing a transition matrix to quantify stance preser-vation: diagonal elements indicate the probability of retaining the original stance (with a value of 1

representing perfect fidelity), while off-diagonal entries capture the extent of stance drift .To illustrate, Fig. 2 presents the empirical transition matrices (averaged across 100 repetitions) from two experiments with OpenAI’s GPT-5.4 (complete results with standard errors in Fig. S3). For a fixed proposition, the (i, j ) entry of the matrix represents the probability of ending in the jth choice, after our proposed generation-extraction process, given the ith choice as the initial stance. We compare the empirical matrices of proposition “We should increase fuel tax” (Fig. 2A) and “The use of AI should be abandoned” (Fig. 2B) against the identity matrix (Fig. 2C), which represents the ideal of perfect preservation of each stance through generation and extraction. We observe three major drift patterns. First, polarization occurs when moderate inputs (“Agree”/“Disagree”) intensify to extreme positions (“Agree strongly”/“Disagree strongly”) (Fig. 2A). 2 MATERIALS AND METHODS 

2.1 INPUT DATA PREPARATION 

We take the 114 debate topics of the debate speeches dataset (Slonim et al., 2021) as propositions and do not use the speeches themselves. The topics are clearly stated but open to competing lines of reasoning, so they support plausible arguments on both sides and test whether a model preserves an intended stance rather than restates a factual claim. 2.2 EXPERIMENTAL FRAMEWORK : GENERATION AND EXTRACTION 

We model AI-mediated human-to-human communication as a two-step generation-extraction pro-cess (Fig. 3). In each trial, a first LLM (Agent 1) is given a proposition along with a specified initial stance and is prompted to generate an argument. (Here, a proposition is a declarative statement, a 

stance is the attitude toward that proposition, chosen from a five-point Likert scale: Agree strongly, Agree, Neutral, Disagree, Disagree strongly as in social science surveys (Johns, 2010; Croasmun & Ostrom, 2011), and an argument is a short passage that expresses the reasoning behind that stance.) For example, if the proposition is about AI abandonment (“The use of AI should be abandoned.”), an initial stance of “Disagree strongly” would lead Agent 1 to produce an argument arguing strongly against abandoning AI. A second call to the same model (Agent 2), with no access to the first call’s context, then extracts the stance from Agent 1’s argument by reading both the argument and the proposition, outputting one of the five stance options (see Figs. S1–S2 for individual examples). In both the generation and extraction processes, options are presented in the prompt as multiple-choice questions, following prior work (Ren et al., 2023; Durmus et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2025). For LLMs that provide token-level log probabilities via API, extraction uncertainty is represented directly by the model’s output probability distribution in a single trial. To account for generation-stage randomness, we repeat trials for each stance of each proposition and average the resulting extraction distributions. Finally, the transition probabilities will be estimated by the average (across trials) probabilities assigned to each of the five choices by the LLM when API-available, or the observed frequencies otherwise. Table S13 in the appendix lists, for every run, the model identifier, the run dates, and which of the two estimates was used. 4Preprint 2.3 TRANSITION MATRIX AND STANCE PRESERVATION RATE (SPR) METRIC 

The transition matrix resulting from this process would be a 5 × 5 matrix. Without further spec-ification, we overload the notation and use the term “the ith choice” to refer to the corresponding stance, where i ranges from 0 to 4, representing the five choices: Agree strongly, Agree, Neutral, Disagree, and Disagree strongly. The (i, j ) entry of the transition matrix represents the probability of returning to the jth choice given the ith choice as the initial choice. Ideally, the transition matrix resulting from this process would be the identity matrix, indicating complete preservation of the original stance throughout the process. We therefore examine the diagonal elements, which represent the probabilities of returning to the original stance after the generation-extraction. We conduct our experiments on 112 of the 114 topics from the debate speeches dataset, reserving the remaining two topics as examples for in-context learning, as discussed in Section 2.5. Each topic undergoes 100 repeated trials, yielding empirical 5 × 5 transition matrices whose diagonal elements (i, i ) quantify stance preservation: the probability that the extraction stage recovers the same stance supplied to the generation stage. We define this diagonal probability as the “success rate” and estimate it by averaging across trials. All raw data and estimated transition matrices are available in our code repository. To quantify stance preservation across the diverse topics in the debate speeches dataset, we define the stance preservation rate (SPR) as the average success rate across all topics and initial stances—that is, the fraction of messages whose original stance survives the generation-extraction process (un-less otherwise specified, SPR assumes a uniform prior over initial stances; alternative priors can be incorporated by weighted averaging over diagonal transition probabilities). This metric provides a single, interpretable measure of how faithfully models maintain communicative intent, and thus of-fers a clear benchmark for comparing model reliability in AI-mediated communication and assessing mitigation strategies. SPR is the complement of the expected 0–1 loss under a uniform prior over initial stances. Any other prior, or any other loss over stance pairs such as one weighted by distance on the Likert scale, is a weighted sum of the entries of the same transition matrix and requires additional assumptions on the weights. These extensions share the same transition-matrix foundation; the strict SPR used throughout this paper represents a general baseline with no additional assumptions. 2.4 STANCE DRIFT PATTERNS 

Off-diagonal entries of the transition matrix record stance drift . We partition them into five patterns defined over the index pairs (i, j ) with i̸ = j. Polarization is the pair set {(1 , 0) , (3 , 4) }: a moderate stance is extracted as the extreme stance on the same side. Moderation is the pair set {(0 , 1) , (4 , 3) }:an extreme stance is extracted as the moderate stance on the same side. Moderation to neutral is the pair set {(i, 2) : i̸ = 2 }: a non-neutral stance is extracted as Neutral. Flipping is the pair set 

{(i, j ) : i ∈ { 0, 1}, j ∈ { 3, 4}} ∪ { (i, j ) : i ∈ { 3, 4}, j ∈ { 0, 1}} : a stance is extracted as a stance on the opposite side. Deviation from neutrality is the pair set {(2 , j ) : j̸ = 2 }: Neutral is extracted as a non-neutral stance. The five sets are disjoint and together cover every off-diagonal pair, so each off-diagonal entry belongs to exactly one pattern. The mass of a pattern for initial stance i is the sum of the transition probabilities (i, j ) over the pairs of that pattern with that i. The five pattern masses for initial stance i sum to one minus the success rate of i. As with SPR, the overall mass of a pattern is the average of its per-stance masses over the five initial stances. We call one minus SPR the stance drift rate (SDR). The five overall pattern masses are the components of the SDR and sum to it. 2.5 MITIGATION ATTEMPTS 

Beyond measuring stance drift , we ask whether straightforward interventions can restore fidelity in AI-mediated communication. Throughout, the default configuration denotes a run with the de-fault prompts (Appendix A.3). We tested four targeted strategies with GPT-4o-mini (full de-5Preprint tails in the appendix), each addressing a distinct hypothesized failure mode. (i) Multiple extrac-tion addresses positional bias , the documented tendency of LLMs to favor options at specific posi-tions (Pezeshkpour & Hruschka, 2024; Zheng et al., 2024): by running extraction five times with randomly shuffled option orderings and averaging the resulting probability distributions, we sought to reduce systematic position effects. (ii) In-context learning supplies three high-quality examples during generation and extraction, aiming to anchor model behavior through demonstration. (iii) As-sertion passes the literal stance options to the extraction prompt and asks the model to choose one of them first and then map it to a letter, with the aim of directing the model’s attention to the stance labels. (iv) Reflection adds one instruction to each of the two prompts: Agent 1 is asked to compare its drafted argument against the specified stance and to revise it if the two do not align, and Agent 2 is asked to compare its selected option against the argument and to revise the choice likewise, with the aim of catching drift before the output is returned.

We consider stance preservation statistically indistinguishable from perfect fidelity if the CI includes 1. Because we perform this test across 112 propositions for each initial stance, we apply Bonferroni correction to widen each CI to account for multiple comparisons and report the proportion of propositions whose CIs include 1. We conducted ablation analyses using GPT-4o-mini (default configuration, SPR = 0 .570 ) to assess potential confounds in the generation-extraction pipeline (Table S1, Fig. S4). First, to test whether the temperature (the parameter that controls randomness when inferring the next token) of the extraction stage drives the low SPR, we re-analyzed the raw results with each trial’s extrac-tion vector replaced by a point mass on its most probable option, which is equivalent to setting the temperature to 0 during extraction. This re-analysis yielded an SPR of 0.571 , indicating no mean-ingful change. Second, to examine positional and formatting biases, we reversed the choice order (negative-to-positive), obtaining a comparable SPR of 0.584 . We further implemented the multiple extraction protocol (Section 2.5) to mitigate potential positional bias; this procedure likewise pro-duced no substantive improvement (SPR = 0 .569 ). Third, we evaluated prompt-level mitigation strategies. In-context learning resulted in an SPR of 0.573 , assertion yielded 0.559 , and reflection yielded 0.586 , none differing meaningfully from the default configuration. Reflection is the one strategy we also tested on GPT-5.4 , in three configurations that each add one change to the previous one: reflection; third-person reflection, which phrases the instruction from the perspective of an impartial third-party reviewer; and third-person reflection with medium reasoning effort (full details in the appendix). The SPR rises from 0.593 to 0.613 , 0.637 and 0.775 ,and polarization falls at every step, from 0.328 to 0.119 (Fig. S5 and Table S5 in the appendix). The two reflection steps move part of the drift of the initial stance “Agree” from polarization into flipping (from 0.076 to 0.252 ). The reasoning step removes most of that flipping ( 0.010 ) and gives the largest gain, but lowers the success rate for “Neutral” from 0.721 to 0.607 . Even in this last configuration, about a quarter of the transition mass still drifts and polarization remains the largest pattern. 

Stance drift is therefore not fully explained by the prompting and decoding factors tested here. 3.5 AN EXPLORATORY HUMAN -EXTRACTION STUDY LOCATES DRIFT AT BOTH STAGES 

The ablation analyses above treat the generation-extraction pipeline as a whole. To probe whether drift originates in generation, extraction, or both, we conducted a targeted human-extraction study (Appendix A.7) on the proposition “The use of AI should be abandoned” using 100 arguments gen-erated by GPT-5.4 (20 per initial stance). Nine human annotators, working without AI assistance, each labeled all 100 arguments via a structured survey; their majority vote served as the reference label throughout. Comparing the human-majority against the known initial stance reveals generation-stage drift; com-paring the AI-majority against the human-majority reveals extraction-stage drift. The results reveal that both stages contribute to stance drift yet show a systematic pattern by initial stance (Table S11; one example argument per drift pattern in Table S12). For “Agree”, flipping to “Disagree” was al-ready present across all 20 generated arguments, implicating generation-stage drift. For “Disagree”, polarization toward “Disagree strongly” was absent from human-majority labels but appeared con-sistently in AI extraction, implicating the extraction stage. Deviation from neutrality showed a mixed pattern: 5 of 20 “Neutral”-initiated arguments were already non-neutral (generation-stage drift), and 13 deviated at AI extraction. 8Preprint To quantify the human–AI performance gap at the extraction stage, we computed, for each individ-ual trial (each single human annotation and each single AI extraction vote), the agreement with the human-majority label. This comparison serves to validate both the task design and the feasibility of high-fidelity extraction. Note that this validation is non-trivial: even under the majority-vote refer-ence, the minimum expected per-annotator agreement is bounded below by 2/9 ≈ 0.222 . Human annotators achieved 0.743 (SE 0.038 ), well above this floor, confirming that the arguments carry recoverable stance signals and that the task admits substantially better-than-chance extraction. The AI shortfall, GPT-5.4 at 0.472 (SE 0.002 ), is therefore consistent with an extraction deficit and not accounted for by ambiguity in the arguments. Given the intensive demands of unaided human label-ing, this study is limited to a single proposition and a small annotator pool; online deployment on platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk would risk contamination from AI-assisted responses, undermining the human-only baseline. It is intended as a proof-of-concept decomposition and hu-man benchmark rather than a comprehensive measurement. 

4 IMPLICATIONS AND OPEN QUESTIONS 

4.1 SOURCES OF STANCE DRIFT 

Stance drift , the failure of LLMs to preserve original positions, emerged consistently across the models and propositions examined here and was only partly alleviated by the decoding, format and prompt changes we tested (Section 3.4). These results motivate treating stance fidelity as a distinct evaluation target in AI-mediated communication. Our conclusion is descriptive: it characterizes stance drift on the tested dataset and does not isolate a causal source. As an initial step toward understanding potential sources of drift, inspection of intermediate gen-erated arguments suggests several recurring features. First, models sometimes mishandle negation-heavy reasoning (e.g., cases in which a proposition containing negated statement is discussed in a negative stance), leading to flipping during either generation or extraction. Second, models may conflate a cautiously phrased supporting argument—written to acknowledge counterarguments or avoid overstatement—with a genuinely opposing stance. We also observed that propositions con-taining terms such as “ban” frequently coincided with flipping or misclassification. The structure of the drift patterns (Table 2) suggests that future work should examine whether different patterns are associated with distinct sources, such as training data biases and architectural limitations in main-taining semantic invariants through paraphrase. These observations are qualitative and specific to the policy-oriented, sometimes negation-heavy propositions of the debate speeches dataset. 4.2 STANCE PRESERVATION AS A BENCHMARK FOR AI-MEDIATED COMMUNICATION 

As for potential future work, although the mitigation strategies tested here yielded limited gains, the framework points to a practical optimization target: stance preservation itself. In particular, SPR measured on datasets such as the debate speeches dataset, could be used as a reward in reinforcement learning to encourage LLMs to maintain stance across generations. More broadly, the generation-extraction framework can be extended beyond stance to subtler attributes such as tone, framing, and contextual emphasis. A particularly promising direction is multi-hop communication chains, where messages pass through multiple LLMs or human–AI hybrids: modelled as Markov chains, they would show whether drift accumulates over successive transmissions and whether stationary distributions mark stable distortion endpoints. For LLM development, SPR provides a communication-fidelity benchmark that complements tra-ditional accuracy-oriented metrics by testing whether models preserve intended positions, not only whether they produce plausible text. For social-science and opinion-sensitive research settings, our results raise caution about using current AI agents as substitutes (Qin et al., 2024) for human sub-jects when faithful preservation of stated positions is essential. In domains such as journalism, public policy, scientific research, education, and everyday communication, reliance on LLMs for drafting, summarizing, or mediating messages may inadvertently amplify, shift, or distort stances, affecting decision-making, fairness, and public perception (Acerbi & Stubbersfield, 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Kelly et al., 2025). 9Preprint ETHICS STATEMENT

We selected proposition “The use of AI should be abandoned” and drew 100 arguments from the 

GPT-5.4 generation results used in the experiments (first 20 arguments of the whole 100 repeti-tions per initial stance). Arguments were independently shuffled for each participant via a Qualtrics survey. Nine annotators each read all 100 arguments and chose one of the five Likert stance labels to each argument independently, without access to any AI assistance. Survey construction was per-formed solely by L.L., who was isolated from all annotation activities. The nine annotators were not involved in survey construction and were therefore unaware of the initial stances assigned during generation, or the structure of the argument groupings. The study was conducted under researcher supervision rather than deployed on a crowd sourcing platform. The majority vote (plurality with a deterministic A →E tie-break) over the nine annotators served as the human-consensus reference label for each argument. A tie occurred in 1 of the 100 arguments. For each of the 100 arguments, GPT-5.4 extraction was stabilized using nine independent extrac-tion votes per argument. All nine votes use the extraction prompt of the default configuration and the original order of the options. The majority AI label was determined from the resulting vote shares. Generation-stage drift for a given argument was identified when the human-majority label differed from the known initial stance. Extraction-stage drift was identified when the AI-majority label dif-fered from the human-majority label, attributing the discrepancy to the LLM extractor rather than the generated content. One example argument per drift pattern is given in Table S12. 17 Preprint Scaling this design faces a structural constraint: unaided annotation of 100 arguments per proposi-tion is time-intensive, requiring approximately 4 hours of sustained effort per annotator. A.8 DETAILS OF MULTIPLE EXTRACTION 

As an attempt to mitigate potential positional biases in the LLM’s responses, we implemented a multiple extraction strategy. This approach involves running the extraction stage five times for each generated argument, with the order of answer choices randomly shuffled in each run, for example: 

A) Neutral B) Disagree C) Disagree strongly D) Agree strongly E) Agree 

After obtaining the five sets of outputs, we aggregate the results by averaging the predicted proba-bilities for each answer choice across all runs. By doing so, we aim to neutralize any influence that the position of an answer choice may have on the model’s selection. A.9 DETAILS OF IN -CONTEXT LEARNING

30 Preprint B.1 POSITIONAL BIAS 

As pointed out in previous studies, the order in which answer choices are presented can signifi-cantly influence respondents’ selections, a phenomenon known as positional bias (Pezeshkpour & Hruschka, 2024; Zheng et al., 2024). However, it is hard to remove positional bias due to iden-tifiability issues and it is not practical to iterate over all possible permutations of answer choices. Besides randomly shuffling the order of answer choices in the multiple extraction strategy we imple-mented, we could take another perspective, following the most existing surveys and questionnaires and ordering answer choices from positive to negative (e.g., Agree strongly, Agree, Neutral, Dis-agree, Disagree strongly) (Johns, 2010), treating positional bias of such order as effect from the communication practices in the real world. For robustness, we also conduct experiments with the reversed order of answer choices to examine the impact of positional bias on LLMs’ responses. Although we could observe the effect of posi-tional bias by comparing the results in Table S1, we find that the SPR of 0.584 (Fig. S4) remains relatively stable compared to the original order setting ( 0.570 ). B.2 TEMPERATURE 

One might argue that the failure of recovering the initial stance is due to the randomness introduced by the temperature setting during extraction. A higher temperature allows for more diverse outputs, which could lead to higher chances of deviating from the initial stance. To address this concern, we conduct additional analysis of our raw data results by adjusting the probability of the most probable answer to 1, and the rest to 0, which is equivalent to setting the temperature to 0 during extraction. Yet the SPR remains at 0.571 (Fig. S4), indicating that the randomness introduced by temperature is not a significant factor in the stance preservation performance of LLMs. B.3 PASS RATE AND AUC We define the pass rate at a threshold: a proposition–initial stance combination passes the threshold if its success rate, the diagonal probability averaged over its trials, exceeds the threshold, and the pass rate is the proportion of combinations that pass.
