Abstract 

Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly operate in private environ-ments and accumulate valuable experience from task execution, tool use, feedback, and local knowledge. Yet such experience is distributed across organizations and cannot be directly shared because of privacy and proprietary constraints. Conven-tional federated learning is insufficient for this setting, as agent capabilities extend beyond model parameters to memory, tools, rewards, skills, and structured knowl-edge. In this paper, we formulate Federated Agent Optimization (FAO) ,which studies how distributed agents can collaboratively improve through con-trolled information exchange while keeping raw data, complete trajectories, and private knowledge local. We define FAO as a multi-objective problem balancing agent utility, privacy leakage, and communication cost, and organize its optimiza-tion space across policy, memory, tool use, reward, and structured knowledge and skills. We further characterize how private experience can be abstracted, protected, aggregated, and adapted into transferable capabilities, providing a unified view of how agents can benefit from one another without direct experience sharing. Finally, we identify the key challenges of FAO and outline several promising directions for future research toward trustworthy federated agent systems. 

# 1. Introduction 

In recent years, large language model (LLM)-driven agents [60, 130, 182] have grad-ually evolved from general-purpose conversational systems [5] toward executing com-plex tasks in real-world environments, becoming an important paradigm for enterprise AI systems [207]. An increasing number of organizations are deploying agents in ap-plications such as customer service [178, 58, 66], software development [146, 19], data analysis [72, 155], knowledge management [177, 197, 160], office automation [191], fi-nancial services [158], healthcare assistance [142, 125, 79, 97], and business process ex-ecution [101, 81, 65, 40]. Unlike traditional language models that primarily focus on content generation [102], enterprise agents [135] typically need to integrate internal knowl-edge [78], business data, proprietary tools, and operational workflows to autonomously 1arXiv:2610.01195v1 [cs.AI] 1 Oct 2026 perform task planning [143, 190], tool invocation [36], environment interaction [13, 28], and outcome evaluation [248]. As their applications continue to expand, agents are evolv-ing from standalone language generation models [45] into complex intelligent systems [236] that can perceive their environments, take actions, and continuously adapt to changing business requirements. During continuous task execution, enterprise agents generate large volumes of new workflows, execution trajectories [12, 63], tool-use records [188, 46], and success or failure feedback on a daily basis [141]. These real-world interaction experiences can help agents continuously refine task planning [143, 190], tool utilization, and decision-making strate-gies [159], thereby improving their practical task performance over time. Meanwhile, as agents are deployed across different organizations [42, 249], departments, and user environments, valuable experience becomes increasingly distributed across isolated local systems. Different agents often face similar or related tasks, and their accumulated suc-cessful experiences, failure patterns, and tool-use strategies are naturally complementary, creating substantial potential for mutual improvement [105]. However, such experience is often tightly coupled with user data, internal knowledge, business rules, proprietary tools, and operational workflows, making it difficult to directly share raw data or com-plete execution trajectories [37]. As a result, large amounts of potentially reusable agent experience remain confined to local environments, limiting effective collaboration among agents and preventing the full value of cross-environment experience from being realized. Federated learning [196, 103, 226] provides a natural technical foundation for miti-gating the experience isolation described above. It enables multiple clients [55] to col-laboratively learn by exchanging and aggregating model updates while keeping raw data local [41], thereby avoiding the direct centralization of sensitive information. However, traditional federated learning primarily focuses on model parameters, whereas the capa-bilities of enterprise agents are determined not only by the underlying model but also by their decision policies, memories, tool use, environmental feedback, and local knowledge and business rules. Therefore, in agent settings [7], parameter aggregation alone is in-sufficient to enable effective cross-agent collaboration. A more fundamental question is how to transform the useful experience, skills, and feedback acquired by one agent dur-ing real-world execution into shareable capabilities that can benefit other agents without exposing local private information. Existing federated learning paradigms have not yet systematically addressed this problem and still lack a unified optimization framework for the broader capabilities of agents. We refer to this class of problems as Federated Agent Optimization (FAO) .FAO studies how multiple agents operating in different private environments can col-laboratively improve through controlled information exchange while keeping raw data, complete interaction trajectories, and local private knowledge decentralized. Unlike con-ventional federated learning, which primarily optimizes model parameters, FAO considers the broader capabilities of an agent, including its policy, memory, tool use, reward mecha-nism, and structured knowledge. We formulate FAO around three interrelated objectives: improving local agent utility, reducing privacy leakage during collaboration, and control-ling communication and system costs. In addition, effective federation should provide meaningful collaboration gains to individual participants rather than improving only ag-gregate performance. In this paper, we establish a unified framework for studying FAO. First, we formally define local agents, private environments, federated protocols, information-sharing bound-aries, and a multi-objective optimization formulation that jointly captures performance, 2privacy, and communication cost. Second, we organize existing and emerging approaches according to the agent capability being federated, including policies, memory, tool use, rewards, and structured knowledge and skills. Third, we characterize how private local experience can be transformed into transferable capabilities through abstraction, protec-tion, aggregation, and local adaptation, and further examine when such transfer leads to mutual improvement across agents. Building on this framework, we identify key chal-lenges in balancing performance, privacy, and communication, ensuring fair contribution and benefit allocation, and reliably evaluating federation gains. Finally, we highlight sev-eral open research directions toward trustworthy, efficient, and continuously improving federated agent systems.

Section 4 presents a taxonomy of FAO according to the agent com-ponents being collaboratively optimized. Section 5 further investigates how local expe-rience can be transformed into transferable capabilities. Section 6 summarizes the key challenges of FAO. Finally, Section 7 discusses future works. 

# 2. Related Work 

Federated Learning and Federated Large Models Federated learning [196, 103, 226] enables multiple clients [55] to collaboratively train models while keeping raw data lo-cal, providing a general paradigm for privacy-preserving distributed learning [80]. Early studies [145] mainly focused on model aggregation under heterogeneous data distribu-tions, while subsequent work further explored communication efficiency [33], personaliza-tion [173], robustness [76, 107], and privacy protection [195, 106]. With the emergence of foundation models and large language models [241, 126], federated learning has been extended to large-scale model adaptation [93], where parameter-efficient techniques such as federated fine-tuning [193, 198], LoRA [171, 14], prompt tuning [239, 27], and knowl-edge distillation [162] are increasingly adopted to reduce computation and communication costs. Despite these advances, existing federated large-model methods still primarily treat model parameters or representations as the main optimization objects. In contrast, an agent [60] typically involves not only an underlying model, but also memory, tool use, rewards, skills, and external knowledge. Therefore, federating a large model is not equiv-alent to federating an agent, motivating a broader optimization paradigm that considers the complete capabilities of agents. 

Multi-Agent Systems and Agent Reinforcement learning Multi-agent systems [69, 221, 98] study how multiple agents coordinate, communicate, divide tasks, and make joint decisions in shared or interacting environments. With the rise of large language models, recent LLM-based multi-agent systems [69, 210, 34] have further explored role specializa-tion, discussion, negotiation, and mutual feedback to improve performance on complex tasks. In parallel, agent learning focuses on continuously improving agent capabilities through execution trajectories [176], environmental feedback [219], reinforcement learn-ing [245, 227], self-reflection [17], and teacher-agent guidance [215]. Despite this progress, most existing multi-agent and agent-learning methods assume that agents can directly ex-change messages, trajectories, or feedback during collaboration, while privacy constraints 3and data isolation across organizations or private environments are rarely considered. This differs fundamentally from federated agent optimization, where agents are expected to benefit from each other while keeping local data, execution experience, and private knowledge decentralized. 

Privacy-Preserving AI Privacy-preserving AI [131] aims to reduce the risk of sensi-tive information leakage while enabling effective model training and inference. Common techniques include differential privacy [6], secure aggregation [118], homomorphic encryp-tion [87], secure multi-party computation [161], and access control [83]. These techniques have been widely adopted in distributed learning [32, 22], cloud-based inference [62], and cross-organization collaboration [26, 132] to protect training data, model updates, and intermediate representations. However, existing privacy-preserving methods mainly focus on data, model parameters, or inference processes. In agent systems, sensitive in-formation may also be embedded in execution trajectories, memories, tool interactions, business rules, and skill representations, which calls for new protection mechanisms tai-lored to collaborative agent optimization.

The mechanisms above illustrate how local experience can be transformed into transfer-able capabilities and ultimately improve other agents. However, they also expose three fundamental challenges that distinguish FAO from conventional federated learning. First, performance, privacy, and communication are inherently coupled rather than indepen-dently optimizable objectives. Second, heterogeneous participants may contribute and benefit unevenly, making fairness central to sustained collaboration. Third, the gains of federation are difficult to verify because agent outcomes are long-horizon, environment-dependent, and not yet supported by dedicated evaluation protocols. These challenges ex-tend the statistical and system heterogeneity already studied in federated learning [86, 96] from model optimization to the broader problem of collaborative agent improvement. 

6.1 Performance, Privacy and Communication Trade-offs 

The three objectives of FAO—task performance, privacy protection, and communication efficiency—form a tightly coupled trade-off. Improving performance generally requires sharing information that preserves sufficient detail about local experience, yet richer up-dates can increase both privacy exposure and communication overhead. Data locality alone does not eliminate this risk: gradients and model updates can reveal informa-tion about local training examples [54, 129], while memories, skill descriptions, tool-use knowledge, and structured artifacts may disclose sensitive content more directly. Stronger protection, such as differential privacy, reduces disclosure but can also degrade the infor-mation available for optimization [1]. Conversely, aggressive compression can substan-tially reduce communication traffic [112], but may remove information that is important for capability transfer. Secure aggregation protects individual updates from direct in-spection [18], yet this can further constrain validation of what is being shared. Client heterogeneity additionally changes the utility of the same update across participants and can induce objective inconsistency in global optimization [181]. The central challenge is therefore not to minimize any single cost in isolation, but to identify operating points at which useful capability transfer remains possible under explicit privacy and commu-nication budgets. This makes the Pareto trade-off defined in Section 3 a central systems question for practical FAO. 

6.2 How to Ensure Fairness in Federated Agent Systems? 

A second challenge is ensuring that the benefits and burdens of federation are distributed fairly across heterogeneous participants. Improvements in federation-wide utility may co-exist with substantially different outcomes for individual clients [108, 127]. Some agents may contribute highly valuable experience but receive limited benefit in return, whereas others may benefit primarily from knowledge produced elsewhere. At the same time, globally shared capabilities can induce negative transfer when local objectives, task dis-tributions, tools, or operating constraints differ. Personalized federated learning provides mechanisms for retaining local specialization under shared optimization [107, 172], but FAO requires a broader notion of fairness that also accounts for non-parametric artifacts such as memories, skills, rewards, and structured knowledge. Contribution estimation is itself difficult because task outcomes result from long sequences of decisions and feed-back may be sparse or delayed [9]; attributing recipient-side gains to particular shared contributions introduces an additional layer of credit assignment [68]. The collaboration 12 gain and pairwise contribution defined in Section 5 therefore provide important starting points, but a complete fairness framework must jointly consider what each participant contributes, what benefit it receives, and what privacy, computation, and communication costs it bears. Trustworthy participation ultimately requires that clients retain the ability to personalize, selectively adopt, or reject shared capabilities whose local value does not justify their associated cost or risk. 

6.3 How to Evaluate Federated Agent Systems? 

A third challenge is establishing credible evidence that federated collaboration actually improves agent capability. Agent performance is inherently difficult to verify because success depends on interactions among reasoning, memory retrieval, tool execution, and environmental conditions. Tool-use research shows that apparently similar tools and interfaces can require substantially different execution behavior, while reasoning and environment-facing actions are tightly coupled in interactive agents [214]. Consequently, a shared artifact that appears semantically valid may still fail after transfer to a re-cipient environment. Verification becomes even harder over time: continual optimiza-tion can introduce stale capabilities, distribution drift, and interference with previously acquired knowledge [218, 233]. Erroneous or adversarial contributions further compli-cate the distinction between genuine capability improvement and harmful shared up-dates [217]. Existing benchmarks evaluate general agent competence, web and desktop interaction, or tool–user workflows [114, 244, 205, 213], while federated benchmarks such as FedScale primarily characterize model and system heterogeneity [95]. FAO requires evaluation protocols that combine these perspectives: controlled client heterogeneity, ex-plicit information-sharing boundaries, repeated collaborative optimization, and credible local baselines. Beyond federation-wide task utility, evaluation should report privacy leakage, communication and system costs, client-level collaboration gains, contribution fairness, negative-transfer rates, and robustness under task or participant drift. Without such benchmarks and metrics, it remains difficult to determine when federation truly creates transferable intelligence rather than merely improving an aggregate score. 

# 7. Future Works 

A central direction for future FAO research is to build trustworthy federated agent systems , where trustworthiness is treated as an end-to-end property of collaborative optimization rather than a single notion of model accuracy or privacy. We highlight several important research questions. 

❶ How can transferred capabilities remain reliable and executable across heterogeneous agent environments? A capability that is effective for one agent may rely on specific tools, interfaces, permissions, task distributions, or workflow contexts that do not hold for another agent. Prior work on grounded planning, tool use, and multi-agent workflows highlights the importance of environment-aware execution and capability composition [77, 67, 104, 73]. Future FAO systems should therefore explicitly model the applicability conditions, dependencies, and execution constraints of transferred capabilities, rather than assuming that capabilities remain valid after cross-agent transfer. 

❷ How can federated agent systems protect privacy while remaining secure against unreliable or malicious contributions? Keeping raw data local does not eliminate leakage from model parameters, memories, textual abstractions, tool knowledge, or structured 13 representations. Differential privacy and secure aggregation provide useful foundations for limiting such leakage [1, 18], while robust aggregation is necessary to tolerate faulty or adversarial participants [15, 21]. Future research should jointly consider confidentiality, update integrity, and the tension between protecting shared information and validating its quality. 

❸ How can fair contribution and benefit allocation be achieved among heterogeneous agents? Different agents may contribute unequal amounts of useful experience, receive different levels of benefit, or even suffer negative transfer. Personalized and fairness-aware federated learning provide useful foundations for preserving local specialization and participant-level utility [49, 82, 156, 111]. Future FAO research should further quantify contribution, collaboration gain, and participation cost, and ensure that agents receive benefits commensurate with the information, privacy, and resources they contribute. 

❹ How should federated agent systems be evaluated? Reliable evaluation should verify whether collaboration genuinely improves local agents compared with credible local base-lines, rather than relying only on shared evaluators or aggregate performance. This is particularly challenging when feedback is sparse, trajectories are long, and environments are stochastic [39, 133, 154, 120]. Existing agent and federated benchmarks provide useful starting points [114, 244, 205, 213, 95], but FAO additionally requires explicit information-sharing boundaries, heterogeneous participants, repeated collaboration, and longitudinal evaluation of recipient-level gains, negative transfer, and robustness under drift. 

❺ How should the full lifecycle cost of federated agent collaboration be measured and optimized? Communication volume alone is insufficient to characterize the cost of FAO. Collaboration may also incur substantial costs for local adaptation, privacy protection, validation, inference, tool execution, storage, synchronization, and continual mainte-nance, especially under heterogeneous computation and model capacities [4, 123, 43]. Future work should therefore develop lifecycle-level cost models that jointly account for the resources required to produce, protect, aggregate, validate, deploy, and maintain shared capabilities. Taken together, these questions suggest that trustworthy FAO should jointly consider execution reliability, privacy and security, fairness, credible evaluation, and lifecycle cost, providing a principled basis for collaborative learning among autonomous agents across organizations. 

# 8. Conclusion 

This paper formulates Federated Agent Optimization (FAO) as a general framework for collaborative improvement across policies, memories, tool use, rewards, and structured knowledge under distributed data and local constraints. By extending federated opti-mization beyond model parameters, we establish a multi-objective problem formulation, a taxonomy of federated agent components, and an improvement framework that con-nects local experience to transferable capabilities and recipient-side gains. Our analysis highlights three central challenges—balancing performance, privacy, and communication cost, ensuring fair contribution and benefit allocation, and reliably verifying the gains of federation—which together motivate the broader goal of building trustworthy FAO systems with reliable execution, privacy and security protection, credible evaluation, and principled cost accounting. We envision FAO as a foundation for scalable, trustworthy, and continuously evolving enterprise AI systems that benefit from collective experience 14 while preserving local specialization and organizational control.

# B. Methods that Federate a Component of an Agent 

This appendix extends Section 4. Table 4 lists the agent-level methods and selected precursors, and the following subsections describe each category together with the work on single agents on which it builds. 

B.1 Federated Policy Optimization 

The policy block ∆ πk carries parameters of the policy. Its form depends on how much access the clients have to the backbone. 

Weights. When the backbone is open, clients fine-tune it on local data with parameter-efficient methods and the coordinator aggregates the updates [229, 216, 235, 203]. Later work addresses clients with different adapter ranks [193, 38, 11] and the instability of adapters under differential privacy noise [171]. Personalized variants keep part of the adapter local [211, 116, 59]. At the other extreme of communication cost, zeroth-order optimization lets clients and server exchange only random seeds and scalar gradients [151]. The same recipe has been applied to agents. GUI agents based on vision-language models are trained on decentralized user data [186, 187, 185]. Fed-SE fine-tunes adapters on the successful trajectories of each client [35]. FedAgent trains the policy by reinforce-ment learning on local trajectories and averages the parameters [29]. It finds the result robust to heterogeneity of tasks but not to heterogeneity of environment dynamics. This agrees with the theory of federated reinforcement learning [144, 50, 89], in which the sub-optimality of the learned policy grows with the heterogeneity of the environments [84]. FedWave federates the role-specific adapters of a workflow whose stages belong to dif-ferent organizations [240]. Frameworks that separate the execution of an agent from its training make such methods applicable to existing agents [117]. 37 Prompts. When the backbone is a frozen model behind an API, the policy is opti-mized through its prompts. Clients can share continuous prompts [61], optimize prompts without gradients by querying the model [170, 113], or share textual prompts that the coordinator merges by summarization [30]. The last option connects FAO to the opti-mization of prompts and agentic workflows [208, 88, 222, 3], where the variables are text and the optimizer is often itself a language model. 

Trade-offs. Weight sharing is the most mature form and the most expensive in com-munication: Fed-SE reports 76.3 MB per round for adapters of rank 8 [35]. Its leakage is well studied. Gradients can be inverted to recover training text, for recent attacks exactly and for large batches [246, 64, 139], and restricting the update to adapters does not remove the risk [163]. Secure aggregation and differentially private training are the standard defenses [18, 1]. Prompt sharing has a small payload and works with closed backbones, but a textual prompt is readable by anyone who receives it and may quote the examples from which it was derived. 

B.2 Federated Memory Optimization 

The memory block ∆ M 

> k

carries experience in abstracted form. Raw trajectories are the most informative content of memory and the most sensitive, so they stay local. What can be shared are the products that agents already derive from trajectories for their own use: reflections on failures [167], insights extracted from successes and failures [237], workflows induced from repeated tasks [194], and reasoning strategies [134]. Several systems let agents share such items through a common store: a pool of prompt–answer pairs [53], a hierarchical memory for the agents of one system [228], and a knowledge base of workflows and fixes built from the trajectories of different agent frameworks [174]. Access control over memory that is shared among users has also been studied [157]. These systems assume that the store is trusted. Federated methods avoid centralizing raw trajectories in such a store. In FoT, each client uploads summaries of its reasoning traces, without the problem instances or task instructions, and the server distills them into a library of insights that all clients can use [212]. FedAgentKE exchanges knowledge units that contain reusable workflows and corrections together with a utility score, and each client retrieves the units relevant to its task [109]. For document collections, federated retrieval-augmented generation [24] answers queries over the collections of several providers without centralizing the docu-ments, protected by confidential computing [2] or homomorphic encryption [238], and it trains the retrievers of the clients by federated learning [121]. 

Aggregation. Memory items are text, so they cannot be averaged. The coordinator clusters the items, removes duplicates, merges items that express the same pattern, and ranks the result by utility or by the number of clients that support it. A language model performs these operations, which makes the coordinator an agent in its own right. 

Trade-offs. The payload is small: in one setting of FoT, the traces of all clients amount to 33K tokens and the distilled library to 3K tokens [212]. The leakage is of a different kind than for weights. An item written in natural language may quote private content, retrieval stores and agent memories can be extracted by crafted queries [223, 179], and embeddings can be inverted [128]. Protection therefore has to act on the text. FoT 38 audits its uploads with a reconstruction attack [212]. Differentially private generation of demonstrations [175, 202] and of synthetic text [204] would add a formal guarantee, but to our knowledge it has not yet been applied to the memory of agents. 

B.3 Federated Tool-Use Optimization 

The tool block ∆ T 

> k

carries knowledge about how to use tools. In the horizontal setting the clients use tools of the same kind, such as customer databases, payment interfaces, or document recognition, and standard interfaces such as the Model Context Protocol [8] make the descriptions of tools comparable across clients. Research on single agents shows what this knowledge consists of [149, 153]. Models learn to call tools from examples of correct calls [165, 137, 150]. Concise and consistent descriptions improve the use of a tool [220], and a description can be refined from the experience of calling the tool [152]. Agents can also create new tools [20, 147, 189]. Two methods federate such knowledge. In FICAL, each client writes a compendium that describes its tools, the scenarios in which to use them, precautions, and the coordi-nation of several tools; the server concatenates the compendiums, and the agents retrieve from the result [200]. Typed federated artifacts replace free text by schema-validated objects with separate fields for privacy handling, conflict resolution, and transfer across models, and they are designed for frozen models of different vendors [25]. Usage statistics, such as the success rate of a tool for a given intent, are a third kind of tool knowledge. Only their sum is needed, so they can be protected by secure aggregation. 

Trade-offs. Tool knowledge in text is small compared with model weights: FICAL re-ports 0.078 MB against 26,000 MB for baselines that exchange parameters [200]. It is not small by nature, however, and a compendium of tool-routing experience can reach 20 MB per client and round [25]. The leakage concerns the systems of the client more than its data: tool schemas disclose the structure of internal systems, and the arguments of recorded calls may contain customer records. Shared tool knowledge is also an at-tack surface. A tool description is an instruction to the agent that reads it, and threats from malicious tool providers are documented for the Model Context Protocol [74]. Ag-gregation of tool knowledge therefore needs robustness against malicious contributions in addition to privacy. Evaluation is a further open issue: the study of typed artifacts reports that a simple retrieval baseline outperforms all of its routing variants based on language models, and concludes that the benchmark cannot measure the benefit of shared routing knowledge [25]. 

B.4 Federated Reward Optimization 

The reward block ∆ R 

> k

carries knowledge about how to judge a trajectory. The problem has a natural division of labor. Clients observe real outcomes and human judgments, but each client has few of them. The coordinator can run a strong evaluator, but language models used as judges show systematic biases [243], and on the trajectories of web agents no single judge is reliable across benchmarks [115]. 

Federated preference and reward models. Existing methods address the alignment of language models with human preferences [39]. In FedRLHF, the feedback and the reward function stay with the client, and the policies are federated [51]. FedBis and 39 FedBiscuit federate the preference model itself: each client encodes its preferences in a binary selector, and the selectors are aggregated [199]. Reward models and preference predictors are also trained by federated averaging and then personalized by local fine-tuning [168, 90], and a federation of client models can serve as a panel of referees [70]. To our knowledge, no method yet federates an evaluator of agent trajectories. 

Building blocks for agents. Three lines of work on single agents provide the pieces. Evaluators can be trained or prompted to judge the success of web navigation and device control [136]. Since feedback arrives at the end of a trajectory, credit must be assigned to individual steps [140]; process reward models, which score each step, serve this pur-pose [110, 23]. Language models can write reward functions from a description of the task [94, 119, 206]. The last line suggests a form of federation with very low cost: a lan-guage model at the coordinator proposes a reward function or a rubric, each client scores the proposal against its private outcomes, and only the scores return to the coordinator. 

Trade-offs. The utilities uk differ across clients, so a single global evaluator may be wrong for every client. The methods above respond by grouping clients with similar preferences [199] or by learning an initialization that adapts quickly to each client [90]. The main leakage channel is the label: a preference or an outcome label reveals how an individual case ended. 

B.5 Federated Structured Knowledge and Skill Optimization 

The block ∆ O 

> k

carries structured knowledge: ontologies and knowledge graphs, rules on the business and the environment, and skills. 

Ontologies. Federated learning of knowledge graph embeddings lets clients with over-lapping entities improve their embeddings without exchanging triples. In FedE, clients upload entity embeddings and the server averages them [31]. Entity embeddings, however, allow private triples to be reconstructed, and FedR therefore aggregates only relation embeddings, under secure aggregation, which lowers both leakage and communication cost [230]. FKGE needs no server: pairs of clients translate the embeddings of aligned entities and relations into each other’s space through an adversarial network with a differ-ential privacy guarantee [138]. FedLU keeps the local embeddings apart from the global ones and transfers knowledge between them by distillation [247]. These methods assume that the entities and relations of the clients have been aligned beforehand. Ontology matching is a classical problem [48]. Language models and agents now reach the level of established matching systems on standard benchmarks and improve on them in complex cases [71, 10, 148], and the combination of ontologies and language models has been used to harmonize data before federated learning [91]. 

Rules. FedWorld federates what agents learn about the dynamics of their environment. Each client converts its private transitions into abstract rules. The server aligns the rules of different clients, collects the evidence that supports and contradicts each rule, and labels the rule as shared, specific to a cluster of clients, private, or unresolved [75]. 40 Skills. A skill packages a procedure so that an agent can load it when needed [180, 242, 225]. In FederatedSkill, clients upload patches to their skill libraries, from which task-specific values are omitted, and an agent at the server merges and rewrites the patches into a library for each client [209]. 

Trade-offs. An aligned ontology helps the other categories, since insights, tool descrip-tions, and rubrics all refer to business concepts. It is also expensive to build and changes slowly, so its communication cost is incurred mostly once. Skills are cheap to exchange: FederatedSkill reports between 99 and 281 KB for a family of tasks over a complete run [209]. The leakage concerns existence and process: the entity embeddings of a client reveal which entities it knows and can expose the relations among them [230], and a skill reveals how a client conducts its business. 

B.6 Comparison 

The five categories occupy different regions of the objective space of Eq. (10). 

Communication. The reported payloads differ by roughly three orders of magnitude, from tens of megabytes per round for adapters to a few hundred kilobytes per run for skills, although the studies report them at different granularities. The order of the categories is not fixed. Accumulated text can grow large, and parameter updates can be compressed to a few kilobytes [151]. 

Privacy. Weight sharing has the best understood attacks and defenses. The methods that exchange text or rules [200, 212, 109, 209, 75] rely on the fact that raw trajectories stay local, and none of them applies a formal privacy mechanism. Two of them support the argument by an empirical audit with reconstruction attacks [212, 209]. The privacy problem thus shifts from the inversion of numerical updates to the disclosure of content in text, for which formal tools are less developed. 

Access to the backbone. Weight sharing requires open weights and a common ar-chitecture. The sharing of prompts, memory items, tool knowledge, rubrics, rules, and skills remains possible when every client uses a different closed model. 

Maturity. Federated fine-tuning and federated knowledge graph embedding are estab-lished. For rewards, methods exist for the alignment of language models but not yet for agent trajectories. For memory, tool use, rules, and skills, the first federated methods have appeared, most of them as preprints. Each of these methods optimizes performance for one component and treats privacy and communication as properties of its design. To our knowledge, none formulates them as objectives to be traded off against performance, across components, as Eq. (10) does. 

# C. Knowledge Types in the Running Example 

This appendix extends Section 5.2. We use one running example. Several banks deploy agents for customer identity verification. Each agent talks to applicants, calls tools for document recognition and registry lookup, and decides whether an application passes, 41 fails, or is escalated to a human reviewer. Customer records and conversation logs cannot leave the bank. 

C.1 The Four Types in the Running Example Experience. The sender shares what it has learned from successes and failures, stated as a pattern and not as a case. In the running example, one bank observes that appli-cations fail at the matching step when the name on the document and the name in the registry are transliterated differently, and that normalizing the transliteration before the lookup avoids the failure. The shared artifact is this insight. Methods that distill such insights from the trajectories of a single agent are well developed [167, 237, 134]. In a federation, a first distillation runs at the client, so that only summaries are uploaded, and a second one at the coordinator, which condenses the summaries of all clients into a shared library [212, 109]. 

Skills. The sender shares a reusable procedure. A skill for verifying a business license may consist of four steps: recognize the document, query the registry by license number, compare the legal representative, and escalate on mismatch. The skill is parameterized, so that the receiver binds each step to its own tools. Skills can be induced from trajectories as workflows or programs [180, 194, 242], and they can be exchanged as patches to a skill library [209]. A skill carries more structure than an insight and is more directly executable, but it also discloses more of the sender’s business process. 

Rewards. The sender shares what it has learned about how to judge a trajectory. Clients hold the ground truth, such as the decisions of human reviewers, but each has few labels. The coordinator may hold a stronger evaluator but has no access to real outcomes. Federated training of the evaluator combines the two. It has been developed for preference models of language models [199, 90] and remains open for agent trajectories (Section B.4). A shared rule in the running example is that a verification dialogue is non-compliant if the agent tells the applicant which field failed the check. An improved evaluator benefits every later optimization step of the receiver, since it supplies the signal on which the policy, the memory, and the skills are tuned. 

Ontologies. The sender shares how its concepts relate to those of others. One bank records the beneficial owner of a company, and another records the ultimate controlling person ; an alignment states whether the two are the same concept and under which conditions. Language models and agents are increasingly used to find such correspon-dences [148], and once concepts are aligned, their embeddings can be improved jointly without exchanging the underlying records [31, 138]. 

Rules. The sender shares what it has learned about the effects of actions. In the running example, one bank finds that three failed one-time codes lock the account of an applicant for 30 minutes, so that an agent should escalate instead of retrying. FedWorld federates such knowledge as abstract transition rules and labels each rule as shared, specific to a cluster of clients, private, or unresolved [75]. 42 Interactions among the types. The five types differ in function and are not ordered by abstraction. Ontology alignment makes insights, skills, and rules easier to transfer, because these artifacts are written in terms of concepts that the receiver must ground, although natural language often aligns concepts implicitly. The reward type acts at both ends of the loop: at the sender it selects which trajectories are worth distilling, and at the receiver it can estimate whether an adopted item helps when observed outcomes are scarce. A federation without aligned concepts or a reliable evaluator finds it harder to tell useful knowledge from noise. 

C.2 From Shared Knowledge to Local Improvement 

The type of shared knowledge does not determine the component in which the receiver stores it. An insight can be written to memory and retrieved when a similar task arrives, added to the system prompt, used as a training example for the policy, or used as a negative example for the evaluator. Table 5 lists the combinations. The relation between the types of this section and the categories of Section 4 is therefore many-to-many: the taxonomy describes what the federation maintains, and the types describe what travels between clients.
