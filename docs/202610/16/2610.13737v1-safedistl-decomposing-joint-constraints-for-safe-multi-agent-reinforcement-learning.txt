# SafeDiSTL: Decomposing Joint Constraints for Safe Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning 

Hongrui Zheng Aditya Singh Rahul Mangharam University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, USA 

{hongruiz,singhadi,rahulm}@engineering.upenn.edu 

Abstract 

Cooperative multi-agent tasks often impose constraints on a shared objective or collective safety. A team level violation cost function can measure this constraint outcome, but does not distinguish which agents’ actions most strongly influence that violation. We present SafeDiSTL, a constrained multi-agent reinforcement learning framework that allows for credit assignment even with joint constraint measurements. We use Signal Temporal Logic (STL) robustness to measure episode-level violation through running-maximum cost increments. Smooth full-episode robustness and differentiable dynamics provide action sensitivities, which allocate each cost in-crement across agents at the same decision time. These sensitivities account for delayed effects when weighting agents’ shares of each cost increment. We study the framework in coopera-tive payload transport, connected navigation, and networked traffic control tasks. SafeDiSTL achieves 1.6-5.1x the safe-success rate in transport, improves navigation safe-success by 11.1%, and delivers 50-68% more throughput in traffic when compared to constrained baselines. Recent developments in multi-agent autonomous systems and their applications in aerial swarms, intelligent transportation networks, and automated warehouses have made multi-agent coordination a central problem. In these environments, multiple agents must operate in shared spaces and achieve collective objectives, including collaborative manipulation, routing, and formation control, while satisfying system constraints such as connectivity and collision avoidance. Constraints in single-agent RL are commonly formulated with Constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs) [2]. Policies are then optimized by reformulating the problem as a primal-dual optimization between reward and costs. Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO) [1] balances updates with more reward or cost consideration using a Lagrangian multiplier. Multi-agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) is a popular paradigm for learning decentralized agent policies in multi-agent problems. Existing work extends CMDP formulations to MARL [11], although generally assuming well-defined, decou-pled cost functions for individual agents. However, a shared constraint definition is important in multi-agent settings. For example, if multiple robots carry a shared rigid payload, a crash between the payload and obstacles is hard to attribute to individual agents. Consider the example shown in Figure 1: three warehouse robots collaboratively transport a large payload to a designated delivery location. At a given point t3, one robot might be required to push in a direction such that the payload avoids the obstacle (green agent pushing up), while the other two maintain progress toward the goal (yellow and blue push right). When encountering a violation such as that in Figure 1.(a), assigning equal responsibility to all agents leads to a deadlock (Figure 1.(b)) without addressing the asymmetrical desired updates to individual agents. This shows that a shared constraint defini-tion alone is not enough. It tells the team of agents how much they violated, but without explicit learning signals to individual agents on how to update their policies. Without careful decomposi-tion, the introduction of a uniform penalty for all agents will hinder task learning progress. If an agent makes significant contribution towards the team’s task progress but not towards violating constraint at a timestep, that individual transition should not incur a penalty in the policy learning process. Credit assignment is an active area of research in MARL. Counterfactual Multi-Agent Policy Gradients (COMA) [9] established counterfactual reasoning for agents on task reward. Existing work [11, 14, 17] considers both individual and shared constraints in MARL problems, but leaves much to be desired in the effective decomposition of the constraint objective. This motivates the need for a principled mechanism to decompose the shared constraint among agents while account-ing for the changing individual responsibilities to enable effective collaboration. Signal Temporal Logic (STL) [16] is a compact formal specification language that expresses logical and temporal requirements over trajectories. The quantitative measure called robustness [7, 8] assigns the margin of success to a trajectory instead of a binary signal. The biggest advantage of using STL is that differentiating its robustness through the full trajectory captures how individual actions influence subsequent constraint outcomes. We use these gradients to allocate cost increments across agents at its original timestep, while cost advantages propagate learning signals to earlier decisions. For the example in Figure 1, we express the shared responsibility as ”Always no collision between payload and obstacle”. Differentiable dynamics help achieve this by propagating individual agents’ influ-ence to joint state space trajectories, and allowing back propagation from the margin of violation to decisions. As shown in Figure 1.(c), the green agent learns to subtly change its behavior by pushing upward rather than to the right, resulting in a successful avoidance of the obstacle. In summary, the contributions of this work are as follows: 1. We introduce an agent- and time-specific attribution mechanism that decomposes shared 2team constraints using STL robustness gradients while preserving the total cost. 2. We develop SafeDiSTL, a constrained MARL framework that incorporates these attributed costs into policy optimization for decentralized execution. 3. We evaluate SafeDiSTL on three tasks, comparing task performance and constraint satisfac-tion with other baselines. 

# 2 Related Work 

Constrained MARL: Gu et al. [11] introduce MACPO and MAPPO-Lagrangian, using constrained trust region updates and Lagrangian policy optimization, respectively. Like MACPO, IsCPO [17] coordinates separate agent policies through sequential updates. IsCPO considers global costs and updates agents in random order, passing optimization information to subsequent agents. Thus, later agents account for the effects of earlier updates on the shared constraint. Both MACPO and IsCPO coordinate policy updates, rather than an explicit allocation of a trajectory’s violation cost according to each agent’s influence. Ling et al. [14] directly address constraint credit assignment through fictitious MARL in a collective setting. Its formulation exploits anonymous interactions represented by aggregate counts of agents in local states and actions, rather than individual identi-ties. This structure supports scalable reward and constraint credit assignment, but is not assumed in our setting, where shared constraints can depend on coupled geometric configurations and action-dependent dynamics. SafeDiSTL instead obtains agent-specific cost allocations from the gradient sensitivity of a shared trajectory specification. 

Multi-agent Credit Assignment: Credit assignment methods for multi-agent systems extract individual learning signals from shared team outcomes. COMA [9] uses a centralized critic and a counterfactual baseline that marginalizes one agent’s action while holding the others fixed. QPD [21] applies integrated gradients along trajectory paths to decompose global action values into individual credits. STAS [5] first redistributes episodic return over time and then uses Shapley attribution between agents. These methods establish counterfactual, gradient-based, and temporal attribution for cooperative reward optimization. Our focus is the attribution of the shared constraint: we differentiate STL robustness through dynamics rather than attribute a learned return prediction. 

Responsibility Allocation: Wang et al. [20] use STL robustness to construct multi-agent rewards incorporating task and safety specifications. Zheng et al. [24] uses STL robustness as a learning signal for coordination of multiple agents. Lyu et al. [15] dynamically distribute collision avoidance responsibility using a control barrier function (CBF)-inspired risk measure. Cosner et al. [6] learn scene dependent allocations for responsibility aware control barrier functions. These approaches demonstrate the value of asymmetric safety responsibility in decentralized control. SafeDiSTL addresses the related problem of allocating learning signals for constraint violations during policy optimization, using STL robustness gradients to construct agent specific update signals rather than allocating online safety filter obligations. 

# 3 Preliminaries 

3.1 Signal Temporal Logic 

Syntax: Signal Temporal Logic (STL) [16] allows us to create spatiotemporal requirements over trajectories τ , which encodes the global constraint in our learning problem. For example, we can express “Always avoid a collision between payload and obstacles” in a formal specification: 3ϕ = □[0 ,5] ¬μcol (ppayload , p obs ), where μcol is a predicate function that measures whether the two objects described by positions p are in collision. The STL syntax is 

ϕ := ⊤ | μg | ¬ ϕ | ϕ1 ∧ ϕ2 | ϕ1 U[t1,t 2] ϕ2. (1) 

⊤ is the truth value, μg := g(τ (t)) ≥ 0 is an atomic predicate, its truth value being determined by the value of the function g : Rn → R. ∧ and ¬ denote the “conjunction” and “negation” Boolean operators, respectively. U denotes the “until” temporal operator, which specifies that formula 

ϕ1 holds until ϕ2 holds in interval [t1, t 2]. Disjunction is defined as ϕ1 ∨ ϕ2 := ¬(¬ϕ1 ∧ ¬ ϕ2);“Eventually” is defined as ♢[t1,t 2] ϕ := ⊤ U[t1,t 2] ϕ; “Always” is defined as □[t1,t 2] ϕ := ¬ ♢[t1,t 2] ¬ϕ;And “Implication” is defined as ϕ1 =⇒ ϕ2 := ¬ϕ1 ∨ ϕ2.

Quantitative Semantics: STL uses robustness ρϕ(τ ) ∈ R to measure how strongly a given trajectory satisfies or violates a specification ϕ. ρ > 0 implies satisfaction and ρ < 0 implies violation. We used the standard definition to calculate robustness [7, 8]. We also utilize smooth approximations [12, 13, 18] of max and min operators to enable auto-differentiation of the robustness value of a trajectory to the states in the trajectory. The end-to-end gradient computation from robustness to policy actions can then be enabled with differentiable dynamics.

# 5 SafeDiSTL 

A shared constraint cost measures the team’s violation but does not distinguish the contributions of individual agents and decisions. SafeDiSTL uses full-episode STL sensitivities to allocate each running-maximum cost increment across agents at the same decision time. Section 5.1 develops this allocation while preserving both the per-step increment and the total episode cost. Section 5.2 incorporates the attributed costs into critic learning and constrained policy optimization, while retaining the original episode cost for the multiplier update.

# 6 Experiments

We compare against reward-only MAPPO and the constrained baselines MACPO and MAPPO-L from Gu et. al. [11]. Baseline optimization details are provided in Appendix A.

6.2 The Constraint-performance Tradeoff 

We first show the key result of the experiments. In Figure 2, we show that SafeDiSTL balances the tradeoff between constraint satisfaction and retaining performance. In each subplot, the y-axes shows constraint satisfaction metrics in percentages, and the x-axes shows performance metrics in normalized returns to those obtained by MAPPO. Our algorithm modifies MAPPO by introducing the dual on constraint and asymmetric cost attribution to individual agents, thus we want to show that SafeDiSTL retains the performance of MAPPO, but does so considering satisfying the shared constraints. In all five scenarios shown in Figure 2, SafeDiSTL balances this trade off by being in the top right quadrant of the plots, while other methods sacrifice MAPPO’s task performance to increase constraint satisfaction. Appendix E examines attribution, cost formulation, and dual-optimizer variants. 

6.3 Cooperative Transport 

We use a transport task with dynamics and control similar to the one in [3], where three force input agents push a shared package around circular obstacles towards a goal. The shared constraint here is package–obstacle clearance, and safe success requires both the payload reaching the goal and no contact between the payload and the obstacle throughout the entire episode. In this benchmark, we consider three different settings with 1-3 obstacles between the starting position and the payload 10 goal. Figure 3 shows the comparison between the four methods. SafeDiSTL has the highest mean safe success rate in all three settings: 35 .6 ± 9.6%, 16 .2 ± 11 .0%, and 3.5 ± 2.3% with one, two, and three obstacles, respectively. MAPPO-L trails closest behind with 7.0 ± 3.7%, 4.5 ± 0.9%, and 

2.2 ± 1.0%. With three obstacles, safe success remains low for all methods in this harder condition, yet SafeDiSTL still achieves highest safe success rate there. Although MAPPO has higher mean goal success in all three conditions, SafeDiSTL more often achieves the goal while satisfying the shared clearance requirement. MACPO on the other hand completely fails in all three settings. Full numerical results appear in Table 1. Figure 4 shows an example rollout comparison between the methods. The same trend shows up here as well: MAPPO reaches the goal, the two constrained baselines has degraded goal reaching capability, while SafeDiSTL manages to go around the obstacle and reaches the goal. 

6.4 Connected Navigation 

In this benchmark, we control four point-mass agents navigating through two staggered obstacle passages to a shared destination. The shared constraint is buffered full episode communication connectivity: at every timestep, the longest edge of the agents’ Euclidean minimum-spanning tree must not exceed 3.325 m. Goal success requires all agents to remain in the destination region during the final 20 control steps. Figure 6 reports the comparison between all four methods. SafeDiSTL has the highest mean goal success rate ( 71 .88 ± 6.13 %) and safe-success rate ( 43 .88 ± 4.40 %) among the four main methods. MAPPO-L attains slightly higher full episode constraint satisfaction rate than SafeDiSTL ( 56 .64 ± 10 .61 % versus 54 .69 ± 8.33 %). MACPO reaches only 4.43 ± 1.96 % safe successes. MAPPO has the highest mean return but almost always violates the constraint. These results show that in the connected navigation benchmark, considering the constraint effectively also helps with task success rate since grouping together through the narrow passage reduces accidental collision with the obstacle that might slow down goal reaching. Full numerical results appear in Table 2. Figure 5 also shows the same conclusion in matched rollout trajectories. SafeDiSTL agents grouped closely together and navigated smoothly towards the goal without any disconnects in the graph. The agents in all other methods disconnected at least once throughout the episode. 

6.5 Networked Traffic Control 

We consider a seven region Networked Macroscopic Fundamental Diagram (NMFD) traffic task [10, 19]. Six peripheral regions exchange destination labeled traffic through a shared central core. Each region meters its outgoing traffic, while core exits are uncontrolled and randomized at every time step. The shared constraint is on the core region’s occupancy. The flow rate of the core region is dictated by the cubic polynomial shown in Figure 7. The flow rate first increases as the number of vehicles in the region increases. After the peak, it drops down to the congestion limit, where the flow rate drops below 1 vehicle per second. Thus the shared constraint is set as ncore ≤ nsafe ≃ 8786 .415 .Vehicle admissions to the core therefore trade off completed trips against core region congestion, while restrictive metering can increase the wait time in the system. The traffic in the system is randomly spawned as source-destination pairing of two peripheral zones into the queue. All vehicles must travel through the core on their way to their destinations. The agents control their own regions’ flow rate for vehicles with all other peripheral regions as destination. Then the system revolves following the controlled core region in-flow rate, and the uncontrolled, randomized out-flow rate. The agents observe the randomize exit rate, and should react to changes for decisions on their control. Core vehicle-hours: the time that vehicles spends on average at the core region, this measures the system delay. 4. Time above core limit: the time that core regions has occupancy higher than the limit. Figure 8 compares all four methods. The training reward for all methods here are completed trips. The goal of this benchmark is to test the trade off between throughput and core congestion. Since core region exit rates are randomized, a smart team of agents should learn to coordinate when to let each destination’s vehicles go without overloading the core. SafeDiSTL retains 71 .4% of MAPPO throughput while reducing the core vehicle-hours to 8.8% of MAPPO and the time above core limits to 0.3% of MAPPO. Although MACPO and MAPPO-L also learn to reduce constraint violation to the same level, they only retain 47 .6% and 42 .5% of the MAPPO throughput, respectively. H.1 Collaborative Transport 

The transport environment contains three force-controlled agents, an unactuated nonrotating square package, and circular obstacles in a planar workspace. The package has side length 0.15 

m and mass 50 ; each agent has mass one and radius 0.03 m, and each obstacle has radius 0.10 

m. Each agent selects a normalized two-dimensional action in [−1, 1] 2, scaled by 0.6 to produce the applied force. The differentiable VMAS-style simulator uses float64 smooth contact, and four 

0.025 s physics substeps per control step. Episodes last 500 control steps. Resets sample one of eight layouts, apply a random quarter-turn and reflection, jitter each obstacle by at most 0.015 m per coordinate, and randomize the agents’ phase around the package. The layouts obstruct the direct route while providing detours. The shared constraint margin is the package-box signed distance to each obstacle, minus the obstacle radius and the required 0.02 m clearance. Goal achievement uses the task’s box-distance threshold of approximately 0.1567 m rather than a point-payload center-distance test. The shared reward combines distance-progress shaping scaled by 100 with the campaign’s approach and joint-push coordination terms, held fixed across algorithms. Package contact is logged separately; it neither adds a reward penalty nor terminates the episode. 

H.2 Connected Navigation 

Four homogeneous planar agents navigate from a compact start formation to a shared circular destination through two staggered obstacle passages. The canonical corridor is 18 m long and 10 m wide; ten static circular obstacles form two five-obstacle barriers. Agent and obstacle radii are 0.75 m and 0.8 m, respectively. Resets randomize barrier locations, opening centers, and opening widths (3.4–3.8 m), perturb the start formation and destination, and apply a global rotation, reflection, and translation. Agents use bounded two-dimensional acceleration commands, maximum speed 1.5 m/s, smooth contact forces, five physics substeps per 0.1 s control step, and a 250-step horizon. The shared reward combines reduction in mean agent-to-goal distance, scaled by 5/ R, with the fraction of agents inside the destination. Goal success requires all four agents to remain within the 2.6 m goal radius throughout the final 20 control steps. The formal constraint is whole-episode buffered graph connectivity. H.3 Networked Traffic Control 

The traffic task is a seven-region NMFD network with six peripheral regions and a shared core. Direct peripheral-to-peripheral transfers are disabled. Each region uses the cubic production func-tion 

G(n) = 4 .133 × 10 −11 n3 − 8.282 × 10 −7n2 + 0 .0042 n, (33) where n is occupancy in vehicles and G is in vehicles/s. Episodes contain 240 control intervals of 30 s, each integrated with ten RK4 substeps. Demand is five times the reference setting: 0.625 vehicles/s per nonlocal peripheral origin–destination pair, with ±20 % reset randomization. Three destinations receive low exit baselines of 0.1 and three receive high baselines of 0.9, randomly permuted at reset; temporal noise and incidents vary core exits within [0 , 1] .Agent i controls a six-dimensional admission vector ui ∈ [0 , 1] 6. Coordinate uid meters traffic in its region bound for destination d into the core; the self-destination coordinate is masked, leaving five effective controls. If xid is the commodity occupancy and ni the region total, its core-entry rate is uid (xid /ni)G(ni), with zero flow for an empty region. The shared reward is completed trips per interval divided by 100. The core constraint is n0 ≤ nsafe , where nsafe ≃ 8786 .415 is the descending-branch solution of G(n) = 1 .

# I Observations and Policy Network Architectures 

This section describes the actor inputs and policy networks used in the reported benchmarks. 

I.1 Cooperative Transport 

For agent i, the observation concatenates its absolute planar position and velocity (four coordi-nates), package position minus goal position (two), package position relative to the agent (two), absolute package velocity (two), and a binary package-on-goal indicator (one). It then includes the relative position of each of the K obstacles ( 2K coordinates) and the relative position and velocity of each of the other two agents (eight coordinates), ordered by agent index. Thus the observation dimension is 19 + 2 K, giving 21, 23, and 25 inputs for the one-, two-, and three-obstacle conditions. The nonrotating package contributes no orientation features, and no assigned push-slot features are appended. Actor inputs are standardized using running means and variances estimated dur-ing training and clipped to [−10 , 10] ; evaluation uses the saved normalization statistics without updating them. 24 The actor is a feed-forward multilayer perceptron with two 64-unit hidden layers, tanh acti-vations, and a linear two-dimensional Gaussian-mean head. A learned, observation-independent logstddev vector specifies the diagonal covariance. Training samples a latent Gaussian action and applies tanh to obtain the normalized force command in [−1, 1] 2; deterministic evaluation applies tanh to the mean. The simulator multiplies this command by 0.6. MAPPO, SafeDiSTL, and the uniform-attribution variants share one actor across the three agents; MAPPO-L and MACPO use separate actor parameters for each agent with the same layer sizes and action parameterization. 

I.2 Connected Navigation 

Each agent receives a 72-dimensional observation. The first 34 coordinates comprise its own planar position and velocity (four), normalized episode time (one), four relative target positions (eight), and three teammate slots (21). The four target entries refer to the shared destination. Each teammate slot contains relative position and velocity from the stored communication packet, a direct-neighbor indicator, a connected-component reachability indicator, and packet age; slots fol-low the agents’ fixed identifiers within the episode. The remaining 38 coordinates describe the ten obstacles using relative position and radius (30), the four corridor-boundary clearances (four), and the flattened two-dimensional world-rotation matrix (four). Positions, relative distances, obstacle radii, and boundary clearances are divided by 10; velocities are divided by the maximum speed of 1.5; time and packet ages are divided by the 250-step horizon. These features are then standardized using running observation statistics and clipped to [−10 , 10] , with the saved statistics held fixed during evaluation. The actor maps these 72 inputs through two 64-unit tanh hidden layers to a linear two-dimensional Gaussian-mean head. It uses a learned, observation-independent diagonal log-standard-deviation vector and tanh-bounded actions in [−1, 1] 2. Deterministic evaluation uses the transformed mean. The simulator additionally clips the command’s Euclidean norm to one before applying the acceleration scale. MAPPO, SafeDiSTL, and the uniform-attribution variants share actor parameters across the four agents, whereas MAPPO-L and MACPO use one independently parameterized actor per agent with the same network dimensions. The policies are feed-forward; communication packet contents and ages are explicit observation features, not a recurrent policy state. 

I.3 Networked Traffic Control 

Each peripheral agent receives 31 observation coordinates: its six destination-specific queues, six local origin–destination demand rates, six destination-specific queues in the shared core, six current effective core-exit factors, normalized episode time, and a six-dimensional one-hot agent identity. Queue occupancies are divided by 1,800; demand rates and exit factors are used directly, and time is divided by the 240-step horizon. The core queues and exit factors are broadcast to all six agents. The observation excludes the latent variables driving future exit disturbances. The identity is already part of the environment observation, so the learner does not append another identity vector. There is no additional running observation normalization, and actor inputs use float32 arithmetic. The actor has two 64-unit tanh hidden layers and a linear six-dimensional Gaussian-mean head, with a learned, observation-independent log-standard-deviation vector. A sigmoid transforms each latent Gaussian sample into an admission command in [0 , 1] . The self-destination coordinate is masked to zero, leaving five effective controls per agent; deterministic evaluation applies the same sigmoid and mask to the mean. MAPPO, SafeDiSTL, and the uniform-attribution variants share 25 one actor across the six agents. MAPPO-L and MACPO instead maintain six independent actors with the same input features, hidden-layer sizes, and action transform. 26
