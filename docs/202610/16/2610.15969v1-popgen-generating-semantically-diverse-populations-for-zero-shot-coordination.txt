## ABSTRACT 

In multi-agent reinforcement learning, exposing a cooperator to partners with di-verse strategies during training is key to better collaboration at test time. However, existing methods typically rely on trajectory-level proxies to measure diversity, which may label two distinct trajectories of the same strategy as diverse. Instead, our key insight is to leverage LLMs’ ability to describe behaviorally distinct agents in environments, similar to how humans can recognize high-level strategies. To this end, we introduce P OP GEN , a framework that synthesizes semantically di-verse agents from scratch using LLMs. P OP GEN first generates a diverse set of textual descriptions of agent behaviors, then translates each behavior into a code policy that can be executed within the environment. We show that cooperators trained against P OP GEN populations achieve robust zero-shot cooperation with unseen partners, outperforming population-play baselines across four collabora-tive environments. We further show that semantic diversity scales with P OP GEN 

population size, improving the cooperator’s worst-case performance, and we iden-tify design choices that preserve population quality while maximizing diversity. Our code is available at https://github.com/labicon/PopGen .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Training agents that collaborate effectively with unseen partners is a key challenge in multi-agent systems like robotics or cooperative video games. The task, known as zero-shot coordination (ZSC), requires a cooperator to coordinate with partners it has never interacted with, whose conventions, skill level, and strategy are unknown at test time. This problem is challenging because cooperative tasks typically admit many equally effective but mutually incompatible conventions, so a cooperator trained with one arbitrary convention may fail when paired with a partner that follows a different one (Hu et al., 2020; Carroll et al., 2019). A common approach to ZSC is population play, which trains a cooperator against a population of partner agents. The effectiveness of population play depends on the diversity of the population against which the cooperator is trained, since a high coverage of the strategy space during training increases the likelihood that the test-time partner is in-distribution for the cooperator, allowing them to collaborate effectively (Strouse et al., 2021; Lupu et al., 2021; Sarkar et al., 2023). Existing meth-ods build such populations by optimizing diversity proxies computed from trajectory statistics (Lupu et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2023). However, these measures may fail to capture what humans perceive as meaningfully distinct strategies (Bettini et al., 2024c; Wang et al., 2025). For example, two chefs may take different trajectories in a kitchen while following the same strategy (e.g., only carrying onions). The issue is not that these trajectories are wrongly classified as different (they are indeed different), but that optimizing for such diversity can lead to undesired training populations that are diverse in their motion but do not cover the distinct strategies that matter for coordination (Sarkar et al., 2023). In contrast to these measures, humans naturally describe trajectories at a higher level, recognizing groups of strategies. We introduce P OP GEN , a framework that leverages LLMs to define semantically diverse behaviors in language space and translate them into executable policies that can interact with the environment. P OP GEN operates in two stages. First, it generates a diverse set of behavior descriptions in natural language, where high-level semantic differences can be expressed explicitly. We use tailored prompting methods to balance the quality of behavior descriptions with their se-mantic diversity, encouraging descriptions that are both meaningful and genuinely distinct. Finally, we show that turning textual behavior descriptions into executable policy code, rather than LLM-generated reward functions, yields partners that more faithfully exhibit their specified behaviors and leads to more robust cooperators. In summary, our contributions are: • We propose P OP GEN , a framework that generates diverse agent populations from scratch by leveraging LLMs’ understanding of semantic diversity. • We show that training a cooperator against a P OP GEN population achieves the best perfor-mance across four collaborative environments when matched against other population-play baselines. • We provide extensive ablations that aim to understand the link between semantic diversity and downstream cooperation, as well as insights on methods to turn textual descriptions into executable agents using LLMs. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Generating diverse populations for ZSC. Zero-shot coordination requires a cooperator to work with teammates it never saw during training (Hu et al., 2020). Population play achieves this by training an agent against a set of partners, and how well population play works depends on the behavioral diversity of the population against which an agent is trained (Bettini et al., 2024a; McKee et al., 2022). Existing methods for generating agent populations vary from pooling checkpoints across random seeds (Strouse et al., 2021) to optimizing explicit diversity objectives over trajectory or action distributions (Lupu et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2023), and more recently to sampling partners from a generative model of human play (Liang et al., 2024). These diversity measures all operate in trajectory, action, or latent space, where distances may not reflect the strategic distinctions a human 2would recognize (Bettini et al., 2024c; Wang et al., 2025). We instead leverage the semantic diversity captured at the text level to generate diverse populations of agents. 

LLM-generated agents. LLMs can translate natural-language specifications into agents in sev-eral forms. LLM-as-Agent systems directly interact with the environment but require costly LLM calls during rollout (Huang et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023), while reward-generation methods trans-late textual objectives into reward functions and train RL policies (Ma et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024). However, designing rewards that faithfully capture high-level behaviors is challenging, as RL poli-cies can optimize the reward without exhibiting the intended behavior (Choi et al., 2025). This chal-lenge is pronounced in multi-agent settings, where interactions depend on other agents. Policy code instead provides a direct representation of behavior by explicitly encoding its decision logic (Liang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2026; Ahn et al., 2026). Recent advances in LLM code gen-eration further enable refining such policies through execution feedback (Fu et al., 2026; Ahn et al., 2026). In our experiments, we show that Policy Code more reliably realizes the specified behaviors than RL policies, resulting in higher behavioral adherence and stronger cooperator performance.

## 4 POP GEN : G ENERATING DIVERSE AGENT POPULATIONS 

We introduce P OP GEN , a framework for synthesizing semantically diverse populations in multi-agent environments. The resulting population can then be used to train a cooperator for zero-shot cooperation. As illustrated in Figure 1, P OP GEN leverages LLMs in two stages. 4.1 STAGE 1: G ENERATING SEMANTICALLY DIVERSE BEHAVIORS 

The first stage feeds an environment description as input to an LLM, and queries it to generate various behaviors that agents could elicit in it (e.g., an “onion-to-pot master” in overcooked ). The objective here is to generate a pool of diverse behaviors, in order to train a cooperator against it (Lupu et al., 2021; Bettini et al., 2024a). Such diversity is hard to measure directly from policies, since two policies can take different actions while following the same strategy (Bettini et al., 2024c; Wang et al., 2025). The tension between quality and diversity of agent populations. An important challenge when targeting textual diversity in the population is to avoid achieving it at the cost of degrading the quality of the generations. For instance, one can simply generate diverse but nonsense text as the behaviors for an environment to optimize diversity. This problem, known as the quality-diversity trade-off in NLP (Zhang et al., 2020), is a result of the two metrics directly conflicting when optimizing for diversity 1. In our setup, we define behavior quality along the following axes: realism (would a real player plausibly play this way?); relevance (is the behavior relevant to the environment?); and specificity (is the behavior specific enough so that it encodes a recognizable style of play?). Concretely, we compute this by grading each axis from 0 to 5 with an LLM 2, and sum these into a score out of 15. POP GEN is carefully designed to address this tension, decoupling the search for diverse behaviors from the selection of relevant ones to preserve quality of generations. The LLM is first queried to generate a set of candidates by oversampling (i.e., generating many more candidates than expected), then re-queried to select the highest-quality candidates among the set as shown in Figure 1. Addi-tionally, P OP GEN employs a simple trick from Verbalized Sampling (VS), which queries the LLM to output each candidate’s probability alongside the generation and has shown strong empirical re-sults (Zhang et al., 2025). We empirically show that P OP GEN can generate behavior populations that are both high-quality and semantically diverse in Section 5.3. 4.2 STAGE 2: I NSTANTIATING BEHAVIORS AS AGENTS 

The second stage translates each textual behavior into a policy that interacts with the environment and other agents using an LLM. An LLM can act as the policy itself, outputting an action at every step (Yin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024), generate a reward function for an RL policy to opti-mize (Ma et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025), or directly implement the decision logic as executable code (Liang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). POP GEN adopts the latter, modeling each agent’s policy as a Python function, which is cheap to execute and avoids training an RL agent per behavior. Section 5.4 compares this choice with SemDiv’s reward-function approach (Li et al., 2025).

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate P OP GEN as a method for generating synthetic populations to train cooperators. Af-ter describing the setup (Section 5.1), we test whether cooperators trained against P OP GEN pop-ulations generalize to novel partners (Section 5.2), analyze how P OP GEN produces semantically diverse agents and why this helps cooperation (Section 5.3), and compare policy code against LLM-generated reward functions for implementing behaviors (Section 5.4). 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Environments. We consider four multi-agent environments with discrete and continuous action spaces. In Point-Mass Rendezvous ( PMR ) (Lowe et al., 2017), two agents must agree on which of four equally valid landmarks to meet at. In Level-Based Foraging ( LBF ) (Albrecht & Ramamoorthy, 2015), agents must time their load actions to jointly collect food whose level exceeds any single agent’s. In Predator-Prey ( PP ) (Lowe et al., 2017), agents choose between capturing low-reward rabbits alone and coordinating to capture high-reward stags. In overcooked (Carroll et al., 2019), two chefs must divide subtasks (filling pots with onions, cooking, serving) to deliver soups. Details are in Appendix A.1. 

LLM models. We employ Gemini-3-Flash as the generator model and adherence classifier, and Gemini-3-Pro as the feedback advisor (see Figure 1) in our experiments (Google DeepMind, 2025). Cooperator training. We compare P OP PLAY , which trains a cooperator via population play against P OP GEN partners, with four population-play baselines. Self-Play (SP) (Strouse et al., 2021) trains MAPPO self-play agents from independent seeds. Fictitious Co-Play (FCP) (Strouse et al., 2021) adds their intermediate checkpoints to cover a range of skill levels. Maximum Entropy Population-based Training (MEP) (Zhao et al., 2023) diversifies the population with a population-entropy objective and trains the cooperator with prioritized partner sampling. CoMeDi (Sarkar et al., 2023) adds partners that minimize cross-play reward with the existing population, encouraging in-compatible conventions, and trains the cooperator with an auxiliary behavior-cloning loss on each partner’s self-play rollouts. All cooperators except CoMeDi share the same PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) backbone and number of training frames. Appendices B.1 and B.3 detail the baselines and their hyperparameters in our setup. 

ZSC evaluation. We evaluate each cooperator’s zero-shot coordination by pairing it with held-out partners that were not encountered during training. These are fixed, scripted agents with seman-tically distinct behaviors, specified in advance without LLMs. The held-out behaviors are never provided to the LLMs during our experiments, preventing P OP GEN from being biased toward the evaluation behaviors. POP PLAY adapts its role to the partner. We examine whether P OP PLAY can show semantically different behaviors when paired with different partners. overcooked provides a natural setting for this analysis, as it requires a cooperator to handle different subtasks according to the partner chef’s preference. Figure 3a illustrates this behavior by showing the same POP PLAY cooperator paired with different partners. When the partner fetches onions, P OP PLAY takes the dish and serves the soup. When the partner takes the dish, P OP PLAY fetches onions and cooks instead. Figure 3b shows the distribution of subtasks completed by the cooperator. The P OP PLAY cooperator performs all subtasks with substantial count, while other methods almost never load the onion (Self-Play, FCP, and MEP) or are biased toward single actions (CoMeDi on pot loading). In particular, Self-Play and FCP exhibit a severe mode collapse in population generation where their generated populations predominantly focus on onion fetching. As a result, the corresponding cooperators rarely need to fetch onions themselves, leading to the absence of onion-fetching behavior with held-out partners. We study these failures in detail in Appendix B.5, highlighting the importance of behavioral diversity in the training population for learning robust partner-adaptive behavior. 5.3 GENERATING DIVERSE POPULATIONS FOR COOPERATOR TRAINING 

Diversity in training agents is the core element driving cooperator performance as it allows the co-operator to adapt to a wide range of partners at test time (Lupu et al., 2021; Bettini et al., 2024a). In this section, we evaluate the diversity of P OP GEN populations. First, we investigate how diversity scales with P OP GEN population size, and measure its downstream impact on the cooperator’s ability to adapt to new partners. We then evaluate how our generation method targets high semantic diver-sity while maintaining the quality of the behaviors (i.e., the quality-diversity trade-off introduced in Section 4.1). 

Larger populations for more diversity, and more robustness. We train overcooked cooper-ators against P OP GEN populations with n ∈ { 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8} distinct behaviors. The training budget is fixed for fair comparison, so every pool contains 8 partners obtained by replicating the n distinct agents (i.e., n = 1 corresponds to 8 copies of a single partner). For each size, we run the experi-ment 5 times (i.e., we generate 5 independent populations and train one cooperator on each), so the variation we report covers the population generation. 7As shown in Figure 4a, semantic diversity increases with larger P OP GEN population sizes. It grows from 0 at n = 1 to 0.51 at n = 4 , and saturates afterwards as the first behaviors already cover most of the environment. These gains in diversity naturally translate into better cooperator performance with new partners (Bettini et al., 2024a), with an inflection point suggesting that the population captures the diversity needed to represent most aspects of overcooked (Figure 4b, +34% perfor-mance when adding the 4th agent). More importantly, larger populations make the cooperator more robust. We measure it as the reward with the held-out partner each cooperator coordinates with least (Figure 4a, bottom). With n ≤ 2, every cooperator fails completely with at least one held-out partner (worst-case reward around 0 in all runs), whereas from n = 3 onwards, worst-case reward significantly jumps (up to 39 on average). This last result means that a larger population is more likely to include a partner resembling any given unseen one, so the cooperator is less likely to face a partner it has never learned to complement. 0.5 0.6 0.7

Preserving training agents’ quality while increas-ing diversity. Naively pushing for diversity tends to erode quality, as introduced in Section 4.1. How-ever, preserving quality matters for cooperator train-ing, as incoherent or degenerate behaviors provide a noisy signal and can lead the cooperator to learn compensatory strategies that are useless, or even harmful, when paired with realistic partners (Liang et al., 2024). We evaluate P OP GEN ’s ability to pre-serve a correct level of quality in agent behaviors while aiming for semantic diversity. We compare POP GEN against three baselines. DIR generates each behavior independently, with no knowledge of previous generations, and thus cannot reach high di-versity but serves as a quality oracle. Verbalized Sampling (VS) (Zhang et al., 2025) is a method de-signed to preserve quality when generating diverse outputs, and was evaluated on standard NLP tasks (e.g., QA, story generation). Finally, SemDiv (Li et al., 2025) is the pipeline closest to ours: it gen-erates textual behaviors sequentially, conditioning each new behavior on all previous ones. For each method, we generate a set of 30 behaviors for each environment listed in Section 5.1, and measure 

diversity and quality 3 as defined in Section 4.1. Figure 5 presents the results. P OP GEN achieves the best trade-off overall. It reaches the highest diversity ( 0.728 ), on par with VS ( 0.726 ), while producing clearly better behaviors at that diversity level ( 12 .74 vs. 12 .03 , a 6% improvement in quality). Compared to SemDiv, P OP GEN gives up 6% 

of quality for a 30% gain in diversity, which is considerable for the task. As expected, DIR obtains the highest quality ( 14 .45 ) but by far the lowest diversity ( 0.446 ), with P OP GEN being 63% more diverse. 5.4 MODELING AGENT BEHAVIORS AS POLICY CODE FOR POPULATION PLAY 

In its second stage, P OP GEN uses an LLM to turn each textual behavior description into an exe-cutable policy that can act in the environment (see Figure 1). In this section, we ablate this part, comparing P OP GEN ’s approach, in which the LLM writes code that directly scripts the agent’s ac-tions ( Policy Code ), and that of SemDiv (Li et al., 2025), in which the LLM writes a reward function used to train a neural agent to exhibit the behavior ( Reward Function ). Both methods start from the same set of textual behaviors, which removes stage 1 as a potential confounder. We evaluate each method on two metrics. The first is downstream performance: the team reward a cooperator trained with each population achieves when paired with new partners. The second is the population’s aver-age adherence to its target behaviors (Section 4.2), which tells us how faithfully each method models the behaviors in the policies, and lets us relate adherence to cooperation.

## 6 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we study semantic diversity, rather than trajectory-level statistics, as a basis for train-ing agents that cooperate robustly with unseen partners. We introduce P OP GEN , which constructs diverse populations by first generating diverse behaviors in text space and then translating them into executable agents through advisor-guided refinement. We show that cooperators trained against POP GEN populations generalize to new partners, outperforming population-play baselines in all four environments studied. We further study the link between semantic diversity and zero-shot co-operation, and show that training against semantically diverse partners improves the cooperator’s worst-case performance by exposing it to a wide range of strategies. We also provide insights on how to preserve the quality of the generated agents (e.g., keeping behaviors realistic) while optimiz-ing for diversity. Finally, we show that implementing behaviors as executable policy code, rather than as LLM-generated reward functions, yields partners that follow their intended behaviors more faithfully, particularly in discrete environments, and leads to more robust cooperators. 

Limitations. Our work focuses on increasing behavior coverage , but we evaluate only against synthetic partners. Prior work reports that training against human-like populations transfers to coor-dination with humans (Carroll et al., 2019; Liang et al., 2024), but a human study remains necessary to confirm that the semantic diversity of P OP GEN also improves human-AI coordination. In ad-dition, because P OP GEN relies on LLMs for both behavior and policy generation, the generated populations, and hence cooperator performance, may vary across runs. 

Future work. Two directions follow naturally. First, online adaptive behavior generation would move behavior generation inside the cooperator training loop, so that P OP GEN can target the co-operator’s current weaknesses (Zhao et al., 2023). Second, the quality of the refinement loop is bounded by the context provided to the LLM. Advances in context engineering (Mei et al., 2025; Khattab et al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2026) and in code LLMs and coding agents (Chen et al., 2021; Rozière et al., 2024; Kimi, 2026) offer a direct path to improving both the advisor’s feedback and the generator’s code. 9ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

Diverse text generation methods. Table 9 presents the generation methods we evaluate, orga-nized by the level at which diversity is encouraged. Instance-level methods (Direct) generate one behavior per independent LLM call with chain-of-thought reasoning (Wei et al., 2022), relying solely on sampling stochasticity for diversity. List-level methods ask the model to produce multiple behav-iors in a single output: One-Pass generates all N behaviors at once, Sequence generates them in batches of k, and Multi-Turn uses conversation history so that each new behavior is conditioned on previously generated ones. SimpleStrat (Wong et al., 2024) first asks the LLM to identify mean-ingful diversity axes (strata) for the environment, then samples behaviors conditioned on randomly selected strata combinations. Distribution-level methods build on Verbalized Sampling (VS) (Zhang et al., 2025), which prompts the model to output behaviors together with verbalized probabilities, encouraging it to sample from the tails of its distribution rather than collapsing to the mode. VS-MT extends this across conversation turns, accumulating history. Finally, we propose VS-Oversample 

(VS-OS), which generates m > N candidate behaviors with verbalized probabilities, then uses a second LLM call to merge and deduplicate them into the N best behaviors. This two-step approach decouples diversity exploration (oversampling) from quality control (merging), allowing broader coverage without sacrificing coherence. C.1 OVERSAMPLING TO PRESERVE QUALITY IN BEHAVIOR GENERATION 

Oversampling ablation. Table 10 ablates how we oversample, comparing One-Pass, Independent, and Sequential strategies at ratios ×2 and ×4 against vanilla VS ( ×1). All variants improve quality over the VS baseline while maintaining comparable diversity, confirming that the merging step filters low-quality candidates without collapsing diversity. Sequential ×2 achieves the best overall balance, reaching the best average quality (13.00) while staying within 0.005 of the highest average diversity (0.696 against 0.701), and we adopt it as the default VS-OS configuration throughout the paper. Increasing the ratio to ×4 leaves diversity essentially unchanged for One-Pass and Independent while lowering it for Sequential, suggesting that an overly large candidate pool gives the merging step too much freedom to prune unusual behaviors in favor of safe ones. 

Diversity analysis and scaling of VS-OS. Figure 9 refines the comparison between VS-OS and its single-pass counterpart. Both achieve comparable lexical dissimilarity, but they diverge on embedding-level measures: VS-OS recovers the worst-case (minimum pairwise) distance and cov-erage that multi-turn VS sacrifices (Figure 9a), indicating that oversample-and-merge fills gaps a single pass leaves unexplored rather than duplicating its distribution. Figure 9b shows why this mat-ters at scale: when upgrading the generator from Flash to Pro, multi-turn VS gains quality ( +1 .00 )but loses diversity ( −0.06 ), whereas VS-OS translates the same upgrade into gains on both axes (+0 .57 quality, +0 .04 diversity). Because exploration and selection are separate stages, a more capable model improves each independently.
