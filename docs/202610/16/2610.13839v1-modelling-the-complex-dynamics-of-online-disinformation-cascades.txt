# Modelling the complex dynamics of online disinformation cascades

Abstract. Disinformation on online social media has been on the rise in the past years. While there is extensive literature modelling social cascades, online disinformation cascades show distinctive characteristics: they provoke debunking reactions, they spread over networks with het-erogeneous connectivity, and they compete for the limited attention of users — which recommendation algorithms may partly offset. We propose a model including these ingredients and study it through Monte–Carlo simulations and mean–field approximations, on random graphs and an empirical Twitter network. We find that debunking reactions are double– edged: they reduce the reach of appealing items but allow unappealing ones to reach audiences they would not otherwise reach. Degree hetero-geneity makes cascades trigger at a much lower content appeal but limits their final reach: heterogeneous networks are easy to ignite yet hard to saturate. Limited attention cancels the effects from degree heterogene-ity, although the mediation of recommendation algorithms may restore them. 

Keywords: Social cascades, Social media, Rumours, Disinformation, Limited attention, Recommendation algorithms 

# 1 Introduction 

The recent explosion of fake news and disinformation raises major concerns about the vulnerability of the online social sphere. This type of content is typically char-acterised by an emotionally arousing tone and novel or surprising claims whose sources are difficult to verify. Such content travels fast and far through social media platforms, which provide immediate communication to a large audience: false news has been found to spread farther, faster, and more broadly than the truth [19]. At the same time, the volume of content reaching each user exceeds their cognitive and attention limits. These restrictions are naturally exacerbated for highly connected users [14] and are mediated by the algorithms that rank what appears in each feed [9]. As the design of social media platforms is op-timised for engagement, it may favour the emotionally arousing content that characterises disinformation [2], enhancing its visibility. arXiv:2610.13839v1 [physics.soc-ph] 9 Oct 2026 2 Guillermo Romero Moreno et al. 

Inspired by epidemiological models, early models from physics studied the spread of rumours by adapting the Susceptible–Infected–Recovered (SIR) model and derived analytical expressions to study how the network topology affects their diffusion [16]. Further model refinements included competing counter– information (or debunking ) cascades [11] or even a whole ‘ecology’ of cascades where each rumour or piece of information needs to compete for attention [20]. However, the relation between disinformation and debunking is not just compet-itive but also parasitic , i.e., a debunking cascade can only emerge from places where the disinformation cascade has already reached [10]. While this type of dy-namics is present in some (dis)information models [4,6], a thorough understand-ing of the dynamics for how counter–information affects the original cascade is lacking. Furthermore, how network topology, limited attention, and recommen-dation algorithms jointly shape the competition between disinformation and its debunking remains unexplored. We propose a model that mimics the dynamics of disinformation cascades in the context of online social media. Our model includes key features of contro-versial information cascades and the online social networks where they spread: 

– The presence of counter–information , which can emerge with some probabil-ity from any user who receives the disinformation, while users who receive the counter–information may gain interest and believe the original disinfor-mation item with some probability as well, creating a backfire effect 

– High heterogeneity in the connectivity of nodes in the network — online social networks such as X (formerly Twitter) exhibit long–tail distributions in the degree of their nodes, with most users having few connections and some users having a number of connections several orders of magnitude higher 

– Limited attention , which reduces the probability that users notice the disin-formation item in inverse proportion to their number of connections. Via Monte–Carlo simulations and mean–field approximations on Erd˝ os–R´ enyi graphs and an empirical Twitter network, we model how these factors interact and may enable or prevent disinformation cascades for a range of model param-eters. We then examine the effect of recommendation algorithms, which may reduce attention limits by boosting the appearance of controversial information in social feeds. Our results show that counter–information is double–edged, that degree heterogeneity makes cascades easier to trigger but harder to saturate, and that limited attention cancels these effects of heterogeneity unless recom-mendation algorithms restore them. 

# 2 Model of online disinformation cascades 

We propose here a model of disinformation cascades that is adapted to the online social media environment. More particularly, we are interested in modelling when the spread of a single, new piece of disinformation is introduced into a social group, potentially by a (few) malicious actor(s) who want to influence a big part of the network. Re-sharing information in online social media incurs very little effort, and it may be enough to see a piece of information once to re-share it. Likewise, once a user posts about something, they are unlikely to post about it in the near future [5]. Therefore, our starting point is the well–known Independent Cascade model [8], where active agents can activate their inactive neighbours in a single attempt with a certain probability and once all attempts are exhausted, the spreading process ends. The system is usually initialised with very few active agents (commonly referred to as seeds ) and the research interest is on cascade sizes depending on the network topology or seed positions [13]. We assume a population where individuals can be in one of three states: 

Inactive ( I) , i.e., individuals who may be exposed or not to the disinformation item but have not posted about it, 

Spreader ( S) , i.e., individuals who re-share / post the disinformation item, or 

Denier ( D) , i.e., individuals who publicly denounce the disinformation item. Individuals are connected through a network that represents the connections between users in online social platforms. The parameter PSS determines the probability of re-sharing the disinformation once seen, which is related to the intrinsic attractiveness or appeal of the disinformation item. Since the information is controversial, there is also a probability PSD ≤ 1 − PSS that the user denounces it publicly (i.e., transition from I to D). Users can analogously be exposed to this counter–information and relay it, or even publicly support the original information when seeing the counter–information (i.e., backfire). # 3 Experimental Results 

In this section, we aim to understand the behaviour of the model and the possibil-ity and size of cascades under different parameter regimes. We run experiments on two different network topologies: an Erd˝ os–R´ enyi (ER) random graph and an empirical Twitter network. Due to its simple topology, experiments on the ER graph serve as a baseline for understanding the spreading dynamics while remov-ing some of the isolated effects of the network topology. The empirical Twitter network provides a more realistic topology of online connectivity patterns, char-acterised by a wide heterogeneity in node connectivity — most individuals are connected to a few others while some individuals have thousands of connections — and other node correlations. We use an undirected version of the Twitter network available at SNAP 1 [15], which consists of a single component, 81 ,306 nodes, and 1 ,342 ,310 edges. Our experiments include results from the mean–field approach — using the average degree ⟨d⟩ for the ER graph and the empirical de-gree distribution pd from the Twitter network — complemented by Monte–Carlo simulations of the spreading dynamics. ER graphs were built of size N = 10 4

and with an edge probability so that the average degree matches that of the Twitter network: ⟨d⟩ = 33 .02.

3.2 The presence of counter–information 

We next examine how the presence of counter–spreaders — or deniers of the disinformation — affects cascade sizes. We run experiments with fixed values of the leakage parameter at λ = 0 .25 (i.e., cross–conversions happen at 1 /4 of the probability of relaying the message) and a fixed initial fraction of seed spreaders at S0 = 10 −3 for different values of PSS and PDD , and compare them to the scenario of no deniers ( λ = 0) for reference. We first examine the case of the ER graph (Fig. 2, top row). When the spread of counter–information is in the sub-critical regime (i.e., PDD ⟨d⟩ = 0 .5 < 1), the final cascade is reduced by about 25% compared to the case of λ = 0. This reduction results from the cross-conversion due to leakage, where that lost amount directly matches with the final number of deniers ( D∞/N ), who got converted after receiving the disinformation item but whose denouncing mes-sage did not cascade. When spreading values are in the super-critical regime (i.e., PSS · ⟨ d⟩ > 1), the final cascade sizes of spreaders ( S∞/N ) are further reduced as PDD · ⟨ d⟩ increases, albeit modestly. On the contrary, an opposite effect emerges in the sub-critical regime of spreaders ( PSS · ⟨ d⟩ < 1): the final cascade of spreaders ( S∞/N ) reach fractions up to 30% of the population while cascades would not trigger if deniers were not present (i.e., if λ = 0). These cases result from the creation of cascades of denouncing messages reaching significant parts of the population, while they also foster cross–conversion to spreaders of the disinformation. Therefore, even when the presence of deniers may generally reduce the size of the cascade of spreaders, they also risk helping the message reach parts of the population where it would not have reached otherwise and find supporters there, creating a backfire effect. Mean–field and Monte–Carlo results agree to a lesser extent in this case, possibly due to finite–size effects. Much greater deviations can be seen in the sub-critical regime, where the distribution of cascades is bimodal: if none of the initial spreaders converts a neighbour into a denier in the initial rounds, the cascade of deniers will not trigger and the cascade of spreaders will die out. However, when running experiments using the more degree–heterogeneous Twitter network, the sub-critical region shrinks, reducing the possibility of a backfire effect (Fig. 2, bottom row). In line with the results from Fig. 1, cascade sizes on the Twitter network are generally smaller, and less affected by the presence of deniers. 

3.3 Limited attention span and recommendations boosts 

While a user may be connected to many other users, meaning that their posts arrive to the user’s feed, the user will typically not see all of them, given that they will spend limited time using the platform and may only browse through the top posts of their feed [14]. On the other hand, the presence of contacts who are not posting about the disinformation item may also have an effect, as they also are generating content and therefore the probabilities of seeing the controversial information from the activated agents diminish. We include these effects of limited attention by assuming that the probability of reading a post from another user is inversely proportional to the number of connections, as the other connections may be posting about other topics. In the model this translates to modifying the transition probabilities to make them node–dependent, by adding a multiplicative factor based on the inverse of the node degree di, i.e. P (i) = P/d i. # 4 Discussion 

Reflections on the model. We have proposed a model of disinformation cascades that includes three important features of controversial information in online social media: counter–information that emerges from the disinformation cascade and that may feed back into it, heavy–tailed connectivity of the social network, and limited attention, which recommendation algorithms may in turn mitigate. The model accommodates a different appeal of disinformation and its debunking (PSS versus PDD ) and the possibilities of opposing the received message (via leakage λ), while remaining analytically tractable: the heterogeneous mean–field approximation agrees well with Monte–Carlo simulations in most regimes. Through our simulations on synthetic Erd˝ os–R´ enyi graphs and an empirical Twitter network, we have examined how these three features and their inter-action affect the spread of disinformation stemming from a few seed accounts. First, the network structure shapes both whether and how far cascades spread. Notably, the full dynamics on the Twitter network are very similar to those from the mean–field approximation, which accounts only for its degree distri-bution and is otherwise random, implying that the degree heterogeneity is the main driver of these network differences. On the degree–heterogeneous Twit-ter network, cascades trigger at a much lower appeal than on the ER graph (PSS ⟨d⟩ ≈ 0.19 instead of ≈ 1, as predicted by the factor ⟨d2⟩/⟨d⟩ in R0) and depend less on the number of initial seeds, as hubs are reached within a few steps and relay the item to large audiences — consistent with the popularity of online content being largely driven by its largest broadcast [7]. However, poorly con-nected users have fewer chances of being exposed and often remain unreached. Heterogeneous networks are thus easy to ignite but hard to saturate. Second, counter–information has double–edged effects on the reach of disin-formation. When the disinformation item can cascade on its own, deniers mod-erately reduce the number of spreaders, mainly by diverting exposed users from relaying the item. When it cannot, a super-critical cascade of denouncing mes-sages may carry it to parts of the population it would not otherwise reach, where some exposed users become spreaders. Debunking may thus become a vehicle for the message it opposes, as a direct consequence of the parasitic coupling between both cascades [10]. This backfire effect is mitigated by degree heterogeneity, which shrinks the region where the item is sub-critical, so the risk of amplifying items that would otherwise fade out is highest in more homogeneous networks. False news has been found to spread farther and faster than the truth [19], which makes the scenario of debunking having a higher appeal than the disinforma-tion item to be unlikely globally. However, susceptibility to disinformation varies across the network, so locally super-critical counter–information may still arise in communities that debunk more actively. Third, limited attention cancels the network effects from having a hetero-geneous degree distribution, much like degree–dependent weights restore the epidemic threshold of scale–free networks [12]. Limited attention thus acts as a natural brake on virality, consistent with most online information failing to spread widely [14,20]. However, recommendation algorithms that preferentially Modelling the complex dynamics of online disinformation cascades 11 

surface novel and emotionally arousing content may remove this brake: increas-ing algorithmic mediation progressively restores the influence of hubs and lowers the point at which cascades trigger. Although our stylised model can help understand how some features of the social media landscape affect disinformation spread, it has yet to be validated against empirical data — e.g., by calibration on observed distributions of cascade sizes [5,7]. Further features of the online information environment could also be added and studied, such as heterogeneous activity rates of platform use [3] or individual heterogeneity in the susceptibility to believe the disinformation [17]. Additionally, more sophisticated seeding strategies of disinformation could be tested, such as the targeting of high–degree nodes or simulating the attacks from coordinated inauthentic accounts and malicious bots [18], as well as the simulation of interventions that would limit their effect [1]. Our results suggest that the vulnerability of online social networks to dis-information cascades is not determined by content appeal alone, but emerges from the interplay between network heterogeneity, limited attention, algorith-mic curation, and the counter–information that disinformation itself provokes. A better understanding of these mechanisms can inform the design of platforms and regulations that limit the potentially harmful impact of disinformation.
