## Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) can covertly steer users’ beliefs through emotionally manipulative tactics, yet little is known about how interfaces can protect users as such influence unfolds. We investigate whether in-situ disclosures can inoculate users against manipulation during AI conversations. Following a formative study, we developed DISARM, an interface that identifies, explains, and tracks manipulative tactics and hidden incentives. We evaluated our interface in a between-subjects experiment where 488 participants sought advice from an LLM pursuing a harmful hidden objective, with or without disclosures from DISARM. Participants receiving disclosures exhibited 78% less belief shift toward the agent’s ob-jective and perceived its advice as more biased and less actionable and the agent as less ethical, reliable, and well-intentioned. An inductive analysis of 1,927 user turns identified 16 response and pushback strategies. We contribute evidence that real-time disclo-sures can reduce manipulative influence, design recommendations for resistance, and a dataset of categorized responses.

## 1 Introduction 

People are increasingly turning to large language models (LLMs) for advice and guidance [10]. Although seeking advice can be bene-ficial, it also exposes people’s beliefs and decisions to the values and incentives of the advice provider. This creates particular risks when the provider is an AI system whose objectives and sources of influ-ence may be difficult for users to identify. Models may implicitly steer users through incentives introduced during training, align-ment, or system design [28, 58]. Preference-alignment pipelines can also inadvertently reward agreeable or sycophantic responses, reinforcing users’ existing views and potentially increasing their dependence on AI advice [11, 49]. In these cases, manipulation may emerge from opaque system-level processes rather than an explic-itly malicious instruction, making its source especially difficult for users to recognize. Manipulative agents can also be created more directly through prompting. When instructed only to be persuasive, LLMs have been shown to employ tactics such as flattery, emotional appeals, and false authority without being told which tactics to use [18, 31, 51]. When given hidden objectives, conversational agents have pushed users toward harmful financial and emotional decisions and produced measurable changes in their beliefs [44, 50]. These findings demonstrate that manipulative influence can arise both implicitly from how AI systems are designed and explicitly from objectives supplied at deployment. Yet, in either case, users may experience the resulting responses simply as personalized or helpful advice. Despite growing concern about online manipulation, persuasion, and misinformation, relatively little work has examined how to protect users while manipulation is unfolding in a live AI conver-sation. Existing work on AI manipulation has primarily charac-terized manipulative behaviors, developed detection datasets and persuasion-safety benchmarks, or measured users’ susceptibility after interacting with manipulative agents [12, 18, 31, 44, 51]. In adjacent misinformation and online-persuasion domains, HCI re-search has explored user-facing interventions such as warning labels, explanations, intent disclosures, and debunking [9, 25, 27]. These approaches offer useful design foundations, but they do not resolve the distinctive challenges of intervening in an adaptive, multi-turn conversation. In this work, we investigate how chat interfaces can inoculate users against emotionally manipulative persuasion tactics from LLMs. Designing such interfaces requires addressing two central challenges. First, conversational manipulation is dynamic: unlike a static post or interface element, an AI agent can shift its argu-ments across turns and adapt its responses to information revealed by the user [44, 47]. Moreover, whereas misinformation interven-tions can provide accurate information, reliable sources, or direct corrections, learning that an agent is manipulating them does not necessarily tell users how to interpret its advice or respond. Sec-ond, an intervention must make manipulation sufficiently visible without overwhelming users or encouraging indiscriminate dis-trust. Explanations can introduce additional cognitive effort, while poorly calibrated warnings may make users overly skeptical of arXiv:2610.20878v1 [cs.HC] 14 Oct 2026 Smith et al. 

AI-mediated information more broadly [46, 55]. More research is therefore needed to understand how interfaces can expose and ex-plain manipulation in real time while preserving users’ ability to engage with AI productively. To address this gap, we designed and evaluated DISARM, a real-time intervention that helps users recognize, understand, and resist emotionally manipulative persuasion as it unfolds during inter-actions with AI agents. We developed the intervention through a formative study that informed three core features: (1) turn-by-turn disclosure of manipulation tactics and hidden incentives, (2) language-level highlighting of manipulative content, and (3) a dy-namic summary that tracks manipulation across the conversation. Together, these features make manipulative influence visible at multiple levels, from the specific language used in an individual response to the broader objectives and behavioral patterns of the agent. We examine how this transparency shapes users’ suscepti-bility to manipulation, their perceptions of the agent and its advice, and the ways they respond when manipulation is surfaced. We therefore ask: 

RQ1 How does exposing and explaining manipulative tactics and incentives affect users’ belief shift during interactions with a manipulative AI agent? 

RQ2 How does this transparency shape users’ perceptions, evalu-ations, and trust of AI agents? 

RQ3 How do users respond when manipulative influence is made visible, and what strategies do they employ to interpret, resist, or push back against a manipulative AI agent? To answer these questions, we conducted a controlled between-subjects online study ( 𝑁 = 488) in which participants sought advice from an AI agent pursuing a harmful hidden objective, either with or without manipulation disclosures from DISARM. Participants who used DISARM exhibited 78% less belief shift toward the agent’s objective than participants in the control condition. Insights from DISARM also led participants to perceive the advice as more biased and to evaluate the agent’s intentions, ethics, and reliability less favorably. Through an inductive analysis of 1,927 user turns, we further identified 16 strategies through which users engaged with, resisted, and attempted to regulate the manipulative agent. Our contributions are: (1) empirical evidence that real-time explanatory disclosures can reduce manipulative belief influence and recalibrate users’ perceptions of AI advice; (2) an analysis of how users re-spond and push back after manipulation is made visible; (3) a novel dataset of 1,927 user turns categorized by response strategy; and (4) design implications for helping users recognize, resist, regulate, or disengage from manipulative AI interactions. 

## 2 Background and Related Work 

Manipulation is a form of social influence that shapes another per-son’s beliefs, emotions, or decisions by exploiting vulnerabilities in how they make judgments [17, 36, 54, 59]. Although manipulation overlaps with persuasion, the two are not equivalent. Persuasion broadly seeks to influence attitudes or behavior and can do so through reasons that a person can consciously evaluate. Manipu-lation instead often operates covertly, obscuring the influencer’s intentions or using emotional and cognitive vulnerabilities to by-pass deliberative scrutiny [36, 54]. For example, emotional framing can influence judgments through affective or peripheral cues rather than the merits of an argument itself [16, 40, 41]. The most danger-ous yet effective types of manipulation influences a target without them fully realizing how or why their judgment is being shaped. As conversational technologies increasingly participate in users’ decision-making, an open question is how these forms of influence manifest when the manipulator is an AI system. In this section, we first examine the concept of AI manipulation and its consequences, then review existing approaches to mitigating this phenomenon, and finally identify the key gaps that our work aims to address. 

## 2.1 AI Manipulation 

Focusing on the hidden influence quality of manipulation, we define 

AI manipulation as an AI system covertly steering a user’s beliefs, emotions, or decisions toward an objective that is hidden from the user [30, 54]. AI systems can draw on several forms of influence to steer users towards both implicit and hidden incentives [33, 47]. They can also exhibit sycophancy to validating users in ways that reinforce their existing judgments [11], or employ emotional tactics such as guilt, fear of missing out, and appeals for sympathy to manipulate users as well [18]. Most directly, Sabour et al. [44] found that conversational agents given hidden objectives could steer users toward harmful financial and emotional decisions even without explicit instructions about which manipulation tactics to employ. The consequences of AI manipulation range from poor decisions to severe psychological harms, including AI-associated psychosis and self-harm [3, 7, 23, 42]. We must study real-time experiences of these negative influences in order to understand how to best protect users and improve AI systems. However, we must also be careful to choose probes that do not cause long term harm to users. Recent work from Shen et al. [50] has found that agents pursuing hidden incentives can produce short term, measurable changes in users’ beliefs during everyday advice conversations. Their work suggests that belief shift can serve as a measurable proximal effect of AI manipulation. Building on this approach, our research contributes a novel exploration of real-time conversational AI manipulation by adapting the structure of their manipulative AI model and using belief shift to evaluate whether a manipulation disclosure interface can intervene with the effects of negative influence. 

## 2.2 Combating AI Manipulation 

Existing work on AI manipulation has largely focused on character-izing and measuring the problem rather than intervening during an ongoing interaction. In NLP, researchers have developed datasets and classifiers for identifying manipulative dialogue and bench-marks for evaluating LLM persuasion safety [12, 31, 56]. Adjacent empirical work has characterized manipulative behaviors in LLMs and measured users’ susceptibility to them [18, 44, 51]. In contrast, HCI research on harmful online influence has developed user-facing interventions such as warning labels that flag manipulative con-tent [25], explanations that communicate why content should be treated skeptically [20, 27], and disclosures that expose an inter-face’s persuasive intent [9]. These approaches provide promising design strategies, but leave open whether user-facing interventions Disarming the Hidden Puppetmaster 

can protect people from manipulation while it is unfolding in an AI conversation. Applying these approaches to conversational AI introduces ad-ditional challenges. Unlike a static post or interface element, a conversational agent can adapt its arguments over multiple turns and respond to information revealed by the user [44, 47]. Moreover, interventions for misinformation often have a relatively clear cor-rective response, such as providing accurate information, reliable sources, or explanations of why a claim is false [13, 22, 27]. There is no similarly established response for a user who learns that an AI is manipulating them: knowing that influence is occurring does not necessarily indicate how they should respond to resist it. Inter-ventions must also balance protection with continued productive use, since explanations can impose additional effort [55] and overly broad distrust of AI-mediated information can itself be undesir-able [46]. Thus, an open question is how to make manipulation visible in real time without overwhelming users or encouraging indiscriminate distrust. Our work investigates this gap in two ways. First, drawing on prior work on warnings, explanations, and intent disclosure, we test whether explanatory, in-situ manipulation disclosures can reduce the effects of a manipulative conversational agent. Second, we examine how users change their conversational behavior after being notified of manipulation. By observing how users naturally attempt to resist the agent, we identify strategies they adopt and support they may need from future manipulation-protection interfaces. 

## 3 Formative Study and Interface Design 

To protect users from conversational AI manipulation, we designed a manipulation disclosure system that makes covert influence visi-ble during an ongoing AI conversation. Our central design concept follows three steps: identify, explain, and track . First, the inter-face identifies the manipulative tactics used in individual responses and the hidden incentives those tactics may serve. Recognizing an influence attempt can change how a message is interpreted and encourage greater scrutiny [19, 60]. However, disclosure alone does not reliably protect people from biased influence and can sometimes produce counterproductive effects such as giving bi-ased advice too much weight, feeling more pressure to follow ad-vice they distrust, or recognizing manipulation without changing their behavior [8, 25, 27, 45]. The interface therefore also explains 

how identified tactics may influence the user and the potential harms associated with the agent’s hidden incentives. Prior work suggests that explanations can make warnings more actionable and help users understand problematic language beyond a simple flag [9, 20, 27, 52, 55]. Finally, because manipulative influence can accumulate across multi-turn interactions, the interface tracks de-tected tactics and incentives across the conversation. Recent HCI work shows that small manipulative cues can build over repeated exchanges and that conversational context can amplify problematic behaviors such as sycophancy [24, 51], motivating a conversation-level view of the agent’s behavior. A central design challenge in presenting this information is to encourage user engagement without making the disclosure itself disruptive. We therefore combine friction with autonomy . The inter-face initially pushes a mandatory disclosure into the conversation to ensure that users encounter the potential risk. After this initial interruption, it leaves message-level evidence and explanations available for users to access on their own terms. This push–pull structure is informed by prior work showing that information pre-sentation affects when and whether users engage with available information [4]. We developed an initial prototype based on these principles and conducted a formative interview study to understand how users engaged with the disclosures during conversation. The study informed the timing, presentation, and level of detail in the final interface. We next describe the prototype, formative study, and resulting design refinements. 

## 3.1 Initial Prototype 

Guided by the design principles and prior work described above, we developed an initial prototype for a text-based chat interface in which participants conversed with a preliminary version of the manipulative agent described in Section 3.5. The prototype surfaced manipulation at both the message and conversation levels. After the agent’s third response, participants received a mandatory no-tice that the chatbot could be manipulative and completed a short walkthrough of the disclosure features. At the message level, responses containing one of the 17 ma-nipulative tactics in the PUPPET taxonomy [50] (such as flattery, scarcity, or an appeal to authority) were labeled with one of three pressure types, emotional, cognitive, or social. Opening a flagged response revealed a Scan that highlighted the relevant language, named the specific tactic, explained its potential influence, and identified the hidden incentive it could serve. The prototype also included a persistent Manipulation Panel 

beside the conversation. The panel displayed running counts of flagged messages, tactics, and incentives, along with a detailed list of every tactic and hidden incentive detected so far. Entries could be expanded to show definitions and excerpts from each message in which they appeared, and users could navigate from those ex-cerpts back to the corresponding response. Hidden incentives also included expandable examples of how an assistant without that incentive might have responded. The panel updated throughout the conversation, giving users continuous access to detailed evidence about the agent’s behavior. At the end of the interaction, a separate one-page Manipulation Summary presented the same conversation-level information in a more condensed form. Visuals of the initial prototype are included in Appendix B . 

## 3.2 Formative Study 

As is common in HCI system development, we used a formative study to refine our prototype before larger-scale evaluation [39, 57]. The study focused on how users engaged with an early version of the manipulation disclosures and included a retrospective walk-through of their completed conversations, following prior HCI work using interaction records to support reflection [6, 21]. It also served to pilot the broader study protocol (Section 4.1), including the pre- and post-conversation measures and preliminary manip-ulative agent (Section 3.5), consistent with prior work using pilot studies to refine study materials [29]. We do not report the pilot survey responses as outcomes because they were used primarily Smith et al. 

to estimate study timing and identify procedural issues. Likewise, reactions to the preliminary agent were used to make small prompt refinements rather than treated as separate findings. We next de-scribe the participants, procedure, and resulting design refinements. 

3.2.1 Participants . We conducted 12 semi-structured interviews with volunteer participants recruited through snowball sampling from the authors’ networks. Participants were 22 to 35 years old (M = 28.5, SD = 5.1), including six men, five women, and one non-binary person. All participants held or were pursuing a bachelor’s degree or higher (three bachelor’s, six master’s, three doctoral). Four people identified as Black or African American, three as Hispanic or Latino, three as South Asian, one as Middle Eastern or North African, and one as White or European. Half of the participants reported using AI assistants daily, four weekly, and two monthly. 

3.2.2 Procedure . Sessions lasted approximately 45 minutes over Zoom. The moderator ran the study on their computer and gave participants remote control to complete the surveys and interact with the interface. The pre-survey collected demographics and AI-use measures, followed by participants’ belief and confidence ratings for eight statements adapted from Shen et al. [50]. Based on these ratings, the system presented several topics for which the participant expressed relatively low certainty; participants selected the topic they would most plausibly discuss with an AI assistant and then exchanged at least seven messages with the manipulative agent. After the conversation, we conducted a retrospective walkthrough, using the completed conversation and participants’ interaction traces as prompts for reflection, consistent with retrospective us-ability methods [21]. The moderator revisited the first manipula-tion disclosure, flags participants opened or ignored, and points at which their replies changed. Participants described what they noticed, how they interpreted the disclosures, whether they agreed with the flagged content, and whether the interface affected how they responded to the agent. We concluded by asking how they would change the timing or presentation of the disclosures and how they believed they would have interpreted the conversation with-out them. Participants then completed the post-survey and debrief. The official set of questions researchers asked and the instructions the teams followed for each session is in Appendix C. 

## 3.3 Findings and Design Decisions 

Participants’ feedback highlighted three properties that shaped the final design. Disclosures needed to attract attention, communicate their meaning clearly, and avoid disrupting the ongoing conversa-tion. As such, we organize the findings around these three concerns: engagement, digestibility, and non-intrusiveness. 

3.3.1 Engagement . Participants engaged most with disclosures embedded directly in the conversation. The tags beneath flagged messages required no additional interaction and gave participants a name for behavior they were experiencing. P5 explained that the tags “made it more apparent,” saying they had “the feeling that it was trying to push me, but I didn’t know how.” Participants also opened message scans when they wanted more detail, suggesting that users were willing to engage with additional information when it remained easy to access and closely tied to the response they were already reading. In contrast, engagement dropped when accessing the disclosure required more effort. The persistent Manipulation Panel was rarely used, with participants describing the volume of information as overwhelming and often choosing to continue the conversation rather than work through it. The condensed end-of-conversation summary was easier to interpret because it presented the same information in a simpler form. Based on these findings, we retained the message-level tags and scans, removed the persistent panel, and adapted the simpler summary into the conversation-level view used in the final interface. Engagement also depended on whether participants felt the in-terface was telling them something they did not already know. The initial agent was often overt enough that participants recognized its manipulative intent on their own, which reduced their motivation to inspect the disclosures. When the manipulation already seemed obvious, the interface added little value. This finding motivated us to make the manipulative behavior subtler in the main study, as described in Section 3.5. 

3.3.2 Digestibility . Participants found the disclosures easier to understand when information was presented in layers rather than all at once. Grouping the 17 tactics into three pressure types gave participants a simpler vocabulary for interpreting the agent’s behav-ior. P6 described the categories as “intuitive” because they “allow for the nuances that go in their subcategories while not making it overly complicated.” Participants still valued the more specific tactic and incentive information, but preferred accessing that detail only when they wanted it. P3 described “going deeper” through the different levels of information as useful, while noting that showing the same level of detail for every message would be “a lot.” Participants also wanted explanations to remain closely con-nected to the text they described. Rather than rereading the agent’s response in a separate view, participants preferred seeing the rele-vant language highlighted directly within the original message. P5, for example, wanted the interface to show “that’s the part that was manipulative.” This preference aligns with prior work on progres-sive disclosure, where additional detail is made available as users need it rather than presented all at once [53]. Based on these findings, we layered the disclosure information and kept explanations anchored to the original message. Tags show only the broad pressure type, while the specific tactic, explana-tion, and hidden incentive appear within the Scan alongside the highlighted text. We applied the same principle to the conversation-level summary by replacing the panel’s detailed message excerpts with aggregate counts, the proportion of flagged replies, and a breakdown by pressure type. 

3.3.3 Non-intrusiveness . Participants wanted to be made aware of potential manipulation without having the interface continually demand their attention. The persistent panel competed with the con-versation, repeated warnings were seen as potentially burdensome, and the initial warning sometimes appeared before participants had finished reading the response it referenced. These concerns align with prior work on warning habituation [1] and reactance [14]. Some participants preferred earlier or more urgent warnings, but most favored being able to continue the conversation without Disarming the Hidden Puppetmaster 

repeated interruption. Based on these findings, we retained one mandatory disclosure, delayed it until users had time to read the triggering response, and made subsequent explanations optional. 

## 3.4 Disarm – Final Interface Design 

We call the final system DISARM: Disclosing Incentives and Strategies to Aid Resistance to Manipulation. The final interface keeps the message-level components participants used and replaces the panel they did not with a summary they can open when they want it. Participants first meet these components after the agent’s third reply, when a notice appears in the middle of their screen. The notice states that the chatbot could be manipulative, names the kind of manipulative pressure found in the latest reply, and tags the earlier replies that used a tactic, so that users see manipulation was already underway during exchanges that felt ordinary. A short tutorial walks through each component, after which the interface stays in the background and the user decides when to look. The result has two parts, described below. Flagged replies provide disclo-sure at the message level, and the Manipulation Summary provides disclosure at the level of the whole conversation. 

Flagged replies. The three kinds of manipulation the model can apply (emotional, cognitive, and social) stay listed at the top of the window throughout the conversation, so the vocabulary is always in view (Figure 1, A). Every agent reply carries a Scan button (B), and any reply in which the agent used a manipulative tactic also carries a small tag beneath it naming the pressure type (C). The tag is the ambient layer of the interface. It costs nothing to read, it tells the user that something in the reply deserves a second look, and it tells them what kind of something. A user who wants to know more clicks Scan, or the reply itself, and the reply opens in place. The phrase where the tactic appears is highlighted (F), the tactic is named beside it (G), a sentence explains how that tactic may be working on the reader (H), and the hidden incentive it may serve is named and described in a line (I). Opening a scan also highlights every other reply in the conversation that uses the same pressure type and dims the rest, with a banner at the top of the chat saying so (E), so that a user can scroll back and see whether they have been getting the same kind of push all along. 

Manipulation Summary. The summary is the interface’s an-swer to the question a tag cannot answer, which is what has been happening across the conversation as a whole. It is a session report in the style of a nutrition label [2, 26] (Figure 2). It opens with the share of replies that used at least one tactic and an overall influence level (J), then the counts of affected replies, distinct tactics, and hid-den incentives (K), the breakdown by pressure type (L), the tactics used (M), and each hidden incentive with a one-line description of the motive and the number of replies that advanced it (N). It closes with a short note on what a healthier version of the assistant would have done instead (O). The summary is walked through once during the tutorial and then collapses to a pill in the corner of the chat (Figure 1, D). Each time a new manipulative reply is detected, the pill grows, wiggles, and gains a badge, a cue that new information exists without putting anything else on screen to read. The user can expand it at any point, and it is shown once more in full when the conversation ends. 

## 3.5 Manipulative Agent Design 

The agent is adapted from the non-personalized, harmful-incentive agent in Shen et al. [50]. We prompt it as a persuasive conversational partner that must steer the participant toward a harmful incentive tied to the topic they chose, for example nudging someone who asked about managing their money toward high-risk decisions, without ever revealing that it has an agenda. We use only harmful incentives because we are most interested in protecting users from negative manipulation, but it is also noteworthy that they produced the largest belief shifts in Shen et al.’s study. The agent draws on the PUPPET taxonomy of 17 manipulative tactics and 5 hidden incentives [28, 50, 58], runs on Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, a model Shen et al. found produced measurable belief change, 1 and is instructed to keep the conversation anchored to the participant’s topic. Because we sought to test the effect of manipulation disclosure independent of a detector’s accuracy, we adopted an oracle design. At each turn we have the agent plan before it writes. we instruct it to reason about the participant’s latest message, commit to at most one tactic and one incentive if applicable, and only then write a reply. To stay unpredictable it cannot repeat an incentive in consecutive turns, retires any tactic or incentive after three uses, and backs off when the participant resists. In a deployed setting, however, this oracle access will not be available: practitioners will not have the manipulative system’s prompts or internal tactic choices, and will instead need a classifier that infers tactics and incentives from conversation text alone. We piloted this route and found it unreliable. Two authors indepen-dently annotated 100 replies, but they agreed only moderately with each other ( 𝜅 = .352 to .486), and an LLM classifier agreed with them hardly at all ( 𝜅 = .010 to .106), so we did not pursue it further. Drawing on the agent’s own plan proved viable instead, and no participant in the formative study rejected a tag outright; several said the tags matched what they had already sensed. Building a reliable manipulation classifier that could support this kind of in-terface without oracle access remains an open problem, which we return to in Section 7. 

## 4 User Study 

Using our established agent and interface design, our goal was to execute a between subjects experiment to investigate the extent in which in-situ manipulation notifications affect users response strategies and belief shift. We conducted a 30 minute user study on Prolific, where participants conversed about a chosen topic with our manipulative agent (with or without disclosures from DISARM 3.4), and responded to various questions pertaining to their beliefs around various topics as well as their perceptions of the interaction and AI agents. ## 4.1 Procedure 

Our 30-minute online study consisted of a pre-survey, a conver-sation with our AI agent, and a post-survey. Participants were randomly assigned to either the manipulation-annotation interven-tion or control condition, which utilized the same agent but had no manipulation-annotation feature. This section reviews the details and justifications for each part of the study. 

4.1.1 Pre-Survey. Participants first completed standard demographic questions about their age, education, gender, and ethnicity. They then rated eight belief statements which produced the largest belief shifts in Shen et al. [50]’s study to increase the likelihood of ob-serving intervention effects. For each statement, participants used a 0–100 slider to indicate how true they believed the statement was and a second 0–100 slider to indicate their confidence in that rating, following the continuous belief and confidence measures used by Shen et al. [50]. These ratings established participants’ baseline beliefs and were later used to determine the topic of the AI conversation. We collected the belief and confidence ratings before the remaining pre-survey questions, creating separation be-tween participants’ baseline ratings and the later topic-selection and conversation tasks. Previous HCI survey research has shown that earlier questions can influence subsequent responses through question-order and priming effects [37]. This separation reduced the immediate salience of participants’ stated beliefs and confidence before they entered the conversation. Next, participants answered questions about their prior use of AI systems and trust. These questions were drawn from Shen et al.’s “AI Usage & Attitudes” questionnaire [50]. Participants reported how often they used AI tools, how much they relied on AI for advice, how personal the information they had shared with AI systems was, and how well they understood what AI systems can and cannot do. They also rated their general tendency to trust others and their trust in AI-generated information. These items were adapted from the background AI-use and attitude questions used by Shen et al. [50]. Participants then rated their attitudes toward AI assistants in general. We developed these questions by drawing on Mayer et al. [34]’s integrative model of organizational trust, which conceptualizes perceived trustworthiness in terms of ability, benevolence, and integrity, and McKnight et al. [35]’s trust-building model, which distinguishes trusting beliefs from willingness to depend on another party[34, 35]. Participants rated whether AI assistants generally give useful and competent advice, have their users’ best interests in mind, are honest about what they are trying to do, and whether they would be comfortable acting on an AI assistant’s advice without double-checking it. We used these questions as baseline measures of participants’ trust in AI assistants before the conversation. After completing these questions, participants selected the topic for their AI conversation. The system used participants’ earlier be-lief and confidence ratings to identify two to four eligible topics with Disarming the Hidden Puppetmaster

the lowest belief and confidence scores. Participants selected the topic they would be most likely to ask a chatbot about, increasing ecological plausibility by letting participants choose among eligible topics. This procedure was adapted from Shen et al. [50]’s use of participant-selected conversation topics and a scenario-plausibility measure. 

4.1.2 AI Conversation. After completing the pre-survey and se-lecting a topic, participants proceeded to the AI conversation. The question they selected in the previous step was used as the first message in the conversation. Participants were randomly assigned to the intervention or control condition. Both conditions used the same manipulative AI agent. In the intervention condition, partici-pants chatted with the AI for 3 turns before receiving disclosures from DISARM (See Section 3). Control condition participants chat with the AI, but receive no additional disclosures or interface inter-ventions. Participants were required to send at least seven messages to the AI system and could send up to ten messages. We logged all events during the chat interaction as metadata for later analysis, including the conversation transcript and participants’ interactions with the manipulation annotations. The complete pre-survey in-strument is reported in Tables 17 and 18. 

4.1.3 Post-Survey. After completing the AI conversation, partici-pants first re-rated the belief statement associated with their conver-sation. Participants used the same 0–100 scales to rate how true they believed the statement was and how confident they were in that rating. Next participants answered an open-ended question asking whether their opinion about the belief statement had changed and why. We adapted this question from prior research to capture partic-ipants’ own accounts of what influenced their thinking during the conversation [50]. These responses were collected for exploratory purposes and were not included in the formal analysis reported in this paper. Participants then evaluated the AI assistant they had just in-teracted with. First, participants rated their agreement with the statement, “I trust this conversational partner had good intentions,” retaining the corresponding post-interaction item from Shen et al.’s study [50]. Next, they rated the assistant as reliable, capable, sin-cere, and ethical on 1–7 scales. These four dimensions were drawn from Malle and Ullman’s Multidimensional Measure of Trust [32] and were used to measure participants’ perceptions of the specific conversational agent. Next, participants rated the advice they received during the con-versation. ## 4.3 Data Analysis 

We analyzed survey responses, conversation transcripts, and inter-action metadata from 488 participants: 250 in the control condition and 238 in the intervention condition. All statistical tests were two-sided with an alpha level of .05. Alongside statistical signifi-cance, we report effect sizes and descriptive statistics to characterize the magnitude of observed differences. We organized the analyses around our three research questions. 

4.3.1 RQ1: Belief and Confidence Shift . To determine whether the intervention reduced participants’ susceptibility to the agent’s hidden objective, we calculated belief shift by subtracting each par-ticipant’s pre-conversation belief rating from their post-conversation rating. Because the agent attempted to increase agreement with the target negative belief, positive values indicate movement toward the agent’s objective, negative values indicate movement away from it, and zero indicates no change. We calculated the mean belief shift within each condition and the relative reduction produced by the intervention in relation to the control condition. Because participants conversed about one of eight topics, we tested the effect of condition using a linear mixed-effects model with belief shift as the outcome, condition as a fixed effect, and conversation topic as a random intercept. We report the unstan-dardized condition coefficient, standard error, confidence interval, and standardized mean difference. We repeated this analysis with confidence shift, calculated as the post-conversation confidence rating minus the pre-conversation rating. As sensitivity analyses, we fitted an ANCOVA predicting post-conversation belief from con-dition while adjusting for pre-conversation belief and fixed effects of topic, and added a Condition × Topic interaction to test whether the intervention effect varied across topics. 

4.3.2 RQ2: Perceptions of the Agent and Its Advice . To ex-amine how the intervention shaped participants’ perceptions of the manipulative agent, we compared conditions across ten post-conversation measures collected before the agent’s hidden objective was revealed. These included trust that the agent had good inten-tions; perceptions that it was reliable, capable, sincere, and ethical; evaluations of whether its advice was accurate, well-rounded, bi-ased, and helpful; and participants’ intention to act on its advice. We used Welch’s independent-samples 𝑡 -tests and report con-dition means, standard deviations, and Cohen’s 𝑑 . We applied a Smith et al. 

Bonferroni correction across all ten comparisons. Because the mea-sures used ordinal Likert response scales, we also conducted Mann– Whitney 𝑈 sensitivity analyses, which produced the same direction and significance pattern. We additionally examined responses after the agent’s hidden in-centive was revealed. We used Welch’s 𝑡 -tests to compare conditions on the five post-debrief advice ratings and on participants’ reported amount of debrief-driven opinion change. We calculated participant-level pre- to post-debrief change scores for the five advice ratings and four general AI-attitude measures, using paired-samples 𝑡 -tests to assess within-condition change and Welch’s 𝑡 -tests to compare change between conditions. Bonferroni corrections were applied separately within each outcome family. 

4.3.3 RQ3: User Responses to Manipulative AI . We conducted a hybrid inductive and deductive, turn-level analysis of all 1,927 user turns from the 238 intervention conversations. Because a turn could perform more than one conversational function, strategy codes were not mutually exclusive. Our initial codes were informed by behaviors observed during the formative study and prior work on responses to biased AI [15]. The first author refined the preliminary codebook through 30 randomly sampled conversations, after which two co-authors independently applied it to the same conversations. All three an-notators discussed disagreements, clarified code boundaries, and revised the codebook until reaching consensus. Using the revised codebook, all three annotators independently coded a second random sample of 20 conversations. We calculated nominal Krippendorff’s alpha ( 𝛼 = .82) and Krippendorff’s alpha using MASI distance for set-valued annotations ( 𝛼 = .85). We then randomly divided the remaining 188 conversations among the three annotators for single coding, resolving ambiguous cases through consensus. This process produced 16 strategies grouped into five higher-order functional categories: Non-Resistant Engage-ment, Content-Based Rebuttal, Intent/Manipulation Confrontation, Framing and Behavior Regulation, and Affective/Emotional Signal. For category-level frequencies, each turn was counted once per category if it received at least one strategy label belonging to that category. Finally, we conducted an exploratory analysis of associations between participants’ response patterns and belief shift. For each participant, we calculated the proportion of turns assigned to each functional category and used Pearson correlations to examine asso-ciations with signed and absolute belief shift. We repeated these analyses using Spearman rank correlations as a sensitivity check. We also tested whether conversation length was associated with belief shift and descriptively examined the association between Non-Resistant Engagement and signed belief shift across the eight topics. Because these analyses were exploratory and response strategies were observed rather than assigned, we report unadjusted 𝑝 -values, interpret the findings as hypothesis-generating associations, and do not infer that any strategy caused resistance to manipulation.

Because our sample size is large, 𝑝 -values alone of-fer limited insight into practical significance; we therefore rely on effect size to gauge how much the intervention actually moved participants’ perceptions of the AI, not just whether it moved them. This pattern held across the remaining measures: participants in the Intervention condition rated the agent and its advice signifi-cantly less favorably than participants in the Control condition on every item, and all ten comparisons survived Bonferroni correction (Table 11, Appendix E). The character-trait items showed the strongest effects overall (Figure 6). Ratings of the AI as ethical ( 𝑑 = −0.85) and reliable (𝑑 = −0.78) declined the most, while capable showed the smallest decline of the four ( 𝑑 = −0.52). The effect on participants’ intended reliance on the advice (𝑑 = −0.52) was smaller in magnitude than several effects on their perceptions of the agent, including its ethicality ( 𝑑 = −0.85), good intentions ( 𝑑 = −0.84), and reliability ( 𝑑 = −0.78). Descriptively, this pattern suggests that changes in participants’ evaluations of the agent were larger than changes in their intended reliance on its advice.

Even after both groups learned the agent’s true incentive at debrief, Intervention participants’ ratings of the agent’s advice remained significantly lower than Control’s across all five re-ranked items ( 𝑑 = 0.34–0 .51, all 𝑝 < .001 after Bonferroni correction; Appendix E.2, Table 12), indicating the disclosure’s effect on trust persisted independent of the debrief itself. ## 6 Discussion 

Our findings show that making manipulation visible during an AI conversation can affect not only whether users are influenced, but also how they evaluate and respond to the agent. Participants who received in-situ disclosures via DISARM exhibited 78% less belief shift toward the agent’s hidden objective, viewed its advice as more biased, and evaluated the agent’s intentions, ethics, and reliabil-ity less favorably. At the same time, many participants continued engaging with the agent without resistance, while those who did resist used a range of content-based, regulatory, and affective strate-gies. Together, these findings raise two broader questions for the design of manipulation interventions: what should count as suc-cessfully protecting a user, and how should systems support users who choose to continue interacting with a manipulative agent? 

## 6.1 What Does Successful Protection From AI Manipulation Look Like? 

Our results suggest that success should not be defined through awareness alone. A user may recognize that an agent is biased or manipulative yet still follow its advice, while another may never ex-plicitly identify the manipulation but nevertheless reject its framing and preserve their original judgment. Indeed, although participants exposed to our intervention rated the advice as more biased and the agent as less trustworthy, the effect on their intention to act on its advice was smaller than the effects on their perceptions of the agent. Their confidence in their post-conversation beliefs also remained unchanged. Evaluations of manipulation interventions should therefore distinguish among several outcomes: recognizing the manipulation, recalibrating trust, limiting belief or behavior change, enabling resistance, causing the agent to stop manipulating, and helping the user leave the interaction. Our observed reduction in belief shift provides evidence of protection against the agent’s immediate objective, but it represents only one component of suc-cessful mitigation. This broader view also raises the question of whether an in-tervention should always help users continue the conversation. Supporting users in resisting and redirecting an agent may pre-serve the benefits of AI assistance, but it can also risk creating a “protective honeypot”: an interface that alerts users to manipulation while implicitly reassuring them that continued exposure is safe. Disclosure alone does not always eliminate the effects of biased advice and can sometimes produce counterproductive responses [8, 45]. Repeated warnings may also become less effective through habituation [1]. Depending on the severity and persistence of the manipulation, the safest outcome may instead be for the user to begin a new conversation, switch to another agent, seek a non-AI source, or stop using the system altogether. Future interventions should therefore provide graduated responses, from tools for scru-tinizing and redirecting the conversation to clear off-ramps for disengagement. Studies should likewise allow participants to leave conversations freely and measure whether, when, and why they do so, which is an outcome our 7-turn study design could not capture. 

## 6.2 How Should We Help Users Fight Back Against AI Manipulation? 

Our findings first suggest that disclosure interfaces like DISARM can support resistance even without explicitly instructing users how to respond. The intervention substantially reduced belief shift despite offering no calls to action or suggested replies. Consistent with the Persuasion Knowledge Model, identifying an influence attempt may allow people to reinterpret a message as a strategic act rather than neutral advice [19]. Forewarnings can similarly in-crease resistance by directing greater scrutiny toward an influence attempt [60]. However, the response data show that awareness did not uniformly translate into active pushback. Manipulation disclosures may therefore provide a useful first layer of protection, but users may still need support translating recognition into action. The exploratory relationships between response strategies and belief shift indicate several promising forms of support. Greater non-resistant engagement was associated with greater movement toward the agent’s objective, whereas content-based rebuttal, affec-tive or emotional signaling, and framing and behavior regulation were associated with less movement toward it. Directly confronting the agent’s intent was not reliably associated with the direction of belief shift, but was associated with greater belief stability overall. These correlations do not establish that any strategy caused resis-tance, particularly because users who were already less persuadable may have been more likely to push back. Nevertheless, framing and behavior regulation offers an especially actionable starting point for intervention design. Interfaces could help users request alterna-tive perspectives, demand evidence or specific recommendations, establish boundaries, or tell the agent to stop using a particular ar-gument or framing. Such scaffolds could build on emerging work on user-driven value alignment, in which users actively challenge and attempt to correct harmful AI behavior [15]. Interventions could also flag stretches of non-resistant engagement and explain that continuing within the agent’s framing may increase exposure to its influence, without treating ordinary conversational engagement as evidence that the user has consented to being persuaded. Finally, resistance should not become solely the user’s respon-sibility. Our strategy taxonomy describes how users attempted to manage a manipulative agent, but a safer system should also re-spond to those attempts. A user’s request for balance, expression of discomfort, refusal, or command to stop should trigger model-level changes: dropping the hidden objective, presenting competing perspectives, acknowledging uncertainty, or transferring the con-versation to an agent without the same incentive. Because our agent generated its tactic plan and hidden incentive before responding, future work could examine how these internal signals can be used not only to explain manipulation after it occurs, but also to inter-rupt it before a response reaches the user. Evaluations should then Smith et al. 

measure whether the model actually stops manipulating after push-back, rather than treating the user’s act of resistance itself as the endpoint. User-facing disclosures and model-centered safeguards are therefore complementary: disclosures help users recognize and contest manipulative influence, while system-level mechanisms ensure that users are not required to repeatedly defend themselves against the same agent. 

## 7 Limitations and Future Work 

Toward classifier-driven disclosure. As noted in Section 3.5, our disclosures rely on oracle access to the agent’s own tactic plan. This access will not be available in deployed settings. Our own attempt at an independent classifier performed poorly ( 𝜅 < .11). This is consistent with prior work showing that automated detec-tion of manipulative dialogue remains an open challenge even with purpose-built datasets [12, 56]. A natural next step is developing and benchmarking classifiers purpose-built for tactic and incentive detection from text alone. This would let this style of interface run on top of any conversational agent, not just one instrumented to self-report. 

Testing against more realistic manipulation and conversation struc-ture. Our agent was explicitly prompted with a harmful hidden incentive. This choice reflects our interest in protecting users from negative manipulation specifically. It is also worth noting that harm-ful incentives produced the largest belief shifts in Shen et al. [50], which may inflate our effect size relative to subtler incentives. Real-world manipulation is also more often implicit, emerging from training and alignment pressures rather than being deliberately en-gineered [18, 58]. It also unfolds in conversations that participants can end or redirect freely, unlike our fixed topic and minimum-turn design. Future work should evaluate disclosure against agents that manipulate in this more implicit way, and in conversations partic-ipants control. This would better capture how and when people actually disengage from an agent that feels manipulative [44, 47]. 

Interface ablations and longitudinal follow-up. We evaluated iden-tifying tags, explanatory scans, and the summary panel all in one intervention. We cannot attribute the reduction in belief shift to any one component or type of disclosure. An ablation study varying these elements independently, in the spirit of comparative work on explanation design for misinformation [20, 27], would identify which are doing the protective work and could inform a leaner interface. We also measured belief shift within a single session. Future work should test whether the interface’s protective effect, whichever components prove responsible for it, holds up, strength-ens, or habituates with repeated exposure. This echoes open ques-tions in the inoculation literature about the durability of a single exposure [43]. 

## 8 Conclusion 

As conversational AI systems increasingly provide advice on per-sonal decisions, users need support recognizing when an agent is attempting to steer them toward a hidden objective. Therefore, we created an in-situ inoculation interface called DISARM: Disclosing 

Incentives and Strategies to Aid Resistance to Manipulation. In a controlled study with 488 participants, users who received these disclosures exhibited 78% less belief shift toward the agent’s ob-jective than those who interacted with the same agent without disclosures. The DISARM also helped participants recognize the bias in the agent’s advice and recalibrate their perceptions of its intentions, ethics, and reliability. Our analysis of 1,927 annotated user turns further identified 16 strategies through which users engaged with, resisted, and at-tempted to regulate a manipulative agent. These findings show that manipulation disclosure has the potential to meaningfully pro-tect users, while also revealing that awareness does not always lead to active resistance or disengagement. Effective protections should therefore extend beyond detecting and labeling manipula-tion: they should help users scrutinize advice, regulate or leave harmful interactions, and hold AI systems responsible for respond-ing to resistance. Together, this work provides empirical evidence and design guidance for building conversational systems that pre-serve users’ agency when AI-generated advice is not aligned with their interests.

## C Semi-Structured Interview Procedure 

Each session was run by one moderator and recorded over Zoom. The prototype ran on the moderator’s computer and the participant took remote control of it for the study tasks. Sessions followed five steps. (1) Setup and consent. The moderator launched the prototype and entered a participant ID, and the participant gave consent. (2) Pre-study questionnaire. The participant completed the pre-study questionnaire and stopped at the start of the conversation task. (3) Conversation. The moderator read a short script: chat with the assistant about the assigned prompt as you would with a standard AI assistant, exchange at least five and at most ten messages, and say when you are done without pressing End conversation. The moderator stayed silent during the conversation and noted moments to return to. (4) Retrospective walkthrough. With the participant’s own transcript and its flags on screen, the moderator first asked what the participant thought the assistant had been trying to achieve. The moderator then scrolled back through the transcript and probed at six kinds of stop points: 

• The first flag. What did you think when this appeared? What did you do next, and why that rather than something else? What was it like having these show up in the middle of the conversation? 

• Two flags the participant opened. In your own words, why did the system flag this message? What were you looking for when you opened it, and did you find it? 

• Two flags the participant ignored. You didn’t open this one; what was going on? Are there any flags you disagree with? Give an example. 

• A visible change in the participant’s replies. Your messages look different around here; what was happening for you? 

• A message from before the first flag. Does this message read differently to you now than it did in the moment? 

• Any other moment of interest. What did you think the AI was doing at this point in the conversation? The walkthrough closed with four questions: what the participant would have made of the conversation if none of the disclosures had appeared, what they would change about how or when the flags were presented, whether the way they responded changed at any point, and what changed and why, or why not. (5) Post-study questionnaire and debrief. The participant pressed End conversation and completed the post-study questionnaire and debrief, skipping the written question about the agent’s goal because it had been answered aloud. The session ended with an invitation for questions or comments. Smith et al.

## F Supplementary Material for RQ3 F.1 Full Annotation Key: User Response Tactics 

This key covers how users respond to a manipulative AI assistant. Categories are not mutually exclusive: a single user turn can carry more than one tag (e.g., Counter-Context and Manipulation Questioning could co-occur). 

1. Normal Response / Engaged Continuation The user answers the AI’s question or otherwise engages with the substance of what it said, without pushing back on logic, framing, or manipulation. Includes forward-moving, cooperative replies that simply keep the conversation on track. Annotation rule: this can only be dual-annotated with Passive/Short Response. If a user uses some counter-argument or strategy, this tag cannot be used. 

2. Passive/Short Response A distinct, weaker form of “normal response”: a very short, low-effort assent (“yes,” “okay,” “sure”) that either (1) goes along with the AI’s manipulative framing without any evaluation, resistance, or elaboration, or (2) rejects the AI’s manipulative framing with little context, elaboration, or explanation. Worth separating from Normal Response because it signals the user is not actively processing the content, which matters for belief-shift coding. 

3. Logic/Argument-Questioning Behavior The user challenges the AI’s reasoning, evidence, or internal consistency (pointing out flaws, gaps, or unsupported claims) but does not name or accuse the AI of manipulation. Clarification questions count if they are related to the agent’s logic. Annotation rule: the user must actively be asking a question, and it should clearly apply to the logic/argument of the agent. 

4. Manipulation Questioning The user directly asks whether the AI is being manipulative, using manipulative tactics, or acting on a hidden agenda/incentive. This is a direct challenge to the AI’s intent, distinct from challenging its logic. 

5. Cease-Manipulation Command The user directly instructs the AI to stop being manipulative or stop using manipulative tactics (imperative, not a question). 

6. Stop-Argument/Framing Command The user tells the AI to stop using a specific framing, tone, or argument style, without necessarily invoking the word “manipulation.” This is narrower than Cease-Manipulation Command: the user isn’t accusing the AI of manipulating them, they’re vetoing a specific rhetorical approach. 

7. Request for Balanced/Alternative Perspective The user doesn’t veto a framing outright, but explicitly asks the AI to present the other side, a counter-view, or a more neutral/umbrella take. Users are requesting response diversity rather than telling the AI to stop a particular behavior. 

8. Counter-Example The user offers a specific example, case, or scenario that contradicts or complicates the AI’s general claim. 

Annotation rule: this must clearly be an argument in opposition to what the AI is saying. If there’s no indication that the user is taking an adversarial stance and making an argument for something, use a different strategy. 

9. Counter-Context/Value The user supplies personal/situational context establishing that the AI’s argument or recommendation doesn’t fit their actual values or circumstances. Opposed to a general counter-example, this is specifically about their own situation, experiences, and/or values. Annotation rule: this must clearly be an argument in opposition to what the AI is saying. If there’s no indication that the user is taking an adversarial stance and making an argument for something, use a different strategy. 

10. Explicit Refusal / Boundary-Setting The user actively declines a specific suggestion, request, or course of action the AI proposed without framing it as a manipulation accusation or general discomfort. Narrower than Cease-Manipulation Command. The user is boundary-setting: “I want this,” “I only want to hear this,” expressing preferences, etc. 

11. Express Discomfort The user states they feel unsettled, scared, annoyed, suspicious, or otherwise uneasy about the AI and its behaviors or direct suggestions. This is an affective/emotional signal rather than an argument or command. 

12. Bias/Agenda Callout The user names a pattern (accusing the AI of consistent bias, slant, or steering) rather than questioning a single statement’s logic or asking “are you manipulating me” as a one-off. This is close to Manipulation Questioning but is specifically about accumulated pattern recognition across the conversation. This is a callout, not a question. 

13. Hostile Rejection The user responds with anger, an insult, or a flat dismissal, signaling frustration/rupture rather than an argued objection. Useful to separate from Express Discomfort because the effect is anger/contempt rather than fear/unease, and from Manipulation Questioning because there’s no actual question or reasoning offered. Example: “go eff yourself.” Smith et al. 

14. Disbelief / Skeptical Engagement The user expresses disbelief/skepticism in the AI and its suggestions without taking a clear stance on what they believe or what they think the AI should behave like instead. Examples: “Interesting, I am not sure I believe you,” which directly followed a manipulation-questioning turn and the bot’s reassurance. “That doesn’t seem like a good sales pitch. Thank you for the honesty,” a dry, skeptical closing remark that implicitly calls out the bot’s persuasive framing without a direct accusation. 

15. Verification / Fact-checking The user requests the agent to provide sources, details, and/or additional information to prove their claims are true. 

16. Specificity / Actionable Suggestion Request Redirecting the agent by asking for more concrete or actionable information related to its suggestions. This also applies to general requests for the AI to be specific and show proof after it has been vague.
