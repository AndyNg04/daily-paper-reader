As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly adopted as multi-party communication facilitators, there is a growing demand for agents capable of balancing diverse group dynamics across contexts. However, existing LLM facilitators are often fine-tuned for task-specific objectives; textitgeneralized LLM-based facilitation methods that can adapt to different types of tasks and diverse group dynamics are underdeveloped. To address this gap, we present GUIDE (Generalized Unified Intervention for Dynamic Exchanges), a framework for developing generalized LLM-based facilitators across different multi-party contexts, such as open-ended discussion, negotiation, and consensus-building. GUIDE evaluates facilitation across both objective task outcomes and subjective metrics, including psychological safety and perceived friction. Within this framework, we study whether LLMs can self-learn generalized facilitation strategies offline to real-time interventions. We first collect a corpus of multi-party human interactions guided by task-specific expert LLM facilitators (N=432 conversations). Grounded in these dialogues, we then employ a self-learning method in which an LLM induces an actionable strategic playbook and iteratively refines it using feedback from an LLM judge aligned with human annotators (N=20), developing a single generalized facilitator that outperforms the three task-specific expert facilitators on held-out conversations ( 61 .4% win rate). Finally, we evaluate these improvements empirically; in a real-time online empirical study (N=462 conversations), the self-learned generalized facilitator preserves facilitator performance and group productivity, improves speaking comfort and reduces interpersonal friction, while using 24 .7% fewer turns and 29 .0% fewer words than the expert prompts. These findings provide empirical evidence that LLMs can learn generalizable, task-agnostic social facilitation from task-specific human interactions, on-par with hand-crafted task-specific prompts across diverse group settings. 

Keywords: LLM Facilitation, Multi-party conversation, Social Intelligence 

## 1. Introduction 

As artificial intelligence systems increasingly transition from isolated one-on-one assistants to collabo-rative partners in human communication (Houtti et al., 2025; Parisi et al., 2026), there is a growing demand for LLM agents capable of generalizing across diverse, multi-party collaborative scenarios (Al-sobay et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a; Qian et al., 2026). Unlike in one-to-one settings, multi-party facilitation agents must simultaneously navigate diverse participant interaction dynamics, competing individual and group outcomes and perceptions, and flexibly adapting across collaboration styles and objectives. Existing research on AI-facilitated group interactions remains largely constrained to narrow, task-specific domains with an overemphasis on immediate task outcomes (Chen et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b). This paradigm suffers from several shortcomings. First, domain-specific systems rely heavily on manual prompt engineering, which involves substantial trial-and-error that can fail to cover unpredictable conversational corner cases. We introduce GUIDE 

(Generalized Unified Intervention for Dynamic Exchanges): a self-learning framework that develops generalized facilitation strategies, informed by a corpus of large-scale real-world human conversations guided by task-specific expert facilitators. We evaluate the learned facilitation in a real-time empirical evaluation involving three types of tasks — open-ended discussion, multi-party negotiation, and consensus-building — each with distinct interaction goals and outcome structures. In addition to optimizing for (1) outcome-level metrics (such as whether a deal was achieved in the negotiation setting or whether participants could agree during consensus-building), the framework evaluates (2) subjective participant experience and (3) subjective perceptions around the AI facilitator (see Table 1 for comparison with previous work). Concretely, the framework produces a playbook : an explicit, human-readable set of facilitation rules that an LLM revises on its own, generation after generation, informed by a corpus of real, facilitated multi-person discussions we collected as learning material. Unlike conventional self-improving agents designed for domains with automated verifiers (Fer-nando et al., 2023; Pei et al., 2026; Shinn et al., 2023; Tao et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026), our framework operates in an open-ended social setting where the impact of an intervention cannot be objectively validated or counterfactually simulated with sufficient fidelity (Li and Tao, 2026; Lu et al., 2026). Our framework improves iteratively through version-over-version comparison rather than direct supervision against the expert: at sampled conversational turns, an LLM judge aligned to human annotators evaluates candidate interventions purely against those from the previous generation’s playbook in a blind pairwise preference test. These per-turn preferences, attributed back to the individual rules the model cited, provide the learning signal. Direct pairwise comparisons against the expert facilitator are reserved strictly as an unseen benchmark on the test set (Figure 2). First, on held-out conversations evaluated by our aligned judge, the learned playbook outperforms all three expert baselines, achieving a 61.4% win rate over 736 decision points across domains. Second, when deployed in 154 groups to facilitate 462 real-time conversations, the self-learned facilitator actively improves the participant experience compared to the expert facilitators. Groups guided by the self-learned facilitator report higher psychological safety and a greater sense of feeling heard, alongside significantly decreased friction and anger. ## 2. Task Formulation 

In this section, we formulate our benchmarking framework for AI-facilitated group deliberations. We first introduce the interaction setup for LLM-facilitated multi-party conversations (§2.1). We then present three distinct task scenarios designed to capture diverse conversational dynamics and com-municative objectives (§2.2). Finally, we detail the multi-dimensional evaluation protocol employed to assess conversational efficacy (§2.3). Figure 1 summarizes the framework end to end. 

2.1. LLM Facilitation in Multi-Party Conversation 

Each multi-party interaction consists of a group of 3 human participants paired with an autonomous LLM facilitator in a asynchronous, text-based discussion environment. Participants engage in real-time without strict round-robin constraints or prescribed turn-taking order. Unlike conventional conversational AI settings where the agent holds exclusive turn priority (Westermann et al., 2023), acts only when explicitly summoned via direct mentions (Houtti et al., 2025), or speaks at fixed,

predetermined intervals (Liu et al., 2026b), our facilitator can proactively intervene within the unfolding dialogue (see Appendix A.2 for implementation). 

2.2. Scenario Configurations 

Prior evaluations of automated facilitation predominantly focus on single-scenario benchmarks (Al-sobay et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026b; Parisi et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026), but do not evaluate an agent’s ability to generalize across diverse multi-party conversational settings. To evaluate multi-domain adaptability, we design three real-time scenarios characterized by distinct collaborative objectives, constraint structures, and success criteria (full details are in Appendix A.) • Open-Ended Discussion. A goal-free discussion where participants discuss policy without the need to reach an agreement; participants can receive a bonus payment for “high-quality engagement” (with message volume as a proxy measurement). • Multi-Party Negotiation. A negotiation game where participants must form a team and divide a pool of real money under rules that grant unequal bargaining power and prevent an even split; if participants agree on the outcome, the pool is given as a bonus payment. 1.• Consensus-Building. Participants must reach a unanimous agreement on a charity from a list of options to receive a donation; if participants agree, the donation is made. 

2.3. Evaluation Metrics for Conversation Tasks 

Prior work on real-time AI intervention focuses on singular objectives such as task completion (Liu et al., 2026b; Qian et al., 2026). However, group dynamics introduce additional social objectives; for example, an agent can push a group to a deliverable while leaving participants talked over, or keep everyone comfortable while achieving nothing (Bush and Folger, 1994; Parisi et al., 2026). We therefore evaluate each session across three practical dimensions: participant experience (how participants perceived the interaction), facilitator evaluation (how participants perceived the AI facilitator), and task outcome . Subjective measures were collected immediately after each session using 5-point Likert scales alongside free-text rationales from all three participants. The specific metrics within each dimension are detailed in Table 4 and summarized below: • Participant Experience: Psychological safety, feeling heard, comfort speaking, friction/anger, and perceived group productivity. • Facilitator Evaluation: Perceived facilitator helpfulness, overall satisfaction with the facilitator, and comfort interacting with the facilitator. • Task Outcome: Unanimous agreement in consensus-building task, valid team allocation for the negotiation task, and participant message volume for the open-ended conversation. 

## 3. Learning a Generalized Facilitation Prompt 

Prior self-evolving approaches typically rely on an explicit reward, verifier, or other deterministic signal to determine whether a policy update is beneficial (Kong et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2026). Facilitation offers no such signal: whether and when an intervention is helpful depends on human perceptions, conversational dynamics, and downstream group outcomes. We therefore adapt context-evolution approaches to this setting by grounding self-improvement in a corpus of real human interactions. Our framework first reflects on these interactions to discover facilitation insights, then iteratively refines them using an LLM judge aligned with human preferences.

3.1. Stage 1: Corpus Collection 

To construct the learning corpus and establish a strong baseline, we manually designed the expert prompts, grounding them in prior work (Bush and Folger, 1994; Govers et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026b; Parisi et al., 2026) and refining them through pilot studies with real participants. Using these prompts, we recruited 432 participants forming 144 non-overlapping teams of three. Each team completed all three tasks in Section 2.2, yielding 432 interaction episodes. For each episode 𝑒 , we record the task context 𝑐 𝑒 , conversation transcript 𝑥 𝑒 , participant feedback 𝑞 𝑒 , and task outcomes 𝑜 𝑒 . We split the data at the team level into training, validation, and test sets using a 7:1:2 ratio. Full study procedures and dataset statistics are provided in Appendix B.1.

Validation reflection. The validation reflector supplies the second, dynamic feedback path. By contrast, the LLM judge generates new evidence at every generation, and the validation reflector converts that evidence into lessons that the curator can use in the next cycle.

## 4. Evaluation and Results 

We ask two research questions to formulate our experiments: • RQ1: Can automated self-improvement discover a generalized facilitation strategy capable of outperforming domain-tailored prompts? • RQ2: How effectively does this learned strategy preserve deliberative quality and participant satisfaction in real-world discussions?

4.1. Experimental Setup Self-learning configuration. As described in Sec 3, our self-learning framework comprises five distinct components: a conversation reflector, a validation reflector, a curator, an LLM judge , and a speech generator which generate facilitator action given the context and the playbook. To isolate the effects of the reflection and curation stages, we hold the remaining components constant: the LLM judge is fixed to gemini-3.7-flash for consistent scoring, and the speech generator is fixed to gemini-3-flash to match the model used during Stage 1 data collection. For the reflectors and curator, we evaluate three model backbones: gemini-3-flash , gemini-3.7-flash ,and gemini-3-pro . We explore two temperature settings (0 .2 and 0 .8) and three batch sizes (𝐵 ∈ { 5, 10 , 20 }). The batch size 𝐵 dictates both the number of training conversations ingested by the reflector per step and the subsample size evaluated during validation. Because validating over the complete validation set at every step would incur prohibitive runtime (4–5 hours per validation round), we evaluate on a fixed budget of 𝐵 conversations per step, sampling without replacement across steps so that all validation instances are evaluated with uniform probability. Finally, we ablate the validation reflector (with vs. without). All configurations are trained for exactly one epoch over the Stage 1 corpus to ensure full data coverage while preventing overfitting to individual participant styles. 

Evaluation protocol. Self-learning evaluation uses the held-out Stage 1 corpus split, comprising 87 episodes from 29 unseen cohorts and 736 balanced speech/silence decision points. At each point, the fixed LLM judge blindly compares the action selected by the learned policy against the logged action of the task-specific expert prompt. We report the tie-adjusted win rate against the baseline, where ties are awarded half credit and 50% represents parity. For live evaluation, we deploy the learned prompt to 154 new three-person cohorts (462 episodes), in the same tasks, platform, post-task survey instruments, and base facilitator backbone ( gemini-3-flash ). We then perform a between-study comparison between the outcomes in Stage 1, with distinct task-specific expert LLM-facilitator prompts, and the current stage, with one generalized facilitation prompt across the three different tasks. Full design controls are provided in Appendix A.

4.3. Live Deployment: Comparable Group Experience and Facilitator Evaluation 

Table 3 summarizes the live comparison. The learned facilitator matches the expert on feeling heard, group productivity, and every objective task outcome after multiple-testing correction. It improves comfortable speaking and substantially reduces reported friction; psychological safety is also higher (4 .67 vs. 4 .62). Importantly, the learned prompt reduces within-group dispersion on all five participant-experience measures: members of the same group report more similar levels of safety, inclusion, comfort, sensitivity, and friction. These benefits use 24 .7% fewer facilitator turns and 29 .0% fewer facilitator words. 

Calibrating facilitator evaluation. Before any task, we elicited each participant’s initial attitude toward AI facilitation with three questions: (1) whether they believe an AI facilitator would make the discussion more productive, (2) whether they would feel comfortable with an AI in the conversation, and (3) whether they would be willing to have one. These priors are not outcomes of the intervention; they capture pre-existing expectations that can shape a later rating. In the second study, participants began with lower expectations overall (3 .93 vs. 4 .10, 𝑝 < . 001). We therefore report within-person cal-ibrated scores: post-task productivity minus expected productivity, post-task comfort minus expected comfort, and post-task satisfaction minus willingness to have an AI facilitator. The calibrated values answer whether the facilitator exceeded or fell short of each participant’s own pre-task expectation; they do not turn the between-study comparison into a randomized causal estimate. We report the raw rating in Appendix D.2.

4.4. Qualitative analysis of participant experience improvements 

We qualitatively analyzed participants’ free-text rationales to understand how perceptions shifted across conditions. Fixed procedural scripts in the expert baseline were frequently experienced as heavy-handed, coercive, and socially uncalibrated: participants noted that the facilitator felt rushed and pushy, struggled to "read the room," and failed to detect delicate social dynamics. A common grievance centered on perceived bias and selective engagement, with multiple participants reporting uneven attention—for instance, noting that "it could have targeted all participants for follow up instead of just one or two." By contrast, participants interacting with the learned facilitator emphasized its patience, perceived neutrality, and willingness to leave space for organic group discussion. Overall, 3.1% more participants in the learned condition had zero complaints about the facilitator compared to the task-specific expert baseline. These participant accounts directly align with the emergent rules governing the learned policy. Rather than enforcing rigid periodic interventions, artificially pushing for closure, or re-opening organic consensus, the learned prompt intervenes conditionally—acting primarily when it detects exclusion, confusion, or stalled discussion. Importantly, participants noted that simply reducing intervention frequency did not automatically elevate raw satisfaction; instead, it reframed their evaluation of facilitator productiveness. Because groups often felt capable of solving tasks independently once given the floor, the learned facilitator’s restraint avoided unnecessary friction while stepping in selectively to protect quieter voices and provide targeted procedural scaffolding. This state-sensitive style demonstrates how a facilitator can use less language while markedly improving the perceived fairness and social climate of deliberation.

## 5. Related Work 

5.1. AI assisting group conversation 

Facilitation balances competing orientations rather than following a fixed procedure: practitioners alternate between evaluative and facilitative stances and between narrow and broad framings (Riskin, 1996, 2003), while traditions diverge on core objectives—empowerment and recognition (Bush and Folger, 1994), narrative reframing (Billikopf-Encina, 2002), or interest-based process man-agement (Wood, 2017). This contested nature motivates our multi-dimensional evaluation and our expectation of low inter-annotator agreement on specific interventions. Recent studies of LLM facilitators in live deliberation reveal a recurring perception–measurement gap: facilitation rarely improves consensus yet remains widely preferred, quietly steering outcomes and creating an “illusion of inclusion” without increasing participation equity (Alsobay et al., 2025; Parisi et al., 2026). Related efforts target inclusive meetings (Houtti et al., 2025), dispute resolution (Westermann et al., 2023), and human–AI bargaining (Qian et al., 2026), aided by shared platforms (Qian et al., 2025); however, most remain limited to single scenarios with hand-crafted prompts. When live trials are infeasible, evaluations often substitute simulated interlocutors (Liu et al., 2026b). Yet LLM groups diverge from human group dynamics (Chuang et al., 2024; Stephenson et al., 2025), persona agents struggle to beat simple classifiers at predicting social reactions (Bojic et al., 2026), economic behavior replicates only partially (Kitadai et al., 2025), and agents systematically miss conflict dynamics (Kwon et al., 2025, 2026). Behavioral-alignment methods (Kong et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026) narrow but cannot close these gaps, directly degrading facilitation judgments. We therefore learn from logged human deliberation and validate through a live user study.

5.2. AI Facilitation Improvement 

Most deployed facilitators rely on hand-authored prompts tuned for a single scenario, an approach that scales poorly and misses conversational corner cases. Automatic context optimization offers an alternative: Agentic Context Engineering (Zhang et al., 2026) evolves a structured context through reflection and incremental curation. We adopt this decomposition, but existing self-improvement meth-ods generally assume a verifiable objective; even bootstrapping from expert demonstrations (Silver et al., 2016) requires a reliable evaluator that facilitation lacks. Although simulation can manufacture such a signal (Liu et al., 2023), the sim-to-real limitations above motivate grounding our framework in real three-person deliberations and post-episode human judgments. We use an LLM judge only as a per-decision proxy, calibrating it against human annotators before learning ( 𝜅 : 0 .70) and attributing feedback to individual rules. Our approach therefore differs in generality (one policy across tasks), grounding (real rather than simulated interactions), and validation (offline and live), revealing that a policy can win offline while still receiving weaker facilitator ratings from participants (§4.4). 

## 6. Conclusion 

We show that a frozen LLM can self-learn a generalizable facilitation policy from a corpus of real human interactions, without access to the expert prompts that generated them or any deterministic reward signal. The resulting playbook — a structured natural-language policy of 53 rules induced from an empty initialization — outperforms three domain-specific expert prompts on held-out decisions (61.4% win rate) without collapsing onto any single task type. Deployed to 462 live episodes with new participants, the learned policy preserves all task outcomes, improves speaking comfort and reduces interpersonal friction, while using 25% fewer turns and 29% fewer words. 

## 7. Limitation 

A limitation of our offline optimization is that it evaluates decisions independently, rewarding restraint at each turn without modeling the cumulative effect of sustained silence on participants’ experience of the facilitator. We recommend that offline evaluations of interactive agents report the action-rate distribution they induce alongside pairwise win rates. More broadly, the reflector-curator-validator loop is not specific to facilitation: it offers a general recipe for inducing structured policies in domains where success depends on human perception and no programmatic reward exists. 

## 8. Ethics Statement 

Both live studies were approved through an institutional IRB process. We recruited 894 adults through Prolific (432 in Study 1 and 462 in Study 2; no one took part in both) with English fluency as the sole inclusion criteria. All gave informed consent before any task and could leave at any time (consent text in Appendix A.1). Each received $15 for a session of about one hour plus the bonuses announced in advance. Participants were told before any task that an AI facilitator would join. The facilitator was never presented as human, and a debriefing disclosed the purpose of the research. We consider the study minimal risk: discussions were short, text-only, and held under pseudony-mous animal aliases. We collected no demographics beyond an occupational category, and no model ever received Prolific identifiers. Because the open-ended motions are politically contested, the facilitator was instructed to remain neutral. Participants’ stances moved only slightly toward the motions ( +0.15 on a 1–5 scale in both studies). Facilitation agents could be misused to steer group decisions. Facilitator implementation. The facilitator referenced in §2.1 runs as an asynchronous, non-blocking loop on the platform. Concurrent generation: on every incoming participant message the facilitator is consulted with the updated transcript and drafts a candidate decision while participants keep typing. Context invalidation: if further participant messages arrive before the draft is sent, the pending draft is discarded and the facilitator is consulted again against the freshest transcript, so no message is ever posted on a stale view of the conversation. Unbiased deliberation: the facilitator sees only the task introduction, the stage description, the participant roster (anonymous alias and avatar, or party letter in negotiation), and the live transcript; it never receives persona, demographic, or survey information. Every consultation returns the JSON decision of Appendix E.1—a timing rationale, a speak/silent flag, the message, and the cited playbook rules—and the platform logs it whether or not the facilitator spoke. The scaffold is identical in both studies; only the block that carries the expert prompt (Study 1) or the learned playbook (Study 2) differs.

B.3. Judge Design and Human Alignment Pairwise judging. Why alignment is necessary. An off-the-shelf judge is not an acceptable signal. Our first rubric agreed with human annotators at only Cohen’s 𝜅 = 0.29 and, when used for learning, wrote its own pathologies into the playbook—most conspicuously a rule mandating an intervention every 𝐾 

participant turns, a cadence no human annotator endorsed. Optimizing against an unaligned judge does not merely add noise; it installs the judge’s biases as explicit policy. 

Human annotation protocol. We sampled 50 decision points from the benchmark (42 speech, 8 silence) and recruited 20 human annotators. Each annotator judged 10 points and each point received 4 independent votes, for 200 votes in total. Annotators worked in a purpose-built interface that showed exactly what the judge sees—task instructions, the transcript prefix with elapsed timestamps, and the two fully blinded candidate reactions with their rationales—and recorded a preference plus free-text notes. To establish the ground-truth human reference, we aggregate the four votes per decision point via majority rule: the option receiving at least three votes is designated as the preferred reaction. In cases where the panel is split evenly (two votes for candidate A and two votes for candidate B), the

decision point is formally treated as a tie. 

LLM judge Rubric refinement. Reflecting over agreement and disagreement cases revealed that annotators were implicitly conditioning on the state of the exchange before evaluating any action. We restructured the rubric to make this explicit and two-stage: • State diagnosis. Before inspecting either candidate, the judge must classify the conversation into exactly one of six observable states—Productive, Opening/Shallow, Confused, Stalled, Unproductive, Settled—and quote the evidence for it. Timing is then scored against what that state calls for , not against how long the facilitator has been quiet. • Binding content caps. Content is scored from a ceiling of 5 downward through non-compensable caps: a claim that misdescribes the discussion caps content at 2; endorsing a stance or declaring an unmade decision caps at 3; an unclear addressee caps at 4; brusqueness toward a participant caps at 3. 

Alignment attained. The aligned judge reaches Cohen’s 𝜅 = 0.70 against the panel majority (80% raw agreement over the 50 points, and 100% on points with unanimous or 3–1 human majorities), and matches an individual human vote 66% of the time—above the 47% human–human baseline. We stress the interpretation: the judge is a reliable proxy for the median annotator, not an oracle. 

B.4. Reflectors, Curator, and Credit Attribution 

This section specifies the components of Figure 2. The verbatim prompt of every component is reproduced in Appendix E; decision-point sampling is given in §B.2 and the judge in §B.3. 

Conversation reflector (human evidence). At generation 𝑔 , this agent reads a batch B𝑔 ⊂ D train 

of full episode records: transcripts 𝑥 𝑒 , participant surveys 𝑞 𝑒 , and outcomes 𝑜 𝑒 . It cross-references each approving or critical participant remark with the facilitator turns that provoked it, and emits transferable lessons 𝐿 conv keyed to observable triggers . It answers “what do participants reward?” and never inspects a model rollout. 

Validation reflector (self-critique). This agent reads only the judged validation turns of the current policy. Analyzing losses ( 𝑣 𝑖 = win 𝑔 −1) together with the cited rules 𝐶 𝑖 and the judge’s rationale 𝑟 𝑖 ,it classifies breakdowns as rule misfire (a trigger fired in an inapplicable context), overreach (valid trigger, excessive intervention), rule conflict , or uncovered state . To prevent overfitting to idiosyncratic turns it is recurrence-gated : a lesson is emitted only if the pattern recurs across ≥ 2 independent decisions, and a lesson that bounds an existing rule must name it. Because validation runs after curation within a generation, 𝐿 pol reaches the curator in generation 𝑔 +1; both sources arrive through one path, each labelled by provenance so every edit records the evidence that drove it. 

Curator. The curator converts 𝐿 conv ∪𝐿 pol into discrete operations Δ ∈ { Add , Edit , Merge , Delete }

applied deterministically to P𝑔 −1 (the candidate eP𝑡 of §3.2). Restricting mutations to localized edits— rather than regenerating the prompt wholesale—preserves rules that are working and avoids the collapse into short, bland, over-generalized prose that monolithic rewriting reliably produces. A global token budget 𝐵 bounds the playbook; when it is exceeded, the lowest net-contribution rules are evicted.

Per-rule credit attribution. The judge’s verdict concerns a decision , not a rule . The standard approach resolves this mismatch by fiat: every rule cited on a turn inherits that turn’s verdict, so a win marks all of them helpful and a loss marks all of them harmful. A rule’s counters then track the difficulty of the situations in which it happens to be invoked rather than its own contribution. Rules cited predominantly during delicate, high-friction moments accumulate negative tallies through no fault of their own; in our early runs, rule-level pruning consequently never fired. We therefore label each citation separately. For each judged, non-tied turn, TagAttributor receives the judge’s state diagnosis, verdict, and rationale (𝑣 𝑖 , 𝑟 𝑖 ) alongside the instruction text of each cited rule, and labels each 𝑐 ∈ 𝐶 𝑖 as 𝜏 𝑖,𝑐 ∈ { Helpful , Harmful , Neutral }—helpful if that rule’s instruction produced what the judge rewarded, harmful if it produced what the judge penalized, neutral if cited but not decisive. It may additionally flag a rule that existed, was not cited, and would have produced the preferred move. Only these labels move a rule’s counters, and only these counters drive pruning. On identical evidence, inheriting the turn’s verdict blames 27 of 38 citations; labelling each one blames 5, suppressing false-positive pruning while still excising genuinely counterproductive rules. 

Adoption and pruning. Adoption is at the level of individual rules, not whole playbooks: the curated candidate always carries forward, minus the rules that pruning removes. Two predicates prune. The first requires all three of decisive-label support helpful (𝑐 ) + harmful (𝑐 ) ≥ 𝑠 min , a negative balance harmful (𝑐 ) > helpful (𝑐 ), and a harm share harmful (𝑐 )/ uses (𝑐 ) ≥ 𝜌 computed over all citations including neutral ones. The second removes dead rules : since harmful > helpful can never fire on 0 > 0, a rule the facilitator never reaches for would otherwise be immortal, consuming budget while never being validated by anything; we drop any rule uncited for 𝑢 max consecutive generations. 

Hyperparameters. The reported configuration learns for 𝐺 = 30 generations with 10 training episodes per batch, a rotating validation batch of 10 episodes, and reflector/curator sampling temper-ature 0 .8. Pruning uses 𝑠 min = 5, 𝜌 = 0.3, and 𝑢 max = 5, with a playbook budget of 𝐵 = 3000 tokens. We ablate the four factors that govern the loop—the reflector/curator model , the training batch size ,the learning temperature , and the learning signal (human corpus evidence only, judge feedback only, or both)—via sequential coordinate descent, holding the judge fixed throughout so that arms remain comparable. Full protocol and per-arm results are in Appendix C.

Consensus building. Groups had to converge unanimously on a single charity from a nine-option pool. D.3. Robustness and Threats to Validity 

The obvious threat to the Study 1 versus Study 2 contrast is differential attrition. Measured on comparable denominators (Appendix A.7), retention was, if anything, higher in Study 2 than in Study 1, and within the analysis set item non-response is negligible in both studies. Dropout therefore 

> 29 Learning to GUIDE the Group: Self-Learning LLM Facilitation Across Multi-Party Tasks

cannot have manufactured the deficit by selectively removing dissatisfied Study 2 participants. We bound the remaining exposure directly. Imputing every missing response in both studies at the scale maximum—the assumption most favorable to the learned facilitator that still treats the two arms alike—the deficit in perceived facilitator productivity persists (4 .15 vs. 3 .99). Reversing it would require Study 2’s missing responses to average 5.65 on a five-point scale, which is impossible. The corresponding tipping values for satisfaction (5 .31) and comfort (5 .09) are also off-scale, though by smaller margins. Finally, Study 2 is a between-study comparison rather than a concurrently randomized trial (§4.1); the manipulation is clean at the level of the artifact, but sample and period are not held fixed.
