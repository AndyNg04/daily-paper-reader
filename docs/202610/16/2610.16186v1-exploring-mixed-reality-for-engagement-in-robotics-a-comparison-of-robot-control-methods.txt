# Abstract 

This paper presents a Mixed Reality (MR) robotics experience that provides an accessi-ble introduction to interacting with a physi-cal robot. The application combines a Turtle-Bot with a virtual animal-tending activity and three control methods: Joystick , Waypoint , and 

Path-Drawing . A mixed-methods user study with 10 participants investigated changes in robotics interest and confidence following the experience and how the control methods influ-enced engagement, workload, and preference. Confidence increased following the experience, with the exploratory analysis indicating a sig-nificant pre–post difference, while the increase in interest was not statistically significant. The control methods produced clear differences in perceived workload and ease of use, with Path-Drawing generally more demanding and diffi-cult to use. Joystick was preferred by eight of ten participants and was associated with better perceived performance and lower frus-tration. However, no statistically significant differences were detected in the other engage-ment dimensions, suggesting that the most pre-ferred method was not necessarily more engag-ing across all aspects of the experience. These findings highlight the importance of considering both usability and engagement when designing how users interact with robots in introductory MR experiences. 

# 1 Introduction 

As robots become increasingly integrated into industry, education, and everyday environments, opportunities to understand and engage with robotics must be accessi-ble to a broad range of people. However, women re-main underrepresented in robotics [Cimpian et al. , 2020; Fields et al. , 2024]. Providing girls with opportunities

Published with the participant’s explicit consent. to interact with robotics may encourage future partici-pation in the field. Previous robotics programs for girls have reported positive outcomes, including increased in-terest, confidence, STEM identity, and aspirations to-wards further STEM learning [Mich and Ghislandi, 2019; Yabas et al. , 2022; Maigaard Kjar Pedersen et al. , 2021]. Furthermore, in one primary-school robotics study, girls’ initially lower self-efficacy increased following the activ-ity [Tosato and Banzato, 2017]. This highlights the im-portance of providing opportunities for girls to gain ex-perience and build confidence in STEM activities. Creating these opportunities requires consideration of not only what participants are exposed to, but how they experience that interaction. Mixed Reality (MR) provides one approach by allowing virtual content to be integrated with interaction with a physical robot. Extended Reality (XR) has been associated with cu-riosity, enjoyment, self-efficacy, and intrinsic motiva-tion in educational settings [Crogman et al. , 2025; Mystakidis and Lympouridis, 2023]. Existing applica-tions such as RoboStage combined MR with embodied robot characters and reported positive effects on per-arXiv:2610.16186v1 [cs.HC] 12 Oct 2026 ceived authenticity and learning motivation [Chang et al. , 2010]. MR therefore provides a promising medium for creating introductory robotics experiences for users with limited previous exposure. However, using MR does not itself ensure that an ex-perience will be engaging. Previous research indicates that the design and framing of robotics activities can influence girls’ engagement [Voutyrakou and Skordoulis, 2025]. Accordingly, this work develops an MR robotics experience motivated by the need to provide girls with limited prior robotics experience an accessible and en-gaging introduction to the field, while also investigating how the method of robot control shapes that experience. This work presents an MR application in which users interact with a physical TurtleBot3 Waffle Pi through an animal-tending activity. Three control methods— joystick control ( Joystick ), waypoint placement ( Way-point ), and path drawing ( Path-Drawing )—are incorpo-rated into the experience and evaluated through a user study that considers robotics interest and confidence, en-gagement, perceived workload, and preference. The intended contributions of this paper are: 

• the design and implementation of an accessible MR robotics experience, informed by research on engag-ing girls in STEM; 

• a comparison of three levels of robot control abstrac-tion within the same MR experience; and 

• findings on how the experience relates to robotics interest and confidence, and how the control ab-straction methods influence engagement, perceived workload, and preference. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Designing Engaging Educational Robotics Activities for Girls 

The design and construction of educational robotics ac-tivities can influence their effectiveness [Voutyrakou and Skordoulis, 2025]. Previous research has identified sev-eral factors that may contribute to girls’ engagement. Reviews recommend collaborative rather than compet-itive activities to support girls’ participation [Sch¨ on et al. , 2020], and collaborative strategies have also been ob-served in group robotics activities [Schina et al. , 2020]. These findings support the consideration of collaborative and non-competitive approaches when designing robotics activities intended to engage girls. Studies involving girls have similarly identified direct human–robot interaction as beneficial and preferred [Maigaard Kjar Pedersen et al. , 2021]. Engagement may also be increased when STEM outreach activities emphasise meaningful real-world or societal applications [Maigaard Kjar Pedersen 

et al. , 2021; Baiz´ an et al. , 2021]. Together, these find-ings suggest that providing active interaction within a collaborative, meaningful context may be valuable when designing robotics experiences intended to engage girls. These findings should not be interpreted as univer-sal gender-based preferences, as STEM experiences and preferences vary between individuals [Jakobi et al. , 2026; Dirsuweit and Gouws, 2023]. Instead, they provide de-sign considerations that informed the use of active robot control, a shared non-competitive objective, and a mean-ingful narrative context in the present work. 

2.2 Design Considerations for Mixed Reality Robotics Activities 

The flexibility of MR creates a broad range of possibil-ities for how users interact with robots, making inter-action design an important consideration. Walker et al. describe a broad design space for Virtual, Augmented, and Mixed Reality human–robot interaction, encompass-ing elements relating to the robot, task, environment, and other agents [Walker et al. , 2023]. This flexibility creates opportunities to tailor MR robotics experiences to particular users and objectives. However, the increased visual information in an immersive environment can increase cognitive load and make relevant information more difficult to pro-cess [Makransky and Petersen, 2021]. Similarly, im-provements observed in immersive robotics applications have not necessarily occurred across all measured learn-ing outcomes [Ou Yang et al. , 2023]. At the same time, research suggests that deliberate application de-sign can reduce these drawbacks, while immersive ele-ments can support curiosity when incorporated meaning-fully into the experience [Makransky and Petersen, 2021; Crogman et al. , 2025]. Robot control is one such de-sign consideration, with methods varying in their level of direct interaction and autonomy. These findings high-light the importance of considering how specific design choices influence different dimensions of the user experi-ence, rather than assuming that MR use itself will pro-duce uniformly positive outcomes. 

# 3 System Design 

The developed system combines MR with a physical mo-bile robot to provide an introductory robotics experience in which users interact directly with both virtual and physical elements. 

3.1 Design Rationale 

The MR application was designed as an accessible and engaging introductory robotics experience, informed by the design considerations identified in Section 2. It com-bines active interaction with a physical robot, a shared non-competitive objective, and an animal-themed narra-tive. Three control methods with different levels of con-trol abstraction were incorporated to explore how robot control influences the user experience, as described in Section 3.3. 

3.2 MR Environment and Task 

The MR application uses colour passthrough, allow-ing participants to observe the physical environment in-cluding the TurtleBot, while virtual game elements are placed within the same space. The virtual elements al-lowed interaction objectives to be created and modified in software without requiring additional physical objects or changes to the robot’s environment. The primary task requires participants to navigate the TurtleBot through the environment to interact with virtual animals 1 positioned throughout the MR space. Each animal displays a need above its head—hungry, thirsty, or bored—which changes in colour as the need becomes more urgent over time. When the robot enters an animal’s interaction bounds, the corresponding need is automatically resolved by displaying food, water, or a toy 2 as shown in Figure 2. After a randomly timed interval, a new need appears, requiring participants to continue navigating between animals throughout the task. The activity did not in-clude a score or leaderboard, keeping the objective non-competitive.

3.3 Robot Control Methods 

To complete the task described above, participants could control the TurtleBot using three interaction methods: 

Joystick , Waypoint , and Path-Drawing (see Figure 3). These methods offer different ways to communicate mo-tion intentions to the robot and vary in the level of ab-straction between participant input and physical robot motion. 

Joystick Control 

The Joystick control method provides continuous di-rect control of the TurtleBot using the Meta Quest con-trollers. Participants use the right joystick to control forward and backward motion and the left joystick to turn the robot. Participants therefore continuously con-trol the robot’s direction and movement while navigating between animals. 

Waypoint Control 

The Waypoint control method allows participants to se-lect a destination within the MR environment using the Meta Quest controller. Participants point the controller toward the desired location, and a virtual marker pre-views the selected position. Pressing the designated controller button places the waypoint, after which the TurtleBot autonomously navigates to the selected loca-tion. This allows participants to specify where the robot should travel without directly controlling its movement along the route. 

Path-Drawing Control 

Path-Drawing control allows participants to specify a de-sired route using hand tracking. A virtual pointer pre-views the starting position. Participants activate path drawing by pinching their index finger and thumb to-gether, then move their hand through the MR environ-ment to create the desired path. Once completed, the TurtleBot follows the specified route autonomously.

Unity manages the virtual environ-ment and participant interactions, while ROS 2 manages robot localisation, control, and autonomous navigation. Communication between Unity and ROS 2 is provided through the ROS–Unity TCP Connector and Endpoint packages. 3

# 4 User Study 

A mixed-methods user study was conducted to evalu-ate the developed MR robotics experience. Quantita-tive measures assessed changes in self-reported robotics interest and confidence and compared engagement and workload across control methods, while qualitative re-sponses provided further insight into participants’ expe-riences and preferences. The study addressed the following research questions: 

RQ1: Among participants with varying levels of prior robotics experience, how do self-reported interest and confidence in learning more about robotics change from before to after completing an MR robotics experience? It was hypothesised that participants would report in-creased interest and confidence in learning more about

RQ2: Among participants with varying levels of prior robotics experience, how do different methods of con-trol abstraction differ in perceived engagement, work-load, and preference? It was hypothesised that Joystick would be preferred due to the directness of the interaction. It was addition-ally hypothesised that participants with prior robotics experience may show a greater preference for Path-Drawing control due to the novelty of the interaction. Engagement and workload were considered together to provide a broader understanding of how different aspects of the interaction shape the user experience. 

4.1 Participants 

A total of 10 volunteers (7 female, 3 male), aged 20–22 years ( M = 21 .6, SD = 0 .70), participated in the study. Five participants had prior robotics experience. Prior headset experience varied, with one participant report-ing no previous use, six reporting 1–2 uses, two reporting 3–10 uses, and one reporting more than 10 uses. Ethics approval was granted by the University of Tech-nology Sydney, Australia (ETH21-5929). All partici-pants provided informed consent before taking part. 

4.2 Study Design and Procedure 

A within-subjects design was used to compare the three control methods, with every participant experiencing 

Joystick , Waypoint , and Path-Drawing control. Before interacting with the system, participants com-pleted a pre-study questionnaire recording their prior ex-perience with robotics and immersive technologies and their initial attitudes towards robotics. Participants rated their agreement with five statements (see Table 1) on a five-point Likert scale. The five statements were re-peated in the post-study questionnaire to assess changes in these measures for RQ1. 4.3 Analysis Methodology 

RQ1: Change in Interest and Confidence 

The three interest items were combined to produce an overall interest score, and the two confidence items were combined to produce an overall confidence score as shown in Figure 4. Given the ordinal questionnaire re-sponses and small sample size, pre- and post-study scores were compared separately using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Interest and confidence were treated as distinct, pre-specified outcomes, and the reported p-values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. These analyses were therefore interpreted as exploratory. Differences between participants with and without prior robotics experience were examined descriptively due to the small subgroup sizes ( n = 5). 

RQ2: Control-Method Comparison 

Friedman tests were used to determine whether partici-pants’ rankings differed between Joystick , Waypoint , and 

Path-Drawing control for each engagement and workload measure, with a significance threshold of α = 0 .05. Holm correction was applied separately across the six workload tests and across the four engagement tests. Kendall’s W

was calculated as a measure of effect size. Where a signif-icant overall difference was identified, pairwise sign tests were used for post-hoc comparisons, with Holm correc-tion applied across the three comparisons for that mea-sure. Rank coding followed the direction of each ques-tion: higher ranks represented more of the named con-struct, except for Performance, where higher ranks rep-resented worse perceived performance. Control-method preference was summarised using selection frequencies. Differences between participants with and without prior robotics experience were examined descriptively due to the small subgroup sizes ( n = 5). 

Qualitative Analysis 

Open-ended responses were analysed using inductive thematic analysis. Relevant excerpts were coded using a codebook with definitions, inclusion and exclusion cri-teria, and representative examples, then grouped into (a) (b) Figure 4: Distribution of pre- and post-study scores for (a) interest and (b) confidence. themes. # 5 Results 

5.1 RQ1: Change in Interest and Confidence 

Pre- and post-study scores were compared to determine whether participants’ interest and confidence changed following the MR robotics experience. The combined interest score increased from 12 .9 ± 2.64 before the ex-perience to 14 .1 ± 1.29 after the experience (mean ±

SD); however, this change was not statistically signifi-cant ( p = 0 .125). The median increased from 13.5 to 15 (out of 15). The combined confidence score increased from 8 .2 ± 1.75 to 9 .4 ± 0.97, with the Wilcoxon test in-dicating a significant increase using the unadjusted sig-nificance threshold ( p = 0 .031). The median increased from 9 to 10 (out of 10). All five participants without prior robotics experience showed a change in at least one interest or confidence item, compared with one of five participants with prior robotics experience. No participant reported a decrease in any of the five statements. However, participants with prior experience had high initial scores, with median pre-study scores of 15 for interest and 10 for confidence.

# 6 Discussion 

6.1 Interest and Confidence in Robotics 

Confidence increased following the MR robotics experi-ence, with the unadjusted Wilcoxon test reaching statis-tical significance ( p = 0 .031). This result is consistent with the confidence component of the RQ1 hypothesis and provides preliminary evidence that an MR robotics experience may support participants’ confidence in en-gaging with robotics. Interest also increased following the experience, but the difference was not statistically significant (p =0.125). High initial interest scores may have created a ceiling effect, with several participants beginning at or near the maximum possible score and therefore having limited opportunity to demonstrate an increase. This may have reduced the measure’s sensitivity to changes in interest and provides important context for the result. More participants without prior robotics experience showed changes in at least one interest or confidence item than those with prior experience, whose high baseline scores left less scope for improvement. While consistent with the hypothesis, the small subgroup sizes ( n = 5) mean this pattern should be interpreted cautiously. 

6.2 Control Method Experience 

Workload differed significantly across all six adapted NASA-TLX dimensions, with the largest differences in Performance and Frustration (W = 0.81 for both). Overall, Joystick was associated with better perceived performance and lower frustration, while Path-Drawing 

was generally the most demanding. The Direct Control ,

Simple Autonomous Control , and Path-Drawing Chal-lenges themes suggest that these differences may reflect how reliably participants translated their intentions into robot behaviour. In particular, implementation diffi-culties with Path-Drawing may have contributed to its higher workload. Engagement differed less between the methods, with Perceived Usability the only dimension showing a sig-nificant difference ( W = 0 .79). Notably, Reward rank-ings were evenly distributed across the three methods (see Figure 6), even though eight of ten participants se-lected Joystick as their preferred method. This suggests that preference was not determined by how interesting or rewarding a method was alone. Qualitative responses provide further context, with participants identifying dif-ferent engaging qualities across the methods, including the novelty of Path-Drawing , despite its usability difficul-ties. Overall, the results of RQ2 indicate that the control methods differed more clearly in workload, usability, and preference than in broader engagement. Furthermore, prior robotics experience did not cor-respond with the hypothesised difference in preference, with the same preference pattern observed in both groups; however, conclusions are limited given the small subgroup sizes. 

6.3 Implications for Designing MR Robotics Experiences 

Taken together, the workload, engagement, and pref-erence findings suggest that control methods can con-tribute different qualities to an introductory robotics ex-perience. A method that is easy to use or preferred is not necessarily more engaging across all dimensions, while a more demanding interaction may still offer novelty or interest. Rather than selecting a control method based solely on preference, the intended experience should therefore be considered when choosing how participants interact with the robot. Providing multiple forms of con-trol may also expose participants to different aspects of robotics, provided that usability limitations do not in-terfere with the intended interaction. The themes relating to Purposeful Interaction and 

Physical–Virtual Connection further suggest that the control interface was only one component of the experi-ence. The animal-based objective gave robot movement purpose, while integrating virtual content with a physi-cally present robot connected the MR activity to observ-able robot behaviour. These findings suggest that intro-ductory MR robotics activities may benefit from situat-ing robot control within a meaningful task rather than presenting control as an isolated technical exercise. Given the project’s motivation to engage girls with robotics, these findings should not be interpreted as identifying a control method that girls inherently pre-fer. Instead, they suggest the value of creating acces-sible experiences that let participants interact directly with robotics without requiring programming knowl-edge, while providing purposeful tasks and different forms of interaction. The Technical Barriers and Path-Drawing Challenges themes also highlight the impor-tance of interaction reliability, as technical difficulties may shift participants’ attention away from the intended robotics experience. 

# 7 Limitations and Future Work 

The study was limited by its small sample of ten par-ticipants, including only five participants in each prior-experience group, meaning subgroup patterns should be interpreted cautiously. The exploratory sample was not designed or powered to assess gender differences, and the findings should not be interpreted as specific to girls or women. High baseline interest and confidence also pro-duced a ceiling effect that may have limited measurable change for RQ1. The control methods differed in input modality, autonomy, implementation reliability, and con-trol abstraction; therefore, differences between methods cannot be attributed to control abstraction alone. Fu-ture work should evaluate the experience with a larger and more varied sample, particularly girls with limited prior robotics experience, and consider longer-term mea-sures of changes in robotics interest and confidence. Technical difficulties with Path-Drawing , particularly hand tracking and path execution, also make it difficult to separate responses to the control concept from its im-plementation. Future work should therefore re-evaluate 

Path-Drawing after improving its reliability. Longer in-teractions and additional robotics tasks could also be in-vestigated to determine whether the findings extend be-yond the short, single-task experience evaluated in this study. 

# 8 Conclusion 

This work demonstrates how MR can combine virtual task elements with physical robot interaction to create an accessible introductory robotics experience. The ob-served increase in participant confidence provides pre-liminary evidence that such experiences could support people with limited robotics exposure, although further evaluation with a larger sample is required. The com-parison of control methods showed differences in work-load, usability, and preference, whereas no statistically significant differences were detected in the other engage-ment dimensions. While Joystick was strongly preferred, participants found engaging qualities across the different methods, demonstrating that preference alone does not capture the complete interaction experience. These find-ings highlight the value of designing introductory MR robotics activities around reliable interaction, meaning-ful tasks, and control methods selected according to the experience they are intended to provide.
