## Abstract 

Physical AI is commonly formulated around an explicitly specified task: given a goal or instruction, a system perceives, reasons, and acts. In many real-world settings, however, important tasks emerge from continuously changing world states before a person explicitly formulates them. We study this complementary problem as Proactive Physical AI, where an AI system continuously observes the physical world, identifies situations that warrant intervention, and initiates appropriate actions at the right time. We develop ProAgent, an end-to-end system built around two components: a Jev-style proactive timing model (dubbed StreamingJev) that determines whether and when intervention is needed, and a Response Model that generates responses for people or invokes available device capabilities for physical execution. To support learning at scale, we develop an automated data pipeline that converts videos into supervision for proactive timing. Experiments demonstrate strong proactive performance under both explicit objectives and standing roles, combining more accurate intervention timing with fewer false triggers than the source-backbone baseline. Real-robot evaluations further demonstrate ProAgent’s ability to identify emerging needs and translate them into appropriate physical actions. ## 1 Introduction 

Artificial intelligence systems are increasingly capable of acting in the physical world, yet they are still commonly formulated around an explicitly specified task (Ahn et al., 2022; Zitkovich et al., 2023; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025). A robot may be asked to pick up an object, a household assistant to operate an appliance, or a multimodal model to answer a question about its surroundings. In practice, however, many useful tasks arise before anyone explicitly asks for them. Consider a kitchen assistant observing a pot on a stove: as the liquid begins to boil over, the important problem is not how to execute an already specified instruction, but whether the system can recognize that intervention is becoming necessary, determine when to act, and take an appropriate response before the opportunity is lost. Similar situations arise when a person is about to leave an important object behind, when a worker approaches a hazardous area, or when an appliance enters an abnormal state. In such cases, intelligence begins not from a human-issued task, but from continuously changing world states. Proactivity therefore changes not only how quickly a physical AI system responds, but also where tasks originate .Prior work on proactive human–robot interaction and context-aware assistants motivates studying initiative beyond explicit task requests (van Den Broek & Moeslund, 2024; Wang et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2025a). We study this setting as Proactive Physical AI . Rather than assuming that a task has already been recognized and specified, a ProPAI system continuously observes the physical world and determines whether the evolving situation calls for intervention. When intervention is warranted, the system must infer what needs to be done, decide when to act, and select an appropriate response for the available executor, which may be a person, robot, or other physical device. This formulation introduces three tightly coupled challenges. First, the system must continuously interpret multimodal observations and identify changes that are relevant to its responsibilities. Second, it must reason about intervention timing: acting too early may be unnecessary or disruptive, whereas acting too late may miss the useful window altogether. Third, the inferred need must be grounded in the capabilities and constraints of available executors so that an intended response can be translated into an actionable physical operation. Together, these challenges extend physical AI from executing a given task to identifying and addressing tasks as they emerge from the world. To investigate this problem, we develop ProAgent , an end-to-end system for continuous observation, proactive decision making, and physical execution. ProAgent separates proactive decision making into two complementary components. A Jev-style proactive timing model, dubbed StreamingJev , continuously processes streaming observations and determines whether the current situation warrants intervention, while a Response Model reasons over the observed context to determine whom to address and what action or communication should follow. To train proactive timing behavior at scale, we introduce an automated data pipeline that converts Internet videos into supervision for both intervention and non-intervention moments, producing 224,268 causal decision sequences from 20,258 source videos without requiring task instructions to be manually specified for every scene. The two models operate asynchronously, allowing environmental observation to continue while responses are being generated or executed. When the response targets a person, the system generates a communicative response; when it targets a device or robot, the system invokes the capabilities exposed by the corresponding executor. This design provides a practical mechanism for connecting continuous world observation to timely and capability-grounded action. Experiments demonstrate that ProAgent outperforms the evaluated standalone multimodal models and streaming assistants in intervention timing and time-aware response quality. Under explicit assistance objectives, ProAgent achieves an event-level F1 of 42.43%, exceeding the strongest evaluated baseline by 6.27 percentage points, while attaining the highest PAUC under both explicit objectives and standing roles. We further evaluate ProAgent in real robotic scenarios with Reachy Mini, demonstrating its ability to identify emerging needs, intervene at appropriate moments, and execute corresponding physical actions. 

## 2 Problem Formulation 

We consider a physical AI system operating in a continuously evolving environment. Conventional task-conditioned physical AI assumes that an explicit task is provided before execution, and the system uses observations of the world to determine how to accomplish that task. Proactive Physical AI (ProPAI) removes this assumption: no concrete task is specified in advance. Instead, given continuous observations of the physical world and a standing context that defines its responsibilities and constraints, the system must determine whether the current situation warrants intervention, when to intervene, and what 

response can be appropriately executed. Task initiation is therefore part of the inference problem rather than a prerequisite for it.

## 3 A Proactive Physical AI System 

Proactive assistance requires frequent decisions about whether to intervene, while generating a response or carrying out an action can take much longer. ProAgent therefore separates timing prediction from response generation, allowing observation to continue while an intervention is underway. As shown in Figure 1, the Timing Model (StreamingJev) determines when to intervene , and the Response Model determines whom to address and what to do . A persistent runtime connects these decisions to the evolving world state and the feedback from earlier actions. Both models receive causal observations together with standing roles, available device capabilities, and interaction history. While the Timing Model evaluates whether a response is warranted, the Response Model incrementally encodes the incoming context. A trigger enables generation from this prepared context, so the system need not wait until intervention is required to begin processing the observations. Observation ingestion continues during generation and execution. The runtime tracks responses and operations already in progress, allowing subsequent timing decisions to account for an ongoing intervention instead of starting a new request at every update. The models maintain separate caches for their respective weights and inputs; shared observations do not require shared KV states. The Response Model is a multimodal foundation model conditioned on this context and the available capabilities. It produces text for external TTS when addressing a person, or function calls when directing a device. The runtime validates and dispatches these calls, then returns execution results to the interaction state. Together with subsequent observations, this feedback allows the system to determine whether the need has been addressed or further assistance is required. The result is a continuing observation–intervention loop rather than a sequence of independent responses. Detailed state management, cache validity, and executor-supported cancellation and recovery are described in Appendix G. 

3.1 Timing Model and Data Pipeline 

The Timing Model (StreamingJev) turns the continuous observation stream into a decision about whether the system should respond. Given causal observations I≤t, standing constraints or role context ct, and output state st, the Timing Model predicts 

yt = fθ (I≤t, c t, s t), yt ∈ { <silence> , <response> }. (9) This decision controls the response channel while leaving content and action planning to the Response Model. When output is inactive, <response> initiates a response and <silence> maintains observation; when active, they indicate continuation and release, respectively. Constraints may specify a condition to monitor or broader responsibilities without naming each emerging task. Ordinary video descriptions identify events but rarely specify when intervention becomes useful. Recent work therefore studies streaming speech and proactive dialogue as supervision for online assistants (Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a). Our event-driven data engine combines Internet videos with existing video annotations to construct this supervision. As illustrated in Figure 2, it organizes actors, objects, and state changes into event-centered records, associates them with relevant constraints, and identifies response windows from supporting evidence. Full-video outcomes help offline annotators locate precursor cues, but each training input contains only observations available up to its decision time, together with prior interaction state. A response window may precede the visible outcome; an event boundary alone does not establish a need to intervene. Constructing these sequences requires both grounding an event in the video and deciding when it becomes relevant to the system’s responsibilities. We use two forms of timing supervision. Explicit-condition timing identifies when evidence supports responding under a specified condition. Open timing uses role constraints or anomalous events to supervise responses without naming the target event in advance. Event descriptions and source annotations ground the construction of these labels. The resulting sequences cover response-window entry, continuation, and exit. Confirmed non-intervention periods provide silence labels, including all-silence examples; unknown intervals are excluded from supervision. Each training input combines causal visual observations with task or role context and the current output state. 3.2 Efficient Streaming Inference 

The Timing Model is evaluated repeatedly as observations arrive, making the cost of each update central to continuous operation. We address this cost at three stages of inference: processing the accumulated history, representing newly observed visual changes, and generating a timing decision (Figure 3). With Incremental Caching , we cache historical post-vision-encoder features and language-model key–value (KV) states. At each update, only newly appended frames undergo visual preprocessing and encoding, while the language model prefills only the uncached suffix. Cached states are isolated by session and reused only when the input prefix and inference configuration remain compatible. Historical reuse does not eliminate redundancy within newly arriving observations. Consecutive video frames often contain similar visual features, even though only a small part of the scene has changed. TimeChat-Online similarly exploits cross-frame redundancy through differential token dropping (Yao et al., 2025). Dynamic Token Dropping (DTD) compares the current frame with the preceding frame when their visual grids match. Specifically, it compares features at the same token-grid locations. A current-frame token is pruned as redundant when its cosine similarity exceeds the threshold τ ,before it enters the language backbone. This reduces the sequence length processed by the language model and the growth of its KV cache. Because intervention can depend on subtle visual changes, the resulting computational savings must be considered together with decision agreement and timing quality.

## 4 Experiments 

We evaluate intervention timing, delivered assistance, physical task completion, and streaming efficiency. Detailed datasets, system configurations, and scoring protocols are provided in Appendices B–F. 

4.1 Proactive Timing Evaluation 

We compare timing on Omni-MMI PA/PT (Wang et al., 2025b) and paired ProactiveVideoQA-derived episodes from EGO and WEB videos (Wang et al., 2025a). Explicit specifies the assistance objective; Role supplies standing responsibilities without revealing the target event. Both use the same videos and references, with reference annotations hidden during inference. ProAgent receives observations every 200 ms. The Base/Ours pair shares a Qwen3.8-27B Worker and input and scheduling budgets; other systems retain their respective interfaces and schedules. Responses are matched one-to-one to reference windows using first visible-text times; unmatched responses are counted against the reference annotations. Table 1 shows that ProAgent achieves the highest PA, PT, and F1 scores. Explicit F1 reaches 42.43%, exceeding the strongest baseline by 6.27 percentage points, with only 0.04 unmatched responses per minute. Under Role constraints, ProAgent leads LiveStar by 1.47 points while reducing FP/min from 7.65 to 0.18. Dispider produces fewer unmatched responses in this condition but attains only 1.09% F1, illustrating why restraint must be considered together with intervention coverage.

4.2 Proactive Interaction Evaluation 

A well-timed response is useful only if it conveys the information needed in that situation. Each system responds at its own trigger times, without reference triggers or a second answer-generation pass. We assess these same trajectories using 

Outcome , which measures complete reference-answer coverage by responses initiated within assistance windows, and ProactiveVideoQA’s PAUC (Wang et al., 2025a), which jointly rewards correctness and timeliness ( ω = 0 .5). Coverage is scored per reference target and averaged within and then across episodes. ProAgent achieves the highest PAUC under both conditions: 0.6131 for Explicit and 0.5121 for Role (Table 2). Under Role, it also leads in Outcome. Under Explicit, GPT-6 provides higher complete-answer coverage (0.1523 versus 0.1432), while ProAgent leads in PAUC. The two measures capture complementary properties: PAUC credits timely partial information, whereas Outcome requires complete coverage. Thus, strong answer generation alone does not ensure the best time-aware assistance. The three closed-source systems incur mean first-text latencies of 14.55–19.81 s, compared with 1.07–1.14 s for ProAgent. Their remote response path includes network transfer and service processing; extended chain-of-thought reasoning, when enabled, can further delay visible output. Such delays can move responses beyond useful assistance windows, limiting time-sensitive performance. ProAgent separates frequent timing decisions from asynchronous response generation (Figure 1), combining short response latency with the highest PAUC in the evaluated configurations. 

4.3 Embodied Evaluation 

We evaluate 60 Reachy Mini cases, with 20 each for Guidance, Warning, and Interaction. These categories cover assistance during ongoing activities, responses to emerging errors or risks, and proactive engagement with human needs, respectively. Systems receive standing responsibilities and available capabilities, without reference triggers or required responses. Time 

measures mean absolute intervention-time error; Outcome measures completion of required events with correct content and entities, assigning zero when prescribed ordering constraints are violated. ProAgent attains the highest Outcome in all three categories (Table 3), exceeding the strongest baseline in each by 0.089, 0.066, and 0.173, respectively. The largest completion-score gain occurs in Interaction, where needs must be recognized through proactive engagement. These results extend the evidence beyond verbal assistance: the system connects inferred needs to completed events involving people and available device capabilities.

## 5 Discussion 

The matched-Worker comparison highlights the importance of intervention quality: first-text latency changes little, yet PAUC rises from 0.3355 to 0.6131 under Explicit and from 0.2828 to 0.5121 under Role. These gains support learned intervention decisions beyond faster response generation alone. ProPAI shifts the source of an action from an explicit task to a need identified under persistent responsibilities. ProAgent provides a modular realization of this shift, while leaving important distinctions for evaluation: recognizing a supplied condition is not unrestricted task discovery, and a timely response in recorded video does not establish physical benefit. Broader evaluation must test whether interventions resolve needs across changing roles and capabilities, including when silence or clarification is preferable. Permission to act remains distinct from an inferred need. These questions connect future improvements in memory, learning, and architecture. Memory should retain unresolved commitments while revising stale beliefs; learning should distinguish intervention value from event timing, executor failure, and recovery caused by others. Native models could jointly represent timing and response, with adaptive computation that preserves observation continuity and response deadlines. Callable skills, including specialized action policies, could broaden feasible interventions while retaining explicit authorization and human control. Appendices G and H develop these directions and their evaluation requirements.

## 6 Conclusion 

We introduce Proactive Physical AI and ProAgent , a system that connects continuous observation to timely communication and physical action under standing responsibilities. Causal video supervision makes intervention timing learnable, while the asynchronous separation of timing and response supports ongoing monitoring during generation and execution. Experiments show stronger intervention timing and time-aware assistance than the evaluated alternatives, together with improved event completion across three embodied task categories. Streaming optimizations reduce the cost of repeated decisions while maintaining high decision agreement. Together, these findings support a practical approach to physical AI that identifies emerging needs and coordinates responses through available human and device capabilities.

## A Connections to existing studies 

ProPAI draws on established research in streaming multimodal interaction, proactive robotics, embodied execution, and learning from observation. We organize these foundations around how an agent continuously observes the world, recognizes a need for assistance, and turns that need into an effective intervention. 

Streaming Multimodal Models and Proactive Assistants. Streaming models make continuously arriving observations available for online reasoning. VideoLLM-online develops causal streaming dialogue training and efficient inference, while Dispider separates continuous perception, response decisions, and asynchronous generation (Chen et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2025). Proactive interaction is also an explicit learning objective: MMDuet2 trains models to choose between responding and remaining silent across multiple turns, and ProactiveVideoQA evaluates response timing together with answer quality (Wang et al., 2026b; 2025a). StreamChat updates visual context during generation, while LiveStar integrates streaming perception, silence decisions, and memory management (Liu et al., 2024a; Yang et al., 2025b). Related approaches reduce streaming computation through event-gated processing and visual token pruning (Ding et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a). ViSpeak studies responses to visual instructions, and Eyes Wide Open studies proactive responses in egocentric streams (Fu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b). These advances provide both a computational foundation and evaluation tools for persistent assistants. Answering a specified question at the right moment is one important setting; identifying which need deserves a response under broader responsibilities introduces an additional decision about the task itself. 

Proactive Human–Robot Interaction and Assistance. Robot initiative has a substantial research history. Existing reviews define proactive human–robot interaction and organize its varied behaviors and technical approaches (van Den Broek & Moeslund, 2024). Recent systems connect these ideas to foundation models: RoboOmni combines multimodal intent recognition, interaction confirmation, and manipulation (Wang et al., 2026a); ProAct couples long-horizon social reasoning with low-latency multimodal behavior on a humanoid robot (Zhang et al., 2026b); and event-driven assistive manipulation infers goals and intervention decisions from human-induced workspace state changes (Liu et al., 2026). These are direct foundations for ProPAI, including precedents for assistance without an explicit task instruction. They also reveal distinct sources of initiative: inferred human intent, social context, and changes in the environment. Code-as-Monitor uses visual programs to monitor constraints and anticipate robotic failures (Zhou et al., 2025). Beyond robotics, sensory-context assistants and mobile agents study how contextual observations reveal needs and support proactive assistance (Yang et al., 2025a; Kong et al., 2026). ProPAI brings these sources into a shared formulation in which persistent roles and constraints guide need discovery, while available people and devices determine feasible responses. 

Physical Reasoning and Capability-Grounded Execution. Vision-language-action models such as RT-2 and π0.5 connect semantic knowledge to robot control and improve generalization across objects, instructions, and environments (Zitkovich et al., 2023; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025). Complementary approaches expose reusable skills to higher-level reasoning: SayCan grounds language-based planning in skill affordances, Code as Policies composes perception and control APIs, and Inner Monologue incorporates environmental feedback into planning (Ahn et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2022). VoxPoser grounds language in three-dimensional value maps for manipulation, while OK-Robot integrates visual-language perception, navigation, and grasping (Huang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024b). These methods provide execution and feedback mechanisms that proactive systems can build upon. Our function-call interface follows this programmable-capability perspective, exposing registered functions without prescribing their underlying controllers. The additional proactive question is how available capabilities affect whether, when, and through whom an emerging need should be addressed. 

Learning from Human Video and Interaction. Human activity and assistance provide supervision beyond robot trajectories. HoloAssist captures collaborative task performance and supports mistake detection and intervention prediction, while RoboVQA combines human and robot experience for grounded long-horizon reasoning (Wang et al., 2023; Sermanet et al., 2023). Predictive video learning offers another foundation: V-JEPA 2 combines large-scale video pretraining with robot interaction data to support action-conditioned prediction and planning (Assran et al., 2025). LiveCC and ProAssist further connect video observations to streaming language and proactive assistant dialogue (Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a). These results motivate learning broadly transferable knowledge from observation while retaining embodiment-specific execution. For proactive learning, observing what happens next must additionally be connected to

Taken together, these foundations motivate a coupled research problem rather than independent perception, timing, and control tasks. The same world event may call for silence under one role and assistance under another; choosing between a nearby speaker and a mobile robot changes the required start time; and successful intervention changes what should happen next. ProPAI organizes these dependencies into a continuous observation–intervention–feedback loop, providing a common setting for studying task discovery, timely restraint, and capability-grounded action.

## D Role-Conditioned Streaming Evaluation 

This evaluation asks whether a system can provide timely, relevant responses when an explicit question is replaced by standing responsibilities. The paired conditions share source videos and reference annotations, so their comparison isolates the change in task conditioning. Timing and response quality are measured from the same generated trajectories, as reported in Tables 1 and 2. 

D.1 Dataset Construction and Paired Conditions 

We construct a 200-video subset from the EGO and WEB portions of ProactiveVideoQA, selecting 100 distinct parent videos from each domain. Selection is based on source content and its compatibility with a shared role catalog, without 

> 18 Proactive Physical AI

using the evaluated models’ scores. The subset is purposefully selected rather than a random sample of the complete benchmark. In the Explicit condition, the system receives the original question. In the Role condition, that question is replaced by an identity and general responsibilities appropriate to the scene. The original videos, reference answers, and reply_timespan 

intervals are retained in both conditions. No new intervention windows, recipient identities, or ordering constraints are added. Reference answers and timing annotations are used only for scoring and are never included in model input. Each source clip is replayed from beginning to end, rather than cropped around its annotated answers. Table D.1 shows two actual prompt pairs. The cooking role covers several responsibilities without naming avocado or prescribing its preparation steps; the driving role does not identify the white car or its expected behavior. This changes the information supplied to the system while keeping the scoring targets fixed.

D.2 Causal Inputs and System Interfaces 

For the rolling-window VLM interface, observations are updated every 200 ms. Each request uses only frames available at its start: the most recent five seconds are sampled at 5 Hz and the preceding 25 seconds at 1 Hz, with duplicate boundary frames removed. Frames are resized to a maximum side length of 512 pixels. This retains approximately 50 frames in a 30-second window rather than sending all 150 observations at every update. Speech is supplied as causal ASR text using Qwen3-ASR-1.7B. The shared ASR schedule records when each finalized utterance becomes available, including endpoint detection and recognition delay. An utterance enters a request only after that availability time and only if its source interval lies within the retained context. The dialogue history contains at most the five latest completed system responses and their timestamps, with a 1,024-token limit; older entries are removed first. Reference replies, future transcripts, and unfinished generated responses are not inserted into this history. A standalone VLM generates a natural-language response or the exact control marker NO_UPDATE . The latter denotes silence; other nonempty visible text is treated as a response rather than retrospectively reinterpreted as silence. Empty output, incomplete control markers, and transport failures are not valid silence. Potential control prefixes are buffered until their interpretation is determined, and visible-response time is measured when text is actually released. For the matched dual-model comparison, the unmodified Qwen3.5-4B and StreamingJev control the same Qwen3.8-27B Worker. The pair uses the same input and scheduling budgets. The Timing Model emits response /silence ; the Worker generates response content when triggered. Timing and response generation operate asynchronously. A trigger freezes the currently available Worker context. While the Worker is busy, further Timing decisions are logged but do not create a second queued response; another response requires a new trigger after completion. LiveStar (Yang et al., 2025b) and Dispider (Qian et al., 2025) retain their native observation schedules, memory, and trigger/response mechanisms rather than being reduced to the rolling-window VLM interface. Their adapters use visual input without adding ASR to the native decision paths. Thus, 200 ms specifies our observation-update setting, not a requirement that every compared method ingest frames or finish decisions at 5 Hz.

F.5 Effects of the DTD Threshold. 

DTD removes a current-frame visual token when its cosine similarity to the token at the same grid location in the preceding frame exceeds the threshold τ . A lower τ therefore produces more aggressive pruning, whereas τ = 1 .0 is an identity configuration that retains all visual tokens. We sweep τ from 0.5 to 1.0 while holding the checkpoint, input histories, and inference backend fixed. Apart from DTD, incremental caching is the only optimization enabled throughout the sweep; prefix-constrained decoding and all other optimization modules are disabled. Lowering τ from 1.0 to 0.5 reduces the retained-token ratio from 100.00% to 16.37%, removing 83.63% of current-frame visual tokens at the most aggressive setting. DTD provides a clear reduction in median latency, while its effects on tail latency and memory are more modest. Relative to τ = 1 .0, τ = 0 .5 reduces P50 latency by 17.8%, from 240.26 to 197.49 ms, and P95 latency by 8.5%, from 270.06 to 247.16 ms. Peak allocated memory decreases by 3.5%, from 9.63 to 9.29 GB. This difference is expected because DTD is applied after the vision encoder: it reduces language-model prefill and subsequent KV-cache growth, but does not avoid visual-encoder computation. Moreover, model weights account for most of the measured GPU memory.

More aggressive pruning introduces a corresponding tradeoff in decision consistency. Agreement with full recomputation decreases from 100.00% at τ = 1 .0 to 93.42% at τ = 0 .5. The more conservative τ = 0 .9 setting retains 67.76% of visual tokens, reduces P50 latency by 10.9%, and preserves 98.65% decision agreement. The deployment threshold can therefore be selected according to the required balance between latency and decision consistency. Because each threshold was evaluated in a single full pass, small non-monotonic differences, particularly in P95 latency, should be treated as descriptive rather than statistically significant. 

## G System Implementation and Training Details 

ProAgent connects continuous observation to intervention through three parts of its implementation: the runtime that maintains context and coordinates the two models, the data pipeline that supplies timing supervision, and the capability interface that turns responses into communication or device actions. This appendix follows that path, complementing the architecture in Section 3. 

G.1 Runtime, Context, and Memory 

Continuous observation and asynchronous response. The Timing and Response Models share an observation source but maintain separate model-dependent states. The runtime makes recent observations, standing responsibilities, available capabilities, interaction history, and ongoing intervention state available to each model. The Timing Model decides whether output should be active; the Response Model determines the response content and executor. Response-context preparation and timing prediction can proceed asynchronously, while generation remains trigger-gated. The evaluated replay and busy-state handling are specified in Appendix D. 

Context availability and state reuse. A context item becomes usable when it is available to the system, not merely when the underlying event occurred. In particular, a transcript finalized after a decision cannot be inserted into that earlier decision. Observation timestamps, derived-information availability, and completed response times therefore have distinct roles in constructing causal inputs. The retained context and sampling settings of each experiment are given in its evaluation protocol rather than assumed to be universal. Each model’s cache corresponds to its own weights, prompt, retained inputs, and position assignments. Sharing observations does not imply sharing KV states across the two models. Reuse requires an unchanged prefix; removing old context, changing its representation, or resetting a session requires updating or invalidating the affected cached states. Appendix F specifies the efficiency comparison and the component-level measurements. 

Interaction history and unfinished tasks. Recent perceptual context explains what has just changed, whereas interaction history records what the system has already said or attempted. Ongoing intervention state serves a different purpose: it distinguishes a need that remains unresolved from a response currently being generated or a device operation still in progress. These are runtime distinctions, not additional classes in the Timing Model’s binary output. They prevent each observation update from being treated as an unrelated opportunity to start the same intervention. Longer-lived task, episodic, and user/environment memory can extend this context beyond the current observation window. Such memory would retain the source and validity of a belief, distinguish uncertain inferences from explicit instructions, and revise stale information when new evidence arrives. Learning when to retrieve, correct, or forget these records is a further design question, developed in Section H.1. 

Output ownership and execution feedback. Releasing the conversational output channel does not cancel a physical operation already dispatched. Likewise, an accepted function call establishes that execution was requested, not that the task succeeded. Returned status and subsequent observations inform whether assistance should continue, change, or end; cancellation and recovery use operations supported by the executor. Coordination of concurrent tasks can build on these distinctions by tracking resource commitments and linking retries to the original request, rather than issuing independent duplicate actions.

Validation, dispatch, and feedback. Before dispatch, the runtime checks provider availability, capability membership, version compatibility, and argument-schema conformance. The registered function executes through its underlying controller and returns a schema-conforming result or a structured error. For example, an unavailable provider returns success: false with the code PROVIDER_UNAVAILABLE ; other errors distinguish invalid arguments, denied permissions, unmet preconditions, execution failure, and timeout. The Response Model receives this feedback in its continuing context and can revise the response or select another available operation. A syntactically valid call does not itself grant permission or establish physical completion. Execution limits belong to the provider and runtime; task completion is established through feedback and subsequent observations. Retries, replanning, and recovery are agent/runtime decisions, not implied by the function signature. The callable boundary can use a local function, an SDK wrapper, or a transport such as MCP, HTTP, ROS, or WebSocket without changing the operation’s declared input and output semantics. 

From device operations to reusable skills. The same boundary supports a progression from device primitives to bounded skills and compositions of skills. A specialized VLA could be exposed as a callable skill: the high-level model selects a goal, while the specialized policy maintains its own perception and low-level control. Its capability description must specify the supported observations, workspace, arguments, and termination conditions. This separation also provides a route to harness-level skill evolution. Execution feedback could motivate revised skill descriptions, parameters, wrappers, or reusable compositions, with proposed updates tested before deployment. These are extensions enabled by the architecture, rather than additional capabilities established by the present experiments. ## H Open Challenges and Research Directions 

The modular Engine is one realization of ProPAI, not a restriction on future architectures. The following questions describe a broader research agenda connecting continuous world understanding, intervention learning, and physical execution. Each box states an open problem; the accompanying text outlines candidate approaches and the evidence needed to assess them. 

H.1 Persistent World Understanding and Memory 

Open Challenge 1 

How can a proactive system connect past commitments, present evidence, and possible futures to recognize when intervention becomes useful? 

Remembering unresolved needs. Memory provides the starting point for persistent world understanding: preserving task-relevant continuity without confusing historical evidence with the present world. A task may remain unresolved after its original visual evidence disappears, while a visible hazard may already have been handled by someone else. A future memory system could combine an event-and-entity representation with explicit task commitments and intervention outcomes, retrieving information according to its causal relevance rather than textual similarity alone. Learning when to write, retrieve, consolidate, revise, and forget may be as important as learning the response policy. User corrections should update the relevant belief without silently changing unrelated memories; uncertain identities and conflicting observations should remain explicit. Evaluation could test resumption of interrupted tasks, redundant interventions, errors caused by stale beliefs, and correction propagation over extended sessions. Comparisons should control context and retrieval budgets and include retention and deletion requirements, rather than equating a larger memory with a better proactive system. A concrete starting point is a layered memory: a short-lived perceptual buffer for recent evidence, an episodic record of interventions and their outcomes, and a persistent task state for commitments that outlive either window. A task record could link its initiating evidence, responsible person or device, completion condition, and next relevant observation. For example, an illustrative cooking assistant should distinguish a pot that still needs attention from one already removed by the user, even if both situations follow the same earlier reminder. Retrieval would then answer not only “what happened?” but “what remains to be done?” This organization suggests different memory access paths for the two models. The Timing Model could consult a compact account of unresolved conditions at each update, while the Response Model retrieves supporting episodes only when planning assistance. The key question is which state can be compressed without erasing the evidence needed to change a decision. Paired histories with identical recent frames but different prior commitments or completed actions would help isolate this ability from short-window visual recognition. 

Anticipating actionable changes. While memory tracks what remains unresolved, prediction connects subtle precursors to the consequences of waiting or acting. For example, an illustrative system might estimate whether an unstable object is likely to fall and whether a warning or available device action could still help. The relevant prediction is not only what the scene will look like, but how possible interventions change the outcomes and the remaining time in which they are useful. A future design could use a predictive model to propose response windows or compare candidate interventions, while grounding the final decision in observed evidence, available capabilities, and uncertainty. Forecasts should remain distinguishable from observations in memory and planning. Evaluation should separate predictive accuracy from intervention benefit: an accurate forecast can still produce an unnecessary, infeasible, or late response. Controlled scenarios and appropriately designed comparisons would be needed to assess the benefit of anticipation rather than assume it from visually plausible predictions. Prediction could also be conditioned on the available executor. A warning that a person can act on immediately and a robot action that requires navigation have different useful starting times. Rather than optimizing forecasting in isolation, a future system could estimate the remaining opportunity for each feasible response and update that estimate as new evidence arrives. Comparisons between observation-only and predictive policies should therefore examine both interventions made possible by earlier preparation and false interventions induced by uncertain forecasts.

H.2 Native Proactive Models and Real-Time Computation 

Open Challenge 2 

How can a model jointly learn continuous observation, intervention timing, and response generation while allocating computation to meet intervention deadlines? 

Joint modeling of timing and response. A native model could internalize some of the coordination currently provided by the Timing Model, Response Model, and Harness. Candidate architectures include a shared streaming backbone with specialized decision and response pathways, or a persistent recurrent state that supports both intervention decisions and language or capability outputs. The scientific question is whether shared representations improve the coupling between timing and response without allowing expensive generation to delay observation updates or degrade restraint. Such a model would need supervision for silence, response initiation, continuation, and closure alongside response content and execution feedback. Silence is not simply the absence of an answer token: it is a decision made while observations continue to arrive. A fair comparison with the modular system should match data, modalities, context, and compute, and assess both response quality and decision latency. Unification would not remove the need for external device interfaces, permission enforcement, or independently observable execution outcomes. One architectural route is to maintain a shared causal state with a lightweight decision pathway and a selectively activated response pathway. Observation updates would continue during response generation, allowing new evidence to revise an unfinished plan before it is dispatched. This raises a concrete state-consistency problem: the system must know which observations support a draft response and when later evidence invalidates it. Another route is to distill coordinated behavior from the modular system into a native model, then extend training to continuous interaction sequences rather than isolated question–answer examples. The modular design provides a useful experimental reference because timing, generation, and execution feedback can be varied separately. 

Adaptive computation. Incremental Caching, Dynamic Token Dropping, and Prefix-constrained Decoding address local sources of overhead. A broader question is how much computation each moment deserves. Slowly changing scenes may permit inexpensive monitoring, whereas an ambiguous change near a response boundary may justify denser observations, retained visual detail, or escalation to a larger model. Candidate policies could adapt observation cadence, token retention, retrieval depth, and response preparation to uncertainty and estimated urgency. They must also preserve coverage of unexpected events that a cheap monitor might otherwise fail to notice. This creates a joint timing and resource-allocation problem. Useful comparisons would report the trade-off among intervention quality, tail latency, energy, and memory under the same event stream and external deadlines. Stress tests could include simultaneous tasks, network delays, long histories, and bursts of sensor input. A configuration should not be called more efficient merely because it silently skips difficult observations or reduces the opportunities at which a response is evaluated. In particular, efficient inference should be studied as a scheduling problem as well as a model-compression problem. Observation encoding, memory retrieval, speculative response preparation, and output decoding compete for a finite compute budget. A scheduler could prioritize a new high-urgency observation over an obsolete draft, or reuse prepared context when the triggering evidence remains valid. Experiments should charge discarded speculative work to the compute budget and count missed opportunities during busy periods. This would distinguish genuine gains from shifting latency into an unreported queue or precomputation phase. 

H.3 From Execution Feedback to Capability Growth 

Open Challenge 3 

How can experience improve intervention decisions, expand the set of executable skills, and refine their composition into reliable assistance? 

> 31 Proactive Physical AI

Learning from preferences and outcomes. Complete events suggest useful comparisons: a timely intervention may be preferable to a late one, restraint to an unnecessary interruption, and a feasible response to an unavailable action. These comparisons are conditional on the task and environment rather than universal reward rules. Future work could investigate temporal preference learning or outcome-based reinforcement learning, using verified execution traces and human corrections to complement broad video supervision. Outcome attribution is a central difficulty. An observed recovery may be caused by another person, by the environment itself, or by the system’s action; a failure may arise from the timing decision, the plan, or the executor. Moreover, logs produced by one policy do not reveal what would have happened under every alternative. Progress requires preserving provenance and distinguishing these sources of uncertainty. Evaluation should use held-out tasks and independent outcome checks, and retain unnecessary interventions and failures rather than training only from successful trajectories. A useful progression would begin with matched timing preferences under the same context, then introduce closed-loop episodes in which an intervention changes later observations. Timing and response could first be optimized separately with the other component fixed, before exploring joint learning. This staged comparison would help determine whether a gain comes from recognizing the need earlier, selecting a better action, or executing it more reliably. Feedback should retain these distinctions: a fluent response, an accepted device request, and a completed physical task are different learning signals. Where counterfactual outcomes are unavailable, preference judgments and observed outcomes should remain separate rather than being treated as interchangeable rewards. 

Specialized skills and cross-embodiment composition. Exposing specialized VLAs as callable functions would let a high-level model reason about goals while each executor handles its local perception and control. The difficult part is establishing a useful capability contract: supported objects, workspace, required observations, expected failure modes, and conditions under which the policy should decline or stop. Capability selection could depend on such information rather than treating every executor as an interchangeable implementation of an abstract command. An expanding skill library also changes which interventions are feasible and how early they must begin. A slower but more reliable executor may require a different response window; losing a manipulation skill may make a human warning preferable to direct action. A meaningful transfer study would freeze the proactive models, vary the declared skill inventory or embodiment, and measure capability selection, task completion, timing, and failures under the changed conditions. Zero-shot substitution should be distinguished from recalibration, wrapper engineering, or policy retraining. A callable-function boundary makes this interface study possible. For example, a manipulation policy could be exposed as a bounded pick_and_place skill whose inputs specify a supported object and destination. The VLA would consume its own visual and proprioceptive observations and generate motor commands; the proactive model would decide whether moving the object is useful, choose an eligible executor, and interpret progress or failure. The wrapper would translate task-level arguments into the policy’s native interface and return explicit status, including whether execution can be interrupted. This division connects high-level initiative to specialized control without requiring the proactive model to generate motor actions itself. Composition adds another question: when does one skill’s result satisfy the next skill’s preconditions? A navigation– inspection–manipulation sequence may require fresh observations between stages rather than an unconditional list of calls. Skill interfaces could expose these dependencies, resource requirements, and completion evidence. Testing the same high-level task with different skill inventories would reveal whether the model adapts its plan, seeks human assistance, or appropriately refrains when the required capability is absent. 

Harness-level skill evolution. Once skills are exposed through a common interface, their selection and composition become another locus of learning. Self-evolution need not begin with changing the foundation model’s weights. A Harness could propose improved capability descriptions, retrieval rules, tool-selection prompts, wrappers, or reusable compositions of existing skills. Repeated failures might expose a missing precondition; repeated successful action sequences might motivate a candidate composite skill. The crucial distinction is between proposing a change and deploying it. Generating a new function name does not create a new physical ability, and a successful trace does not establish general correctness. A candidate development loop would retain the triggering evidence, generate a versioned proposal, test it on recorded cases and independent held-out scenarios, and evaluate it in a sandbox or suitable simulator before any authorized physical trial. Promotion to the live registry would require explicit acceptance criteria, compatibility checks, and a rollback path. Changes that affect physical behavior or permissions would require the corresponding authorization. The evolving component should not be allowed to redefine the evaluation criteria, grant itself new permissions, or disable the safeguards used to assess it. 

> 32 Proactive Physical AI

The unit of improvement can be made explicit. Repeated selection errors may motivate a clearer capability description; a recurring argument failure may motivate a wrapper check; a successful multi-step procedure may motivate a reusable composite skill. Each proposal should identify the failure pattern it addresses and preserve cases where the previous version already worked. An illustrative inspection routine, for example, could evolve to request a second view when the first is occluded, rather than treating every uncertain observation as a negative finding. Such an update changes the evidence-gathering procedure, not necessarily the foundation model or physical controller. Evaluation should separate gains from additional memory, better routing, new compositions, and genuinely improved executors. A frozen base model with fixed evaluation tasks would provide one way to isolate improvements in the Harness itself. Relevant outcomes include transfer beyond the triggering failure cases, regression frequency, test and deployment cost, and recovery after an accepted change later proves unsuitable. 

H.4 Long-Term Human and Multi-Actor Collaboration 

Open Challenge 4 

How should an always-on system coordinate evolving needs, shared devices, and human preferences while preserving people’s control over intervention? 

Proactivity creates obligations beyond executing a requested command. An intervention may interrupt one person to help another, occupy a shared device, or reveal information that should remain private. A future coordination layer could represent task urgency, resource commitments, explicit user preferences, and authorization requirements without treating inferred personal attributes as permission. Uncertain preferences may call for clarification, while withdrawal of consent should change the allowed behavior even if a task remains technically feasible. Coordination also requires an account of task ownership. A person may already be addressing a problem that a robot has just detected, or two devices may offer overlapping capabilities. A future system could track who has acknowledged a task, which resources are committed, and what evidence would justify a handoff or renewed reminder. Communication is then part of coordination rather than merely the final output: a brief clarification can resolve ambiguous responsibility before an unnecessary action is taken. Personalization could adjust reminder frequency or preferred assistance channels while keeping explicit instructions distinct from preferences inferred from sparse behavior. Evaluation should include overlapping needs, conflicting requests, unavailable devices, changing permissions, human takeover, and situations where no intervention is appropriate. Task completion alone cannot capture unnecessary interruption, privacy violations, or failure to relinquish control. Longer-term studies could examine whether assistance remains useful after personalization and repeated exposure, while keeping data retention and correction under user control. These questions extend embodied evaluation beyond a fixed scenario inventory to sustained collaboration in changing environments. Longitudinal evaluation would be particularly informative here. The same participants could encounter recurring and novel situations across sessions, with changing responsibilities and deliberate task handoffs. Besides completion and response timing, useful measurements include duplicate interventions, successful handoffs, corrections needed from users, and the persistence of outdated preferences. Comparing a fixed policy with memory-only and adaptive variants would help separate the benefits of remembering past interactions from those of changing the intervention policy itself. These directions interact rather than forming independent additions. Memory changes which events appear new or unresolved; better prediction changes when evidence becomes actionable; new skills change what can be done; and self-evolution changes the procedures used to select and compose those skills. A native model may internalize some of these relationships, while an explicit Harness keeps their effects inspectable. The unifying research question is how to improve the complete path from an emerging need to a timely, appropriate, and verifiable response without losing causal grounding or human control.
