ABSTRACT 

Embodied AI agents in Augmented Reality (AR) could enable more natural collaboration in immersive analytics by engaging users through spatial, conversational, and visual interaction. However, designing such agents requires careful attention to context. We pro-pose a preliminary conceptual framework for context-aware design of embodied agents structured around three dimensions of spatial, social, and semantic. This framework is further categorised into embeddedness (how agents are situated in the contexts) and aware-ness (how agents perceive and respond to the contexts). To explore this, we present Cobot, an embodied AI agent designed as a de-sign probe. Cobot supports context-sensitive interaction through features such as gaze cues, spatial sound, and task-aware behaviour. We reflect on its design and identify implications for future context-aware embodied agents. 

Index Terms: Embodied agents, immersive analytics, data visual-isation.

Mean-while, advances in AI—particularly large language models (LLMs) —have enabled more natural interactions with complex systems through conversational interfaces. While most AI interactions oc-cur through text or voice, immersive environments introduce the potential for embodied AI agents—spatially embedded avatars that represent AI systems and engage with users in the real world. Embodied AI agents offer the potential for more “natural” col-laboration with underlying AI systems. Beyond the growing capabilities of LLMs, embodied agents must also meet the social and interaction expectations shaped by human-to-human collabo-ration. In this paper, we focus on context [24] as an integral factor in en-abling natural interaction with embodied AI agents. For such agents to support seamless collaboration, they must be context-aware and able to understand and respond appropriately to the situation in which the interaction occurs. Disregarding context can disrupt the effectiveness of the user experience. We explore the conceptual space of context-aware behaviour for embodied AI agents within immersive analytics systems, emphasis-ing three key dimensions: spatial context (where the collaboration takes place), social context (the norms and dynamics of the inter-arXiv:2610.21484v1 [cs.HC] 15 Oct 2026 action), and semantic context (the meaning and relevance of the data and tasks at hand). The contributions of this work are: 1) A preliminary conceptual framework of context for embodied AI agents in immersive ana-lytics; 2) a reflective design case study of Cobot using this frame-work; 3) observations on design factors in context-aware embodied AI agents. 

2 BACKGROUND 

In Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), context is broadly under-stood as the set of circumstances that shape and give meaning to interaction. Context can be broadly categorised into task , user , and 

environmental characteristics that a system or tool will be used [14]. Embodied interaction [8], which emphasises physical movement and senses in interaction as part of the cognitive process, empha-sises that the context in which action occurs is critical to interpret-ing the meaning of that action and outcome. 

2.1 Immersive Analytics 

Immersive Analytics [3, 15] can be defined as “the use of engaging, embodied analysis tools to support data understanding and deci-sion making”. Immersive analytics has been broadly investigated across a wide range of domains, from molecular simulation visu-alisation [28] to using embodied axes to construct 3D scatterplots for data analysis [7]. Despite interest in the intersection of immer-sive analytics and artificial intelligence , the current literature ex-ploring their intersection is sparse [30]. Some examples include ImmersiveIML, developed by Cordeil et al. [6], which enabled AI-supported user annotations of tree point cloud data in virtual real-ity. ImmersiveIML aimed to enable users to annotate point clouds more accurately by augmenting human capabilities with AI. Qu et al. [22] investigated using 3D visualisation to visualise outputs from a machine learning model to predict whether patients had ”Rhab-domyosarcoma”, a rare soft tissue cancer. Outside of sparse ex-amples, researchers such as Wang et al. [30] argue that there is a general lack of ”emerging AI techniques” and ”limited integration of AI” explored in the context of Immersive Analytics .

2.2 Human-AI Collaboration 

Human-AI Collaboration [31] can be described as “artificially in-telligent agents and systems” that aim to collaborate with humans. The collaborative factor opposes the traditional view that artifi-cially intelligent agents and systems are a “black box” with the intent of outperforming humans in benchmark tasks [31]. Early demonstrations of Human-AI collaboration include Winograd’s SHRDLU system [32]. SHRDLU showcased a human convers-ing with a computer using text input to perform operations such as moving virtual objects and querying domains through a graph-ics terminal. Winograd aimed to create a system that was flexible 

and adaptable to different types of users, as opposed to adhering to a rigid command-based syntax [32]. Since the advancement and mass adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) [25], research has been increasingly investigating how AI could anthropomor-phise itself as a “collaborator” and assistant. For example, GitHub Copilot 1 is a conversational agent that allows developers to “auto-complete” code while programming within an IDE and has seen enabled productivity gains from developers utilising the tool [34].

2.3 Anthropomorphism and Agent Embodiment 

Anthropomorphism refers to “non-human agents with human-like 

characteristics , motivations , intentions or emotions ” [9]. When de-signing non-human agents, it is integral to consider anthropomor-phic factors to make users feel more co-present with their agents [20] and to reduce discomfort [10]. For example, Garcia et al. [10] found that in a study comparing a humanoid to a non-humanoid avatar, museum-goers preferred the non-humanoid avatar for con-veying information about exhibited objects. Seminal work by Nowak and Biocca [20] found that less human-like images elicited higher social and co-presence than human-like images. Schwind et al. [26] argue for using ”childish” features in avatar design, namely 

stylisation (snub noses and round head shapes), to avoid uncanny valley effects. Strong atypicalities in avatar design (particularly in humanoid avatars) can elicit eeriness and promote factors such as threat avoidance [5], which can create barriers to human-agent in-teraction. 

2.4 Context-Aware Computing 

Schilit et al.[24] describe context-aware computing as software that “examines and reacts to an individual’s changing context”. Con-text relative to context-aware computing can be delineated between three aspects: 1) where you are ; 2) who you are with ; 3) what re-sources are nearby [24]. Inspired by these aspects as social and 

spatial dimensions, we argue that embodied AI agents should also consider semantics [11]. Semantics concerning AI can be described as an interpretation function between things and symbols [33]. Mc-Carthy [16] argues that to make AI human-level , systems need to understand the “common sense” of an informatic situation, reason-ing and translating semantic information that could be implicit (e.g. user intention and role). 

3 CONTEXT IN EMBODIED AGENT INTERACTION 

How we design embodied AI agents in immersive analytics systems remains an open question. The behaviours these agents should ex-hibit, how they look, and how they respond to the user and the environment must all be considered. To support these considera-tions, we propose that context can be presented as a fundamental framework to help support designers when creating these agents. We classify context for embodied AI agents in immersive analytics into three dimensions: Sp spatial context describes how agents inhabit, move within, and respect physical space; So social con-text describes the agent adhering to social norms in interaction; and  

> Sm

semantic context describes how agents respond meaningfully within the flow of tasks and conversation. These dimensions can be further classified into embeddedness 

and awareness . Embeddedness describes the factors that situate the embodied agent in a particular dimension (e.g. rendering a shadow below the agent helps situate the agent in spatial context). Aware-ness refers to the factors in the dimension that the embodied agent should sense and actively reflect back to the user (e.g. the agent should be aware of the physical space and move in it appropriately to enforce spatial context). In our conceptual framework, it is important that the embodied agents are designed to account for factors within these contexts; an agent should feel situ-ated and aware of its spatial, social, and semantic context. Agents that do not consider these dimensions and their factors will create friction with the user and potentially leave them confused and frus-trated (effects that we hypothesise further in section 5).

4 COBOT AS A DESIGN PROBE 

We built Cobot, an embodied AI agent for immersive analytics, to explore several of the contextual factors identified in our prelimi-nary conceptual framework. In this paper, we present Cobot as a de-sign probe—being an “object designed to ask questions and present challenges in an open-ended, often provocative manner” [29]—into our framework. Our aim is to use Cobot to examine this conceptual space and stimulate critical reflection and discussion. Cobot is a virtual assistant that works alongside analysts in AR to support immersive data exploration. Designed to feel like a help-ful collaborator, Cobot responds to spoken commands, creates vi-sualisations like maps or graphs in the user’s physical space, and adapts its behaviour based on the analyst’s actions and surround-ings. Rather than acting as a tool to be operated, we wanted Cobot to appear to the user as a partner, helping users make sense of com-plex data by maintaining spatial awareness, providing relevant vi-sual feedback, and supporting more natural, conversational interac-tion. Cobot was developed in Unity 2019.4.28f1 and ran on the Mi-crosoft Hololens 2 2. Cobot was built on top of CADET [17], an augmented-reality based immersive analytics tool for visualising spatial and event-based data. In CADET, users can create maps, plot entities and events on those maps, read event data, create 3D graphs of entity interactions, and view timelines. 

4.1 Appearance 

Cobot was visually designed as a cartoon-like floating robot in-spired by characters such as EVE from Pixar’s Wall-E 3 and Wheat-ley from the Valve game Portal 4, with eyes and ears that can emote (see Figure 1, right). This aesthetic design was chosen to avoid the uncanny valley [18] issue with human-like avatars. Cobot is visually rendered using an environment map captured from the user’s environment (supporting environment lighting Sp1 ), reflect-ing lights and other elements in the environment on its surface. This helps embed Cobot into the physical space, increasing the user’s impression of plausibility. 

4.2 Voice 

Users interact with Cobot by speaking to it. While an ideal em-bodied agent would continuously listen to the user and automati-cally identify when it should take action, we implemented a manual trigger mechanism to ease the implementation. Similar to existing smart assistants (e.g., Google Assistant, Siri), Cobot utilises a wake word detector [2] to initiate listening, employing a Voice Activa-tion Detection (VAD) library to detect the end of a user’s utterance and trigger an action. Cobot responds with voice ( vocalisation So3 )audio that is spatially registered. It also emits a low hum, helping users maintain awareness of its presence and position. 

4.2.1 Action inference 

To generate actions within the system from the user’s voice, the system relays the user’s query to OpenAI’s ChatGPT (using the gpt-3.5-turbo model), accompanied by our custom system prompt. This system prompt describes the scene and the user’s surrounding visualisations, and instructs the LLM to generate Lua code to control Cobot. This LLM model and prompting enables contextual factors of agent role So1 , task intent So4 , and conver-sation history So5 .

4.3 Behaviour 

Cobot’s behaviour is state-based, transitioning between states based on the user’s actions. Cobot’s behaviour has three states: idle , lis-tening/thinking , and active .

4.3.1 Idle 

While idle, Cobot adopts a side-by-side pose, staying on the user’s right side outside their field of view ( user position Sp6 ). This be-haviour was adopted to feel less confrontational when not convers-ing with Cobot. Cobot maintains distance from the user to avoid stepping into their personal space (tuned to 80cm) to enforce prox-emics So6 . When the user turns around (rotates), Cobot moves ac-cordingly around the user’s personal space to stay on the right side of the user. This occurs after a short delay of 1 second, allowing the user to get Cobot’s attention by staring at it ( user gaze So8 ) as an alternative to using the wake-word. 

4.3.2 Listening/Thinking 

In listening/thinking state, Cobot moves in front of the user and pro-vides eye contact as a social attention So2 cue, orienting toward the user. Cobot provides head movements and emotes as social collab-orative cues of expression So1 . As an example, shown in Figure 2, Cobot will tilt its head when it is “thinking” (i.e. processing the LLM prompt). Such an anthropomorphic cue reduces user frustra-tion and potential acceptance of the agent as a collaborator rather than a tool. 

4.3.3 Active 

Finally, in active state, Cobot is free to navigate from point to point based on the actions generated by the LLM (the actions specified in the Lua code), under the constraints specified below: 1. Trajectory: When the Lua code requests Cobot to go to a new location, the system first calculates a straight line trajectory, unless the line overlaps with the user’s personal space radius. Should this be the case, the trajectory is changed to a Bezier curve, using Cobot’s current heading as start tangent and adjust-ing the end tangent to carefully avoid the user’s personal space (enforcing proxemics So6 ). 2. Avoid collision with the user at all costs: Because the user is allowed to walk freely through the virtual space, it is possible for the user to walk into Cobot’s path. To prevent this, we’ve added a repulsive force between the user and Cobot, the magnitude of which is inversely proportional to the squared distance between

5 USE CASE SCENARIOS 

In this section, we contrast the Cobot design probe against an AI agent that has not considered context across a use case scenario. In this scenario, “ Sophia ” is a data analyst exploring a dataset of tracks from shipping vessels to understand patterns, trends and outliers. We begin by describing an interaction with Cobot as a context-coordinated AI agent (illustrated in Figure 4): Context-coordinated AI agent Sofia is standing at her workspace, wearing an AR headset. Cobot hovers nearby, slightly behind her right shoulder not in the way, but present. Sophia turns her head to look at Cobot and says, “Show me global shipping activity this week.” Cobot thinks for a minute and moves around and in-front of Sophia. Cobot projects a map of the world with all shipping data rendered as paths on the map. Cobot returns to Sophia’s side. Sofia leans in to inspect: “Cobot, Zoom in on that dense cluster near Singapore.” Cobot moves towards the map again, zooming into selected region. Individual vessel tracks emerge. Sofia asks, “Cobot, what’s going on with that one?” Cobot says “That’s the MV Horizon Dawn. It’s been stationary for 14 hours.” Sofia looks at Cobot and asks, “List the events with that ship.” Cobot thinks by tilting its head, navigates to a clear part of the room, and a mini-timeline of events appears in-front of Cobot. As Sofia walks around to view from another angle, Cobot smoothly repositions, never occluding her view or drifting through her body. In the following callout, we describe an embodied AI agent that is the antithesis to our conceptual framework (illustrated in Fig-ure 4, right ). Non context-coordinated agent Sofia activates the system. The embodied AI materialises directly in front of her, clipping through Sophia and mak-ing her feel uncomfortable. Sophia moves backward to provide space and says, “Show me global shipping activ-ity.” The agent pauses without feedback. After some time, Sophia turns and realises that the agent has created the map behind her, failing to be aware of the spatial context. This makes Sophia feel frustrated as she had been waiting for no reason. She tries to inspect Singapore. Sophia asks: “Zoom in on Singapore.” As the agent is unaware of context (e.g. user gaze), Sophia needs to clarify the particular visualisation to zoom in on, adding friction to the collaboration. Lastly, Sophia asks: “Show me all shipping events”. The agent teleports to another part of the room (failing to main-tain physical coherence), surprising Sophia, and creates a timeline of events overlapping another visualisation. As we can see in this antithetical use case scenario, an agent that does not coordinate the spatial, social, and semantic contexts cre-ates friction in the interactions. This friction can invoke negative feelings in the user, including frustration, surprise, and uncomfort-ableness. Furthermore, these forms of interaction likely increase cognitive load due to not matching the user’s mental models and expectations, and may have negative impact on trust.2 

6 IMPLICATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

Many of the identified features of the Cobot design probe cross-cut through contexts, implying strong relationships between certain factors within the contexts. To illustrate, consider Schmandt and Bolt’s “put-that-there” paradigm [4], where a user may ask an agent to move or place something in their spaces. In this case, the embod-ied agent needs to coordinate across user gestures So9 , physical environment Sp4 , and task intent Sm4 . There are likely consistent design patterns that exist across these dimensions that can assist designers in their process. We observe, at least in our design probe, that the semantic di-mension is predominantly handled by the LLM. Agent role Sm1 ,task intent Sm5 , and conversation history Sm5 , are integrated into the prompts. Proximity to relevant visualisation Sm2 requires some awareness of the physical environment Sp4 , but again, the LLM is responsible for determining what aspects of the environ-ment and visualisation are relevant. Informal testing suggests that Cobot’s navigation features, while simplified approximations of social proxemics So6 , support reason-ably natural collaboration. For some factors, “good enough” solu-tions or best practices may suffice. Future work should aim to iden-tify and evaluate these, as well as explore which contextual factors are most critical to effective interaction. The conceptual framework is preliminary. We believe there may be more factors to identify. Our exploration has been predomi-nantly single-user, future iterations of the framework should exam-ine multi-user collaboration. 

7 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we identified and explored a context framework for embodied agents, encompassing spatial, social, and semantic di-mensions. Coordinating these dimensions is a central concern in the design of effective embodied systems. Through Cobot, we demon-strate how framing design around context can be realised and ad-dress potential pain-points that effect user experience. The frame-work we present is preliminary; we encourage the research com-munity to further refine and develop this theoretical framework. Future work should investigate multi-user collaboration, examine the effects of the individual factors, and establish appropriate de-sign patterns to help realise effective embodied AI for immersive analytics.
