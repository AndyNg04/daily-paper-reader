# PID-Gated Continual Prompt Adaptation Under Noisy Feedback 

Zhentao Xu 

Independent Researcher Sunnyvale, CA, USA

## Abstract 

Deploying LLM agents in non-stationary environments requires continually revis-ing their instructions as the task drifts, yet rewriting after every reported mistake can turn noisy feedback into persistent errors. This raises an often-overlooked scheduling question in prompt adaptation: when should feedback trigger a last-ing instruction update, and at what editor-call cost? Taking inspiration from proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control, we introduce a family of lightweight gates that react to the current error, accumulate evidence over time, and roll back deterioration after an edit. We benchmark these gates against both simple baselines and advanced prompt optimizers (TextGrad and GEPA), over a diverse suite of drifting tasks, LLM parameter sizes, feedback-noise levels, and random seeds. PID-gated continual prompt adaptation yields a more favorable Pareto trade-off between task performance (high accuracy, low error) and editor-call budget than the baselines considered, pointing to control-theoretic scheduling as a simple, general tool for robust prompt adaptation under unreliable feedback. We open source our code at https://github.com/zhentao-xu/pid-gated-agent-learning .

## 1 Introduction 

Consider an agent guessing a hidden integer under noisy high/low feedback: it holds a belief about the plausible range [L, U ] that must tighten as evidence accumulates, yet a single mislabeled “higher” reply can widen the wrong bound and lock in errors for many rounds. This is the general dilemma of adapting instructions in a changing environment: revising after every reported mistake can turn a useful rule into a persistent source of errors, while waiting leaves the agent following an outdated rule. The challenge is deciding when a mistake warrants a lasting change. Language models offer several ways to learn from feedback: revising an answer [11], retaining verbal experience across attempts [18], or optimizing prompts through textual feedback [26] and candidate search [1, 24]. In each, however, the scheduling decision is fixed: revisions fire after every attempt or after every observed error subject to a call ceiling, offering no principled way to withhold an edit when the feedback itself might be wrong. We instead ask a complementary scheduling question: 

when should feedback trigger a persistent instruction update? This connects prompt adaptation to concept drift [7] and continual learning [23]. Proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control [3] suggests three complementary responses: reacting to current error, accumulating evidence over time, and monitoring deterioration after an edit [4]. We translate this intuition into discrete editing gates and compare them against online TextGrad and GEPA using one LLM-based editor and structured predictors. Across our empirical study (Section 5, Table 1, Figure 2), the PID gates collectively yield a more favorable Pareto trade-off between task performance and editor-call budget than the baselines.

## 2 Related Work 

Self-evolving LLM agents and noisy evaluators. Methods that improve LLM behavior from natural-language feedback split by what they update. In the textual loop, Self-Refine [11], Reflexion [18], TextGrad [26], OPRO [24], and GEPA [1] revise prompts, chains-of-thought, or candidate instructions in response to observed errors, and skill-library agents such as Voyager [20] and Skill-Weaver [28] accumulate reusable textual skills. In the parameter loop, STaR [27], Self-Rewarding Language Models [25], RLHF [13], and Constitutional AI [5] convert feedback into weight updates. Our PID gates target the textual loop, where every edit is a stochastic LLM rewrite of a persistent arti-fact, so the scheduling question of when to invoke the editor is especially acute. The feedback signal itself is unreliable in both regimes: LLM-as-judge suffers position, verbosity, and self-preference biases [14, 22, 29], sycophancy [17], and models cannot reliably self-correct [8]. This motivates evidence-gated editing rather than reacting to every reported error. 

Control theory for learning and continual adaptation. Feedback control has long informed machine learning: SGD-momentum as PI control [2], KL-weight scheduling in VAEs as PID regulation [16], constrained RL as a PID-Lagrangian [19], and deep-learning optimizers as continuous-time PID [6]. Our work instantiates PID intuition outside the parameter space of a differentiable model, on natural-language artifacts driven by a stochastic LLM editor. On the continual-learning side, catastrophic forgetting [12] and the stability-plasticity dilemma have driven decades of research [7, 15, 21, 23]. Classical parameter-space defenses (elastic weight consolidation [9], replay memory [10]) have natural prompt-space analogues in the buffered evidence and rollback rules used here.

A PID controller reads the histories {eτ , v τ }τ ≤t

and decides whether to invoke the LLM editor g, which returns θt+1 = g(θt, {vτ }). Because g is a stochastic actuator with no gradient and θ is a persistent state whose bad edits compound, the controller’s role is scheduling ( when and on what evidence to call g) rather than choosing the prompt itself; the goal is to track yt at low editing cost while remaining robust to corruption in ˜yt. In the guess-number game, for instance, θt encodes the agent’s belief about the plausible value range as integer bounds [L, U ]; on each turn ( xt), the LLM predictor f emits a numeric guess ( ˆyt) conditioned on this belief; the environment scores it against a hidden target ( yt) and returns high/low/correct feedback with a distance bucket ( ˜yt) that the sensor may flip; the comparator packages this into 

(et, v t), e.g., “guess was too low, distance 10–19”; when the controller fires, the LLM editor g rewrites 

[L, U ], tightening the belief for θt+1 .23.2 PID-inspired Gating 

We map each classical PID component onto a discrete editing gate. All three gates share the same eligibility check (unused call budget, error evidence since the last reset, no mandatory pause) and invoke the same LLM editor g, which receives the current prompt and up to K recent observed examples (including correct ones; K = 8 in our experiments); invalid proposals retain the prompt and every model call counts against the budget. ## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Tasks 

We design three simple but representative tasks to test the self-evolving agent’s capability across different output types (categorical and numeric) and drift patterns (sudden reversal, abrupt state shift, and smooth continuous drift). Together they probe how quickly the agent detects change, how it accumulates evidence under noisy feedback, and how gracefully it tracks a target that never stops moving. Hidden targets, boundaries, and initial rules are seed-randomized and never shown to the editor, so the study isolates LLM-mediated policy editing from end-to-end agent reasoning. • Parity (categorical, sudden rule flips). Inputs are integers 1–10; the hidden rule assigns Red/Blue by parity, and the mapping reverses near steps 30, 60, and 90. Feedback is a categorical color, corrupted independently with probability η. Stresses categorical rule detection under discrete reversals. • Guess number (numeric, abrupt state shifts). The agent guesses a hidden integer in [1 , 100] 

that shifts abruptly and periodically; feedback is high/low/correct paired with a distance bucket. Stresses numeric adaptation when the target state jumps. • Track moving target (numeric, smooth continuous drift). The hidden integer follows a smooth continuous trajectory (e.g., sinusoidal, rectangular, or exponential); our implementa-tion uses a sinusoid completing ∼ 1.5 cycles over 120 steps, with no discrete change points. Stresses continuous tracking without visible boundaries a controller could anchor to. 

4.2 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate seven policies (No Edit, Fixed Cadence, Proportional (P), Integral (I), Derivative (D), TextGrad, GEPA) on the three tasks under three feedback-corruption levels η ∈ { 0, 0.1, 0.2} with 

T = 120 steps per trajectory, using the same LLM as both predictor and editor; results are mean ±

std over 5 seeds per cell. No Edit keeps the initial prompt unchanged for the full trajectory (zero edits, zero editor calls) and serves as a no-adaptation reference; Fixed Cadence fires an edit every 15 steps regardless of the error signal and serves as a data-independent control. Seeded inputs, hidden targets, and controller settings are paired across corruption levels for matched comparison. Evaluation 3metrics are task-specific: classification accuracy for Parity (higher is better), mean absolute error (MAE) for Guess number (lower is better), and root-mean-squared error (RMSE) for Track moving target (lower is better). Model calls are counted at every generation stage; tokens and deterministic candidate evaluations are reported separately. TextGrad and GEPA use the default parameters from their published libraries; interface and accounting details are given in Appendix B. We report primary results on Qwen3-14B (Table 1) and a scaling-up ablation on Qwen3.8-27B (Table 2).

## 6 Conclusion and Future Work 

We introduced PID-gated scheduling (Proportional, Integral, Derivative) that decides when to invoke LLM prompt editors for continual prompt adaptation under drifting tasks and noisy feedback. Across three drifting tasks, the PID gates collectively yield a more favorable Pareto trade-off between task performance and editor-call budget than the baselines considered, typically matching the strongest optimizer at a fraction of its call cost (Section 5, Tables 1–2, Figure 2). Future work includes the

TextGrad [26] and GEPA [1] use their pinned official packages, with inference routed exclusively through the common LLM-based editor. Both trigger after two observed errors since the previous invocation and require at least three remaining calls. Each restarts from the currently deployed prompt and receives at most eight past/current observations. Neither accesses clean evaluation metrics or future observations. These adaptations compare online use of the algorithms with measured resource costs, rather than reproducing their original task suites or claiming that all implementations are equivalent. 

TextGrad. Version 0.1.8 uses an explicit engine for each of three stages: TextLoss critiques the current structured prompt against the task specification and observed feedback; backward produces textual gradient feedback; TextualGradientDescent proposes an updated variable. Gradient memory is zero. Loss and backward stages request concise critiques of at most 100 words; the update retains the package’s required variable tags. Deterministic prediction is outside the autograd graph: the optimized variable is the structured policy prompt itself. Missing tags or an invalid structured proposal retain the deployed prompt and consume all three calls. 

GEPA. Version 0.1.4 starts a fresh search population from the deployed prompt, uses instance-level Pareto parent selection, and permits at most three model reflections per invocation. Accepted proposals must strictly improve on a common training minibatch before full validation. Alternating history entries form disjoint training and validation subsets when at least four observations are available, with the newest in training; smaller histories reuse the same observations in both. Minibatches contain at most three training examples. Optional merging is disabled, and perfect-score minibatches may skip reflection. Validation is past noisy evidence, not an independent estimate of future task performance. The common client counts all reflection calls. Candidate-example evaluations include rejected and malformed candidates and are logged separately. A nominal metric-evaluation limit provides an additional termination condition for skipped reflections; the official search checks it between iterations, so realized evaluations can exceed that nominal limit. Every proposal, selected parent, fitness evaluation, validation score, and final selection is retained. An invocation containing an invalid proposal may still deploy a different valid candidate; invalid-proposal count is therefore distinct from final prompt changes. Unexpected model or library failures halt the run rather than being relabeled successful no-ops. 9C Robustness Across Feedback-Noise Levels
