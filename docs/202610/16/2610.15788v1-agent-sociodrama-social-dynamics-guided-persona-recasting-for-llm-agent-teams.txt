ABSTRACT 

Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly deployed as teams, yet they fail in characteristically social ways: information holders stay silent, dominant speakers anchor groups on wrong answers, and norm violations go unsanctioned. We present Agent Sociodrama, a framework for modeling and validating evolv-ing social dynamics in LLM agent teams. Under fixed personas, agents’ emo-tional states and directed, asymmetric relationships are updated every round by a unified external LLM judge and shape their subsequent interactions. Across six LLM backbones and five social scenarios, we assess social fidelity through classical social-theory patterns and agreement with human judgments. The result-ing trajectories exhibit structured social patterns and model-specific differences. We then introduce Trajectory-Guided Persona Recasting (TPR), which diagnoses group-dynamic failures from interaction transcripts and social trajectories and re-casts agent personas before rerunning the task. Across four collaborative bench-marks, TPR improves performance from both heterogeneous and randomly initial-ized agent persona compositions, yielding up to 83.3%p of accuracy improvement over the initial casting. On representative distributed-information cooperation and mixed-motive bargaining tasks, we outperform SOTOPIA and AgentVerse in three of four comparisons, by up to 37.5%p in Pareto rate. These results show that vali-dated social trajectories provide actionable signals for repairing LLM agent teams. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly deployed in teams (Wu et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024): they debate answers (Du et al., 2024), pool distributed evidence, teach one another, negotiate, and manage shared resources. Yet sustained multi-agent interaction requires more than assigning each agent an initial role. For a team to function as a social system over time, its members must form, maintain, and revise relationships in ways that remain coherent with the interaction. Existing agent systems often encode social context through static role instructions or unstructured memory, without an explicit and validated account of how directed relationships evolve over time. We take a different route. We argue that longitudinal role play requires treating a team not as a collection of fixed personas, but as a dynamic social system. A persona specifies who an agent is, but not how its relationships with others develop over time, including whom it comes to trust, avoid, align with, or oppose. These relationships must therefore be represented as evolving interaction states. The key challenge is not merely to model changing relations, but to ensure that those changes remain socially faithful over time. To instantiate this idea, we propose Agent Sociodrama, a longitudinal role-playing framework for LLM agent teams. Agent Sociodrama operationalizes stable personas together with evolving emo-tional and relational states, providing a structured account of how a team’s social configuration develops over repeated interaction. 2 RELATED WORK 

Social simulation and coordination dynamics. LLM social simulation spans memory-based societies (Park et al., 2023), social-intelligence evaluation (Zhou et al., 2024), coordinated role play (Wang et al., 2025), and social reasoning and longitudinal interaction (Mou et al., 2025; Goel & Zhu, 2025). LLM teams exhibit information-pooling failures (Stasser & Titus, 1985), underused expertise (Pappu et al., 2026), unshared evidence (Li et al., 2026), and coordination errors (Cemri et al., 2025). BenchForm examines conformity (Weng et al., 2025), while MultiAgentBench evalu-ates collaboration alongside task completion (Zhu et al., 2025). Drawing on affect (Russell, 2003), reciprocity (Gouldner, 1960), group boundaries (Krackhardt & Stern, 1988), and conflict dynam-ics (Pruitt & Kim, 2004), Agent Sociodrama models evolving affect and directed relationships as persona-conditioned states. Their trajectories complement transcripts in diagnosing team dynamics and guiding persona recasting. 

Persona composition and multi-agent optimization. Personality research motivates stable be-havioral specifications (McCrae & Costa, 1987), while persona composition effects depend on task structure (Keluskar et al., 2026). MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2024) and AutoGen (Wu et al., 2024) provide role-based coordination, whereas EvoAgent evolves agent configurations (Yuan et al., 2025). Automated design optimizes computational graphs (Zhuge et al., 2024), prompts and topolo-gies (Zhou et al., 2026), workflows (Zhang et al., 2025), and code-defined agents (Hu et al., 2025); learned orchestration adapts agent activation (Dang et al., 2025). Agent Sociodrama searches per-sona assignments from a fixed library between task runs. Social trajectories guide proposed changes, while task outcomes determine configuration selection, linking persona composition to each team’s observed interaction dynamics. 

3 AGENT SOCIODRAMA : S IMULATION WITH MEASURABLE SOCIAL STATE 

We study multi-agent social simulation with dynamically evolving relationships. Given N agents 

A = {A1, . . . , A N }, each with a fixed persona, they interact over T rounds within a shared narrative scenario. While prior work on multi-agent collaboration or debate either assumes static inter-agent relationships (Li et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024) or does not explicitly model them (Du et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024), we let every interaction update the agents’ relationships and emotional states, which in turn shape how they communicate in subsequent rounds. This feedback process gives rise to unscripted social dynamics, such as shifting alliances, declining trust, and growing disagreement. The simulation takes as input a persona for each agent ( P ), initial emotional states ( S0), pairwise re-lationships ( R0), and a sequence of scenario events. The persona captures the stable core of an agent, its temperament, values, and characteristic style of interaction, and remains fixed throughout a run, whereas the agent’s actions, emotional states, and directed relationships evolve continually across rounds. Two-phase action generation. A fixed or random speaking order of agents would favor later speakers, as they observe more context before responding. We therefore decouple whether and how 

to speak from what to say. In Phase 1, every agent independently receives its state (P, S, R ) and the shared context, and output a communicative urgency u ∈ [0 , 10] , a mode (public, private, or silence), a target agent, and a one-sentence communication intent . Because no agent has spoken yet in the current round, these decisions are made under identical information, preventing earlier utterances from influencing the public/private choice. In Phase 2, agents speak sequentially in descending order of urgency; silent agents are skipped and incur no inference cost, and each speaker observes the utterances of earlier speakers together with any private messages addressed to that speaker. Urgency-based ordering avoids advantages arising merely from speaking position, while allowing agents to remain silent avoids unnecessary speech in every round. 

Emotional update ( US ). US is a persona-conditioned LLM call that receives the trait block of Pi,the current state Sti , and the visible events Eti . The call returns a signed change δS , which is clipped to [−0.3, 0.3] to limit abrupt changes within a single round. The emotional state is then updated as

Relational update ( UR). An event in Etij triggers UR, an LLM call conditioned on the trait block of Pi, the current relationship Rtij , and the triggering event. It returns a favorability change 

δfav ∈ [−0.3, 0.3] , an optional status update, a one-sentence event summary, and an importance score imp ∈ [1 , 10] . We apply fav ← clip( fav + δfav , −1, 1) . Status combines gradual threshold-based transitions with consequential-event updates from UR (e.g., betrayal or alliance); when both apply, the latter takes precedence (Appendix B). Event summaries and importance scores are stored in Mij with their rounds. Memory stores up to K entries as a compact relationship history for future updates without retaining the full transcript. When full, it evicts entries with the lowest retention scores, gradually removing routine interactions while preserving consequential events longer. Emotional and relational updates are computed independently within each round from visible events and persona, avoiding circular dependencies and allowing their roles to be analyzed separately. 

4 THE CASTING CONTROLLER 

Given a persona composition, the simulation framework in Sec. 3 generates an interaction trajectory 

Y that records the agents’ actions and the evolution of their emotional states S and directed rela-tionships R. Crucially, persona composition is itself a consequential design choice. With the same backbone and the same private information, changing only the persona casting raises GPT-5-mini’s 5Preprint top-1 accuracy on the Student-council hidden-profile task from 16.7% to 100.0% (Sec. 5.3). We therefore introduce a casting controller that treats persona composition as its decision variable and optimizes it guided by the measured social trajectories (Fig. 3). A casting c = ( P1, . . . , P N ) assigns one persona to each of N agents, with each Pi drawn from a li-brary of five archetypes, P = {BALANCED , DOMINANT , ANALYTICAL , AGREEABLE , RESERVED }.Each task poses a distinct coordination problem: Student-council requires agents to surface dis-tributed evidence, whereas Solar-farm negotiation requires them to reconcile competing interests. From an initial casting, the controller evaluates task performance, diagnoses the interaction trajec-tory, and selectively recasts agents to improve the team’s ability to solve the task. Objective. Each task defines a scalar utility u(c)

computed from the interaction outcome. The con-troller aims to identify a casting that maximizes ex-pected utility, c⋆ = arg max c E[u(c)] . We estimate the expectation over repeated episodes. The utility is defined by the task’s primary metric: top-1 ac-curacy for the Student-council hidden-profile task, the negative L1 distance to the expert ranking for NASA Moon Survival and Lost at Sea, and Pareto-efficient settlement rate for Solar-farm negotiation. Across tasks, only the task context and utility def-inition vary; the recasting procedure, archetype li-brary, and diagnostic logic remain fixed. 

Iterative persona recasting. Searching over all 

|P| N possible castings is unnecessary and compu-tationally costly. The measured interaction trajec-tories provide signals about which agents to recast and how . Given a initial casting c0, each iteration proceeds in three steps (Algorithm 1): 1. Evaluate. Run the scenario r times under ck, recording the utility u, the transcript, and the trajectories of emotional states S and directed relationships R for each run. 2. Diagnose. An external LLM examines each run’s persona composition, transcript, final out-come, and the trajectories of emotional states (St)Tt=1 and directed relationships (Rt)Tt=1 . It identifies whose information was suppressed, which agents dominated unhelpfully, and how the team should be recast, providing a one-sentence rationale for each change. 3. Recast. Obtain ck+1 by majority vote over each agent’s recasting decisions across the r runs. The loop terminates when the task target is reached, no persona change is proposed, or after at most three recasts. 

Role of social trajectories in diagnosis. The transcript records what agents say, while the trajecto-ries summarize how their states and relationships evolve. The controller examines these signals at three levels. At the individual level, the emotional state S helps distinguish neutral silence from with-drawal after repeated dismissal. At the dyadic level, a directed relationship Rij tracks how agent i’s stance toward agent j evolves, revealing when an information holder is gradually disregarded despite outwardly polite dialogue. At the group level, patterns in R reveal emerging alliances, polarization, and shared responses to norm violations that are not explicitly articulated in the conversation. Using these signals and the transcript, the controller localizes failures to particular agents and relationships rather than falling back on generic advice such as “encourage more discussion.”

These two tasks capture distributed-information cooperation and mixed-motive bargaining, respectively. 5.2 SOCIAL DYNAMICS FIDELITY ON AGENT SOCIODRAMA 

We evaluate social dynamics fidelity from three perspectives: (1) whether the trajectories respond coherently as social pressure varies, (2) whether LLMs exhibit distinct social dynamics under the same scenario, and (3) whether their emotional and relational changes agree with human judgments.

6 CONCLUSION 

We present Agent Sociodrama, a new framework for modeling and validating evolving social dy-namics in LLM agents. We track directed, asymmetric relationships and emotional states, validating cross-model comparability under a unified judge, agreement with classical social theory, and statis-tical reliability. The casting controller then turns these trajectories into targeted interventions: using relational and emotional trajectories and transcripts, it identifies problematic agents and relation-ships and recasts their personas, matching or surpassing the strongest manual casting or published reference in collaborative tasks. These results show that social dynamics can serve not only as mea-surable properties of LLM teams, but also as actionable signals for improving their composition. 10 Preprint

A LIMITATIONS 

Several limitations point to future work. The fidelity evaluation relies on a single unified LLM judge and five designed scenarios; replicating the observed regularities with additional judges and structurally different scenarios would strengthen the external validity of the measurements. The op-timization experiments use 12 episodes per casting, which remains small relative to the variance of LLM interaction; we therefore report consistent directions across tasks, backbones, and initializa-tions rather than per-cell significance claims. Finally, casting is a coarse, episode-level intervention; the same measured trajectories could drive finer-grained repairs such as runtime mediation, targeted information routing, or norm enforcement, which we view as the natural next step for measurement-driven multi-agent optimization.

E PERSONA -R ECASTING EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Tasks. Tab. 5 summarizes the four collaborative tasks. Student Council, NASA Moon Survival, and Lost at Sea are information-integration tasks in which decisive knowledge is privately dis-tributed across members; Solar-farm Negotiation is a mixed-motive task in which parties with pri-vate preferences must reach a feasible and Pareto-efficient agreement. Each task is played by four agents, Avery, Blake, Casey, and Drew. Private knowledge remains fixed under recasting.In the three information-integration tasks, each episode consists of an individual ranking before discus-sion, four discussion rounds, and a final individual ranking; the team answer aggregates the four final rankings with a Borda count. Top-1 accuracy checks whether the team’s first choice is the hidden-profile optimum, and L1 is the sum of absolute rank differences from the expert ranking over the 15 items. In Solar-farm Negotiation, four parties with private scoring tables and private acceptance thresholds negotiate five issues with three options each over four rounds, and the final agreement is formed by an issue-wise majority over the parties’ final proposals (Abdelnabi et al., 2024). An agreement is feasible when every party meets its threshold, and Pareto-optimal when it is feasible and no other feasible agreement makes some party better off without making another worse off; because the outcome space contains only 35 = 243 agreements, the Pareto frontier is computed exactly by enumeration. All task metrics are computed by rule from the final outputs rather than by an LLM judge.

We sampled 25 dialogue excerpts by stratified sampling over scenarios, LLM drivers, the two mea-sured dimensions ( R and S), and the three change directions (increase, unchanged, decrease), and added one attention-check item with an unambiguous answer.
