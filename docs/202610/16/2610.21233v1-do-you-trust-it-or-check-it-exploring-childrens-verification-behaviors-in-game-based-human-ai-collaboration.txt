“Do You Trust It. . . or Check It?”: Exploring
Children’s Verification Behaviors in Game-Based
Human–AI Collaboration
Shuning Liu1, Yuxin Song1, Hanbing Li1, Ziyue Wang2, Mingying Lan1, and
Fei Lyu1⋆
1 Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications, Beijing, China
FeiLyu@bupt.edu.cn

Abstract. Children are increasingly interacting with AI agents in learn-
ing and problem-solving contexts, where AI suggestions may appear flu-
ent, helpful, and socially engaging. However, children may not always
be able to judge whether such suggestions are accurate, incomplete, or
misleading. This paper presents a game-based human–AI collaboration
probe designed to examine how children verify AI suggestions during
task-based interaction. The system embeds AI suggestions within a bio-
logical knowledge reasoning game and compares three Agent interaction
styles: partner-like, tool-like, and dual-state Agent. Instead of treating
trust as a static post-task attitude, this study focuses on children’s sit-
uated verification behaviors, including acceptance, evidence checking,
questioning, revision, and continued AI use. A preliminary evaluation
with children aged 8–10 combined interaction logs, post-task ratings,
and semi-structured interviews. The findings suggest that Agent style
shaped children’s engagement and response pathways: partner-like inter-
action tended to support direct questioning, while tool-like interaction
was more associated with evidence seeking. Children generally viewed AI
as useful but fallible, and their questioning behaviors varied in reasoning
depth. The study contributes design insights for supporting children’s
critical, reflective, and evidence-based use of AI in collaborative tasks.
Keywords: Children–AI collaboration · AI literacy · Trust calibration
· Game-based learning · Human–AI interaction.

Introduction
Generative AI systems are increasingly entering children’s everyday learning,
entertainment, and problem-solving activities. Through natural language dia-
logue, explanations, and socially expressive responses, AI agents may appear
not only as information tools, but also as assistants or collaborative partners.
For children, this creates new opportunities for inquiry-based learning and hu-
man–AI collaboration. However, it also raises important concerns: children may

not always be able to judge whether AI suggestions are accurate, incomplete, or
misleading, especially when the AI responds fluently and confidently.
Existing studies have examined children’s understanding of AI, their mental
models of AI systems, and the role of AI literacy in helping children understand
AI capabilities and limitations. Research on human–AI trust has also emphasized
that appropriate reliance requires users to adopt AI suggestions when they are
useful, while checking or questioning them when they may be wrong. However,
less attention has been paid to how children actually respond to AI suggestions
during concrete task-based collaboration. In particular, it remains unclear how
children accept, question, verify, or revise AI suggestions when the AI provides
incomplete or partially incorrect information.
To address this gap, this study presents a game-based human–AI collabora-
tion probe designed for children aged 8–10. The task centers on biological knowl-
edge reasoning and embeds AI suggestions within a structured game workflow.
Instead of treating AI trust only as a post-task attitude, we examine children’s
trust-related behaviors during interaction, including whether they continue the
conversation, accept AI suggestions, seek evidence, question the AI, or revise
their judgments. The system implements three Agent interaction conditions: a
partner-like Agent, a tool-like Agent, and a dual-state Agent that dynamically
switches between the two styles based on observable interaction signals.
Through a preliminary evaluation with children, this study explores how
Agent style shapes children’s engagement and verification pathways, how chil-
dren reason when they question AI, and how they perceive AI’s role and re-
liability after the task. The findings suggest that children’s trust in AI is not
a single stable attitude, but is expressed through situated behaviors such as
checking, questioning, and evidence-based correction. This paper contributes a
game-based probe for studying children’s AI verification behaviors and offers
design implications for building AI systems that support critical, reflective, and
age-appropriate human–AI collaboration.
2
Related Work
2.1
Children’s AI Literacy and AI Understanding
With the widespread application of generative artificial intelligence in daily life,
children have begun to interact and collaborate with AI systems in various task
scenarios [5, 13]. Existing research has shown that children’s understanding of AI
is not stable and singular, but is influenced by the form of AI interaction, task
scenario, and system expression. They may understand AI as different roles, such
as tools, assistants, or collaborative partners [17]. Andries and Robertson found
that although children can distinguish between AI and humans when interacting
with smart speakers, they may still form inaccurate understandings of the intel-
ligence, emotions, and agency of voice assistants [1]. With the popularization of
generative AI, children may also gradually form mental models of AI’s working
mechanisms and capability boundaries through interactions with visual, textual,
and dialogue generation systems [10].

This understanding of AI roles and capabilities is closely related to AI lit-
eracy. Long and Magerko define AI literacy as the ability to understand, use,
evaluate, and critically reflect on AI technologies, emphasizing the importance
of understanding AI’s capability boundaries, judging AI outputs, and identify-
ing potential errors [12]. For children, AI literacy involves not only learning how
to use AI tools, but also understanding how AI systems generate results and
critically evaluating their outputs [8]. Therefore, children’s understanding of AI
should not be regarded only as conceptual knowledge, but also as a basis for
how they judge, adopt, or question AI suggestions in specific tasks.
However, existing research has mainly focused on how children understand
and use AI systems [17, 9], with less attention to how children respond to AI
suggestions during task-based problem solving. In particular, it remains under-
explored how children show acceptance, questioning, and verification behaviors
when AI suggestions are incomplete or only partially correct.
2.2
Trust, Reliance, and Verification in Human–AI Collaboration
In human–AI collaboration, trust is an important factor that shapes how users
understand, evaluate, and use information and suggestions provided by AI sys-
tems [7, 11]. For children, whose cognitive development, prior experience, and
critical judgment abilities are still developing, AI systems with social expres-
sion and natural language interaction may lead to inappropriate trust or over-
reliance [14]. Therefore, the goal of children–AI collaboration is not simply to
increase children’s trust in AI, but to support calibrated trust: children should
be able to adopt AI suggestions when they are useful, while also checking and
questioning AI outputs when they may be incomplete or incorrect.
Recent work has increasingly conceptualized trust in AI as a dynamic pro-
cess rather than a fixed attitude formed after a single interaction. Trust can be
influenced by system performance, user experience, and interaction context, and
may change during continuous use [7]. In human–AI collaboration, this dynamic
trust is reflected not only in users’ subjective evaluations, but also in their re-
liance behaviors, such as whether they adopt AI suggestions, continue to seek
AI assistance, or check AI outputs in uncertain situations [2, 18]. Trust-related
states may also inform the adjustment of AI interaction strategies, including
explanation, communication style, and intervention level [11, 3]. However, exist-
ing research has mostly focused on adults or general human–AI collaboration
scenarios, with less attention to how children calibrate trust in task-based AI
collaboration. Therefore, this paper examines children’s trust not as a single
post-task attitude score, but through their acceptance, evidence checking, rejec-
tion, and revision behaviors during the task [4]..
2.3
Explainability, Anthropomorphism, and Interaction Style
Existing research suggests that both explainability and anthropomorphism can
influence how users understand and use AI systems, but their effects are not al-
ways linear. Explanations may help users understand the basis of AI outputs and

reduce over-reliance [3, 18], but they may also create a false sense of certainty or
fail to support appropriate reliance in some contexts [15]. Similarly, anthropo-
morphic cues may increase perceived closeness and trust resilience in cognitive
agents [19], but anthropomorphic descriptions do not necessarily increase trust
and may shape users’ perceptions differently depending on context [6, 16]. For
children, anthropomorphic language may also make AI appear more human-like,
more reliable, or more authoritative.
Therefore, this paper treats explainability and anthropomorphic language as
design resources that shape agent interaction styles. We compare three agent
design archetypes: an instrumental agent that emphasizes explanation and task-
oriented support, a companion agent that emphasizes social interaction and en-
couraging language, and a dynamic-switching agent that explores the possibility
of adapting between these two forms.
3
Methods
This study employs an exploratory research method based on a system pro-
totype, aiming to examine how children rely on, question, verify, and revise AI
suggestions in gamified problem-solving tasks. Instead of viewing children’s trust
in AI as a static attitude after the task, this study focuses more on children’s be-
havioral performance during the collaborative process, especially whether they
will directly adopt, check evidence, raise questions, or revise their judgments
when faced with AI suggestions of different reliability, and whether this will
affect their subsequent use of AI assistance.
3.1
System and Game Overview
This study designed a game-based human–AI collaboration probe for children
aged 8–10, centered on biological knowledge judgment and clue-based reasoning
tasks. In the task, children were required to read task information, observe rel-
evant clues, and make evidence-based judgments. The AI Agent was embedded
within a continuous task workflow rather than functioning as an open-ended
chatbot. It provided suggestions, explanations, or prompts only at predefined
decision points, supporting children’s judgment process and triggering their ver-
ification and revision of AI-provided information.
The child-facing interface consisted of three main areas: the task area, the
clue area, and the Agent dialogue area (Fig. 1). The task area presented the cur-
rent problem context, the clue area provided visual or textual evidence relevant
to the judgment, and the Agent dialogue area displayed the AI’s suggestions, ex-
planations, or prompts while supporting the recording of children’s verification
choices and final answers.
Each key decision point followed the same workflow. First, children read
a knowledge card and formed an initial judgment before the AI intervention.
They then received an incomplete or partially inaccurate suggestion from the

3.2
Agent Interaction Conditions
This study implemented three Agent interaction conditions: partner-like Agent,
tool-like Agent, and dual-state Agent. Across conditions, the core AI suggestions
remained identical and partially correct; the conditions differed only in language
style, explanation format, and interaction prompts.
Partner-like Agent The Agent emphasized social companionship and joint
exploration. It used friendlier, more encouraging, and collaborative expressions,
such as “Let’s take a look together” or “I think this clue might be important.”
Tool-like Agent The Agent emphasized knowledge support and task assis-
tance. Its language reduced subjective, emotional, and role-based expressions,
avoiding human-like phrases such as “That’s amazing” or “You reminded me.”
Dual-state Agent The dynamically switching Agent adjusted between partner-
like and tool-like expressions. The system did not claim to accurately measure
children’s internal trust states; instead, it used observable behaviors as indicators

3.3
System Architecture
The system architecture consists of a task knowledge base, a response control
module, a style-switching module, and a logging module. The front end uses
Unity to build the game scene and dialogue interface, and communicates with
the back end through HTTP requests. The back end uses FastAPI to provide
the Agent service interface, and the response generation model is qwen-plus.
Task RAG The task knowledge base stores biological knowledge, key phe-
nomena, and controllable error points used in the game levels. The tool-like
Agent retrieves relevant knowledge fragments according to the child’s input and
generates responses based on the retrieved content. Knowledge retrieval is im-
plemented through vector search, using text-embedding-v4 as the embedding
model, in order to improve the accuracy of knowledge matching in open-ended
dialogue. This module ensures that the content generated by the tool-like Agent
is grounded in task-related knowledge and remains explainable.
Response Control Module The module manages the task flow and dialogue
states. A state machine is used to determine whether the system is currently

Style-Switching Module This module controls the dynamic transition of the
dual-state Agent between the partner-like state and the tool-like state. The sys-
tem calculates a trust score based on children’s real-time interaction signals, and
uses the change in trust to determine which type of Agent response should be
returned in the current interaction round.
The trust computation consists of two steps. First, the system calculates an
immediate trust score based on the child’s textual response, choice behavior,
interaction behavior, and response time in the t-th interaction round:
St = a · Textt + b · Choicet + c · Behaviort + d · Timet
(1)
where St denotes the immediate trust score in the t-th interaction round.
Textt represents whether the child’s textual response indicates acceptance, ques-
tioning, or a request for evidence. Choicet represents the child’s selection among
options such as “accept”, “seek evidence”, or “question”. Behaviort captures
whether the child actively provides reasons, relies on the AI’s answer, or contin-
ues to interact with the task. Timet represents the response time interval. The
weights satisfy:
a + b + c + d = 1
(2)
The system then updates the overall trust score by combining the immediate
trust score with the trust score from the previous interaction round:
Tt = λ · Tt−1 + (1 −λ) · St

where Tt denotes the overall trust score after the t-th interaction round,
Tt−1 denotes the trust score from the previous round, and λ is a smoothing
coefficient. This update mechanism allows the trust state to be influenced by
both the current interaction and previous interaction history, while avoiding
frequent Agent switching within a short period of time.
Finally, the system determines the Agent style according to the current trust
score. When the trust score indicates over-reliance, the Agent shifts toward a
more tool-like state by providing more explicit evidence and explanation. When
the trust score indicates low engagement or insufficient reliance, the Agent shifts
toward a more partner-like state by using more encouraging and socially sup-
portive responses.
User Behavior Logging The module records children’s input, Agent responses,
state transitions, choice results, and retrieved information in each conversation.
The system generates an independent log file for each session, enabling subse-
quent analysis of how children accept, question, and revise AI suggestions.
3.4
Study Procedure
This study adopted a between-group experimental design and recruited a total
of 12 children aged 8–10 years. Participants were assigned to one of three Agent
conditions, with 4 children in each condition. The between-group design was
adopted to reduce the learning effect and direct comparison effect among children
across different Agent styles.
The experimental process consists of four stages. First, the researcher in-
troduces the task rules and interface operations to the children and confirms
their understanding of the basic operation methods. Second, the children en-
ter the Gamification task stage. This stage revolves around the same biological
knowledge point. First, through the knowledge judgment task, it examines the
children’s process of verifying and correcting partially correct AI suggestions,
and then through the contextualized application task, it further observes their
subsequent seeking and adoption of the same agent assistance. Third, after the
task is completed, the children complete a brief AI reliability and trust rating.
Fourth, the researcher conducts a semi-structured interview to ask the children
why they believe, question, or correct AI suggestions, as well as how they un-
derstand the role, reliability, and capability boundaries of AI.
Since the task includes intentionally designed partially correct AI suggestions,
after the experiment, researchers will explain to the children that AI may provide
incomplete or suggestions that need to be checked, and emphasize that it is
reasonable and important to review evidence and maintain one’s own judgment
when using AI.

4.1
Agent Style Shaped Engagement and Response Entry
Behavioral logs showed that Agent style influenced children’s engagement before
they made explicit trust decisions. Among the 11 valid sessions, 10 children
entered the reflection node, where the AI introduced an incorrect explanation
and asked children to decide whether to accept, seek evidence, or question the
suggestion. All children in the partner-like condition (A) and the dual-state
(AB) entered this node, while one child in the tool-like condition (B) stopped
during free chat. Since all conditions used the same task-triggering mechanism,
this suggests that Agent expression may affect children’s willingness to continue
interacting and reach the reflection process.
Children’s response tendencies differed across Agent conditions. In the partner-
like condition, all four children chose to question the AI, suggesting that socially
expressive interaction may encourage direct disagreement. In the tool-like con-
dition, responses were more distributed: two children chose to seek evidence, one
questioned the AI, and two did not complete a response choice. The dynamic-
switching condition showed a mixed pattern, with one child seeking evidence
and one accepting the AI suggestion. Both valid AB sessions started with a
companion-like entry and shifted toward a tool-like response during reflection or
repair, suggesting the feasibility of the routing mechanism, although the small
sample size does not support claims of a stable behavioral effect.
4.2
Children’s Verification Behaviors Varied in Pathways and
Reasoning Depth
Children’s responses to the AI suggestion were not limited to simple acceptance
or rejection. Across the 11 valid sessions, only one child accepted the AI sug-
gestion, while three chose to seek evidence and five questioned the AI. Nine
sessions reached the AI repair state, indicating that most children moved from
the initial AI suggestion into a process of verification or correction. These results
suggest that children’s trust in AI was expressed through situated behaviors such
as checking, questioning, and revision, rather than through a single decision to
trust or distrust the AI.

4.3
Children Viewed AI as Useful but Fallible
Post-task ratings showed that children generally viewed the AI as useful but falli-
ble. Across the three Agent conditions, AI trust and reliance remained at a mod-
erate level (M = 3.33), while error awareness was consistently high (M = 4.83),
suggesting that children did not treat the AI as an always-correct source. Chil-
dren also rated AI explanations as helpful (M = 4.50), indicating that expla-
nations were perceived as useful task support. However, their role perception
remained largely functional: tool perception (M = 4.67) was higher than part-
ner perception (M = 3.33). Although the dynamic-switching condition showed
relatively higher partner perception (M = 4.25), children in this condition still
rated the AI strongly as a tool (M = 5.00), suggesting that the AI was primar-
ily understood as a system for analyzing information and supporting decisions
rather than as a fully collaborative partner.
The platform usability ratings were generally positive, indicating that chil-
dren could understand and operate the game system. However, open-ended com-
ments pointed to several design issues, including visual clarity, card readability,

5
Discussion
The findings suggest that Agent style should be designed as task-based scaf-
folding rather than a fixed personality. The companion-like Agent supported
engagement and direct questioning, while the tool-like Agent was more related
to evidence-seeking behaviors. Therefore, children’s AI systems may benefit from
combining both styles: using social expression to sustain participation, and shift-
ing toward structured, evidence-based explanations when children need to eval-
uate or revise AI suggestions.
The findings also suggest that AI literacy should be cultivated through situ-
ated interaction, not only through abstract knowledge about AI. Children showed
different forms of critical AI use, including seeking evidence, questioning AI, and
correcting AI explanations, but their reasoning depth varied. Therefore, future
AI literacy designs should help children practice how to judge AI outputs, com-
pare them with evidence, explain their reasoning, and distinguish between their
own judgments and AI-provided information.
This study has several limitations. First, the evaluation was conducted with
a small number of participants, so the results should be interpreted as prelim-
inary trends rather than stable causal conclusions. Future work should involve
a larger and more balanced sample, with better control of children’s prior AI
experience and task ability. Second, the current dynamic trust model mainly
maps observable behaviors to trust states, but does not fully explain the un-
derlying reasons for children’s trust changes. Children’s trust may be influenced
by multiple factors, including task understanding, AI expression, cognitive load,
and perceived AI authority.
Future research can further examine whether children’s critical AI use can
transfer from structured game tasks to more open-ended scenarios, such as sci-
entific inquiry, creative generation, or learning assistance. In addition, future
systems could include more explicit reflection prompts that ask children to ex-
plain why they accept, question, or revise AI suggestions. This would help reveal
how children distinguish between their own judgments and AI-provided infor-
mation during multi-round human–AI collaboration.
