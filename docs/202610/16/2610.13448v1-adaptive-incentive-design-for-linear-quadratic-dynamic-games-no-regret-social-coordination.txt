# Adaptive Incentive Design for Linear-Quadratic Dynamic Games: No-Regret Social Coordination 

Lantian Zhang, Yibei Li, Georgios Vasileiou, Lihua Xie, Fellow, IEEE , and Silun Zhang 

Abstract — Incentives can align private interests with col-lective goals, but their design becomes intertwined with learning when agents’ preferences are unknown. This pa-per develops an adaptive incentive-design framework for multi-agent linear–quadratic dynamic games, where a sys-tem planner can simultaneously learn agents’ private pref-erences and steer their strategic behavior towards the so-cial optimum. To this end, we first characterize the complete family of affine incentives that implement the social opti-mum as the unique open-loop Nash equilibrium while en-suring exact budget balance. When agents’ preferences are unknown, we develop an online algorithm to estimate their preference parameters and establish strong consistency of the estimates under a weak trajectory-dependent excitation condition, without requiring persistent excitation or i.i.d. trajectories. We then integrate this estimator into an active-learning incentive mechanism that combines certainty-equivalent coordination with decaying random probing. We prove that the probing generates sufficient information through the agents’ equilibrium responses and the system dynamics, ensuring consistent preference learning from the resulting closed-loop trajectories. We show that the in-duced trajectories converge almost surely to their socially optimal counterparts, while cumulative social-cost regret relative to the full-information oracle and cumulative net-budget imbalance grow sublinearly. Simulations of a trans-boundary pollution game demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework. 

Index Terms — Incentive design, inverse learning, linear-quadratic dynamic games, stochastic observations, and regret analysis.

Cyber-physical systems increasingly comprise strategic de-cision makers, including humans and AI agents, whose actions are shaped by individual objectives and strategic responses to one another and their environment. Individually rational decisions may therefore conflict with collective goals, which makes system-level coordination a fundamental challenge.

This paradigm has shown its effectiveness in various domains, including smart grids, where pricing and rewards promote efficient energy consumption [2], [3]; transportation systems, where congestion tolls influence travelers’ route choices and improve traffic efficiency [4]; and environmental governance, where carbon taxes and emission pricing encourage firms to reduce emissions and promote sustainable development [5], [6]. A core obstacle in incentive design is the information asymmetry between the system planner and strategic agents, where agent preferences are rarely known in advance [7], [8]. This is because agents pursuing private interests are often unwilling to disclose their preferences and, in some settings, may themselves lack complete knowledge of their own objective functions. Adaptive incentive-design methods address such asymmetry by learning agents’ preferences from their responses while simultaneously adjusting the incentive policy [9]. Under the assumption that agents follow asymptot-ically convergent equilibrium-seeking dynamics, [10] develops a two-timescale adaptive algorithm that couples preference learning with incentive adaptation. By contrast, no-regret adaptive incentive design methods [11] integrate learning and incentive selection within a single-timescale loop. They achieve vanishing average regret to a foresighted oracle via an explicit exploration-exploitation trade-off. Other approaches address incomplete preference knowledge in incentive design through social-gradient methods [12], without explicitly esti-mating private costs or equilibrium sensitivities, and also under boundedly rational agent responses [13]. Adaptive incentive design becomes even more relevant and challenging in dynamic environments, where current actions alter a shared environment state and thereby shape agents’ fu-ture costs and strategic responses. The planner must therefore coordinate entire state–action trajectories, rather than isolated one-shot decisions, while balancing immediate behavioral ef-fects against their long-term consequences. Incentive problems in such games with dynamic players and environments occur in many applications, including, e.g., transboundary pollution games [5], building energy pricing [14], and transportation games [4], [15]. Linear–quadratic (LQ) dynamic games pro-vide a canonical setting for this problem, where linear dy-namics capture action propagation through time, and quadratic objectives encode heterogeneous valuations of system states and control effort while retaining a tractable Nash structure. arXiv:2610.13448v1 [eess.SY] 9 Oct 2026 2

This paper develops an adaptive incentive design framework for multi-agent LQ dynamic games that actively learns agents’ unknown preferences from noisy responses and simultaneously steers the induced Nash equilibrium toward the social opti-mum. The main contributions of the paper are threefold: 1) First, we formulate stochastic adaptive incentive de-sign for multi-agent LQ dynamic games and, under full information, characterize the complete family of affine incentives that implement the social optimum and promote budget balance (Prop. 1). 2) Second, we propose an online learning algorithm to estimate agents’ unknown preference parameters and, by martingale convergence theory, prove its strong con-sistency without requiring persistent excitation (PE) or i.i.d. trajectory data (Thm. 1). This relaxes standard identifiability conditions in cost learning (see, e.g., [16]– [18]), and enables preference learning and incentive design in a closed loop. 3) Third, we develop an adaptive incentive scheme based on certainty equivalence and decaying random probing. We establish that the probing propagates through agents’ equilibrium responses and the system dynamics to gen-erate sufficient excitation (Lem. 3, & Thm. 2), while the resulting open-loop equilibrium trajectories converge to the social optimum. The proposed scheme achieves vanishing average regret relative to the full-information oracle and vanishing average net budget (Thm. 3). 

A. Related Work 

Incentive Design and Stackelberg Games. Classical control-theoretic studies for incentive design established incentive controllability [19], [20], and affine incentive construction un-der different information structures [21]. Related work devel-oped dynamic and LQ leader–follower formulations [22]–[24], while functional incentive design in LQ systems has also been studied using bilinear incentives [25]. An interesting recent work [26] studies dynamic VCG mechanisms in unknown Markov decision processes. With known agents’ preferences, these results provide the structural foundation for incentive problems. For unknown follower utilities, Lauffer et al. [27] developed a no-regret learning algorithm for a finite-state Markov model with a single follower. Our focus is instead on continuous-state, multi-agent LQ dynamic games, where the planner adapts affine payments to asymptotically implement socially optimal state–action trajectories. 

Inverse Optimal Control and Inverse Games. Preference learning in dynamic decision-making process is closely related to inverse optimal control, initiated by Kalman [28], which infers cost parameters from observed optimal behavior. In LQ settings, existing work investigates cost reconstruction and identifiability [17], [29], [30], including partial and noisy observations [16], [31], [32]. Inverse-game methods extend this perspective to cost identification through observed Nash equilibrium behavior [33]–[36]. Beyond cost identification from given trajectories, this paper studies a learning-and-decision closed-loop setting in which each incentive depends on past estimates and shapes the trajectories used for subse-quent learning. We account for both the history dependence and regression bias induced by observation noise and establish strong consistency under a trajectory-dependent excitation condition. The proposed adaptive incentive scheme then gen-erates sufficient excitation to satisfy this condition. 

Game-Based Control Systems. Game-based control systems describe a regulator influencing a lower-level noncooperative dynamic game, with controllability and stabilizability studied in [37], [38]. More closely related, Liu and Guo [39] consid-ered adaptive stabilization under unknown system dynamics, with the regulator and agents learning the dynamics and adjusting their strategies online. Our setting differs in both the source of uncertainty and the regulation objective: the physical dynamics are known, whereas the agents’ cost parameters are private, and incentives are designed to align the induced Nash equilibrium with a prescribed social optimum. 

Adaptive Control. Our framework is structurally related to adaptive control, where recursive identification is coupled with online adaptation of the decision. Classical self-tuning regulation combines recursive identification with certainty-equivalent or diminishing-excitation control [40]–[43], with extensions to adaptive LQG [44], data-driven LQ design [45], and nonlinear systems [46], [47]. Unlike direct control of a system with unknown dynamics, however, the planner here chooses payments rather than the agents’ control actions. Probing must therefore propagate through the equilibrium response before it excites the cost regression. Our analysis quantifies this indirect excitation and its consequences for preference-learning accuracy and social-cost regret.

VIII. C ONCLUDING REMARKS 

Under the information asymmetry, we studied adaptive incentive design between a planner and L agents that interact through a dynamic game, in which the agents play an open-loop Nash equilibrium strategy. The agents’ costs depend on preference parameters over the system state and actions, which are unknown to the planner. We proposed an online learning algorithm to estimate agents’ unknown preferences and es-tablished its strong consistency under a non-PE condition on the observed trajectories. Building on this, we developed an adaptive incentive algorithm by using certainty-equivalent de-sign and decaying random probing, and proved that it achieves sublinear regret and net budget relative to the oracle policy. Future work includes extending the framework to closed-loop Nash equilibrium strategies, partial observations, and nonlinear dynamics with nonlinear incentive mechanisms.
