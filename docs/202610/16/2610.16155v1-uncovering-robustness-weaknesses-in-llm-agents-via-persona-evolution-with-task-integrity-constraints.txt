## ABSTRACT 

Interactive-agent evaluations commonly rely on standardized LLM-based user simulators that exhibit broadly cooperative behavior, potentially overlooking fail-ures that arise under diverse but realistic user interactions. This can lead to overly optimistic estimates of agent robustness and provide limited failure signals for training agents that generalize across users. Existing approaches largely rely on predefined user behaviors, which may miss agent- or domain-specific weaknesses, while failure-oriented search can reduce task success by invalidating the underly-ing task rather than exposing genuine robustness failures. To fill this gap, we intro-duce T IPER , a task-integrity-preserving persona evolution method that automati-cally discovers agent-specific user policies exposing robustness weaknesses while keeping the underlying task valid and solvable. T IPER evolves persistent user per-sonas through reflection-guided behavioral mutations and task-wise Pareto selec-tion under a task-integrity constraint. Across four interactive-agent benchmarks, TIPER yields lower held-out avg@3 scores in all 12 GPT-5.4 settings, with ob-served reductions of 5.8–33.7 percentage points, and in all 28 agent–domain pairs spanning seven agents, with a median observed reduction of 22.9 points. Further analysis yields a behavior-to-failure taxonomy that connects discovered user be-haviors to recurring agent failure modes. Together, these results show that T IPER 

enables more diagnostic robustness evaluation of interactive agents and provides informative failure analysis for future agent robustness improvement. Code repos-itory: https://github.com/UCSB-NLP-Chang/TIPER .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in reasoning and tool use have made LLM agents increasingly capable of com-pleting multi-turn collaborative tasks that require both user interaction and external actions (Yao et al., 2024; Barres et al., 2025; He et al., 2025). However, existing evaluations of collaborative agents often oversimplify user dynamics by relying on LLM-based user simulators that are primar-ily instructed to facilitate task completion. Real users, in contrast, vary substantially in how they communicate, provide information, respond to agent requests, and cooperate toward a shared goal. Without explicit behavioral specifications or training, LLM simulators tend to default to broadly helpful and cooperative interaction patterns, failing to capture this diversity (Naous et al., 2026). As a result, current evaluation protocols may systematically underrepresent challenging yet realistic interaction modes and consequently overestimate agent performance. The same bias can also affect training: agents optimized against a narrow set of simulator behaviors may overfit to these interac-tion patterns and generalize poorly to diverse users (Yu et al., 2026). These limitations motivate the systematic identification of user policies that reveal weaknesses in agent robustness, enabling more precise evaluation and providing informative interaction patterns for subsequent agent training. Recent work has begun to evaluate interactive agents with non-collaborative or behaviorally diverse users. NCUser (Shim et al., 2026) introduces predefined classes of non-collaborative behavior,

AgentNoiseBench (Wang et al., 2026) moves toward agent-targeted evaluation by searching for behaviors that degrade agent performance, but operates over message-level perturbations from a fixed taxonomy rather than persistent user policies that shape the interaction over multiple turns. Second, reduced task success does not nec-essarily indicate an agent weakness. Challenging user behaviors can instead make the underlying task infeasible, confounding agent robustness with task validity. This concern is nontrivial in exist-ing benchmarks: under the τ 2-Bench audit standard (Barres et al., 2025), our audit of 872 τ -Trait conversations finds task-critical user-simulator errors in 244 cases (28.0%) (Appendix D). We there-fore require discovered user policies to preserve the validity and feasibility of the underlying task throughout the realized interaction, a constraint we term task integrity . We introduce T IPER , a task-integrity-preserving persona evolution method for automatically iden-tifying user policies that expose weaknesses of a given agent. T IPER iteratively evolves persistent user personas by reflecting on observed interaction failures and proposing behavior-level mutations likely to reveal additional weaknesses. Task-wise Pareto selection preserves complementary failure patterns across tasks, while benchmark-specific validity controls and trajectory audits distinguish agent failures from failures caused by task invalidation. Across 12 settings spanning four interactive-agent benchmarks, we observe lower held-out GPT-5.4 task success under T IPER -discovered personas in every setting, with mean differences of 5.8–33.7 percentage points and a median difference of 16.1 points. In the primary GPT-5.4 evaluation, the pooled task-critical simulator-error rate among exposed failed rollouts is below 2.5% , indicating that the discovered failures reflect genuine robustness weaknesses that are missed under standard user simulation. The discovered personas also exhibit stronger behavioral realism than benchmark-default users under human-referenced User-Sim measurements, despite realism not being explicitly optimized during search. Beyond performance degradation, we analyze failures across eight do-mains to characterize how different user behaviors expose distinct agent failure modes, yielding a behavior-to-failure taxonomy for more diagnostic evaluation and future agent training. ## 3 TASK -I NTEGRITY -P RESERVING PERSONA EVOLUTION 

To approximate this constrained objective, we perform G generations of evolutionary search over the natural-language space P. We initialize A0 := {p0}. As illustrated in Figure 2, generation 

g ∈ { 0, . . . , G − 1} samples a parent persona ppar from the active set Ag , collects trajectories on a minibatch Bg , and reflects on them to propose a child persona. The child undergoes text-level checks and the search-time integrity gate before task-wise Pareto selection updates the active set. This section describes the serial search used for τ 3-bench, Lost-in-Conversation, ColBench, and VitaBench. More details of experiments are described in Appendix B. To separate candidate generation, model selection, and final evaluation, we partition the tasks into three disjoint subsets. The search set Xsearch is used to generate candidate personas and to perform the initial parent–child comparisons in each generation. The rank set Xrank serves as a validation set for the trajectory-level integrity gate, task-wise Pareto selection, and final persona selection. The test set Xtest is held out throughout search and selection and is used only for the final evaluation. Text-level checks. Before rollout, two filters screen each proposal. The first rejects personas that are unclear, instance-specific, or inconsistent with the assigned task. The second rejects near-duplicates using lexical and embedding similarity against the proposal history. Together, these filters prevent invalid or redundant candidates from consuming rollout budget and keep the search focused on transferable behavioral changes. Proposals that fail either check are revised within a retry loop of at most M attempts, whereas those that pass enter the search-time integrity gate. Both text-check feedback and gate-rejection reasons are supplied to the proposal model, allowing subsequent attempts to address the identified issue while preserving the intended mutation direction. 3.2 SEARCH -T IME INTEGRITY GATING

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate T IPER through four research questions: • (Q1) Failure-revealing persona evolution. Can T IPER discover substantially more failures across held-out test tasks than the default and alternative policies? • (Q2) Robustness across agents and components. How does failure exposure with T IPER vary across evaluated agents, mutation models, and simulator backbones? • (Q3) Integrity and behavioral alignment. How well do the generated trajectories preserve task integrity and align with human-user references? • (Q4) Behavior-to-failure characterization. How do task-preserving user behaviors vary across benchmarks, and what agent failure modes are associated with them? 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmarks and agents. We evaluate T IPER with GPT-5.4 (OpenAI, 2026a) on 12 settings drawn from four interactive-agent benchmarks: τ 3-bench (Barres et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2026), Lost-in-Conversation (Laban et al., 2026), ColBench (Zhou et al., 2025), and VitaBench (He et al., 2025). To study generalization across agents, we rerun the persona search for six additional propri-etary and open-weight agents: GLM-5.2 (Z.ai, 2026), Claude-Sonnet-5 (Anthropic, 2026), DS-V4-Flash (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026), MiniMax-M3 (MiniMax, 2026), Qwen3.7-Plus (Alibaba Cloud Community, 2026), and MUSE-Glimmer (Meta Superintelligence Labs, 2026). This cross-agent study covers four representative domains: Telecom, Math, Coding, and Delivery. Unless otherwise specified, we use GPT-5.4 as the simulator backbone and GPT-5.5 as the mutation model. GPT-5.2 and GLM-5.2 serve as the judges. More implementation details are provided in Appendix B. 

Baselines. We compare T IPER with the benchmark-default user simulator, the fixed user programs from NCUser (Shim et al., 2026), and an objective-matched variant of PPol (Chopra et al., 2026). The PPol variant uses the same agent-difficulty objective, task partitions, and search budget as TIPER . All methods are evaluated on the same test tasks and undergo the identical trajectory-level integrity audit described in Appendix E. 

Evaluation protocol and metrics. Each T IPER candidate that reaches rank-set evaluation is run twice per task. We select the persona with the lowest mean rank-set score and fix it before test evaluation. We then conduct three independent test rollouts per task. After integrity filtering, we report valid avg@3, defined as the mean task score over the valid rollouts among the three test trials. We also report pass k, defined among tasks with three valid rollouts as the fraction with at least k fully successful rollouts, where k ∈ { 1, 2, 3}. For ColBench, valid avg@3 is the mean fraction of unit tests passed, and a rollout is fully successful only if it passes all unit tests. We define failure exposure as the reduction in task score from the default condition to the persona condition after integrity filtering. In the following experiments, every test task has at least one integrity-valid rollout in each reported condition. Policy comparisons additionally report the failure-conditioned task-critical error rate under an adapted τ 2-Bench rubric (Barres et al., 2025). The User-Sim Index measures behavioral alignment between trajectories induced by T IPER and human-user reference trajectories (Zhou et al., 2026). Because integrity filtering can change the tasks eligible for pass k, we interpret these metrics descriptively. 4.2 MAIN RESULTS 

TIPER consistently exposes additional agent failures on held-out tasks. As shown in Table 1, the selected personas reduce both avg@3 and pass 3 in all 12 settings, with median reductions of 16.1 and 15.0 percentage points, respectively. The largest avg@3 reductions occur in Math, Telecom, and Delivery, at 33.7, 25.3, and 25.0 points, with corresponding pass 3 reductions of 54.1, 35.2, and 22.5 points. Because persona selection does not use the test tasks, these results indicate that the discovered behaviors transfer to unseen tasks within each domain. 

Cross-agent generalization. TIPER consistently exposes failures across different agent models. Across 28 combinations of seven agents and four domains in Table 2, avg@3 decreases in every setting and pass 3 decreases in 25, with median reductions of 22.9 and 20.4 percentage points, re-spectively. The effect is particularly consistent in Telecom, where avg@3 decreases by 13.0–39.5 points and pass 3 by 18.6–44.5 points across all seven agents. The magnitude varies more in Math, with avg@3 reductions ranging from 0.8 points for GLM-5.2 to 33.7 points for GPT-5.4. 

Baseline comparison. To assess the value of agent-conditioned persona search, we compare T IPER 

with fixed behavior programs and objective-matched PPol across four domains and two agents (Ta-ble 3). Across all eight agent–domain settings, T IPER produces the largest avg@3 reduction, ex-ceeding the strongest alternative by a median of 7.5 percentage points. Overall, T IPER exposes more agent failures than either fixed behaviors or objective-matched PPol. 4.3 INTEGRITY AND BEHAVIORAL ALIGNMENT 

Integrity. Integrity is assessed at the trajectory level, and trajectories judged invalid are excluded be-fore task scores are aggregated. Diagnostic setup. We construct 139 contrastive pairs across eight domains in two benchmarks for GPT-5.4. Each pair contains a successful default rollout and an integrity-valid failed T IPER rollout on the same task. Two judges independently assign multi-label user-behavior annotations, yielding 287 labels across the 139 trajectory pairs. The figures include only annotations on which both judges agree. Appendix F.4 provides the annotation protocol and filtering details. 

Benchmark-specific user behaviors. The right panels of Figure 3 summarize the relative frequen-cies of six displayed behavior categories, normalized within each benchmark. In τ 3-bench, sparse information, incremental requests, and single-step requests account for 28.1%, 21.9%, and 20.8% of the displayed annotations. VitaBench is more concentrated: incremental requests account for 54.9% of the behavior annotations, followed by bundled actions at 24.6%. This difference is consistent with their interaction structures. The τ 3-bench tasks are sensitive to how information is provided and maintained, whereas VitaBench more strongly tests whether an agent can accumulate require-ments and execute multi-part workflows. 

Behavior–failure pathways. Figure 4 reveals a many-to-many relationship between user behaviors and agent failures. ## 6 RELATED WORK 

Interactive evaluation and user simulation. Static benchmarks do not fully capture interactive systems: stronger non-interactive performance does not always translate to better human–LM inter-action, which also depends on the interaction process and user experience (Lee et al., 2024). Tool-agent benchmarks accordingly evaluate agents through multi-turn interactions with LLM-simulated users (Yao et al., 2024; Barres et al., 2025). However, such simulators can be overly cooperative and behaviorally uniform, misrepresenting performance with real users (Zhou et al., 2026; Seshadri et al., 2026). Unconstrained simulation also exhibits a formalism ceiling and amplifies hand-crafted directives (Zhu et al., 2026), making simulator validity essential for reliable evaluation. 

Persona-driven behavioral stress testing. Natural-language personas provide a flexible way to di-versify simulated user behavior and can induce trait-consistent interactions (Jiang et al., 2024). Prior work models non-collaborative users or conditions simulators on human traits (Shim et al., 2026; He et al., 2026), while long-horizon studies show that agent performance depends on how user intent unfolds over time (Laban et al., 2026; Tack et al., 2026). Persona-conditioned simulators can nev-ertheless exhibit persona drift, role confusion, and behavioral echoing (Luo & Laban, 2026). Most closely related, PPol evolves persona generators to improve human-likeness and behavioral coverage while preserving task goals (Chopra et al., 2026). In contrast, T IPER performs agent-conditioned search for failure-revealing personas and verifies task integrity on the resulting trajectories. 

Reflective prompt evolution and adversarial search. Prompt optimization treats natural-language instructions as discrete search objects. ProTeGi derives textual gradients (Pryzant et al., 2023), EvoPrompt uses LLM-guided evolution (Guo et al., 2024), and GEPA combines trajectory reflection with Pareto selection (Agrawal et al., 2026). Related red-teaming methods generate difficult test cases (Perez et al., 2022) or use quality-diversity search to uncover varied failures (Samvelyan et al., 2024). TIPER adapts these ideas to interactive user simulation by evolving persona-conditioned 9Under review policies, retaining complementary failures through task-wise Pareto selection, and filtering invalid candidates through text-level checks and trajectory-level integrity gating. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We introduced T IPER , which evolves instance-agnostic personas to expose failures overlooked by benchmark-default user simulation while preserving task integrity. Across the 12 GPT-5.4 settings and 28 agent–domain pairs, T IPER yields lower observed avg@3 scores compared to default user policy, with a 22.9-point median reduction across agent–domain pairs. Its observed reduction is largest among the compared policies in all eight direct comparisons, where task-critical simulator-error rates are at most 2.5%. Ablations support the integrity gate and text checks, while trajectory analysis links exposed failures to execution, grounding, and replanning errors. T IPER thus provides a practical framework for targeted, behaviorally grounded robustness evaluation.

Rollouts use a 7,200-second simulation timeout and at most 100 interaction steps. The main search driver is a serial one-parent-to-one-child loop: a parent is selected from the cur-rent Pareto active set using the implementation’s instance-win-frequency policy, a child is proposed from the parent’s minibatch trajectories, and the child is screened before full rank evaluation. The minibatch comparison uses a zero tie tolerance, so a child must strictly increase raw difficulty (equiv-alently, strictly lower the performance score used in the main text) on the minibatch. 

Search-time score and invalid-trajectory handling. The search score is raw difficulty: for each completed rollout that is neither an infrastructure error, timeout, nor max-step termination, it is 

1 − Vi(ξ). This score is not conditioned on the integrity judgment; in the main notation, it is equivalent to minimizing eJi. Infrastructure errors, timeouts, and max-step trajectories are excluded from the difficulty estimate; the latter two are recorded separately and constrained by the timeout-rate gate. Integrity is a separate hard gate rather than a term in the search reward. Whenever an evaluated trajectory is judged invalid, it is excluded from the reported task score after that judgment; its count is retained for the separate integrity statistic. Test reporting uses three trials per task, whereas search and rank evaluation use two. The search-time severe gate is failure-conditioned: only failed rollouts are judged for the severe-error rate, while the integrity analysis evaluates every rollout. 

Finite-sample metrics. For each task, we run three independent test trials. After the integrity check, invalid trials are removed before aggregation. The task score is the arithmetic mean of the re-maining valid trial scores. We compute pass k only on tasks with three valid trials: a task contributes 17 Under review to pass k if at least k of those three trials are full-task successes. Thus, pass 1, pass 2, and pass 3

correspond to at least one, two, and three successes out of three valid trials, respectively. Tasks with fewer than three valid trials are omitted from pass k, and invalid-trial counts are retained separately. At the end of search, we select the single active persona with the lowest rank-set avg@2. We fix this persona before test evaluation and report all held-out metrics from a fresh three-trial batch; test outcomes do not affect persona selection. B.2 BASELINES AND COMPONENT PROTOCOLS 

Alternative user policies. The benchmark-default condition uses no evolved persona. The re-ported NCUser comparison contains the four non-default fixed programs shown in Table 3; the no-persona condition is the default reference. These programs are controls rather than a search procedure. PPol retains its generator-based evolution but uses the same agent-difficulty objective as T IPER for the reported comparison. It receives the same search-rollout budget, uses a 100-step evolution horizon, and generates one persona for each held-out task from its final program. These protocols are not identical portfolio constructions, so the comparison is reported as a comparison of user-policy controls rather than as a matched algorithmic benchmark. 

Cross-agent evaluation. Persona evolution is rerun separately for each evaluated agent on Tele-com, Math, Coding, and Delivery, while GPT-5.4 remains the default user-simulator backbone and GPT-5.5 remains the mutation model. Apart from the evaluated agent’s model-specific reasoning setting, the search and evaluation settings match the main experiment. For each agent, one persona is selected on the rank set and evaluated with the same disjoint task protocol and three-trial test batch. The agents are GPT-5.4, GLM-5.2, Claude-Sonnet-5, DS-V4-Flash, MiniMax-M3, Qwen3.7-Plus, and MUSE-Glimmer. 

Component sensitivity. For mutation-model rows, persona evolution is rerun from the same task split and budget with GPT-5.4 as the user simulator. For user-simulator-backbone rows, the persona texts discovered with GPT-5.4 are held fixed and only the backbone that renders the dialogue is changed; evolution is not rerun. The mutation-model runs use the same rank-set selection and test reporting protocol. The user-simulator-backbone runs reuse the GPT-5.4 rank-selected persona and evaluate it directly on the test set. The search-component ablations remove either the trajectory-level integrity gate or the text-level persona check while holding models, task splits, and rollout budgets fixed. ## E INTEGRITY AND BEHAVIORAL EVALUATION 

E.1 INTEGRITY ANALYSIS PROTOCOL 

The search-time gate and analysis-time check serve different roles. The search-time validity filter examines failed rollouts and rejects a candidate when the failure-conditioned severe-error rate ex-ceeds 0.05. For the integrity analysis, every rollout is checked with the configured integrity judges; a rollout marked invalid is removed before computing the task score, while the number of invalid failed rollouts remains in the separate failure-conditioned integrity rate. The integrity audit uses the task-critical user-simulator-error rubric adapted from τ 2-Bench (Bar-res et al., 2025). Integrity labels follow the benchmark-specific audit prompts in Appendix B. A rollout is marked invalid if either judge flags a task-critical user-simulator error. The pooled rate counts violating rollouts rather than individual error labels, keeping the integrity measure tied to the benchmark’s standard user-simulator error definition. The search-time severe judge is applied only to failed rollouts (verifier score below one), whereas the analysis-time check is applied to all evaluated rollouts. This separation keeps the search gate focused on failures that could otherwise be attributed to the user while preserving a separate integrity statistic for analysis. The finite-sample gate is intentionally conservative: when few rollouts fail, a single integrity violation can reject a candidate rather than allow an uncertain failure to influence selection.

## F BEHAVIOR AND FAILURE TAXONOMIES 

Overview. Our diagnostic framework separates two questions: what task-preserving behavior did the simulated user exhibit? , what concrete error was observed in the trajectory? Accordingly, Fig-ure 4 connects user behavior to the canonical agent failure mode. The user-side taxonomy below contains 10 behaviors organized into four behavioral families. The agent-side taxonomy contains six canonical failure modes and a residual M0 category. Each trajec-tory receives one failure-mode label; the residual M0 option prevents cases from being forced into an unsupported canonical mechanism. F.1 USER BEHAVIOR TAXONOMY 

F.1.1 REQUEST STRUCTURE 

This family describes how the user packages or reveals parts of the assigned workflow. All requested actions and constraints remain within the original task. 

Incremental requests. The user reveals an existing subrequest or constraint only after the agent has begun or committed to an earlier part of the workflow. The behavior changes when task information is disclosed, but does not introduce a new objective. It tests whether the agent can reopen and revise a partially constructed plan rather than treating its initial scope as final. 

Bundled actions. The user combines multiple valid actions, approvals, contingencies, or depen-dent operations from the assigned workflow into a single request or confirmation. The agent must decompose the request, identify dependencies, and ensure that every requested operation is either completed or explicitly resolved. 

Late caveat. The user reveals an existing task-consistent condition or concern when the agent is about to confirm or execute an otherwise completed plan. The caveat does not alter the assigned goal or introduce a contradictory fact; it changes only the timing at which relevant information becomes available. The agent must suspend the pending action, revise the plan, and revalidate the affected constraints. F.1.2 PACING 

This family describes how the user controls the rate and granularity at which the agent is allowed to progress through the assigned workflow. The set of required actions is unchanged; only the permitted step size differs. 

Single-step requests. The user insists on advancing one small step at a time, declining bundled guidance and repeatedly requesting micro-confirmations. This constrains the agent to a reactive, locally scoped interaction pattern and tests whether it can integrate local progress into a complete diagnostic or resolution plan. 

Wants human. The user requests escalation to a human agent or specialist while retaining the assigned goal and remaining available to provide information or continue if escalation is unavailable. The behavior tests whether the agent handles an escalation request without prematurely abandoning the task. F.1.3 UNDER -S HARING 

This family describes how the user withholds or degrades the information the agent needs, without altering the underlying facts of the task. All withheld information is recoverable through the agent’s available tools or by asking. 

Sparse information. The user omits disambiguating facts, identifiers (account, order, itinerary), or device and state details that the original task supplies upfront. This forces the agent to infer, guess, or anchor on incomplete evidence rather than grounding its plan in confirmed information. 

Vague references. The user refers to task elements through ambiguous pronouns, abbreviations, indirect choices, or underspecified preferences instead of the explicit option identifiers or precise 35 Under review values available in the task. This invites the agent to over-interpret an ambiguous instruction as an explicit authorization. 

Incomplete verification. The user initially supplies only part of the verification information but provides the remaining details when the agent asks an appropriate follow-up. The behavior changes when verification becomes available; it does not require the user to refuse a task-critical request or make the task infeasible. 

Terse prompt. The user phrases requests more briefly and with less evaluative framing than the original task, reducing the conversational scaffolding available to the agent. The reduced scaffolding exposes weaknesses in retrieval, ranking, or policy application that a more elaborated prompt would mask. F.1.4 NON -C ORRECTION 

This family describes how the user responds to agent errors and partial progress. The user neither in-troduces false information nor changes the success criterion; the behavior consists only of declining to volunteer a correction. 

Uncorrected assumptions. The user lets an incorrect agent inference, partial match, or incom-plete piece of work stand without correcting it, rather than verifying the option explicitly or asking for supporting evidence. The behavior exploits the agent’s tendency to settle on the first plausible interpretation it produces. F.2 AGENT FAILURE MODE TAXONOMY 

The agent-side taxonomy describes the specific mechanism by which an agent’s decision deviates from what it would have done under a cooperative, non-adversarial persona, given that the devia-tion was avoidable and associated with the persona-induced decision shift in the paired trajectories. Failures that reflect a genuine absence of information, a deliberate task-breaking user action, or an evaluation artifact rather than an agent decision error are excluded from this taxonomy. The result-ing closed set contains six canonical modes, together with a residual category for failures that do not fit any of them. 

M1 – Trusting unverified user reports. The user describes a device or system state imprecisely, approximately, or with ambiguous terminology, and the agent accepts this description at face value instead of checking it against an available diagnostic tool. The agent then proceeds to troubleshoot, or closes the task, based on the unverified state rather than the true one. 

M2 – Premature partial-match commit. Multiple items or issues belong to the same order or request, and the user confirms only one of them. The agent executes an irreversible or one-shot action on the confirmed item without first checking whether the remaining items in the same request still need to be addressed, leaving them unresolved. The user remains available to confirm the remaining items when asked, so the behavior does not itself remove the information needed to finish the task. 

M3 – Spurious constraint inference. The user discloses multiple options or issues piecemeal, or phrases a request in a way that does not exactly match policy wording. The agent infers, without grounding, that the requested combination is mutually exclusive or otherwise disallowed, and rejects a request that was in fact feasible. 

M4 – Over-abandoning conditional requests. The user gives a conditional instruction (e.g., pro-ceed only if a condition holds), and that condition can in fact be verified using an available tool or already-known information. Instead of verifying the condition and acting on the result, the agent treats the unresolved condition as a reason to abandon the request entirely. 

M5 – Assuming tool unavailable. The agent incorrectly believes that it lacks a tool or capability needed to complete a subtask, when that tool is in fact available, and abandons the subtask or asks the user for clarification instead of attempting to use it. 

M6 – Incomplete option enumeration. The agent commits to the first feasible option, or to an action on a still-ambiguous record, without first enumerating the full set of feasible alternatives or 36 Under review resolving the ambiguity. This can result in a worse option being selected, or the wrong record being acted on, even though a better or correct option existed and was reachable. 

M0 – Others. The failure is an avoidable agent decision in the paired contrast, but its mechanism does not match any of M1–M6. This category avoids forcing cases into the closed set and is retained as an explicit option during annotation. F.3 PER -T RAJECTORY ANALYSIS 

Unit of analysis. We analyze paired trajectories from the same task. Each pair contains a success-ful run under the benchmark-default simulator and a failed run under a T IPER persona that passes the integrity check. The task, tools, and evaluated agent are fixed across the pair; only the simulated user behavior changes. This contrast helps separate failures exposed by the persona from tasks that the agent already fails under the default simulator. For each pair, we analyze the pathway persona → user behavior → agent decision shift → failure .

We annotate the user behavior and agent decision shift separately. We also record a decisive moment ,which identifies where the agent’s behavior diverges, and a paired default reference , which records what the agent did at the corresponding point in the successful default run. These fields ground the annotations in the paired trajectories. Behavior labels are assigned from the T IPER trajectory without using the failure-mode label. Failure modes are assigned from the paired trajectories without using the behavior label. This separation avoids defining the two annotations in terms of each other. F.4 ANNOTATION PROTOCOL 

Two judges annotate each item independently. They assign user-behavior labels from the realized TIPER trajectory, then assign a cause category and canonical agent failure mode from the paired default–T IPER trajectories. The fixed codebooks are shown to both judges, but neither judge sees the other’s response. Behavior and failure-mode labels are assigned in separate passes. Only labels retained by both judges are displayed in the analysis figures. For the single-label cause and failure-mode analyses, a label is retained only when the judges agree; disagreements are excluded rather than adjudicated. F.5 ANALYSIS -S ET CONSTRUCTION 

The diagnostic set is formed after the held-out evaluation. We first retain tasks for which the benchmark-default condition succeeds and a returned T IPER persona produces a failure after in-valid trajectories have been removed. All retained behavior-analysis pairs pass the task-integrity check. Invalid trajectories are excluded before behavior annotation and therefore do not appear as behavior cases. We then retain only pairs with a clearly identifiable user behavior, agent decision shift, and observed failure; infrastructure failures and unresolved single-label cause or failure-mode disagreements are excluded. The reported 139-pair count is therefore a diagnostic sample, not an estimate of the frequency of any behavior in the benchmark. F.6 CONSTRUCTING THE ANALYSIS FIGURES 

The behavior composition plot reports the six task-preserving behavior types shown in the main text: sparse information, incremental requests, single-step requests, uncorrected assumptions, vague references, and bundled actions. The four other valid but less frequent taxonomy items—late caveat, incomplete verification, wants human, and terse prompt—remain in the codebook but are not plotted. Task-breaking and out-of-codebook raw labels are discarded before aggregation. Percentages are renormalized over the mutually retained annotations in these six displayed categories within each benchmark; they are therefore not shares of all 287 annotations. A trajectory may contribute more than one displayed behavior. The Sankey plot uses mutually retained behavior and failure-mode labels from the cases and connects domain, behavior, and canonical agent failure mode. Because 37 Under review behavior coding is multi-label, one trajectory may contribute multiple ribbons; ribbon width denotes annotation count rather than the number of distinct trajectories. 

## G ADDITIONAL DISCUSSION 

G.1 RELATION TO ITERATIVE RED -T EAMING AND PERSONA ENVIRONMENTS 

PAIR (Chao et al., 2024) and TAP (Mehrotra et al., 2024) iteratively refine semantic jailbreak prompts against a target language model. They do not directly instantiate a persistent user pol-icy for a fixed tool-mediated task or enforce trajectory-level task integrity. Persona-conditioned environments such as SOTOPIA (Zhou et al., 2024) and PsySafe (Zhang et al., 2024) instead study social intelligence and multi-agent safety. T IPER complements these directions by searching task-preserving simulator policies against a fixed evaluated agent. G.2 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

TIPER searches fixed conversation-level personas rather than adversaries that adapt their strategy turn by turn. Its model-based judges provide empirical integrity evidence rather than formal certi-fication, and the ColBench adapter’s workflow stages introduce benchmark-specific human priors. Evolutionary search also requires more model calls than fixed simulation, while strict minibatch improvement can limit further accepted mutations once a parent reaches the score floor. Our independent-judge protocol does not measure the false-negative rate of the integrity check against human labels. Moreover, the failure-conditioned gate can have high sampling variance when a candidate produces few failed rollouts. The Airline experiment additionally measures transfer across benchmark releases, so its search, rank, and test distributions are not identical. Finally, ex-cluding disagreements from the diagnostic taxonomy may favor easier-to-classify failures; its path-way frequencies should therefore be read as descriptive of the agreement-filtered set. Task-wise Pareto selection can also yield many nondominated candidates as the rank-set dimension grows. In that case, KeepK caps the frontier using mean rank-set score; the Pareto step is designed to preserve complementary task profiles before truncation rather than guarantee diversity in high dimensions. Future work can develop adaptive user policies, tie-aware search gates, and targeted agent training with the discovered personas. 38 Under review
