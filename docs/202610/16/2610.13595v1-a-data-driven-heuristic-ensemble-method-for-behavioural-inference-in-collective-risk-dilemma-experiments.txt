Abstract 

The collective-risk dilemma has become a paradigm for studying cooperation in a wide variety of social experiments, initiatives that generate increasingly rich datasets describing individual actions over successive decision-making processes. Beyond the purely descriptive analysis of such empirical datasets, a methodological framework is required to interpret and reproduce the ob-served dynamics, a scheme that may be skewed by exogenous biases. Here, we introduce a data-driven, non-parametric method for simulating individual decisions together with a validation protocol for assessing the fidelity level of the outcomes they generate. Our methodological contribution is based on an empirical heuristic ensemble: agents are represented by a finite set of be-havioural heuristics, probabilities conditioned on different observable features of the game state, endowed with a hard-voting arbitration that determines their per-round final decisions. Background 

Collective-risk dilemmas (CRDs) have become a central experimental framework for studying cooperation in situations where individuals must contribute private resources toward a collective target while facing the pos-sibility of a shared loss if the target is not achieved [1]. Originally developed to investigate climate-change mitigation, CRDs capture the tension between individual incentives to free-ride and the collective need for coordinated ac-tion. The seminal experiment by Milinski et al. demonstrated that groups can coordinate contributions when collective failure entails substantial risk [1]. Subsequent studies showed that cooperative outcomes depend strongly on contextual factors such as inequality in resource distributions [2–4], uncer-tainty regarding collective risks [5], and intergenerational considerations [6]. Together, these experiments generated increasingly rich datasets describing individual contribution dynamics over repeated interactions. In parallel, CRDs have inspired a substantial body of theoretical and computational research aimed at understanding the mechanisms that pro-duce cooperative outcomes. The risk of collective failure fundamentally al-ters strategic incentives and can promote cooperation beyond what is ex-pected in standard public-goods settings [7]. Subsequent work extended this perspective through evolutionary and agent-based approaches, highlighting the importance of adaptation, social influence, and population heterogeneity 2in shaping collective outcomes [8]. Studies incorporating inequality further demonstrated how differences in available resources affect both individual behaviour and collective success [9]. More recently, experimental studies have provided additional evidence that behavioural heterogeneity plays a key role in collective-risk settings. Resource heterogeneity can generate highly unequal effort distributions even when groups achieve collective goals [3]. Likewise, long-term collective-risk experiments demonstrated that repeated exposure to collective risks can modify social norms and promote sustained cooperation [10]. These findings suggest that aggregate outcomes such as total contributions, target achieve-ment, or payoff distributions provide only a partial picture of collective ac-tion, as similar outcomes may emerge from distinct behavioural pathways. The growing availability of high-resolution experimental data creates new opportunities to investigate these mechanisms directly. CRD experiments routinely record individual decisions across multiple rounds, making it pos-sible to analyse how participants respond to their own previous actions, the behaviour of others, collective progress toward a target, and changes in their available resources. This is the case of the xAire citizen science experiment, conducted as a pop-up intervention in two public spaces in Barcelona us-ing portable digital interfaces [11, 12]. The experiment generated detailed state–action records from CRD games under different resource distributions and inequality treatments, providing a rich empirical basis for behavioural inference [13]. Experimental and theoretical evidence in different experi-ments thus suggests that individuals may simultaneously react to several feedback channels, exhibiting persistence in their own behaviour, conditional responses to others, sensitivity to collective progress, and adaptation to re-source availability [2, 7, 9]. Consequently, observed decisions may result from the interaction of multiple behavioural heuristics rather than from a single dominant strategy. The methodology presented here [14] addresses this challenge by adopting an inference-oriented perspective in which behaviour is characterised through probabilistic relationships between observable states and subsequent actions. Rather than assigning participants to predefined behavioural classes or spec-ifying strategy distributions a priori , the method estimates empirical condi-tional action probabilities directly from experimental observations. All be-havioural parameters are inferred from observed state–action records, elim-inating the need for external calibration data or pre-specified behavioural models. The underlying premise is that recurring behavioural patterns can 3be represented as state-conditioned decision rules linking actions to feedback available during the interaction, including individual history, social informa-tion, collective progress, and resource availability. In addition to identifying behavioural traits directly from empirical evidence, our methodological ap-proach enables us to generate large sets of synthetic data, allowing for the minute exploration of tested scenarios or the consideration of experimental configurations beyond those that have actually been implemented. As a worked example, we apply the methodology to the xAire CRD dataset [13]. The dataset contains sequences of contribution decisions to-gether with information on players’ previous actions, collective progress, and resource availability, making it well suited for identifying behavioural regu-larities and evaluating alternative behavioural hypotheses (heuristics in the forthcoming sections). Although illustrated here using xAire and CRD ex-periments, the same perspective can be applied to other behavioural exper-iments involving (unique or successive) decisions and observable contextual variables. By identifying empirical probabilistic links between states and actions, the methodology provides a transparent and reproducible approach to characterising behavioural mechanisms, comparing behavioural heuristics, and building generative models grounded in observations. 

Method details 

The method generates synthetic populations of agents that play a CRD and reproduces a set of emergent macro-indicators of an empirical system. It follows a four step workflow: (i) the identification of a set of behavioural heuristics, {H0, . . . , H m}, each defined as a contribution probability condi-tioned on one observable feature of the game state; (ii) the estimation of those conditional probability distributions directly from experimental data; (iii) a simulation pipeline in which agents sample contributions from the es-timated distributions, either under a single-heuristic or under an ensemble that combines all heuristics through a plurality vote; and (iv) a multi-pattern validation protocol comparing synthetic and empirical macro-indicators. Em-pirical sampling and hard voting are both standard in the literature; what we add in this method is the way the behavioural heuristics are put together to decide one action from several competing heuristics. Simulation pipeline 

Under a single-heuristic simulation every agent follows one heuristic for all rounds; under the ensemble every agent evaluates all heuristics each round and arbitrates. The procedure is given in Algorithm 1, which is written for an arbitrary number of games and treatments; the two concrete run sizes used in this worked example are stated here first, since both are referred to throughout the rest of the paper. The matched run contains the same number of games and treatment configurations as the original dataset, 77 games split across the three treatments, and is the basis for the scores in Table 2. Alarger run of 30 000 games, 10 000 per treatment, the extensive run , is used to characterise the method itself (Fig. 5) and to draw the expected ensemble trajectories in both panels of Fig. 4, since a run of only 77 games is too noisy to draw them (the Equal treatment has just six games); the empirical curves in that figure use all 77 games, and the scores of Table 2 come from the 

matched run only.

It is a non-parametric scheme: behavioural dominance emerges from the decision process itself, with no pre-defined strategy proportions in the population 10 or additional assumptions. Each component corresponds to a distinct pro-jection of the game state, own past action, peers’ past actions, collective progress, and own resource state, which in the worked example are exactly the four feedback channels the experimental interface displayed after each round. Figure 2 works through one round of this rule, including a two-two tie resolved by the random draw. We use a plurality vote for two reasons. It adds no parameters, and it keeps the rule easy to read: the executed action is just the one that most heuristics point to. Other choices exist. Fitting a single model jointly on all four state variables is the most direct alternative, and weighted or soft-max votes are others; we did not test these here. It is also worth saying where the method sits relative to familiar ones. Unlike learning models such as experience-weighted attraction [17] or reinforcement learning [18], the heuristics do not update inside a game; each is a fixed conditional dis-tribution read off the data. And unlike the Strategy Frequency Estimation Method [19, 20], which infers how common each strategy is in a population, we fix no proportions in advance and let the vote settle each round.

As we have stressed above, in the matched run we simulate the same number of games and treatment configurations as the target dataset and compare synthetic and empirical macro-indicators chosen to span the individual/collective and static/dynamic dimensions, so that no indicator trivially encodes the logic of any heuristic. This matches the aggregation used in the source study, so the two sets of numbers can be read side by side. To make these numbers interpretable, each metric is reported not only for the ensemble but also for (i) a uniform-random null in which agents draw actions uniformly from {0, 2, 4} MU allowed by their remaining endowment and (ii) the four single-heuristic models, so that the ensemble’s fidelity is read relative to chance and to its own components. Because one 77-game random draw is noisy, the null is the mean of independent replicates of the matched run. Table 2 and the overlays in Fig. 4 report the validation against the worked-example dataset. One number in Table 2 needs a caveat. The com-mon fund is reproduced almost exactly, at nMSE 0.010 for the ensemble, but that closeness is partly built in. The heuristics are seeded on empirical contri-bution probabilities, so the total contributed over ten rounds is already near the data before any arbitration happens, and matching the integrated fund is therefore close to guaranteed. The demanding tests are the round-resolved and distributional patterns. There the ensemble does well but not perfectly: the average-contribution nMSE is 0.283 , and Fig. 4(b) shows the clearest gap, the late-round fall in contributions, which the ensemble smooths over rather than reproduces. The ensemble is closer to the data than the mean of the random null on all eight indicators, by 1.6 to 2.2 standard deviations of the null on the payoff and inequality indicators and by 4.2 to 7.5 on the dynamic ones. This is not true of every single heuristic: on average Hpeer and Hbudget 

are no closer than chance on the two inequality indicators, so neither carries, 13 on its own, information about how final wealth is dispersed. The scalar Gini difference is a coarse number that can be small by coincidence, which is why we also report the Lorenz-area metric, which compares the whole curve; the two rank the five models in the same order. Two further properties of the worked-example instance are given in Fig. 5, generated from the extensive run : how often the plurality vote is decisive for this set of four heuristics, and how the similarity scores settle as the number of simulated games grows. As shown in Fig. 5(a), strong majorities, where three or four heuristics coincide, are only about 37% of decisions ( 5.1% unanimous and 32 .0% three-to-one). A further 40 .7% are three-way splits decided by a single vote, and the remaining 22 .2% are two-two ties settled by the random draw. The vote is therefore decisive most of the time, but a large share of decisions is only weakly determined by the heuristics. Figure 5(b) shows the two common-fund scores, DTW and nMSE, computed here per treatment and averaged over the three treatments, and therefore on a different scale from the pooled values of Table 2. Both settle once a few hundred games per treatment are simulated. At the sizes of the matched run, 25 to 50 games per treatment in the figure, they still fluctuate by about 13 to 34% (one standard deviation) around their converged value, so differences between models in Table 2 that are smaller than that should not be over-read. Together with the validation scorecard of Table 2, these results characterise how the method behaves and how much sampling uncertainty its scores carry. 

Limitations 

The conditional distributions are estimated from a single dataset; ap-plying the framework to a new dataset requires re-estimating them, and the worked-example validation does not by itself establish transfer to other populations or game structures. The four heuristics are one instantiation, chosen for the observable state of the worked-example game; other datasets may require a different set of heuristics, which the framework admits by construction. The discretization of conditioning variables introduces a bin-width choice that affects the estimated probabilities. Plurality voting is one of several possible arbitration rules; weighted, softmax, or priority-ordered are possible alternatives. The round-by-round fit is the weaker side of the ensemble. Because each heuristic reads only the previous round, the ensem-ble cannot generate a coordinated end-game drop in contributions, which is what the data show in the final rounds. Data and code availability 

The source code implementing the method is available at Project-xAire-EHE (GitHub repository). Starting only from the deposited data, it regen-erates Tables 1 and 2 and the data-driven Figs. 3, 4 and 5, including the synthetic runs of the four heuristics and of the ensemble, and it checks the outcome against stored reference values. The repository also holds the fig-ures of the article. Without the 30 000-game extensive run the pipeline takes about two minutes; with it, about two hours on one core. The empirical data are not duplicated in the repository. The worked-example dataset is available in the companion data article [13] and in the CORA repository [25].
