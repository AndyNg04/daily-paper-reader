ABSTRACT 

When humans collaborate with AI agents on long-horizon tasks, what happens to the agents’ contributions? For solving long-horizon tasks, humans place greater importance on ongoing interaction with AI agents ( e.g., revising plans) than on an agent’s ability to complete the task in a single attempt. However, most agen-tic benchmarks have focused more on automation. Strong performance on such benchmarks does not imply effective real-world collaboration. Professional work-flows offer a useful setting, as they often involve interdependent steps. To address this gap, we introduce K OLAB , a new dataset of 115+ hours of screen recordings of 50 professionals from 40 occupations collaborating with agents on realistic, long-horizon problems. K OLAB also contains participants’ collaboration and out-put quality judgments. Our analysis reveals diverse collaboration patterns, high-lighting the importance of when and how professionals coordinate with agents. A collaborative agent must do two things that automation benchmarks never test: anticipate whether experts will adopt, modify, or reject its contribution once it leaves the chat window to guide its next steps, and assess collaboration qual-ity from the unfolding workflow without constant expert feedback. Our bench-mark operationalizes both: (1) forecasting how professionals use agent contri-butions at diverse workflows, and (2) verifying their judgments of collaboration satisfaction and output quality. Findings show that frontier models, both agen-tic and non-agentic, overestimate collaboration with experts and struggle to dis-tinguish between high- and low-quality collaboration. Longer workflow context does not consistently improve these predictions. As frontier agents cannot yet un-derstand long-horizon collaboration with experts, we position K OLAB as a valu-able dataset for studying human–agent collaboration grounded in real professional workflows. 1

1 INTRODUCTION 

As AI agents become more capable of complex reasoning and knowledge work, recent research has examined whether they can complete such tasks autonomously in a single attempt, given a single instruction (Patwardhan et al., 2026; Vidgen et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026b; Merrill et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2024). However, real-world problem solving may not follow this autonomous setting: human goals can evolve, earlier decisions shape subsequent work, and errors may require intervention before they propagate (Tack et al., 2026). In professional work, for example, workers formulate requirements, produce intermediate artifacts, revise decisions, and move between agents and domain-specific tools. Thus, strong autonomous task performance may not translate into effective human-agent collaboration (Zou et al., 2025; Mozannar et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2026). Despite the need for studying effective human-agent collaboration, existing datasets provide limited grounding in real collaborative workflows. They are often not publicly released, rely on predefined tasks constructed or sourced by researchers, or focus on a single professional domain such as software development (Wang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026; Shao et al., 2026b). As a result, benchmarking long-horizon human-agent collaboration workflows remains underexplored.

To address this gap, we introduce K OLAB , a dataset of screen recordings capturing human-agent collaboration on long-horizon professional problems. We represent each workflow as a graph of semantically meaningful human and agent actions and their dependencies (Figure 1), allowing us to understand when and how collaboration occurs in the workflow. In this example, an architect completes a long-horizon task in five steps, and agent outputs can influence later steps. A task delegated in Step 2 (source node) produces information (target AI node) that is later modified and used in Step 3 (M ODIFY ). Human responses to agent outputs may also occur outside the chat interface. The architect ultimately adopts and executes the resulting plan in Revit, an action not directly observable by the agent. Our analysis reveals that human-agent collaboration workflow varies in when and how professionals engage with AI agents. Burstier workflows are associated with longer periods of independent work before interacting with AI, suggesting that professionals may organize their collaboration around periods of independent work rather than continuous AI interaction. Workflows with more concurrent human-agent activities are associated with lower collaboration satisfaction, suggesting that parallel activities may introduce additional coordination costs. Beyond characterizing collaboration, we ask whether frontier multimodal large language models (MLLMs) and agents can understand their own place in a professional’s workflow. We frame this as two capabilities that a deployed collaborative agent needs but that autonomous benchmarks never measure. 

Anticipating the fate of a contribution (RQ1). In long-horizon work, an agent’s output is rarely the end of the story: it is adopted, modified, or rejected, often outside the chat interface ( e.g., an architect incorporating an agent’s plan in Revit). An agent that can recognize what happened to its output after the fact (1a) could turn naturalistic screen recordings into feedback signals for training and for auditing deployed agents, without asking the expert to label anything. An agent that can forecast this response at delivery time (1b)—before the human reacts—could act proactively: asking a clarifying question when rejection is likely, flagging the parts it expects to be edited, or escalating to the human when its contribution carries risk that would propagate to later steps (Oh et al., 2026). Because such forecasts depend on how much of the collaboration the agent remembers, we vary the available history from the preceding steps (1c) to the entire workflow so far (1d), which directly informs how much context a long-running or computer-use agent must retain to model its collaborator. 

Judging the collaboration itself (RQ2). Expert ratings of collaboration satisfaction (2a) and output quality (2b) are the ground truth for whether human-agent collaboration works, yet they are expen-sive to collect and unavailable at deployment. Crucially, such a veri-fier must separate genuinely successful interactions from dissatisfying ones, rather than echoing the predominantly positive ratings. We observe that frontier multimodal agents still struggle to anticipate how professionals use agent outputs and to recover their judgments of collaboration quality. For example, Gemini 3.1 Pro and GPT-6 achieve only 39.1% and 51.3% F1 in forecasting adoption behavior, while their predictions of collaboration satisfaction correlate weakly with professional ratings (Spearman’s ρ = .08 and 

.38 ). Providing longer workflow context or additional modalities does not consistently improve these predictions. Further analysis reveals systematic biases: models often favor certain adoption labels and overpredict high ratings, limiting their ability to distinguish successful from poor collabo-ration. These findings suggest that current models may rely on their own prediction tendencies rather than reliably understanding professionals’ behaviors and judgments from the workflow. These re-sults highlight an important challenge for developing collaborative agents that can anticipate human decisions and adapt to how their contributions are actually used. Our main contributions are as follows. • We present K OLAB , a new dataset of 115 hours of screen-recorded human-agent workflows from 50 professionals solving their own real-world problems, comprising 508 steps across 90+ softwares. Each workflow includes a final deliverable with step-level human ratings of collaboration experience and output quality. • We analyze human-agent collaboration dynamics using three metrics: collaboration burstiness, workflow topology, and AI retention rate. • We benchmark frontier MLLMs on their ability to understand human-agent collaborative work-flows when solving professional problems. • We uncover systematic MLLM failure modes, revealing prediction biases that vary by model and input modality. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Long-horizon agent and workflow benchmarks. Autonomous agent benchmarks cover desktop applications (Xie et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2026), enterprise software (Dai et al., 2026), hybrid GUI/-CLI and scientific computer use (Li et al., 2026; Jang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026a), professional deliverables (Patwardhan et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026b; Xu et al., 2026), and long-running agent behavior (Maldaner et al., 2026). Some include human base-lines but do not release human action trajectories. Workflow-GYM (Zhu et al., 2026) collects expert workflow trajectories but uses them as demonstrations to evaluate autonomous agents. Human-only process datasets such as ScholaWrite (Le et al., 2026) and LawFlow (Das et al., 2025) capture expert work in a single domain (scholarly writing and legal reasoning) without agent collaboration. Among the benchmarks in Table 1, K OLAB uniquely combines multimodal trajectories of professionals col-laborating with agents on self-defined problems with step-level human ratings. 3Human-agent collaborative workflows. Prior work argues that autonomous task performance does not ensure effective collaboration (Shen et al., 2026), and that the role humans play relative to an AI system shapes the interaction (Wan et al., 2022); indeed, varying LLM access measurably changes how people work (Christenson et al., 2026). Existing evaluations often rely on simulated users (Wu et al., 2026; Yao et al., 2024; Vijayvargiya et al., 2026; Qian et al., 2025) or human intervention in short shopping and web browsing tasks (Huq et al., 2025; Mozannar et al., 2025). Collaborative Gym (Shao et al., 2026a) supports real or simulated users but covers only three prede-fined tasks. CollabSkill (Shao et al., 2026b) assigns professionals problems from existing datasets; Chen et al. (2026) study interaction-level satisfaction with software-engineering agents; and Wang et al. (2025) compare separate human and agent workflows. Our study setup is most similar to that of Hayati et al. (2026) who investigate human-agent collaboration in interdisciplinary research using only chat logs. Meanwhile, our work also captures the complete workflow, including how professionals act on agent contributions in their work that may happen outside the chat interface.

KOLAB is built to understand human-agent collaborative workflows when humans solve long-horizon problems. 

Problem criteria. To ensure that the collected workflows represent substantive professional prob-lem solving rather than generic computer-use activities, we required each problem to satisfy four criteria: • Real-world. The problem must originate from the professional’s actual work. Professionals were not given tasks from existing workflow datasets or scenarios. • Domain-specific. The problem must require the application of professional knowledge, skills, or judgment from the participant’s field. Tasks that could be completed without domain exper-tise, such as routine personal activities or generic information seeking, were excluded. • Complex and long-horizon. The problem must require a sequence of interdependent steps to achieve its final goal. Participants were asked to plan a workflow requiring at least one hour of work and consisting of at least five steps, with each step involving multiple computer actions or decisions. • Deliverable-oriented. The problem must result in a concrete final deliverable. Each step must also produce an intermediate output that can be evaluated. Arts & Design Arch. & Eng. Data release. We publicly release K OLAB with seven components: (1) screen-recording videos for all sessions; (2) the workflow documents written by the professionals, including goal, context, and step names/actions; (3) the human–agent chat logs, including agent tool calls; (4) optional input and output files of each workflow; (5) human ratings for collaboration experience and output quality, along with step-level output rubrics written by professionals; (6) the workflow graph per session, with step alignment, timestamps, and adoption labels; and (7) post-session survey responses. 

4 UNDERSTANDING HUMAN -A GENT COLLABORATION DYNAMICS 

4.1 DATA PROCESSING AND ANNOTATION 

To make the workflow structured, each step in the workflow is represented as a temporal graph, where each node v ∈ V corresponds to a human or agent action at a time interval τ (v) =[ts(v), t e(v)] ⊂ R≥0. The research team and professionals refined these graphs together. An exam-ple graph for one step is shown in Figure 12 in the Appendix. A node can branch when activities occur at overlapping timestamps and loops happen when the same action is repeated. Formally, each workflow is represented as follows: 

G = ( V, E, a, w, τ ), (1) where V is a set of human ( H) or AI ( A) action nodes; E ⊆ V × V is the set of directed edges, with 

(u, v ) ∈ E indicating that action v directly follows from action u.After graph construction, two human annotators labeled how humans incorporated agent outputs. A human node is identified as a source node if its action triggered the agent’s generation ( e.g., “design a floor plan ”). An agent node is the target AI node if it generates an output that is subsequently acted on by the participant. The human node that acts on this AI output is labeled as follows. • ADOPT : The agent output is observably incorporated into the workflow as-is, such as directly using an agent-generated design or adding a suggested paper to a literature review. • MODIFY : The agent output is incorporated with modifications, e.g., the human revises agent-generated text before using it. • REJECT : The agent output is not incorporated after being reviewed, such as when the human discards it or clearly expresses a negative sentiment and proceeds with a different approach. Hu-mans may work with an agent steadily or in short, intensive episodes separated by longer periods of independent work. Using these three metrics, we analyze how human-agent collaboration varies in its temporal rhythm (B), topology of concurrent work ( W ), and integration of agent contributions ( R). Our analyses are intended to describe the collaborative patterns observed in K OLAB , rather than as population-level comparisons across professional domains. Figure 3 shows the results. 

#1: Burstier workflows reflect more planning before interacting with the agent. Collaboration burstiness was relatively consistent within individuals across two sessions (Spearman ρ = 0 .59 ). We define solo lead-in as the proportion of a step completed before the first AI interaction, averaged over all steps in a session. This reflects the proportion of time a person spends working independently before first interacting with AI. For example, if the first AI interaction occurs 4 minutes into a 10-minute step, the solo lead-in is 40%. As shown in Figure 3a, burstier workflows had longer solo lead-ins ( ρ = 0 .35 , p < 0.001 ). Consistent with this pattern, participants who reported having a clearer plan before using AI in the post-interaction survey also had longer solo lead-ins ( ρ = 0 .53 ,

5 EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 

5.1 BENCHMARK TASKS 

Our benchmark tasks aim to answer the following research questions as summarized in Table 4. • RQ1: (AI Adoption) How well can frontier MLLMs forecast how humans use agent contribu-tions in long-horizon professional workflows? • RQ2: (Human Rating) How well can frontier MLLMs predict human ratings of collaboration satisfaction and output quality given long-horizon human-agent collaboration workflows? 

RQ1 (AI Adoption). We evaluate whether MLLMs can predict whether humans A DOPT , M ODIFY ,or R EJECT agent contributions under two settings—recognition (1a) and forecasting (1b) —which differ in whether the human response is observable or not (Table 4). Each input begins at the source node, where the human delegates to the agent ( T start source ). Forecasting clips average 14.0 minutes (SD = 13 .7; max=73.0), while prediction clips average 8.8 minutes ( SD = 11 .7; max=72.0) with more details in Table 10. We are also curious whether longer context improves or degrades model performance. Thus, for Experiment 1c (step-N forecasting), the model must forecast the AI adoption label for each target AI node in the last step, while providing the preceding 1, 2, or 3 steps as additional context. For the longest context setting, motivated by how human annotators often need to watch the entire video context to determine the adoption label, we introduce Experiment 1d (workflow-level forecasting). In this setting, we provide the entire workflow up to the current target AI node. 

RQ2 (Human Rating). An evaluator should distinguish genuinely successful interactions from those in which professionals were dissatisfied or produced lower-quality outcomes, rather than simply reflecting the predominantly positive ratings. Thus, we investigate whether MLLMs can similarly recover professionals’ ratings from multimodal, long-horizon human-agent collaboration workflows. Performance on forecasting AI adoption. Table 5 shows each MLLM’s performance with video only as input. Claude performs the best for both recognizing and forecasting human adoption be-havior, yet even the upper-bound only reaches an F1-score of 0.676 aside from GPT-6, which was evaluated on fewer instances than other models. Full details of model comparison with the common instance sets are shown in the Table 15 and Table 16 in the Appendix. Since the default AI agent used in this study is Claude, we hypothesize that it has a strong advantage in understanding interac-tions within its own interface. Even so, Claude’s F1 for forecasting human adoption is only 0.492, dominated by A DOPT . It has substantially lower performance on M ODIFY and R EJECT . Among the remaining models, Gemini 3.1 Pro performs the best when the human’s actual response is observ-able, with an F1 score of 0.617. Gemini models performs particularly poorly at forecasting R EJECT ,even worse than the open-weight Qwen models. Gemini 3.1 Pro is very biased toward generating ADOPT . 52% of true M ODIFY and 71% of true R EJECT are predicted as A DOPT by Gemini 3.1 Pro (Figure 7). Why should an AI model care to understand human collaboration with other AI models? Humans orchestrate various software tools in our dataset, and their workflows are not necessarily limited to a single AI model. Even within our study, professionals used other AI systems, such as Gemini, Chat-GPT, and Grok, in addition to Claude. This makes it increasingly important for agents to understand human collaboration with other models, rather than only interactions involving themselves. 

Does longer context help models anticipate human adoption behavior? We investigate whether providing more workflow context improves MLLMs’ performance on forecasting human’s behavior for AI adoption. Using 114 instances in which both the source and target AI nodes appear in the last step of each session, we extend the context to include up to three preceding steps (Experiment 1c) or the entire workflow up to the target AI node (Experiment 1d). Longer context seems to provide limited improvements, with macro-F1 changes below 0.07 for most video and graph settings even though none of the improvements with video are statistically significant (Figure 4). Chat logs benefit the most from additional context, with Gemini 3.1 Pro improving by 0.12 F1 when given three preceding steps ( p < 0.01 ). However, this improvement does not persist when the entire workflow is provided. Longer context can also hurt performance, as Qwen-32B drops from 0.43 to 0.32 F1 with the full graph ( p < 0.01 ). For forecasting whether humans adopt agent contributions, models often strongly prefer certain labels although the true labels are M ODIFY or R EJECT (Figure 7). Gemini 3.1 Pro and Flash over-predict A DOPT . With video inputs, Gemini 3.8 Flash forecasts A DOPT for 83.8% of examples compared with a 65.9% ground-truth rate, and Qwen3-32B instead favors M ODIFY , predicting it in 87.3% of cases compared with 19.1% in the ground truth. These biases largely persist with graph and chat inputs as well (e.g., Flash generates A DOPT for 82.3% and 74.0%, and Qwen3-32B outputs MODIFY for 67.7% and 62.0%). Models therefore exhibit distinct forecasting biases, which vary with both the model and input modality. For human-rating prediction (Figure 8), some models predict similar ratings all the time. With video, Gemini 3.1 Pro predicts 4.83 for a true rating of 1 and 4.95 for a 5 (Experiment 2a); in Experiment 2b, it predicts 5.00 and 4.94. Graph input improves this separation, with gaps between true ratings of 1 and 5 reaching 1.95 and 2.45 for Experiments 2a and 2b. Claude and Qwen variants also show a similar pattern. Claude’s predictions range only from 3.3 to 3.9 across the entire 1–5 rating scale with video input in both experiments, while the ratings of Qwen variants range from 3.04 to 4.15. However, GPT-6 already separates low and high ratings with video and remains the most stable across modalities. These systematic biases raise a more fundamental question: to what extent are MLLMs actually grounding their predictions in the observed workflow? If models understand the workflow context, their predictions should vary with the human behaviors and outcomes they observe. Some models repeatedly produce similar labels or ratings despite different inputs and ground-truths. It suggests that their predictions may be driven by model-specific priors instead of the workflow itself. 11 6 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We have introduced K OLAB as a new dataset to benchmark frontier multimodal agentic models on their capabilities to support human-agent collaboration better on long-horizon workflows. Our results show that even frontier MLLMs, including GPT-6, struggle to predict and understand hu-man–AI collaboration. As a future work, we will explore ways to improve these capabilities by constructing training datasets or developing sandbox environments where models can practice col-laborating with simulated users before real-world deployment. ETHICS STATEMENT 

Understanding how humans collaborate with AI agents is important for developing AI systems that better support human needs and decisions. However, evaluating human-agent collaboration also raises ethical concerns. First, models that inaccurately predict whether humans will adopt, mod-ify, or reject AI contributions may misrepresent human intentions and preferences. Our findings highlight the limitations of current MLLMs in understanding professional human-agent collabora-tive workflows. Relying on these models as proxies for human judgments could lead to misleading assessments, particularly in professional domains where decisions require specialized expertise and contextual understanding. Second, collecting and releasing real-world professional workflows raises privacy concerns, as screen recordings and interaction logs may contain sensitive information. To mitigate these risks, we remove all personally identifiable information (PII) in our data. Our work aims to support the development of human-centered collaborative agents while emphasizing that model predictions should not replace human judgments or undermine human agency. We collected K OLAB through two sessions per participant, in which we recorded the participant’s computer screen. Each session lasted approximately one hour or longer and centered on a problem chosen by the participant. After data processing and due to time constraints, some problems con-tained fewer than five steps; however, each interaction session still lasted approximately one hour. To capture different patterns of human-agent collaboration, we used two protocols. In Session 1, participants were encouraged to interact with the agent at least five times while completing their workflow. In Session 2, participants were encouraged to interact with the agent at each step of the workflow. The two sessions did not have to address the same problem. However, participants could use the second session to continue or extend the work from the first session. For example, a partic-ipant might use the first session to develop a branding logo and use the second session to create a marketing advertisement based on the resulting brand identity. Participants were required to interact with agentic AI, Claude, using our credentials during each session and were free to use other AI agents when appropriate for their work. For example, an architect might use Claude for planning and reasoning while using ChatGPT to generate or render architectural images. At the time of data collection, most participants used the default Claude model (Sonnet 5.5), while a few used Opus 5. Each participant planned their workflow before the recording sessions to ensure that their problem satisfied our task criteria. After the recording sessions, they updated the workflow document and completed surveys about their collaboration experience and output quality. During each recording session, participants were encouraged to think aloud; we used the transcriptions to construct the workflow graphs and to guide the annotation of response labels. A.4 DATA PROCESSING AND ANNOTATION DETAILS 

The actions from workflow video are aligned with steps documented by each professional. Claude was used to map video timestamps to the corresponding steps, after which professionals reviewed and corrected the alignments. We then represented each workflow as a directed graph, where each node corresponds to a human or AI action and is associated with a workflow step. Nodes with out-degree greater than one denote a branch into concurrent sub-paths, and nodes with in-degree greater than one denote a merge of previously concurrent branches back into a single line of work. The authors and professionals jointly reviewed and refined these graphs. Graphs can include branches when activities occur in parallel and loops when the same action is repeated, allowing the represen-tation to capture non-linear workflow structures as shown in Figure 12. After graph construction, two human annotators annotated how agent outputs were incorporated into the workflow. For each observed response to an agent output, annotators identified the source node, corresponding to the human action that initiated the request, and the agent node, corresponding to the resulting agent re-sponse. They then identified the subsequent human node in which the response was acted upon and labeled the user’s response as A DOPT , M ODIFY , or R EJECT . A DOPT indicates that the AI output was incorporated without modification; M ODIFY indicates that it was incorporated with changes; and R EJECT indicates that the user explicitly rejected the output or did not incorporate it into the workflow. Because an AI output may be acted upon later in the workflow rather than immediately after it is produced, these annotations explicitly link each user response to the AI output it concerns. A.7 MODEL SELECTION 

For proprietary models, we evaluate leading video-capable multimodal models from major providers: Gemini 3.1 Pro and Gemini 3.8 Flash (Google), GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI), and Claude Opus 5.5 . Gemini natively accepts video as an input. GPT-6 Astra has no native video modality, so we sample evenly-spaced frames from each clip as its input; this also lets GPT-6 Astra serve as a frame-sampled point of comparison against Gemini’s genuinely video-native processing. We additionally evaluate Claude despite it likewise lacking native video input (also requiring frame sampling), because Claude is the specific agent our study participants interacted with during their recorded sessions, so evaluating it tests whether the very model embedded in the human-AI collab-oration can itself judge or predict the outcome of that collaboration. For open-weight models, we evaluate two representative video-capable Qwen3-VL variants (Bai et al., 2025): Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct , a mixture-of-experts model (30B total parameters, 3B active per token) included to test whether MoE-style efficiency scaling preserves video-temporal understanding, and Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct , a dense model of comparable total scale that instead invests its full parameter budget in deeper visual-language fusion at every token. Comparing the two isolates the effect of the MoE-vs-dense architectural choice at a fixed parameter scale. A.8 SAMPLE SIZE AND SELECTION
