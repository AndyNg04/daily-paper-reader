Abstract 

As LLM agents are increasingly deployed in high-stakes enterprise settings, understanding how they handle sensitive information in agen-tic dialogue has become critical. Privacy leak-age in such settings requires reasoning about whether information flows respect the contex-tual norms under which they originated. We ex-tend CIMemories (Mireshghallah et al., 2025) to CIM EMORIES DIALOGUE , a benchmark of 4,410 two-agent dialogue instances grounded in Contextual Integrity theory. Using this benchmark, we systematically evaluate 7 open-weight models from the Qwen3 and Llama 3.1 families across conversation lengths of 1 and 6 turns under three prompting conditions. We find that task capability and privacy leakage are fundamentally coupled: more capable models are systematically leakier, with no model in our evaluation able to decouple the two. Thinking models consistently leak more than their non-thinking counterparts, a pattern we attribute to the extended reasoning surface created by chain-of-thought. Leakage compounds with conversation length, rising up to 3× from 1 to 6 turns. Our results suggest that the coupling between task capability and privacy leakage is not an artifact of misconfiguration but a struc-tural feature of current agentic systems. Our code and data will be made available here 1.

Afoundational framework for this is Nissenbaum’s theory of Contextual Integrity (CI) (Nissenbaum, 2010), which holds that privacy violations occur not when information is shared, but when it is shared in ways that contravene the norms of the social context in which it originated. For example, sharing medical data with a physician may be considered appropriate, whereas sharing the same data with a bank loan officer would violate expectations of contextual integrity. CI theory has since been adopted to study privacy in LLMs (Mireshghallah et al., 2024; Ghalebikesabi et al., 2024), establishing that models routinely make contextually inappropriate disclosures that content-level filtering cannot catch while not sharing enough information required to complete the task. Previous work on benchmarking privacy leakage in LLMs studies contextual privacy in single-agent memory/action settings (PrivacyLens (Shao et al., 2024), ConfAIde (Mireshghallah et al., 2024), and CIMemories (Mireshghallah et al., 2025)), or stud-ies multi-agent leakage as a systems/security prob-lem (TAMAS (Kavathekar et al., 2025), AgentLeak (Yagoubi et al., 2026)). Existing MAS safety/privacy benchmarks show that multi-agent systems create new leakage channels (pathway through which sensitive data propagates beyond intended boundaries), but they 

do not isolate how dialogue dynamics, model capability, reasoning mode, and agent role shape contextual-integrity violations. While existing benchmarks focus on a range of tasks and scenar-ios, we choose to extend CIMemories because it is grounded in realistic task scenarios and annotated with memory-attribute-specific CI labels. In this work, we introduce CIM EMORIES DIALOGUE ,an agentic dialogue benchmark for systematically studying how design decisions, such as the choice of prompt, model family, and number of conver-sational turns, contribute to privacy leakage. Our experiments span 7 sender and 5 receiver models from 2 model families across 2 conversation lengths, covering reasoning models and their non-reasoning variants in both the sender and receiver roles, with sizes ranging from 4B-70B. Contextual privacy and memory in LLM agents. 

Privacy in LLMs has increasingly been studied through the lens of CI (Nissenbaum, 2010), which defines privacy violations as information flows that contravene the norms of the social context in which the information originated. ConfAIde (Mireshghal-lah et al., 2024) was the first benchmark to operationalize CI for language models, evaluating whether a model can recognize when sharing a piece of information would be contextually inap-propriate. PrivacyLens (Shao et al., 2024) extends this paradigm from recognition to action, mea-suring whether models respect CI norms during free-form generation rather than merely identifying violations in isolation. AirGapAgent (Bagdasarian et al., 2024) takes a system-design perspective, proposing an agent architecture that prevents con-textually inappropriate disclosures by structurally limiting the information available to the LLM at inference time. Most directly relevant to our work, CIMemories (Mireshghallah et al., 2025) intro-duces a compositional benchmark for evaluating CI over persistent user memories; however, each of its instances captures a one-shot disclosure decision in a single-agent setting, whereas our CIM EMORIES 

DIALOGUE benchmark studies how leakage unfolds through interactive elicitation between two memory-bearing agents over a multi-turn dialogue. Together, these works establish CI and memory-aware privacy evaluation as central concerns, but 

none of them model privacy leakage as a product of dialogue between memory-bearing agents. 

Privacy in Collaborative Interactions Privacy can also arise as a tension internal to ordinary, co-operative collaboration: agents share a goal but hold private interests, and leakage arises through cooperation rather than attack. PAC-Bench (Park et al., 2026) studies two-agent collaboration under explicit privacy constraints where agents complete tasks by making tool calls like invoking external functions, APIs, etc. PrivacyBench (Mukhopadhyay et al., 2025) eval-uates a single RAG-based agent whose retrieval mechanism surfaces a user’s data to an adversar-ial probe. PrivacyBench also treats secrets as bi-nary ground-truth objects with explicit confidant lists, whereas our CI labels are attribute-level judge-ments of contextual appropriateness derived from social task norms. Zou et al. (2026) instantiates the coordination-privacy trade-off in a decentral-ized calendar scheduling domain with oracle and DCOP baselines. Juneja et al. (2025) constructs MAGPIE dataset, high-stakes negotiation tasks in which private information is integral to task reso-lution. However, MAGPIE does not focus on as-sessing contextual integrity norms. Each establish that multi-agent privacy is partly a collaboration problem, but their task is constrained to a domain and the private information is task-bound rather than user memories. In contrast to these efforts, we evaluate leakage emerging organically from coop-erative dialogue and isolate the dialogue channel to attribute leakage cleanly. 

Mitigating privacy leakage in agents. A sepa-rate line of work focuses on attacking or improving models rather than constructing a diagnostic bench-mark of natural multi-agent memory disclosure. LeakAgent (Nie et al., 2025) trains an attacker agent via reinforcement learning to generate ad-versarial prompts that elicit privacy leakage, such as training-data or system-prompt extraction. Pri-vAct (Cheng et al., 2026) approaches the problem from the opposite direction, using multi-agent pref-erence training to internalize contextual privacy preservation in agent behavior. Both presuppose a measurement substrate that determines when leak-age has occurred and whether a given disclosure constitutes a CI violation in context. Closer to a model-side intervention, CI-RL (Lan et al., 2026) trains the policy to internalize CI norms directly: it elicits explicit reasoning about contextual appro-priateness (CI-CoT). Our work is complementary :it provides the kind of measurement framework needed to evaluate such red-teaming attacks and preference-trained defenses systematically.

3 Experimental Setup 

3.1 Dataset 

We extend CIM EMORIES (Mireshghallah et al., 2025), a benchmark for evaluating contextual in-tegrity in memory-augmented LLMs, to a two-agent setting. The original benchmark consists of 10 synthetic user profiles, each containing an average of 147 personal attributes spanning nine in-formation domains (finance, health, housing, legal, mental health, relationships, among others), paired with 49 social task contexts per profile ( e.g. , com-municating with a psychiatrist, negotiating with a landlord, or corresponding with an employer). Each attribute-task pair is annotated with a binary contextual integrity label indicating whether it is appropriate to share that attribute when completing the task, produced via a GPT-5 labeler instantiated with privacy personas from Westin’s survey (funda-mentalist, pragmatic, unconcerned) (Kumaraguru and Cranor, 2005). Unless specified, our work re-ports a weighted combination of the labels on these three personas (weights are taken from Mireshghal-lah et al. (2025)). In our extension CIM EMORIES DIALOGUE ,each instance pairs a sender user profile with a receiver user profile. The sender’s memories represent the agent whose privacy is primarily at risk. The receiver’s profile captures the personal at-tributes of the agent playing the role defined by the social context. For instance, a psychiatrist’s own memories when the task involves a mental health consultation. Crucially, this role is not fixed to a profile: the same receiver may act as a psychiatrist in one context and a landlord in another, mirror-ing real-world settings where a user or assistant assumes different roles depending on the task and conversation. Each agent can access only its own memories. We use the full 10 user profiles available from CIMemories dataset, and any profile can serve as either sender or receiver. Pairing each profile with the 9 others (excluding same-profile pairings) across all 49 task contexts yields 10 × 9 × 49 = 4, 410 (sender, receiver, task context) instances. 

3.2 Agent Setup 

We simulate synchronous multi-turn dialogue be-tween two LLM agents. Sender agent always initi-ates the conversation by directing the interaction to-ward the given social task. Receiver agent responds in the role defined by the task context, drawing on its own memory. We vary the number of conversa-tion turns across {1, 6}, operationalizing short and extended interactions. This variation reflects the intuition that privacy risks compound as dialogue progresses and more information becomes contex-tually salient or elicitable over time. The prompt used by the agent to define the task is given in the appendix Figure 7. 

3.3 Models 

We evaluate 7 sender models against 5 receiver models spanning 2 families, multiple parameter scales, and reasoning versus non-reasoning vari-ants: • Qwen3 family (Team, 2025): Qwen3-4B-Instruct (we consider this non-reasoning vari-ant), Qwen3-4B-Thinking (reasoning variant), Qwen3-8B (non-reasoning variant), Qwen3-8B-Thinking (reasoning variant), Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct • Llama 3.1 (Grattafiori et al., 2024): Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct To manage computational costs, we evaluate the full set of 7 models at 6 turns, and a controlled subset for 1 and 6 turn: Qwen3-4B-Thinking, Qwen3-4B-Non-Thinking, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. This subset is designed to support comparisons along two axes: model family (Llama vs Qwen) and reasoning mode (thinking vs. non-thinking within the Qwen3 family). 

3.4 Prompt conditions 

We study three system prompt conditions (PC), applied symmetrically to both agents: • Control Prompt (PC0): Agents receive no privacy-related guidance; only the task is specified. • Privacy-Aware Prompt (PC1): Agents are explicitly instructed to protect sensitive per-sonal information when completing the task. • Task Maximization Prompt (PC2): Agents are instructed to leverage all available informa-tion to complete the task as fully as possible. 4 Evaluation Procedure 

The evaluation follows a two-stage pipeline designed to first detect revealed memories and then assess whether those revelations constitute genuine privacy violations under the CI framework. 

Stage-1: Memory reveal detection. Revealed memory attributes are detected using Qwen3-8B as an automated judge, via a modified version of the REVEAL prompt from Mireshghallah et al. (2025) (reproduced in Appendix Figure 10). Our modifications improve upon the original in five ways: 1) instructions were clarified for precision, 2) the prompt explicitly distinguishes between par-tial and full reveal. Full reveal is defined as mes-sage explicitly stating EVERY component of the attribute, and partial reveal is defined as stating at least one component of the attribute but not all of them, 3) few-shot examples were added to anchor the model behavior, 4) outputs are structured as JSON to enable reliable programmatic parsing, and 5) the number of memory attributes shown to the prompt at a time are limited to 10 to match the capabilities of smaller models. 2

Detection is performed both at the individual turn level and over the full conversation transcript,

All results unless mentioned report full reveal numbers. Partial reveal leakages are mentioned in appendix. 

Stage-2: Contextual Integrity Labelling. Not all revealed items constitute privacy violations. A memory attribute being revealed might be crucial for task completion or might be benign informa-tion, such as the user’s name. To detect genuine violations, we adopt the reference-label pipeline from Mireshghallah et al. (2025), using GPT-5 as the judge with the original CI-operationalizing prompt unchanged. GPT-5 labels serve as the reference truth for whether the revealed memory attribute represents an inappropriate information flow under the CI framework. GPT-5 is retained for this role to preserve comparability with the CIM EMORIES baseline. 

Metrics. We adopt two complementary metrics. 

Completeness is measured at the context level as the proportion of attributes that are essential to share in order to complete the task. Leakage Rate 

is measured at the context level as the proportion of inappropriate attributes leaked anywhere across the full multi-turn dialogue. This helps us capture cumulative exposure over the course of a conver-sation rather than at any single generation or turn. The leakage rate metric is decomposed into full 

and total leakage ( total leakage = full + partial 

variants, corresponding to the disclosure categories detected in Stage 1 of the evaluation procedure. Unless specified, we have reported the full leakage. 

5 Experimental Results 

RQ1. Do frontier LLMs respect the CI of user memories in an agentic dialogue setting? We evaluate this question in the 6-turn setting, aver-aging over prompt conditions. Figure 3 plots full leakage rate against task completeness for the 7 sender models; each ellipse is one sender and each point is one of the 5 receiver partners. We make the following observations: 

Completeness-leakage trade-off. Higher task completeness is associated with higher pri-vacy leakage across sender models. At the sender ×receiver granularity of Figure 3, the as-sociation is strong. Qwen3 4B Instruct, the highest-performing agent-1 model, achieves a mean com-bined completeness of approximately 0.456 while also exhibiting a mean leakage rate of approxi-mately 0.047. By contrast, Llama 3.1 8B achieves a mean completeness of only 0.245 but maintains a mean leakage rate of 0.008. No sender in this evaluation occupies the high-completeness, low-leakage region of the plot: models that share more of what the task requires also disclose more at-tributes that contextual integrity would withhold. 

Thinking versus non-thinking senders. Across the Qwen family, the reasoning variants consis-tently exhibit higher leakage rates than their non-thinking counterparts. As agent 1, Qwen3 8B Thinking averages a combined leakage rate of 0.022 at a completeness of 0.330, whereas Qwen3 8B Non-Thinking averages 0.018 leakage at a com-pleteness of 0.278. A similar pattern holds for the 4B family: Qwen3 4B Thinking averages a leakage rate of 0.040 at a completeness of 0.405, com-pared to 0.036 leakage at 0.398 completeness for Qwen3 4B Instruct. The 8B result aligns with emerging findings on reasoning-model privacy. The extended chain-of-thought process, while improving task perfor-mance, appears to create additional surface area for private information to be surfaced and com-municated. Green et al. (2025) show that thinking tokens do not uniformly help and can in turn trap models into undesirable behaviors. Our results suggest that privacy leakage may be one such trap, though the 4B pair shows that reasoning mode alone does not determine leak-age. While Llama 3.1 70B model offers a meaningful improvement in completeness over the 8B variant, both remain low in their scores compared to the Qwen family. Smaller Qwen models exhibit disproportionately high leakage. The Qwen 4B models - both Instruct and Thinking variants - are among the highest leak-ers in the evaluation. 6 Conclusion 

We introduced CIM EMORIES DIALOGUE , abenchmark of 4,410 two-agent dialogue instances that extend contextual-integrity evaluation from one-shot disclosure decisions to interactive, multi-turn elicitation between two agents. Using it, we evaluated 7 open-weight sender models and 5 receiver models from 2 model families across conversation lengths, reasoning models, and three prompting conditions, isolating how dialogue dynamics and model characteristics shape privacy leakage. Our central finding is that task capability and privacy leakage are coupled rather than independent. This coupling is sharpened by two factors. First, reasoning models consistently leak more than their non-thinking counterparts. Second, leakage compounds with conversation length, rising up to 3× from one to six turns for thinking models as more attack surfaces are exposed. 7 Limitations 

Our findings should be read in light of several limitations. The benchmark is built on synthetic data: we extend CIM EMORIES , whose ten user pro-files and memories are model-generated rather than drawn from real individuals, so the attribute distri-butions and contextual-integrity norms may not re-flect real-world memory or dialogue, and the small number of underlying profiles limits the diversity of sender–recipient pairings. Our CI labels are bi-nary and therefore omit graded sensitivity and other determinants of disclosure norms, including time-dependence: the same attribute can change status as a relationship or context evolves, which this op-erationalisation does not model. We also study only dyadic interaction; real deployments often involve more agents, tool use, and external memory, which introduce leakage channels our two-agent setting does not capture. Our metrics depend on LLM judges at two stages: Qwen3-8B for disclosure detection and GPT-5 for contextual-integrity labeling. Any error or bias in either judge propagate into our reported numbers. Finally, our coverage is partial: we evalu-ate 7 open-weight models from 2 families (with the 1- and 6-turn settings run on only a subset for com-pute reasons), all interactions are in English, and dialogues are simulated from templated prompts, so behavior may differ for other models, languages, or naturally occurring conversations. 

8 Ethical Considerations 

This work uses no real personal data: all profiles, memories, and social contexts derive from the syn-thetic CIM EMORIES benchmark, so it involves no human subjects and poses no direct risk to real individuals’ privacy. We recognise that a bench-mark characterising privacy leakage has dual-use potential, since the same measurements could in principle guide an adversary; we judge the benefit of a rigorous, contextual-integrity-grounded mea-surement framework to outweigh this risk, and we frame our contribution as diagnostic and defensive. We also note a normative caveat: our CI la-bels are operationalised through a specific privacy-persona typology and a single model labeller, and privacy norms vary across cultures and individuals, so claims about which models are “more private” should be read within the bounds of this opera-tionalisation rather than as universal judgements. To support scrutiny and reproducibility, we will release our benchmark extension and evaluation code. We build on CIMemories (Mireshghallah et al., 2025), released under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Our use and redistribution as a derivative benchmark is consistent with these terms. We release CIMEMO-RIES DIALOGUE and our evaluation code under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, for research use consistent with the original dataset’s terms. Some of the code for the paper has been gener-ated using AI Assistants.

A Example Appendix 

A.1 Related Works Privacy and adversarial risks in multi-agent LLM systems. A separate line of work treats multi-agent privacy as a security problem, asking how co-ordination between agents opens leakage channels that an adversary can exploit. TAMAS (Kavathekar et al., 2025) benchmarks adversarial risks across a range of multi-agent scenarios, attack types, and tool-use configurations, but privacy is treated as one risk dimension among many rather than exam-ined through a CI lens. AgentLeak (Yagoubi et al., 2026) maps the full stack of privacy leakage chan-nels in MAS, including inter-agent communication and tool-use surfaces, and constructs an attack tax-onomy over them. While these benchmarks surface architectural attacks unique to MAS, their framing is adversarial and channel-centric: they measure whether sensitive data can be extracted, not whether cooperating, memory-bearing agents inadvertently violate the contextual-integrity norms of a benign interaction . As a result, neither isolates CI viola-tions in persistent-memory dialogue, which is the regime our benchmark targets.

All open-weight models were served using vLLM on 2 × NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs. Generat-ing all dialogue simulations across 7 sender × 5 receiver models, 2 conversation lengths, and 3 prompt conditions over 4,410 instances required approximately 88200 GPU-hours. Stage-2 contextual-integrity la-beling used GPT-5 via the OpenAI API ( 16000 total calls totalling 350$); we did not run this model locally. For all models, we use the de-fault settings available through vllm. Agent generations used default settings with a maximum of 8192 tokens per turn. We did not perform conventional hyperparam-eter tuning, as this is an evaluation rather than a training study. The one search we conducted was over the batch size of memory attributes shown to the Stage-1 judge: we compared sizes of 5, 10, 20, and all-attributes on a stratified subset of 50 examples with manual verification, selecting 10 as the best trade-off between detection accuracy and efficiency. Stage-1 disclosure detection used Qwen/Qwen3-8B as an automated judge with temperature 0 and a maximum token budget of 8,192. Stage-2 contextual-integrity labeling used GPT-5 accessed on May 10, 2026 via the API with default decoding settings; this model was not run locally.
