Abstract 

Advances in large language models (LLMs) have brought the vision of a general-purpose personal assistant closer to real-ity. Such assistants could provide a unified natural-language interface to a broad ecosystem of applications. However, real-world applications do not readily support training at scale because access is often restricted and their state is difficult to reset. To address this challenge, we introduce Terrar-ium, a framework that synthesizes executable App Worlds from real-world application information, generates training task packages with evaluator-side rubrics from these worlds, and iteratively validates and refines both through agent roll-outs. For App World synthesis, Terrarium organizes public Web information and in-application observations into struc-tured app specifications that guide the construction of self-contained runtimes with callable operations and mutable, re-settable state. For task synthesis, Terrarium constructs tasks grounded in App World state together with evaluator-side out-come rubrics that assess success without prescribing reference trajectories. We construct a training suite of 275 App Worlds and 13,344 tasks for outcome-based reinforcement learning across three Qwen3 model scales (4B, 8B, and 14B param-eters). Across these scales, models trained with Terrarium outperform the corresponding Qwen3 models by an average of 18.34 percentage points on the τ 2-bench and 10.94 per-centage points on the AppWorld bench. These results suggest that app-grounded world and task synthesis offers a scalable path toward general-purpose assistants capable of operating across broad application ecosystems. 

# 1 Introduction 

Thousands of applications have become deeply integrated into daily life, creating an increasingly complex application ecosystem (Wen et al. 2024). A general-purpose personal assistant (Muthusamy et al. 2023; Liang et al. 2024) could provide a unified natural-language interface to this ecosys-tem. Users express what they want to accomplish, and the assistant interacts with the relevant applications, plans the necessary operations, and carries out the task (Qin et al. 2023, 2025). Advances in large language models (LLMs) have brought this vision closer to reality (OpenAI 2023; Gemini Team 2024; Kimi Team 2025). Training LLM agents across this ecosystem still depends on realistic, controllable, and scalable environments (Zhou et al. 2023; Xie et al. 2024; Trivedi et al. 2024; Barres et al. 2025). However, constructing environments that are simultane-ously realistic, controllable, and scalable remains challeng-ing. Real-world applications contain rich workflows, state dynamics, and service rules, but using them for repeated training is constrained by restricted access and the difficulty of resetting state (Garg et al. 2025; Chae, Park, and Ritter 2026). Manually engineered environments (Zhou et al. 2023; Xie et al. 2024; Rawles et al. 2024; Trivedi et al. 2024) offer greater control, but each additional application requires sub-stantial implementation and validation effort, making broad ecosystem coverage difficult. Automated synthesis can re-duce this cost and expand coverage (Sullivan, Hartmann, and Koller 2025; Fang et al. 2025; Song et al. 2026; Wang et al. 2026). However, most existing pipelines begin from generic scenarios, often producing environments that sim-plify the interactions that LLM agents must ultimately han-dle in real-world applications. This simplification limits the training signal available for developing the capabilities re-quired for reliable task completion. These limitations moti-vate a promising direction that uses rich information from individual applications to define executable worlds and their training supervision. To address this challenge, we introduce Terrarium, a scalable framework for app-grounded world and task syn-thesis. Terrarium first synthesizes executable App Worlds from real-world application information. From these worlds, arXiv:2610.14860v1 [cs.LG] 10 Oct 2026 Terrarium generates training task packages with evaluator-side rubrics. To ensure end-to-end consistency across these artifacts, Terrarium validates them jointly through agent rollouts. Rollout feedback drives subsequent rounds of re-finement and validation, forming a closed-loop construction process. Figure 1 provides an overview of the executable App Worlds and training tasks constructed by Terrarium across diverse applications. The Terrarium construction pipeline consists of two main stages: App World synthesis and task synthesis (Fig-ure 2). Terrarium treats real-world application information as a blueprint for App World construction. It organizes public Web information and GUI-agent traces into structured app specifications that capture application capabilities and con-ditions, workflows and entities, and operations and observ-able outcomes. Once validated and frozen, each specification guides the construction of a self-contained App World with app-specific state, callable operations, transition logic, and a resettable seed state, enabling repeated interaction without access to production backends. As a common intermediate representation, the app specification supports a shared con-struction process across diverse applications while retaining app-specific workflows and constraints. Task synthesis builds on these worlds by grounding user intents in concrete App World states and pairing them with evaluator-side outcome rubrics. The rubrics define intended outcomes and prohib-ited side effects without prescribing reference trajectories. From the resulting base tasks, Terrarium derives Intent Ex-ecution supervision for carrying actionable intents to valid outcomes and Intent Completion supervision for identifying and requesting missing, execution-critical information before changing application state. We construct a training suite of 275 App Worlds and 13,344 training tasks derived from 8,878 grounded base tasks. We train Qwen3 models (Yang et al. 2025) at three scales (4B, 8B, and 14B parameters) using Group Relative Policy Optimization (Shao et al. 2024) with evaluator-side outcome rewards. Across these scales, models trained with Terrarium outperform the corresponding Qwen3 models by an average of 18.34 percentage points on the τ 2-bench (Bar-res et al. 2025) and 10.94 percentage points on the App-World (Trivedi et al. 2024). Data-scaling experiments span-ning 35 to 275 App Worlds show that both benchmark micro-averages improve consistently as the training corpus grows. Supervision-composition ablations show that combining In-tent Execution and Intent Completion improves over Intent Execution alone across every benchmark subset. Together, these results show that Terrarium provides effective su-pervision across model scales, benefits from broader app-grounded training data, and supports complementary capa-bilities for executing and completing user intents. Our main contributions are summarized below. 1. A scalable framework for App World and task syn-thesis. We introduce Terrarium, which transforms real-world application information into executable, resettable App Worlds and grounded training task packages with evaluator-side rubrics. Agent rollouts jointly validate and iteratively refine the synthesized worlds, tasks, and eval-uators through a closed-loop construction process. 2. An open-source implementation and training suite. 

We implement Terrarium at scale and construct a train-ing suite comprising 275 App Worlds and 13,344 tasks. We will open-source the framework together with the resulting App Worlds and task packages to support repro-ducible research on training general-purpose application agents. 3. Large-scale training and systematic evaluation. We train Qwen3 models at 4B, 8B, and 14B parameters using GRPO and evaluate them on τ 2-bench and AppWorld. Across model scales, Terrarium yields average gains of 18.34 and 10.94 percentage points, respectively, over the corresponding Qwen3 models. Data-scaling experiments and supervision-composition ablations further demon-strate the benefits of expanding the app-grounded training corpus and combining complementary task variants. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 LLM Agents Interacting with Applications 

LLMs are increasingly used as agents that translate user in-tent into actions within applications. A major line of work enables such interaction through structured tools and APIs, covering tool invocation, large-scale API retrieval, and plan-ning and execution over real-world services (Schick et al. 2023; Qin et al. 2023; Patil et al. 2023; Du, Wei, and Zhang 2024; Song et al. 2023). API-Bank and BFCL complement these methods by evaluating API selection, argument gener-ation, and multi-step function calling (Li et al. 2023; Patil et al. 2025). Another line extends agents to direct GUI con-trol, including app agents visual understanding and ground-ing (Hong et al. 2024; Cheng et al. 2024; Zheng et al. 2024; Deng et al. 2023), element- aware GUI testing (Wu et al. 2025), native GUI interaction (Qin et al. 2025), and learning interface dynamics from execution-validated inter-actions (Wu et al. 2026; Bai et al. 2024). Recent benchmarks further shift from isolated commands to long-horizon tasks involving dynamic state, domain policies, and users: Tool-Sandbox evaluates stateful conversational tool use (Lu et al. 2025), τ -bench introduces policy-constrained user conver-sations (Yao et al. 2024), τ 2-bench allows users and agents to modify shared state (Barres et al. 2025), and Mobile-World combines underspecified instructions, agent–user in-teraction, and hybrid GUI and MCP actions (Kong et al. 2026). These works develop and evaluate agents in increas-ingly complex applications. Terrarium instead focuses on the training substrate, using Web search results and GUI-agent traces from a particular real-world application to con-struct an app specification, an executable App World, and outcome-based training tasks for Intent Execution and Intent Completion.

# 3 Terrarium 

3.1 Overview 

Terrarium converts real-world application information into executable App Worlds and outcome-based training tasks. Its central representation is an app specification that records supported capabilities, workflows, entities, operating condi-tions, and observable outcomes. The specification bounds a controllable surrogate of the source application. Figure 2 summarizes the construction pipeline. Web search and GUI-agent traces are consolidated into an app specification, which guides synthesis of an app-specific run-time and a resettable seed world. Terrarium then constructs persona-conditioned base tasks and outcome rubrics, using calibration rollouts to repair inconsistencies among the task, evaluator, and world. An App World comprises the state schema, model-visible operations, transition logic, and seed state for one application. A base task pairs this runtime with a user intent, optional state enrichment, and an evaluator-side rubric. Terrarium constructs Intent Execution and Intent Completion tasks from the base tasks. Calibration rollouts evaluate these tasks and trigger revisions to the task, rubric, or App World when construction errors are detected. The accepted tasks, evaluators, and App Worlds then provide self-contained artifacts for LLM training. 

3.2 From Real-World Application Information to App Specifications 

Application information collection. Application-level understanding requires both functional breadth and direct interface grounding. Given an application name, an LLM uses Web search to collect public descriptions, usage instruc-tions, intended users, and operating conditions. A GUI agent then interacts with the application and records action traces, page screenshots, and accessibility trees. Web results iden-tify candidate capabilities, while GUI traces ground them in visible interaction paths, input requirements, and observable outcomes. 

App specification construction. An LLM consolidates the collected information into a semantic application sum-mary and normalizes each supported capability into work-flow steps that describe information gathering, decision points, and application operations. It then associates these steps with business entities and records how references and values flow through the workflow. From this entity flow, Ter-rarium derives model-visible operation contracts. Observa-tion operations expose candidates and constraints, whereas action operations implement atomic state changes. Their ar-guments must be traceable to the user, world state, fixed schema values, or earlier operation returns. When a self-contained world requires details absent from the collected information, Terrarium may introduce application-typical design choices, which are treated as synthetic world design rather than claims about the production backend. The vali-dated specification is then frozen as the semantic contract for world construction. 

3.3 App World Construction 

Specification-constrained runtime. The app specifica-tion defines operational semantics but not an executable world. Terrarium synthesizes an app-specific state schema, model-visible operations, and transition logic under this con-tract. App Worlds share a small protocol for initialization, op-eration invocation, state inspection, and reset, while retaining application-specific schemas and transitions. 

Search-guided seed-world construction. To populate the runtime, a schema-conditioned search agent collects repre-sentative entity instances, value and state distributions, and edge-case patterns from public sources. Unlike application-level search, this stage is conditioned on a fixed runtime schema and targets data needed to instantiate its business objects. The agent normalizes the retrieved material into a source pool and derives rules governing entity counts, relations, and state distributions before generating a task-independent seed world. Individual tasks may add contract-checked state enrichment, while reset restores a fresh seed and prevents cross-episode leakage. Calibration may reopen the seed only when a downstream rollout identifies an up-stream defect. 

3.4 Task Generation 

Given an executable App World, Terrarium constructs grounded tasks and their corresponding evaluators, derives two complementary forms of post-training supervision, and validates their joint behavior through rollouts. 

Persona-conditioned task authoring. To diversify user contexts and expressions, we follow prior work on persona-conditioned task synthesis (Ge et al. 2024). Terrarium com-bines each sampled persona with application capabilities and seed-world information. An LLM uses this context to author a plausible use scenario, while the App World continues to determine the executable state and outcome. Iterative au-thoring prioritizes underused scenarios and undercovered application capabilities. 

Grounded tasks and evaluators. Conditioned on a se-lected scenario, Terrarium constructs each base task with a user intent, optional task-specific state enrichment, and an evaluator-side outcome rubric. The rubric specifies the intended outcome and prohibited side effects without pre-scribing a reference trajectory. A grounding pass checks that target entities exist, prerequisites can be satisfied, and the outcome is judgeable. After execution, the evaluator uses the state difference, interaction trajectory, operation returns, and final response to produce a binary reward. The policy sees only the user intent and model-visible world interface. 

Supervision variants. From each base task, Terrarium constructs outcome-based supervision without prescribing a canonical action sequence. We call an intent actionable when a rubric-valid execution exists and every execution-critical input is either supplied by the user or recoverable through the model-visible App World. 

Intent Execution. For an actionable base task, Terrarium rewrites the user intent as a natural instruction that preserves the requested outcome and application constraints while re-moving explicit hints about the available operations. The App World, episode state, and outcome rubric remain unchanged, and ambiguous rewrites are excluded. The evaluator rewards a valid final state and response while allowing any interaction strategy that satisfies the same constraints. Figure 3: Category distribution of 275 App Worlds. 

Intent Completion. For eligible Intent Execution tasks, Terrarium removes or obscures one user-supplied field re-quired by the operation contract, transition logic, or outcome rubric. The field must be necessary for the original objec-tive and unrecoverable from the remaining intent, valid de-faults, or read-only operations. The derived rubric rewards a focused clarification question before any state-changing ac-tion. Guessing the missing value, changing state, or claiming completion results in failure, and the expected state differ-ence remains empty. The episode ends with the clarification question without simulating a subsequent user response. The two variants share the same App World and underlying objective. Intent Execution rewards completing an action-able intent, while Intent Completion rewards eliciting nec-essary user-only information without changing application state. Requiredness and recoverability are used to construct the tasks and are not exposed to the policy. 

Rollout-guided refinement. The generated tasks remain provisional until the task, evaluator, and App World operate consistently during execution. Static checks cannot verify this joint behavior, so Terrarium launches a calibration rollout for each provisional task and examines the trajectory, state changes, operation outputs, final response, and evaluator out-put. Failures are classified as policy failures, task or rubric defects, seed or runtime defects, or infrastructure failures. Confirmed construction defects revise the responsible arti-fact, after which the affected task is rerun. Policy failures do not modify the task semantics, and infrastructure failures are retried. This loop continues until the task, evaluator, and App World pass the closure check or the task is excluded. Only tasks that pass this final check are retained as Intent Execution or Intent Completion supervision.

4.3 Diversity and Complexity of Synthesized App Worlds 

Figure 3 shows that the App Worlds cover a broad range of application categories. The 275 worlds span 8 categories, each accounting for 6.9%–16.4% of the corpus. This cov-erage broadens the application contexts represented during task generation, including differences in user goals, work-flows, and operating conditions. We characterize the structural complexity of the synthe-sized App Worlds using functional breadth, workflow depth, state structure, and available operations. Capabilities mea-sure functional breadth, and workflow steps measure proce-dural depth. Entity types and state tables characterize the rep-resented business state, while agent-visible operations define the interface through which a model can observe and change that state. Figure 4 reports the corresponding per-world dis-tributions over the final set of 275 accepted App Worlds. Figure 4 reveals two properties of the synthesized corpus. First, substantial structure is present in a typical App World. The median world combines 7 capabilities and 36 docu-mented workflow steps with 9 entity types, 10 state tables, and 14 agent-visible operations. This combination provides the procedural, state, and interface structure needed for multi-step interaction and state-based outcome evaluation. Second, the corpus spans a broad range of structural scale. At P90, ca-pabilities and operations both double relative to the median, entity types and state tables increase to 19, and workflow steps increase to 58. The upper tail across all 4 dimensions indicates that the synthesis process produces worlds of vary-ing scope and avoids collapse to a uniform shallow template. These measurements establish the structural breadth of the corpus, while the external evaluations examine whether su-pervision from these worlds transfers beyond the synthesized environments. 

4.4 Scaling App-Grounded Training Data 

We study whether transfer improves as the app-grounded training corpus scales. We construct nested corpora contain-ing 35, 70, 140, and 275 App Worlds by randomly sampling App Worlds without replacement, with each larger corpus retaining all worlds from the preceding smaller corpus. We train Qwen3-4B on each corpus using the same GRPO con-figuration. The Qwen3-4B (Thinking) model serves as the zero-world reference. Figure 5 shows that both benchmark micro-averages im-prove consistently as the app-grounded training corpus ex-pands. Relative to the zero-world reference, training on 35 App Worlds improves performance by 13.67 percentage points on τ 2-bench and 8.04 percentage points on AppWorld. Across the trained models, increasing the corpus from 35 to 275 App Worlds raises performance from 47.12 to 56.83 on 

τ 2-bench and from 19.66 to 23.76 on AppWorld. These con-tinued gains suggest that scaling app-grounded environments and their associated supervision is a promising direction for further improving cross-application transfer.

Intent Completion supervision. Combining Intent Com-pletion with Intent Execution improves every benchmark subset. The combination raises the τ 2-bench from 39.57 to 56.83 and the AppWorld from 20.68 to 23.76. Auxiliary task variants. Handoff and Confirmation intro-duce different behavioral trade-offs rather than uniform im-provements. Including Handoff reduces AppWorld by 3.08 points, improves Airline and Telecom by 4.00 and 2.63 points while reducing Retail by 4.39 points. This pattern suggests that Handoff can help when tasks cross agent, user, or system boundaries, but may also encourage transferring responsibil-ity when the benchmark expects the agent to complete the task within the application. Including Confirmation reduces the τ 2-bench and AppWorld by 3.59 and 1.71 points, respec-tively. Because Confirmation rewards requesting approval before a state-changing operation, it can conflict with eval-uation tasks that expect immediate execution of an already specified request. Overall, neither auxiliary variant improves both benchmarks, indicating that Handoff and Confirmation encode narrower behaviors whose value depends on whether a task actually requires delegation or approval. Among the evaluated mixtures, Intent Execution and Intent Completion provide the strongest balance between completing actionable requests and eliciting information when execution is not yet possible. 

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduced Terrarium, a scalable framework for app-grounded world and task synthesis to support training general-purpose assistants across broad application ecosys-tems. Terrarium transforms real-world application infor-mation into executable, resettable App Worlds and grounded task packages with evaluator-side rubrics, using agent roll-outs for closed-loop validation and refinement. We imple-ment Terrarium at scale to construct 275 App Worlds and 13,344 training tasks, and will open-source the framework and resulting artifacts. GRPO training across Qwen3 models with 4B, 8B, and 14B parameters yields average gains of 18.34 percentage points on τ 2-bench and 10.94 percentage points on AppWorld over the corresponding Qwen3 mod-els. Data-scaling and supervision-composition studies fur-ther demonstrate the benefits of broader app-grounded train-ing data and complementary task variants. Together, these results suggest that app-grounded world and task synthesis offers a scalable path toward general-purpose assistants ca-pable of operating across diverse applications. References

A.1 Models, objective, and supervision sampling 

The final models are trained with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al. 2024), using a binary task-outcome reward. We initialize 4B, 8B, and 14B Qwen3 Thinking models (Yang et al. 2025). The same optimization recipe is used at all three model scales: 

• Model scales: Qwen3 Thinking 4B, 8B, and 14B. 

• Objective: GRPO with a binary task-outcome reward. 

• Learning rate and duration: 7 × 10 −7 for 340 update steps. 

• Rollout batch: 64 groups per step and 16 samples per group, yielding 1,024 rollout samples per step. 

• Supervision sampling: Intent Execution and Intent Com-pletion are sampled at a 70/30 ratio. 

• Infrastructure: the slime framework (Zhu et al. 2025) on 24 NVIDIA H20 GPUs. The primary corpus contains 8,867 Intent Execution tasks and 4,477 Intent Completion tasks. Thus, the stored task-count proportions are 66.45% and 33.55%, respectively. 

A.2 Outcome judge and reward construction 

Qwen3.5-397B-A17B-FP8 (Qwen Team 2026) serves as the evaluator-side judge during GRPO. For each rollout, the eval-uator is given the task-specific outcome rubric, the pre/post business-state difference, the interaction trajectory, operation returns, and the assistant’s final response. The rubric defines acceptable outcomes and prohibited side effects without pre-scribing a canonical operation sequence. The parsed verdict is converted to a binary reward: a valid pass receives one and a valid failure receives zero. The policy sees only the user intent and model-visible operation schemas and returns. It does not see the eval-uator rubric, structured expected evidence, or reference traces. Expected-evidence records may specify acceptable final facts, state-difference shapes, and forbidden states, but may not contain a required tool sequence or step-by-step solution. 

Judge prompt and input. The judge receives the following system prompt (line breaks are added only for typesetting):                                                             

> You are an evaluator for aminiapp agent episode. Judge only the final outcome using the rubric and evidence. Do not require afixed tool sequence. DB diff and actual tool returns are primary evidence for committed business writes. Do not treat offers, future suggestions, or clarification questions as committed state changes unless atool call, DB diff, or final answer explicitly says the action has already been completed. Return strict JSON only.

The corresponding user-prompt template is:                              

> Evaluate this episode. Return JSON with keys: passed (boolean), reward (number), rationale (string), failed_criteria (array of strings), failure_classification (string or null). Reward must be 1.0 for pass and 0.0 for fail. Allowed failure_classification values for failed episodes: agent_failure, task_query_issue, rubric_or_judge_issue, expected_evidence_issue, seed_enrichment_issue, backend_behavior_issue, tool_schema_or_description_issue, runtime_or_infra_issue. {JUDGE_INPUT_JSON}

The placeholder is replaced by an indented JSON serializa-tion with the following schema:             

> {"task_id": "...", "env_id": "...", "user_query": "...", "rubric": "...", "metadata": {}, "expected_evidence": {}, "compact_db_diff": {}, "full_db_diff": {}, "db_diff_metadata": {}, "trajectory": [], "tool_returns": [], "final_answer": "..." }

Judge decoding uses temperature 0, a 4,096-token generation limit, and thinking disabled. Although the response schema includes a numeric reward, the training pipeline recomputes it from the parsed passed field, assigning 1.0 to a pass and 0.0 to a failure.

# C Closed-Loop Validation and Refinement 

C.1 Why rollout closure is needed 

Static checks can validate schemas and references but cannot establish that a user intent, task-specific seed, runtime, and evaluator behave consistently during execution. Each provi-sional task is therefore executed in a calibration episode. The review jointly inspects the policy trajectory, operation re-turns, final response, business-state difference, and evaluator verdict. A defect is routed back to the artifact responsible for the inconsistency, and the affected task is rerun after repair.

C.3 Closure condition 

A task reaches closure only after all of the following hold: 1. its package is mechanically valid and the runtime can import, reset, expose the model-visible tools, execute op-erations, finish, and emit a serializable state difference; 2. the query, seed enrichment, operation contracts, rubric, and expected evidence refer to consistent entities, states, and boundaries; 3. any task-, evaluator-, seed-, runtime-, or infrastructure-side issue found in rollout review has been repaired and the affected task rerun; 4. the final reviewed outcome is either a pass or a policy-side failure; and 5. no unresolved construction- or infrastructure-side failure remains. Infrastructure retries do not count as semantic refinement. A confirmed construction repair increments the task’s refine-ment history. Tasks that exceed the allowed repair policy are excluded.
