## Abstract 

Alignment methods typically treat user behavior as a reliable proxy for underly-ing preferences. However, this assumption breaks down when users hold belief-dependent cognitive biases, risking irreversible “confirmation traps” and harmful feedback loops. In this paper, we formalize when an AI system can distinguish true user preferences from behavior shaped by cognitive bias. Modeling this interaction as a multi-armed bandit, we first prove a non-identifiability theorem: under passive observation, a static binary biased user produces action trajectory distributions strictly identical to an unbiased user in a transformed environment, forcing any action-only estimator to perform no better than random guessing. We then show that active preference elicitation breaks this observational equivalence. Specifically, we prove that an Observer applying a finite sequence of adversarial zero-reward interventions of length mδ makes the post-intervention action distributions arbitrar-ily separable. Our theoretical results suggest that robust alignment requires actively intervening to tell informed preferences from biases. 

## 1 Introduction 

AI systems that adapt to a user increasingly treat that user’s behavior as ground truth for underlying preferences. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) trains language models directly on human choice dynamics [1]. Scalable-oversight research aims to extend this role to settings where human supervisors cannot fully verify the target system [2, 3]. However, this practice rests on an assumption rarely tested: that a human agent’s actions faithfully reveal its internal preferences. Building upon prior work that highlights the general fragility of this proxy [4] and its vulnerability to feedback loops [5, 6, 7], we study the case where the user suffers from a systematic cognitive bias, updating honestly on distorted experiences. Confirmation bias is the primary instance of such distortion [8, 9, 10], with well-documented behavioral and neural manifestations in human reinforcement learning and self-assessment [11, 12]. An observer trying to infer hidden preferences from action channels alone is reading a signal corrupted by the very mental state it seeks to recover. Prior work infers preferences mainly from learning or exploring agents [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18], whereas demonstrator suboptimality is merely transient and fades with more observation (2). A cognitive bias acts as a permanent, belief-dependent filter through which the user’s posterior converges to a distorted belief. This raises a fundamental question: can an observer restricted purely to action trajectories ever distinguish a cognitively biased user from an unbiased one? 

We show that passive behavioral observation fails in two severe ways. First, bias is not a transient inconvenience. Under a belief-dependent cognitive kernel that attenuates negative feedback and anchors prior expectations (Definitions 3.1), Thompson Sampling [19, 20, 21, 22] plays a strictly suboptimal arm forever with positive probability, collapsing logarithmic regret into linear regret (Theorem 4.1). Passive behavioral traces create an observational equivalence between biased and unbiased agents, leaving protocols like RLHF blind to this failure mode. Instead, human-AI systems that learn from behavior should treat active elicitation as a first-class part of oversight design whenever the user may be a confirmatory reasoner. ## 2 Related Work 

Behavioral proxies for preference and learning from imperfect demonstrators. Alignment pipelines train reward models directly from human preference comparisons [1]. Scalable oversight techniques like sandwiching [2] and debate [3] rely on this same assumption that user choices reflect 2true preferences. When raters exhibit systemic biases, literature often treats these issues as data quality flaws that can be fixed through better auditing and data collection [4]. Similarly, work on learning rewards from imperfect demonstrators assumes that human suboptimality stems from temporary exploration or active learning that clears up with additional data [13, 14, 16, 15, 17, 18]. Closest to our question, [34] illustrates that a fixed, known irrationality model can help or hurdle reward inference. Instead, we ask the prior and fundamental question that whether the bias model is identifiable from behavior at all. 

Confirmation bias and sycophancy. Confirmation bias is well documented in human reasoning and sequential decision-making [8, 9, 35]. In human reinforcement learning, this bias appears as an asymmetric updating rate that favors positive feedback [11, 12, 36]. Standard alignment protocols aggravate this human bias because they treat the user as an omniscient oracle rather than a learner who may hold incorrect beliefs. To maximize immediate human approval, language models adopt sycophantic behaviors by mirroring the user’s initial assumptions [37, 38, 39]. This dynamic creates a self-reinforcing loop: the sycophantic model validates the user’s biased expectations, preventing disconfirming feedback and locking the user inside a confirmation trap. 

Bandit algorithms and distorted perceptions. Unbiased Thompson Sampling matches classical logarithmic regret lower bounds [40, 21, 20, 19, 22]. Deterministic baselines such as Upper Confi-dence Bound (UCB) achieve the same rate without maintaining a belief state to distort [41]. Existing analyses of Thompson Sampling under misspecified priors show only bounded regret degradation [42, 43]. Similarly, behavioral bandits with statically distorted value perception still achieve sublinear regret [44]. In contrast, our work proves an unbounded trap with positive probability caused by belief-dependent, self-reinforcing distortions. This distinction is particularly relevant because human decision-makers are often modeled as Bayesian bandit learners [45]. 

Non-identifiability, active intervention, and behavioral feedback loops. We cast passive non-identifiability causally: a biased user in one environment and an unbiased user in a rescaled one are observationally equivalent [24]. This partial identification [25] stems from a confound inside the agent’s perception rather than in the observer’s causal estimates [26]. Similar constraints exist in reward learning, where demonstrations and comparisons recover rewards only up to equivalence classes [46, 47].Active interventions resolve such confounds [30], as studied in experiment selection [31], causal bandits [32], and Bayesian experimental design [33]. Our closed-form horizon mδ

(Proposition 5.3) gives a fixed-confidence identification bound [48] for this hypothesis. Mechanically, our approach repurposes reward-poisoning techniques [27, 28, 29, 49] as diagnostic tools for active preference elicitation [50, 51, 52]. In contrast, relying solely on passive behavior leads to degenerate recommendation loops [5, 6, 7, 53].

## 4 Theoretical Analysis: Passive Limitations 

This section establishes the theoretical boundaries of inferring user preferences and bias under passive observation, demonstrating why standard monitoring fails without active intervention. First, we show that belief-dependent confirmation bias (Def. 3.1) forces Thompson Sampling into an “confirmation trap” (Theorem 4.1), degrading logarithmic regret to linear. Second, we prove that static channel biases (Def. 3.2) induce an observational equivalence (Theorem 4.3) when Aobs observes action histories hat alone, making passive identification impossible. While Theorem 4.3 establishes this impossibility for static channels, fully characterizing the asymptotic separability of dynamic kernels lies beyond the scope of this work. Figure 2 outlines the overall theoretical framework.

## 7 Discussion 

Our results isolate a structural failure mode for alignment from behavior: when actions are jointly shaped by an unknown environment and a distortion of experienced evidence, passive observations can not reveal which mechanism generated them. Within our model, active interventions can break this limitation and turn uninformative action traces into signal. We establish this under controlled assumptions, not a deployment-ready test of human cognition. 

Scope of the formal claims. The paper uses different bias abstractions for different questions. The confirmation-trap result concerns a belief-dependent kernel with persistent suppression of negative evidence, whereas exact passive non-identifiability is proved for a memoryless static channel. These abstractions expose complementary mechanisms, but they do not yet provide one unified characterization of confirmation bias. Human choices may instead reflect graded and time-varying bias, risk preferences, habits, strategic behavior, changing goals, or simple model misspecification. Our identification guarantees hold relative to the assumed model class. Future work should extend this framework to continuous and heterogeneous types, dynamic kernels or alternative policies. 

From separability to useful intervention. The computable horizon mδ is a conservative upper bound. While empirical separation requires far fewer rounds ( 16 –27 ), even this lower range is far too disruptive for real-world interactions, where forcing 16 consecutive failures would cause severe user frustration, loss of trust, or abandonment. Furthermore, identifying a bias does not imply correcting it or improving user welfare. Practical protocols must therefore adapt intervention strength to real-time uncertainty, stop as soon as information gain no longer justifies the user burden, and measure downstream welfare rather than separability alone. 

Empirical and human validity. Our simulations confirm our analytical bounds under set assump-tions, but they do not validate these behavioral dynamics in human users. When an LLM acts as the observer, it extracts signals to detect user bias with interventions, but the gap between a peak AUROC of 0.92 and a lower accuracy highlights a critical calibration bottleneck. Additionally, we show that LLMs have some limitations mirroring self-confirming bias. Validating distortion kernels and calibrating stopping rules across heterogeneous users require joint studies with behavioral scientists. 

Intervention design and ethics. While forcing zero rewards is analytically clean, it risks manipulat-ing user experience. Non-coercive protocols reduce forced failures, but they do not resolve core issues of consent, transparency, or false-positive harms. Fabricating outcomes should be strictly prohibited. Safe evaluations must rely on consented, low-stakes environments with verifiable counter-evidence. These frameworks must account for cases where user reflects a correct belief rather than a harmful cognitive bias, requiring ground-truth benchmarks. Finally, randomized controls and calibrated interventions are necessary to separate belief updates from user fatigue, annoyance, or distrust. 

Population information and future directions. The deeper open problem lies in populations. With many users sharing an unknown environment, batch observation may recover the environment while passive data may classify most users, reserving intervention for the ambiguous few. Indeed, with an unrestricted distribution of environments, a biased population can still remain observationally equivalent to an unbiased population under a transformed environment distribution. Even with those constraints, an adaptive policy that optimizes information gained per unit of user burden is a natural bridge from our worst-case construction to deployable elicitation. The broader lesson stands either way: behavioral agreement does not prove genuine preference, and no volumes of logs repairs a structural confound. Active elicitation can provide missing signal, but only if its calibration, human costs, and governance are treated as core scientific questions rather than implementation details. 9References
