# Who Should We Listen to More? Welfare-Aware Preference Acquisition for Pluralistic Alignment 

Ruomeng Ding ∗1, Tianwei Gao *1 , Lianrui Geng 1, Mohit Bansal 1, and Zhun Deng †1 1University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill 

Abstract 

Pluralistic alignment needs to accommodate diverse preferences under limited human feedback. Using AI simulators to label preference comparisons reduces annotation costs, but variation in simulation accuracy across individuals can leave some people’s preferences poorly represented in training. We study how to allocate human feedback across people to construct training data for pluralistic alignment, and how this allocation a ff ects the trained policy. To make this allocation explicit and controllable, we define a social welfare objective over individual supervision utilities that measure label quality. Each query’s marginal welfare gain depends on its expected supervision improvement, the recipient’s current utility, and application-designated priority. Welfare-Aware Preference Acquisition (WAPA) uses auxiliary reference models to estimate query gains and individual utilities, then selects queries by estimated marginal welfare gain while updating recipient utilities. We prove exact optimality of the acquisition algorithm for the estimated welfare objective under a fixed query budget and model predictions. Experiments on six Roleplay collections and four OpinionQA regions demonstrate improved supervision and controllable allocation tradeo ff s. Policy training on four Roleplay collections improves preference prediction for both seen and unseen individuals. When prioritizing a designated group and poorly represented individuals, acquiring human labels for 20% of comparisons recovers 75–86% of the average predictive utility gap between policies trained entirely on AI-generated labels and entirely on ground-truth labels. Our code is released at https://github.com/ZDCSlab/ Welfare-Aware-Preference-Acquisition. 

## 1 Introduction 

Aligning language models with human preferences often requires costly human feedback. Large language models provide preference labels at scale [Lee et al., 2024]. Here, they serve as AI simulators, using individual profiles to predict preferences over response pairs. Under a limited budget, human feedback is allocated to selected comparisons, while AI-generated labels provide supervision for the rest [Miranda et al., 2025]. For pluralistic alignment, which seeks to accommodate diverse preferences [Sorensen et al., 2024], di ff erences in simulation accuracy

A shared budget therefore raises the question: who should we listen to more when selecting feedback for pluralistic alignment? We study preference acquisition as training data collection for pluralistic alignment. Preference-based training uses such data to learn individual and group preferences [Singh et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026]. Allocating a limited human-feedback budget determines whose preferences receive more accurate supervision for downstream training. Existing acquisition methods select feedback to improve policy learning [Muldrew et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2026], while active elicitation supports preference inference and personalized responses [Shenfeld et al., 2025; Blair et al., 2025]. Selecting useful feedback does not by itself specify how supervision gains should be valued across recipients: allocations with similar average gains can benefit di ff erent individuals. We make the value of these gains to their recipients explicit in the acquisition objective and examine how the resulting allocation a ff ects downstream policy alignment. This raises two connected questions: ❶ how should acquisition balance supervision improvements across people , and ❷ do these allocation choices translate into better alignment with diverse preferences after policy training? 

Following Castricato et al. [2025], we call an individual represented by a user profile a persona .We distinguish two priorities in valuing an improvement in supervision for a given persona: (1) 

Current disadvantage in supervision quality . Among queries with similar expected supervision gains, acquisition should place greater weight on personas whose preferences are currently represented less accurately. This priority is dynamic: as a persona’s estimated supervision utility improves, its disadvantage decreases. The goal is to avoid leaving some personas poorly served even when average supervision quality is high [Chakraborty et al., 2024; Ramesh et al., 2024]. (2) Fixed application-designated importance . An application may assign higher, fixed weights to personas whose needs or preferences are especially relevant to its purpose, independent of how accurately their preferences are currently represented. For example, drawing on the preference setting of Mukherjee et al. [2026], an educational assistant designed for guided practice may place greater weight on users who prefer Socratic guidance over brief answers. These two priorities need not align: a persona that is especially important to the application may already be well represented, whereas a poorly represented persona may not belong to the application-priority group. We define each persona’s supervision utility as a measure of how accurately the training su-pervision represents their preferences. We aggregate these utilities through a social welfare objective [Zhong et al., 2024] that expresses the two allocation priorities. The marginal welfare gain of a query depends on its expected supervision improvement, the recipient’s current utility, and their application-designated priority. To our knowledge, we provide the first welfare-based formulation of fair human-feedback budget allocation for pluralistic alignment training data. Human preference labels are unavailable before acquisition, so query gains and persona utilities must be estimated. We introduce Welfare-Aware Preference Acquisition (WAPA), which uses auxiliary reference models to estimate both quantities from errors in AI-generated labels. At each step, WAPA selects the query with the largest estimated marginal welfare gain and updates the recipient’s estimated utility before the next selection. We prove that this greedy rule exactly maximizes the estimated welfare objective under a fixed query budget, with model predictions held fixed. ## 3 Method 

In this section, we introduce the Welfare-Aware Preference Acquisition (WAPA) framework. Our approach is guided by two principles: ❶ quantify each query’s welfare gain by estimating persona supervision utilities and social welfare without observing the true preference labels; and ❷ allocate the shared labeling budget to maximize estimated welfare. We first introduce our cross-model approach to utility and welfare estimation, then present our welfare-aware acquisition algorithm. Finally, our theoretical analysis examines how the welfare objective balances the two desiderata in query allocation and establishes the algorithm’s optimality for the estimated welfare objective.

## 4 Experiments 

We trace the e ff ects of welfare-aware acquisition from the queries selected to the policy trained on them, guided by three research questions (RQs). 7• RQ1: How e ff ective is WAPA at preference acquisition? We test how much prediction error each method removes with the same label budget, across welfare settings (Section 4.1). • RQ2: How do welfare parameters shape acquisition tradeo ff s? We examine who benefits as subgroup priority γ and sensitivity to supervision disadvantage ρ vary (Section 4.2). • RQ3: Does WAPA improve downstream alignment? We test whether the selected queries improve preference prediction after downstream alignment (DPO) (Section 4.3). 

Datasets and acquisition protocol. Roleplay provides six persona collections with synthetic pairwise preferences [Singh et al., 2026]; OpinionQA provides four regional subsets with 3–5-option questions [Santurkar et al., 2023]. Roleplay supports acquisition and DPO; OpinionQA evaluates acquisition only. Main-text Roleplay results cover Africa, Asia, North America, and Europe; the other collections appear in Appendix G.2. The base model is Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, with Qwen3.8-Max, GLM-5.2, and DeepSeek-V4-Flash as references. The main query budget covers 20% of all comparisons. A fixed, randomly selected 10% of personas form the Target group. We compare equal-weight mean welfare ( γ, ρ ) = (1 , 1) and target-priority, inequality-sensitive welfare (2 , −5), fixing ε = 0 .5. Acquisition is simulated o ffl ine: methods select a complete batch from frozen predictions before revealing dataset labels, which serve as ground truth. Only the Oracle accesses labels during selection (Appendices E.1–G.1 and F.3). 

Compared methods. Random samples uniformly; Entropy scores base-model uncertainty; Reference Disagreement scores variance across reference predictions. Active preference learning (APL; Muldrew et al., 2024) ranks implicit preference certainty. Bayesian active learning for preference modeling (BAL-PM; Melo et al., 2024) combines reference disagreement with prompt diversity. Entropy and Reference Disagreement apply welfare weighting to their own scores under the same settings as WAPA; APL and BAL-PM are welfare-agnostic. No queries retains initial predictions. Welfare-optimal Oracle uses all labels to maximize scalar welfare at the same query budget. Implementation details appear in Appendix F.3.

4.2 How do welfare parameters shape allocation and utility tradeo ff s at the acqui-sition stage? 

At a 20% budget, changing from (1 , 1) to (2 , −5) raises WAPA’s Target utility by 3.72–4.09% while reducing Mean by 0.11–0.20%; Tail also decreases in most datasets. We next vary one parameter at a time to separate these e ff ects, keeping Target membership fixed (Figure 3). 

Designated subgroup priority. At ρ = 1, raising γ from 1 to 2 improves Target utility by 9.01–10.06 percentage points across the four main datasets while reducing Mean and Tail. Higher subgroup priority directs more benefit toward Target members, who need not be initially worst-o ff . The selected query share confirms this shift: at ρ = 1, doubling γ concentrates over a third of the budget on the roughly 10% Target group. Improving this designated group can therefore leave less feedback for poorly predicted personas outside it. 

Sensitivity to current disadvantage in supervision quality. Changing ρ from 1 to −5 improves Tail by 0.08–0.29 percentage points at γ = 1. At γ = 2, lowering ρ from 1 to −5 raises Mean by 0.41–0.62 points and Tail by 1.16–1.54 points relative to (2 , 1), while reducing Target utility. Stronger sensitivity to supervision disadvantage moderates the costs of prioritizing Target. As queries raise Target members’ estimated utilities, concavity reduces the marginal welfare gain from further queries to them. Queries outside Target can then become more competitive. Target membership specifies whom to prioritize; γ sets the strength of that priority, while ρ controls sensitivity to current disadvantage in supervision quality. Their joint setting preserves a Target gain over (1 , 1) without the full Mean and Tail costs of subgroup priority alone. ## 5 Conclusion and Discussion 

We formulated preference acquisition for pluralistic alignment as the allocation of a shared human-feedback budget across people. Our welfare objective values supervision gains accord-ing to recipients’ current supervision utilities and application-designated priorities. WAPA estimates gains and utilities using reference models and selects queries by marginal welfare gain. Its greedy rule exactly optimizes the estimated objective under a fixed budget and fixed model predictions. This formulation makes the distribution of supervision improvements an explicit choice in training data collection. Our experiments show that these priorities shape acquisition outcomes, and that training on the resulting data improves preference prediction for seen and unseen personas. The findings connect decisions about whose feedback to acquire with how well a trained policy predicts diverse preferences. 

Future directions. Evaluating this connection requires benchmarks that follow feedback al-location through to policy training. We advocate developing such benchmarks with explicit acquisition priorities and measures of how benefits are distributed across people. Evalua-tion with represented users would complement preference prediction with their judgments of generated responses.

## A Related Work 

Table 3 summarizes the four research directions reviewed below. 

A.1 Active acquisition for preference modeling and policy alignment 

Human preference feedback supplies supervision for alignment, and active acquisition decides which feedback to obtain under a limited budget. Existing methods connect this selection to policy training. Active Preference Learning (APL) uses generation entropy and implicit preference certainty to select feedback for direct preference optimization (DPO) [Muldrew et al., 2024]; ActiveDPO uses gradients of the policy’s implicit reward to guide acquisition and alternates selection with DPO updates [Lin et al., 2026]. Other methods target preference-model estimation: Bayesian Active Learning for Preference Modeling (BAL-PM) combines epistemic uncertainty with prompt diversity [Melo et al., 2024], Active Reward Modeling uses Fisher information and cross-prompt comparisons [Shen et al., 2025], and Active Preference Optimization (APO) selects uncertain contexts with policy-error guarantees under preference-model assumptions [Das et al., 2026]. Active Multi Preference 17 Optimization (AMPO) selects response subsets for self-play preference optimization [Gupta et al., 2025]; Joint Preference Optimization broadens the preference object to instruction– response pairs [Bansal et al., 2025]. These methods improve the use of feedback for learning. We study how the supervision gains from a shared human-feedback budget should be distributed across personas for pluralistic alignment. 

A.2 Personalized and pluralistic alignment 

Pluralistic alignment encompasses perspective coverage, steerability, and representation of population preferences [Sorensen et al., 2024]. OpinionQA studies demographic opinions, PRISM links individual feedback to participant backgrounds, and the Community Alignment Dataset shows why diverse response candidates matter [Santurkar et al., 2023; Kirk et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026]. Methods use these forms of heterogeneity in di ff erent ways: Group Preference Optimization adapts to group preferences [Zhao et al., 2024], PAL learns personalized reward models [Chen et al., 2025], CultureSPA constructs data for cultural alignment [Xu et al., 2025], and FSPO uses few-shot preference optimization on synthetic data organized by user to train a person-alized model [Singh et al., 2026]. MaxMin-RLHF and Group Robust Preference Optimization incorporate worst-group priorities into policy training [Chakraborty et al., 2024; Ramesh et al., 2024]. A complementary line actively elicits individual preferences: AMPLe infers multidimensional preference weights from comparisons [Oh et al., 2025], PReF combines active queries with reward factorization and inference-time personalization [Shenfeld et al., 2025], GATE elicits task specifications through open-ended interaction [Li et al., 2025], and reflective verbal reward design uses dialogue and critiques to construct individualized reward models [Blair et al., 2025]. Variational Preference Learning (VPL) supports active latent-preference inference; its active policy-adaptation experiments use simulated control tasks, while its language experiments evaluate personalized reward modeling [Poddar et al., 2024]. Selection can also concern whose feedback to obtain: Annotator-Centric Active Learning (ACAL) selects annotators to capture diverse judgments in subjective NLP tasks [van der Meer et al., 2024], and Ding et al. [2026] jointly select respondents and questions using information gain and response propagation. Mahbub et al. [2026] query uncertain items for poorly represented personas, then use the feedback to constrain coherent labels for in-context steering. These are close precedents for selective elicitation and recipient-aware acquisition. Personalized and pluralistic alignment provide the goal our acquisition serves. Our focus is allocating a shared human-feedback budget across personas to construct supervision for policy alignment: which personas and comparisons should receive human feedback, given explicit welfare priorities over supervision quality? Our batch is selected before its labels are observed. This allocation constructs supervision for persona-conditioned policy training and complements welfare objectives applied during policy optimization. 18 A.3 Human–AI feedback allocation 

Hybrid preference routing (HyPER) directly studies which comparisons should receive human or model annotations [Miranda et al., 2025]. It predicts the performance of reward models trained on candidate mixtures and selects a routing configuration using those predictions. Its experiments evaluate reward modeling and best-of-N response selection; transfer to DPO or reinforcement-learning policy training is left open. Preference-Calibrated Active Learning allocates budget between ground-truth labels and judgments over AI outputs to improve statistical estimation [Dong et al., 2026]. In our setting, acquisition assigns a limited human-feedback budget to selected comparisons; the remaining comparisons receive AI supervision. The allocation determines how accurately the training supervision represents each persona’s preferences. We evaluate this distribution through social welfare, using reference-based loss estimates for both query gains and persona utilities. All comparisons contribute to alignment: acquired labels and unqueried AI proba-bilities jointly supervise DPO [Rafailov et al., 2023], allowing us to test whether acquisition improvements translate into better preference prediction by the aligned policy. 

A.4 Social welfare and fair acquisition 

Social choice and welfare-sensitive learning provide inspiration for explicitly valuing improve-ments across people [Conitzer et al., 2024; Cousins, 2021]. Zhong et al. [2024] learn party-specific rewards from o ffl ine comparisons and optimize a collective policy under Nash, Utilitar-ian, and Egalitarian welfare objectives. These welfare objectives specify how a policy should serve di ff erent parties. Our acquisition objective specifies how to allocate the human feedback used for alignment, evaluating welfare over persona-level supervision quality. Fair acquisition supplies related inspiration: minimax sampling allocates observations across groups [Shekhar et al., 2021], FAIR coordinates collaborative acquisition for mutual benefit [Xu et al., 2023], and fair active learning combines exploration with constrained classification [Camilleri et al., 2024]. Cai et al. [2022] already optimize data-collection budgets using group-specific learning curves and utilities over group performance. Our formulation evaluates persona-level preference supervision under human and AI labeling, with reference-based loss estimates providing both query gains and current utilities. Our guar-antee concerns exact optimization of the estimated acquisition objective under fixed predictions; our DPO experiments evaluate the resulting policy.

## B Limitations 

Evaluating pluralistic alignment in free responses. Our main policy evaluation measures preference prediction on fixed response pairs. Appendix J complements it with exploratory human rankings of generated responses on a selected sample. Raters assess suitability for supplied persona profiles; this does not directly measure satisfaction among the people whose preferences are modeled. Sample selection and di ff erences between human and LLM judge rankings further limit generalization. Broader evaluation should use representative samples and feedback from the represented users to assess preference fulfillment and the distribution of benefits across people. 20 Scope of the theoretical guarantees. WAPA’s exact optimality guarantee concerns the estimated acquisition welfare objective under fixed predictions. Theorem C.6 further relates supervision quality, training fit, and model complexity to prediction risk on fresh personas and prompts. This result assumes independently sampled personas and prompts, conditionally independent labels, acquisition that is independent of pool labels, and bounded empirical fitting error. It accounts for dependencies induced by shared personas and prompts, but does not establish that maximizing acquisition welfare maximizes downstream policy welfare. Extending these guarantees to iterative acquisition that adapts to observed labels and updated policies remains open.

## F Detailed Experimental Setup 

Unless otherwise specified, all random seeds are set to 42. Human and judge evaluations report bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs) as specified in Appendix J. 

F.1 Models and Acquisition Evidence 

The target model is Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. Collection records give the provider-reported reference-service versions as Qwen3.8-Max, GLM-5.2, and DeepSeek-V4-Flash. F.2 Reference-Service Workload 

Cost accounting. The query budget counts acquired labels. Reference inference, query allo-cation, DPO training, and evaluation are separate computational costs. Equal query budgets therefore compare label e ffi ciency and do not imply equal total cost: Entropy uses target pre-dictions, whereas WAPA additionally uses reference predictions. Allocation operates on frozen scores without further model calls. Reference token usage is reported below; training compute 33 is reported in Appendix F.5, and available evaluation and generation resource information in Appendix F.6. Genera-tion and implicit-scoring runtimes were not consistently recorded, so compute accounting is stage-specific. ## H Chat Templates 

The first two subsections give the templates for collecting preference probabilities for acquisition. Braced placeholders are filled with the individual’s profile, question, and candidate responses or survey options; labels are not included. Templates 1–6 retain the original wording, with whitespace adjusted for display. They request JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) outputs or use Extensible Markup Language (XML) style delimiters. The final subsection describes the shared 57 Roleplay training/generation context. Reference models return explicit probabilities, whereas the target model is scored using option-letter logits. 

H.1 Roleplay Preference Collection 

Both model roles receive the profile and response pair in the user message. Each pair is presented in its original and swapped A/B orders. Probabilities are mapped back to the original response orientation and averaged across the two presentations.
