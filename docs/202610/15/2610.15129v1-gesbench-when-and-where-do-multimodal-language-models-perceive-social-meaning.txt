## ABSTRACT 

Human social perception is incremental: we infer intent before an utterance is complete, revise our interpretation as new cues arrive, and attend to specific facial, gestural, or prosodic signals that shape social meaning. Current multi-modal LLM benchmarks largely evaluate final-answer understanding over com-plete episodes, rewarding post-hoc recognition of social meaning rather than the unfolding perception required for real-time interaction. Drawing on interaction-ally embedded Gestalt principles of multimodal human communication, we intro-duce G ES BENCH : a human-grounded benchmark for cue-level social perception in multimodal LLMs. GES BENCH uses cumulative sub-utterance clips to mea-sure when models form stable interpretations of speaker intention and social af-fordance, and human gaze-guided corruption to evaluate whether regions attended by humans are functionally important for model judgments. Built from more than 

6000 utterance-level clips from scripted and unscripted conversational datasets, GES BENCH includes human annotations of unfolding judgments and gaze on a representative subset. Across open and closed multimodal models, we find a clear human-model gap: humans form predictive understandings early and revise more often, while models often hedge or repeat across different clips. G ES BENCH shifts social evaluation from whether models eventually recognize meaning to when and where that meaning is formed. Our codes are publicly available at https: //anonymous.4open.science/status/GesBench-share-212C .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Multimodal language models are increasingly evaluated on their ability to understand social mean-ing: recognizing intentions, emotions, conversational norms, or appropriate responses. Yet, most benchmarks test this ability after the model has observed a complete episode. This evaluation set-ting rewards post-hoc recognition of social meaning, but does not test a capability that is central to real-time interaction: perceiving meaning as the multimodal scene unfolds. Human social perception is not delayed until the end of an utterance (Schlangen & Skantze, 2011). We often form provisional interpretations from partial words (Grosjean, 1980), facial expressions, gaze shifts, gestures, pauses, and prosody, and revise these interpretations as new cues arrive. For example, when a friend responds to a vacation suggestion with “Well, actually I...”, we may already anticipate disagreement, especially if this fragment is accompanied by a furrowed eye brows. If the utterance continues as “...I was thinking of the exact same place!”, and the expression changes into raised eye brows and a smile, the interpretation is rapidly revised. The social meaning of the utterance is thus not simply recovered from the complete signal but constructed, tested, and updated through sub-utterance cues (SUC). This view is closely aligned with interactionally embedded Gestalt principles of multimodal human communication of Trujillo & Holler (2023): people bind partial multimodal signals into coherent utterance-level interpretations, while also predicting the speaker’s intended social action and the response opportunities available to the listener. For embodied agents and multimodal LLMs that are expected to participate in face-to-face interac-tions, this distinction matters (Fung et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b). An agent that can answer correctly after seeing a complete video may still fail to support fluent interaction if it cannot form useful provisional interpretations, attend to communicatively relevant cues (Qi et al., 2025; Upad-hyay et al., 2026), or update its beliefs smoothly as the utterance progresses. Existing multimodal and social benchmarks evaluate many important abilities, including video understanding (Zhang et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2024), social reasoning (Mathur et al., 2025), emotion recognition (Lian et al., 2024), and social acting (Wu et al., 2025). However, they are less suited to measuring the unfolding process by which social meaning is formed from fine-grained multimodal cues. We introduce G ES BENCH , a human-grounded benchmark for evaluating cue-level social percep-tion in MLLMs. GES BENCH operationalizes interactionally embedded Gestalt perception along two complementary axes. First, to measure temporal unfolding, we decompose each utterance into cumulative clips and ask both humans and models to infer the speaker’s intended social action and the listener’s possible response actions. This allows us to measure when are perceptions formulated and how they update through the process. Second, to measure spatial grounding, we collect human eye gaze while annotators interpret utterances, construct human-attended cue profiles, and corrupt relative regions to test whether those cues are functionally important for models. Our contributions are three-fold: (1) We formulate cue-level social perception as an evaluation prob-lem for MLLMs, shifting the focus from final-answer recognition over complete videos to the un-folding process by which social meaning is formed. (2) We propose a human-grounded spatio-temporal evaluation protocol: cumulative sub-utterance clips measure how interpretations update, while gaze-guided corruption tests whether perturbing human-attended regions changes model judg-ments. (3) We evaluate open and closed source MLLMs and identify a human model gap in unfold-ing social perception: humans make earlier commitment in predicting, revise more often while models tend to remain uncertain with little information and make fewer revisions. Together, these results show that current multimodal LLMs can often recover social meaning after observing an utterance, but still differ from humans in how that meaning is formed. G ES BENCH 

provides a benchmark for studying this gap and for developing models that are more predictive, interactive, and attentive to the fine-grained cues that structure real-time communication. 2Preprint. 

## 2 RELATED WORKS 

MLLM benchmarks. An abundance of benchmarks have been proposed to measure multi-modal perception of MLLMs. Liu et al. (2024); Meng et al. (2024) proposes comprehensive image under-standing evaluations for MLLMs, including general perception and reasoning tasks. There are also benchmarks for specializing domains, for example on object hallucination (Li et al., 2023), identi-fying truthful statements (Kang et al., 2025), and moral reasoning (Jiao et al., 2025). Specifically, for video understanding, Fu et al. (2025); Li et al. (2024) provide general evaluation frameworks regarding videos from film clips to everyday recordings. Other benchmarks focus on social abilities of MLLMs. Zhang et al. (2025a) benchmarks MLLMs in perceiving multi-modal communication signals with a distribution of social tasks. Strachan et al. (2024) has tested the theory of mind (ToM) in MLLMs by testing their abilities in interpreting the other party’s minds through tasks such as identifying indirect requests. Mathur et al. (2025) benchmarks MLLMs in understanding long videos and through correct reasoning traces, but it does not explicitly address the temporal updating characteristic. For multi-agent tasks, Zhou et al. (2023); Wu et al. (2025) evaluates the performances of MLLM-powered agents under certain social interaction settings and against various social targets. 

Gestalt evaluations in AI models. Attempts have been made to study gestalt principles on both traditional machine learning tasks like computer vision as well as modern MLLMs. Sha et al. (2016) has studied classification problems with regards to the gestalt principles in human vision to evaluate whether the models are recognizing holistic objects. Kim et al. (2021) also points out that some computer vision models are exhibiting gestalt principles in terms of closure recognition. Regarding MLLMs, Dayanandan et al. (2024) has leveraged the dual thinking framework (Kahneman, 2011) to help improve MLLMs’ performances when facing adversarial data. However, these benchmarks either focus on low-level Gestalt principles, or do not specifically address Gestalt perception in social interaction. G ES BENCH , to our knowledge, is the first benchmark built with real social data and designed to incorporate gestalt features in model and human perceptions. 

Spatial grounding and visual cue evaluation in models. Prior work studies whether vision-language models ground answers in appropriate image regions using heatmaps (Selvaraju et al., 2016; Christian et al., 2026), attention analyses (Abnar & Zuidema, 2020), or region-level ground-ing objectives (Zhang et al., 2025c; Chen et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024; Qi et al., 2025). These methods provide useful diagnostics, but they are typically post-hoc or centered on static image un-derstanding rather than the unfolding perception of social meaning in video. Recently more works have been proposed to study whether aligning with human eye gaze help model perception (Peng et al., 2025; Pani & Yang, 2025). G ES BENCH instead uses human gaze collected during utterance interpretation to construct human-attended profiles, and evaluates whether corrupting these regions changes model judgments more than corrupting matched comparison regions. 

## 3 THE GES BENCH BENCHMARK 

GES BENCH combines scripted and unscripted conversational data to cover both clearly bounded utterances and natural dyadic interaction. Scripted clips are from MELD (Poria et al., 2019) and MIntRec2 (Zhang et al., 2024) while Seamless Interaction (Agrawal et al., 2025) provides unscripted face-to-face conversations. Our benchmark contains more than 6000 utterance-level clips, with hu-man text and gaze annotations collected on representative subsets with around 600 videos. 3.2 TEMPORAL PIPELINE 

Inspired from approaches which provide models with incremental language (Madureira &Schlangen, 2020; Kahardipraja et al., 2021) we create cumulative versions of each utterance video. Each cumulative clip contains the signal observed from the start of the utterance up to a given point and are given to the models incrementally context. Concretely, we start with a raw video V of single utterance. To create cumulative short clips, we first cut it into short clips of equal length l:

C1, C 2 . . . , C n and let Concat [C1, C 2, . . . , C n] to be their sequential concatenation. We define the set of Cumulative Video Clips (CVC) with respect to V , by concatenating the clips cumulatively: 

Dk = Concat [C1, . . . , C k], k = 1 , 2, . . . , n (1) By design, each Dk has length kl (we take l = 0 .5 s in practice). The CVC set is then given to both human annotators and the models. We obtain an answer for each video and for both Q1 and Q2.We denote human response for a video Dk under question Q to be HQ(Dk), and model response to be MQ(Dk). In the temporal benchmarking pipeline, this would yield the human response set 

{HQ(Dk)}nk=1 and model response set {MQ(Dk)}nk=1 , where Q ∈ { Q1, Q 2}.To obtain the human answers for CVC, we employ a crowdsourcing approach. Paid annotators are recruited on Prolific 1 platform, and the annotation is performed with Covfee (Quiros et al., 2022). We ask them to speak out the answers, which are then transcribed using WhisperX (Bain et al., 2023). The answers are then extracted per question. A model reply answers both questions in one go and we split it into Q1 and Q2 with an LLM instruction to extract the answers verbatim; Appendix C.1 to C.2 shows that our conclusions do not depend on this step. Annotators already answer each question in its own field. Sometimes an annotator says “nothing has changed” or similar even when we prompted them to provide reasons. To mitigate this, we carry their previous answer forward when this happens. Appendix A.2 and B details the annotation setup, model prompts, and postprocessing.

In the spatial domain, interactionally embedded Gestalt perception is reflected in how perceivers bind communicatively relevant visual signals while filtering out incidental ones. Human viewers do not attend uniformly across a scene; they often orient toward regions that afford social interpretation, such as faces, gaze direction, hands, body orientation, and visible articulators. This motivates us to measure whether certain regions attended by humans are important to model perception or not. To obtain a human-grounded cue profile for a given clip, we collect eye gaze while annotators watch complete utterance videos and answer the two benchmark questions. For each video V , annotators wear eye-tracking glasses while viewing. The recorded gaze points are mapped back to the video, producing a per-frame profile of human attention(Figure 3). On top of this, we construct a series of corrupted videos. For a displacement x and a radius r, let eDn(x, r ) be the video obtained from 

V by drawing on every frame a circular mask of radius r times the shorter edge of the video. Its center is determined by x: x = 0 means centered at human gaze point, and non-zero means shifting the disc by an offset of xw horizontally and xh from the gaze center: w, h means width and height of the video respectively. This way, we obtain a grid of varying (x, r )s and a control of untouched video with r = 0 . In practice we choose x ∈ { 0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5} and r ∈ { 0.1, 0.2, 0.3}. The corrupted videos are then given to the models with the same prompts, and the answers are extracted as described in B.2. 3.4 METRICS

Revision Types (RT). Gestalt perception also includes actively revising one’s own answers. A di-rect semantic comparison, however, is insufficient to tell the differences, since modifications such as adding a clause, restating a reading, and completely throwing out the previous reading can change an answer similarly in embedding space. To this end, also inspired from the Similarity-Num Steps mea-sure in Social Genome (Mathur et al., 2025), we consider the natural-logic relations (MacCartney & Manning, 2009) with the entailment between adjacent clips. Terminological caution. We intentionally avoid referring to GMS as saliency measures. This distinction is important because the term “saliency” has been used inconsistently across the literature for heatmaps, salient maps, etc. (Arrieta et al., 2020, Sec. 5.3). Just as Raman et al. (2023) argued in their work about information saliency, perturbation-based methods can create semantically invalid inputs (Adebayo et al., 2018; Lapuschkin et al., 2019) (Molnar, 2020, Sec. 9.5.3.3), making it unclear whether changes in model output reflect the importance of the removed cue or artifacts introduced by the intervention. Our analysis is designed with these limitations in mind. We build an empirical profile of where human observers look while interpreting multimodal utterances; it is not a ground-truth map of perceptual or causal significance. Likewise, GMS measure how much a model’s open-ended judgment changes when human-attended or comparison regions are corrupted. They therefore quantify intervention-based corruption sensitivity, not preattentive or causal necessity. A low GMS indicates that the model is sensitive to corrupting human-attended regions; it should not be interpreted as showing that those regions are intrinsically salient for the model.

Humans revise perceptions more often. Classification of RT also separates models and humans as shown in figure 5. Annotators replace their reading (shift/contradict) around 55% of the time on both questions, and copy their previous answer very sparingly. The closest model to human is Gemini3.6-flash at around 45% for both questions, still lower than humans by a gap at 10% level. On the other hand, models other than the two Gemini ones all show a much more repetition manner over adjacent responses: their pairs are often dominated instead by copy , especially for Ming-Lite-Omni which repeats itself at around 85% and 90% for Q1 and Q2, and also includes Qwen2.5-7B/3B and Gemma. In cases where they do move, they elaborate or restate more often: the previous reading is retained and new words are appended or rephrased around it, so the answer moves while the hypothesis is never at risk. In the Appendix C.3 to C.5 we provide more results in support for our conclusion. We note that frequent revision and early anticipation are not in tension. Anticipation is a rank that identifies which utterance an early reading is about, whereas a contradiction concerns what is claimed about it: a reading can keep its scene and still reverse its claim. ## 5 LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSION 

Limitations. GES BENCH is limited to short, face-to-face utterances with clearly bounded speak-ers, drawn from a mixture of scripted and unscripted datasets. Broader settings such as longer conversations, multi-party interactions, or noisier environments, may require additional annotation protocols. Human annotations are collected on a representative subset rather than the full bench-mark. Our metrics use sentence-embedding similarity as a scalable proxy for semantic agreement, which may miss subtle pragmatic distinctions between open-ended responses. Finally, human gaze based corruption should be interpreted as a human-grounded corruption-sensitivity test, not as a causal account of human perception or as a saliency estimator. 

Conclusion. We introduced G ES BENCH , a human-grounded benchmark for evaluating how multi-modal LLMs form social interpretations as utterances unfold. Its temporal pipeline uses cumulative sub-utterance clips to measure whether models could make early predictive perceptions in alignment with human understanding, while its spatial pipeline uses human gaze to test model sensitivity to human-attended visual cues. Across several datasets, we find a consistent human-model gap: hu-mans make more specific, and more aligning perceptions with other humans at early stages when they see the video. They also revise more often without being sparing with shifting or contradict-ing their previous answers. Finally, models also vary significantly on whether their perception is 9Preprint. influenced by human gaze patterns. These results suggest that final-answer accuracy is insufficient for evaluating socially interactive agents; models must also form useful provisional interpretations, revise them incrementally, and ground the judgments in communicatively meaningful cues.

There were informed of our measures to store, process, and use their recorded data. No dangerous content or measures appear or is exposed in any of the annotation tasks.

# Appendices 

## A DATA CURATION AND DETAILS 

A.1 DATASET SELECTION RATIONALE 

We selected source datasets according to four criteria. First, the data should contain face-to-face con-versational utterances in which social meaning is expressed through multimodal cues such as speech, facial expression, gaze, etc. Second, utterance boundaries should be sufficiently clear to support cu-mulative sub-utterance clipping. Third, the data should include short-to-medium utterances, since very long videos make dense human annotation and eye-tracking collection less practical. Fourth, the benchmark should include both controlled and naturalistic interaction settings. MELD (Poria et al., 2019) (GPL3.0 license) and MIntRec2 (Zhang et al., 2024) (CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0 license) provide scripted utterances from television dialogue. Although scripted, these clips retain many perceptual demands of everyday social understanding: viewers must integrate lexical, prosodic, facial, gestural, interpersonal, and turn-taking cues to infer speaker intention and possible responses. Prior work also suggests that television dialogue shares important linguistic properties with spontaneous conversation (Quaglio, 2008; Bednarek, 2018), while offering cleaner utterance boundaries and greater accessibility for annotation. Also, simulated stimuli has been used for study-ing human responses in social interactions in neuro-science (Redcay & Moraczewski, 2020). This aligns with our goal, as gestalt principles are also highly based on findings from neuro-science side (Koffka, 2013; Shamma et al., 2011). We deem that the task of social signal perception is of the similar end for what simulated signals could afford. In fact, these controlled stimuli are fre-quently used for simulated narratives (Tikka et al., 2023; Jääskeläinen et al., 2021) and there are also arguments that they offer good balance by having controlled scenarios as well as decent level of ecological validity (Fan et al., 2021). Seamless Interaction (Agrawal et al., 2025)(CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0 license) also complements these sources with unscripted dyadic conversations, providing more natural face-to-face interaction. We therefore combine scripted and unscripted sources to balance cue diversity, annotation feasibility, and ecological validity. We do not include in-the-wild activity-video datasets as primary sources because many clips contain weakly bounded utterances, unclear speaker roles, fewer face-to-face exchanges, or audio/visual conditions that make sub-utterance annotation difficult (Grauman et al., 2022; Rossetto et al., 2025). Future versions of G ES BENCH could extend the protocol to longer, noisier, multi-party, and more culturally diverse interactions. A.2 ANNOTATION SETUP

Human answer processing Human answers are naturally separated in the two fields so they don’t experience this extraction process. With the transcribed human text answer for both questions, we employ a carry-forward operation. Sometimes humans say “no, nothing has changed” or similar words when their perception did not change, and in this case it can be misleading as the pure lin-guistic meaning is different from the true perception. We detect this pattern and carry their previous answers forward to replace the explicit indication that their perception did not change in such cases. We argue, however, that this operation does not significantly impact our methodologies. For Spec 

and Ant , the answers are compared to either the same perceiver’s own ones at different stages, or another human annotator’s final answer. This also makes RT a conservative comparison instead of an inflated one: carry-forward is actually making copy patterns for humans, and humans are still having larger proportion of shift/contradict and very few copies even after it. B.3 GAZE MANIPULATION DETAILS 

Every video in S has gaze from 3 annotators, recorded with Pupil Core. Samples with confidence below 0.6 or that fall outside the video area are dropped. For each frame of a clip, each annotator’s gaze position is the per-axis median of their samples within ±0.1 s of the frame time; if there are none, their nearest sample within 0.5 s is used. The frame’s gaze point is the medoid of the annota-tors’ positions, i.e. the one with the smallest summed distance to the others, so it is always a point an annotator actually looked at. Frames on which no annotator has a position are not masked. 23 Preprint. The disc has radius r · min( w, h ). For x > 0, the offset direction is fixed per clip: we count which quadrant the gaze point falls in over all frames and move the disc towards the opposite corner (gaze mostly top-right is shifted to the bottom-left, and so on), by xw horizontally and xh vertically. If the shifted center stays inside the frame, a disc that crosses the frame edge is cut off there. If it would leave the frame along one axis, the shift along that axis is reversed, placing the disc on the other side of the gaze point.

∆ is what the perceiver’s updates added over repeating its first answer. A perceiver that copies forward scores exactly 0, and ∆ > 0 means later readings moved towards what people perceived. We do this experimnet on T2 in alignment with the Ant measure. Figure 15 shows that annotators gain around +0 .09 on Q1 and +0 .044 on Q2, so their revisions are productive rather than noise. Ming-Lite-Omni, Qwen2.5-Omni-7B/3B gain at most 0.021 on either question, with intervals at or near zero: their whole cumulative sequence is worth little more than its first answer. The Gemini models gain more than annotators (above +0 .15 on Q1, around +0 .05 and +0 .08 for Q2). This does not mean they update better than people: ∆ is measured from each perceiver’s own starting point, and annotators often start closer to the target (Figure 4), leaving less headroom.
