# Multi-Agent Learning of Reactive Strategies in Iterated Games 

James P. Wolfe 1, Joshua B. Plotkin 2, ∗

ABSTRACT Learning agents, whether humans or algorithms, adapt to one another rather than to a fixed environment. Selfish learning need not produce a good outcome for the group, especially when learners face a social dilemma. The iterated prisoner’s dilemma (IPD) is the classical example, but it is usually studied in populations of agents who imitate successful strategies. Here, we instead study selfish gradient ascent learners on the two-dimensional space of reactive strategies . A reactive strategy is described by two continuous traits: reciprocity, the chance of cooperating after the co-player cooperates, and forgiveness, the chance of cooperating after the co-player defects. Each learner updates its strategy in the direction that most rapidly improves its payoff against all other learners, by an amount governed by its 

learning rate . We show that heterogeneity in learning rates can qualitatively change the long-term outcome: a population with uniform learning rates converges to always-defect (ALLD), whereas a population with variable learning rates approaches generous-tit-for-tat (GTFT), which produces nearly optimal collective payoffs. In simulations, every population passes through a transient state of minimal forgiveness, in which the distribution of reciprocity determines the outcome. Variable learning rates promote cooperation because slow learners preserve high reciprocity until the population reaches this state. We prove sufficient conditions on the distribution of reciprocity for convergence to ALLD or to tit-for-tat (TFT). Variable learning rates also allow cooperation to recover from perturbation, which is provably impossible under uniform learning. These results suggest that purely selfish and myopic learning can produce persistent cooperation provided the population has intrinsic variability in learning rates. 

# I Introduction 

Cooperation is costly to the individual who provides it, and yet it underlies social life at every scale of bi-ological organization. In a single encounter between self-interested players, the prisoner’s dilemma admits only one Nash equilibrium, mutual defection, and co-operation cannot persist in a well-mixed population. Repeated interaction changes this. When the same play-ers meet many times, each can condition its behavior on the history of play, so that the prospect of future re-ward or retaliation, the “shadow of the future,” makes reciprocity possible [1, 2]. Reciprocal strategies such as tit-for-tat and generous tit-for-tat reward cooperation and forgive or punish defection, and they can stabi-lize cooperation that would be unstable in a one-shot game [3–5]. The iterated prisoner’s dilemma (IPD) is the canonical framework for studying how reciprocity allows cooperation among self-interested individuals to arise and persist [2, 6]. The same repeated structure that makes reciprocity possible also makes the IPD strategically rich. A strat-egy may condition each move on the entire history of play, so the space of possible strategies is enormous. The Folk Theorem of repeated game theory makes the size of the resulting equilibrium set precise: once the horizon is long enough, nearly any individually rational payoff can be supported at a Nash equilibrium, and the set of equilibria expands as the game lengthens [7–9]. This multiplicity is what makes cooperation possible, but it also leaves the outcome undetermined, since equi-librium analysis alone cannot say which of the many equilibria a population will reach. A strategy space of this size is a challenge for any learning rule, and for this reason the IPD has become a natural testbed for multi-agent learning [10, 11]. At the same time, the learning rules studied in this setting may capture some-thing about how humans and other organisms adjust their behavior, not only how artificial agents might be designed. Two research traditions address strategic change in this game. Evolutionary game theory models popula-tions whose strategies spread by selection or imitation, whereas the theory of learning in games models in-dividuals who adjust their own strategies in response to payoffs [12]. Here we study a population of selfish learners who update reactive IPD strategies by gradi-ent ascent on their payoffs against the population as a whole. We show that a single, naturalistic form of indi-vidual variation, namely heterogeneity in learning rates, can determine whether such a population converges to unconditional defection or to stable and nearly socially optimal cooperation. Evolutionary game theory provides a natural point of comparison for our study [13]. In that framework, strategies change by payoff-biased imitation or differ-1arXiv:2610.15054v1 [physics.soc-ph] 10 Oct 2026 ential reproduction rather than by gradient ascent [14, 15], and the aim is not to design effective algorithms for multi-agent problems but to model how strategies spread and are inherited among humans or other or-ganisms. The IPD, in particular, is used to model the evolution of altruistic behavior. Much of this research considers a discrete set of strategies, most notably evo-lutionary versions of Axelrod’s tournament [2], but sev-eral studies treat continuous strategy spaces. Nowak & Sigmund [4] studied the space of reactive memory-one strategies, in which a player’s policy depends on the co-player’s previous move. In their simulations of payoff-biased imitation, populations usually evolve to-ward unconditional defection; occasionally, the recip-rocal strategy tit-for-tat permits the rise of the more forgiving strategy generous tit-for-tat, which resists in-vasion by defectors. Imhof & Nowak [16] extended this analysis by adding mutation. They found the same role for tit-for-tat, but with mutation the population does not remain at generous tit-for-tat: it cycles between co-operative and uncooperative phases indefinitely. We instead consider a gradient-ascent learning pro-cess in a population of agents engaged in pairwise iter-ated games. Each individual plays the rest of the popu-lation and updates its strategy locally, in the direction that most increases its payoff. Gradient ascent assumes that players have enough cognition to compute their gradient, but that they are myopic, making only local changes to their strategies. Gradient ascent therefore sits between two extremes: classical economic analy-sis, which assumes perfect rationality and immediate convergence to a Nash equilibrium, and evolutionary game theory, which assumes payoff-biased imitation with no cognition at all. Neither extreme predicts an outcome here. The Folk Theorem [9] guarantees a con-tinuum of Nash equilibria for infinitely repeated games but gives no guidance as to which one is chosen, and payoff-biased imitation can cycle indefinitely without settling on any stable equilibrium [6, 16, 17]. Prior work on learning dynamics in repeated games falls into two broad categories: (i) theoretical analysis of convergence for RL or gradient dynamics, often in two-player, two-action games; and (ii) empirical studies evaluating a wider range of learning algorithms across broader classes of games. Empirical studies of this sec-ond kind are more relevant to us. Several empirical studies show that learning agents, including but not limited to those using gradient ascent, frequently fail to solve the social dilemma of the IPD, tending to reach a non-cooperative resolution [10, 11, 18, 19]. These studies, however, often do not examine the dynamics of larger strategy spaces, considering only the one-dimensional “probability of cooperating.” In this one-dimensional case, theoretical studies show that under certain gra-dient ascent algorithms the strategies of both players converge on “always defect,” the only Nash equilibrium, or else their average payoffs converge on the payoffs at that equilibrium [20, 21]. One study avoids the failure of gradient ascent in the IPD by allowing agents to in-clude, with some weighting, not just their own payoff but also the average payoff of the group when calculat-ing gradients [22]. In a larger strategy space, Foerster et al. [23] demonstrated the difficulty of reaching cooper-ation without foresight. Studies of learning in the IPD also rarely consider the dynamics of many agents updat-ing their strategies simultaneously. McAvoy et al. [24] show that while two agents updating their strategies against one another by gradient ascent typically fail to achieve socially optimal cooperation, a large popula-tion of learners well-mixed in their interactions, who encounter any one individual rarely, can succeed. But in that model the interactions, and updates, are random and occur one at a time. Like Imhof & Nowak, we study a large population on the space of reactive memory-one strategies, but our agents update by gradient ascent rather than imitation. As in the evolutionary game theory literature, our aim is not to design a more effective algorithm for multi-agent learning but to model how individuals adjust their strategies under a different set of assumptions. And in contrast to McAvoy et al. [24], where agents meet one at a time, we study learning that occurs si-multaneously across the population, so that each agent’s update reflects the strategies of all others at once. We are most interested in how individual variation in one parameter can affect population-level outcomes. There is reason to believe individual variation in mi-croparameters might produce globally different out-comes, even though agents’ updating rules are other-wise identical. Barfuss & Meylahn [25] show that indi-vidual stochastic fluctuations among agents undergoing reinforcement learning on the space of memory-one strategies in the IPD increased overall cooperation lev-els. Meng et al. [26] show that heterogeneous networks, when coupled with heterogeneous strategy updating rates, are more conducive to cooperation. We endow our agents with minimal knowledge: each agent knows only which direction of strategic change most rapidly im-proves its payoff. Beyond that direction, each agent has a single intrinsic parameter, its learning rate, that gov-erns how strongly it acts on that information. We show that variation in the learning rate across the population, with no other source of noise or foresight, produces a population of seemingly stable cooperation where otherwise there would not be. 2II Model 

A The iterated prisoner’s dilemma with reac-tive strategies 

We consider a simplified iterated prisoner’s dilemma (IPD) where, in each round, a pair of agents simulta-neously choose whether to cooperate, paying a cost c

to confer a benefit b > c to the other agent, or to de-fect, paying no cost and conferring no benefit [2, 27, 28]. Strategies for the infinitely-repeated game can be arbi-trarily complex, prescribing each round what action to take conditioned on the history of all prior rounds. We focus on a class of so-called “reactive,” memory-one strategies [3], which are parameterized by two probabil-ities, (p, q) ∈ [0, 1 ]2, that specify the chance a player will cooperate each round in response to the action taken by the co-player in the preceding round. The parame-ter p is the probability a player cooperates given their opponent cooperated in the previous round, i.e. their 

reciprocity . The parameter q is the probability a player cooperates given their opponent defected in the previ-ous round, i.e. their forgiveness . The space of reactive strategies includes many well-known examples such as always cooperate (1, 1 ), always defect (0, 0 ), tit-for-tat (1, 0), and generous tit-for-tat (1, 1 − δ), which always reciprocates cooperation but forgives a defection with probability 1 − δ [4]. To ensure that the infinitely repeated game has a unique stationary distribution, and that long-term pay-offs to both players do not therefore depend on the first round of play, we restrict ourselves to a compact subset of reactive strategies (p, q) ∈ [ε, 1 − ε]2, for small 0 < ε < 1/2. This strategy space can also be seen as reactive strategies in the context of a small rate of implementation error, ε. The long-run average payoff per round to a strategy x1 = ( p1, q1) playing against 

x2 = ( p2, q2) is then given by

The degree of reciprocity measures how strongly a player conditions its behavior on the co-player’s last move, and it separates conditional from unconditional cooperation: tit-for-tat is maximally re-ciprocal, with r = 1, whereas always-cooperate and always-defect share r = 0, cooperating with the same probability whatever the co-player did.

D Evolution of strategic density for infinite-population limit 

In the limit of large population size N → ∞ we track the population’s strategies not as individual agents, but as a generalized distribution (possibly a continuous density) 

Ft(x) over the strategy space [ε, 1 − ε]2 at time t. Each strategy x then moves along the average, over the whole population, of its payoff gradients against the strategies it plays. Even though the population’s distribution may be continuous, we still consider discrete learning steps; taking a limit of small steps and rescaling time produces a continuous-time PDE, which we state in Sec. S1.2 of the Supplemental Material. We also allow a distribution 

Gt(η) of learning rates, independent of x at t = 0, and correlated with x for t > 0, since an agent’s learning rate determines how far it moves at each step. This continu-ous process is the alternative “Eulerian” formulation of the individual-based “Lagrangian” formulation above. To simulate this process, which admits continuous density functions, we approximate distributions with a fixed lattice over [ε, 1 − ε]2, each lattice point with an associated frequency (akin to a “histogram approxima-tion” of a continuous distribution). Then, when mass moves between points it is redistributed via bilinear in-terpolation. These are standard methods for numerical solutions to PDEs, adapted to a discrete-time process. Details on the formulation and implementation of this continuous “Eulerian” model can be found in Sec. S1 of the Supplemental Material.

# III Results 

Individual differences in learning rates can qualitatively alter the course of strategic change in a population un-dergoing gradient ascent (Fig. 2). When agents all share the same learning rate ( ηi ≡ η for all i), the population converges to unconditional defection, eventually leav-ing every agent with payoff near zero. Convergence to defection is the outcome one would expect of selfish gradient learners in a social dilemma. But when agents vary in their learning rates, the population instead con-verges to a generous tit-for-tat strategy (GTFT), which sustains a high rate of cooperation and nearly socially optimal payoffs (Fig. S1 of the Supplemental Material). To understand how variation in learning rates pro-duces these divergent outcomes, we consider a pop-ulation of agents with strategies xi = ( pi, qi) initially drawn independently and uniformly from the space of reactive strategies [ε, 1 − ε]2. B Transient Unforgiving State 

The transient unforgiving state, in which all agents i lie along the unforgiving line qi = ε, is reliably observed in both uniform and variable learning rate simulations. Empirical distributions of p values upon entering the transient unforgiving state are shown in Fig. S3 of the Supplemental Material, top row. p, integral to ultimately stimulating cooperation. The long-term fate of the population, either ALLD or GTFT, depends on the distribution of p values (reci-procity) when the population first enters the transient unforgiving state. In fact, in simulations that enter the transient unforgiving state under variable learning rates, if we endow the agents with sufficiently low uniform learning rates from this point in time onward, the popu-lation still converges to GTFT. Theorem 1. Let μ be the distribution of reciprocity p when all agents have q = ε. If U (μ) < 0, the population converges to ALLD in finitely many learning steps. If P (μ) > Q(μ)

and the dynamics are restricted to q = ε, the population converges to TFT in finitely many learning steps. 

A proof of the theorem can be found in Sec. S3 of the Supplemental Material. The intuition for convergence to TFT is that an agent at ALLD, the least reciprocal strat-egy on the line, has gradient exactly ε (P(μ) − Q(μ)) ,and that whenever this gradient is positive, every other agent’s gradient is positive as well, and at least as large. This follows from a Chebyshev-type inequal-ity for similarly ordered functions [29]: the numerator 

(bs − c)( s + 1) and denominator factor (1 − (p − ε)s)−2

increase with the opponent’s degree of reciprocity s. The denominator factor also increases faster for a more recip-rocal focal agent, so a more reciprocal agent’s gradient weights the positive contributions of highly reciprocal opponents more heavily. If P(μ) > Q(μ), one learn-ing step therefore moves every agent toward higher reciprocity; since (bs − c)( s + 1) is increasing in s, this movement can only increase P − Q, so the updated pop-ulation satisfies the same inequality and the next step again moves every agent toward TFT. The condition 7for ALLD is preserved in the same way: U(μ) bounds every agent’s gradient from above, and because u is increasing, movement toward lower reciprocity can only decrease U.However, only the ALLD half of the theorem de-scribes the full two-dimensional dynamics. Under 

U(μ) < 0, the unforgiving line is invariant: a negative gradient in p implies a negative gradient in q, so the one-dimensional analysis is in fact a full description of the two-dimensional dynamics. Under P(μ) > Q(μ),though, the line is provably not invariant: to reach TFT, the agents will all eventually need s > c/b, beyond which every agent’s gradient in q will be positive, so that the population leaves the q = ε line. Thus, this restriction is an idealization. Theorem 1 resolves the fate of a wide range of distri-butions on q = ε (Fig. S4 of the Supplemental Material). Given the parameters of a truncated Beta distribution, for example, a simple calculation determines whether the population goes to ALLD or TFT, or whether the distribution satisfies neither criterion and its fate is left undecided. Neither initial distribution of agents in the transient unforgiving state, with uniform or variable learning rates, provably reaches ALLD or TFT (even under restricted dynamics) via Theorem 1. C Evolution to GTFT

We do not have a formal analysis of the movement in two dimensions with many agents, and so we provide a visual summary of the population’s move-ment instead. We anticipate that, in most situations, a “move to TFT” when restricted to the q = ε axis, as in our analysis of the transient unforgiving state, would coincide with a move to GTFT if the agents were not restricted. D Stability of GTFT 

GTFT is the only stationary distribution restricted to the boundary p = 1 − ε (Theorem 2), and it persists under variable learning rates but not under uniform learning rates. GTFT is not locally stable against perturbations to the interior, however. A population displaced from GTFT in a direction that lowers its p − q cannot return to GTFT under uniform learning rates (Theorem S2 of the Supplemental Material). For variable rates, how-ever, perturbation from GTFT produces a slow transient followed by damped oscillations and a return to GTFT. We begin by showing there is only one stationary distribution on the boundary p = 1 − ε, namely GTFT, or (1 − ε, 1 − c/b − ε). This is formalized in the following theorem. 

Theorem 2. When ε > 0 and c /b < 1 − 2ε, the only distribution ρ(x) on the p = 1 − ε axis such that ∇qΠ(x) = R ∇qπ(x, y) dρ(y) = 0 for all x = ( 1 − ε, q) in the support of ρ is a Dirac mass with frequency 1 at q = 1 − c/b − ε.

A proof of this theorem can be found in Sec. S4 of the Supplemental Material. The intuition is that each oppo-nent contributes to an agent’s gradient in q with a sign set by the opponent alone: positive if the opponent’s de-gree of reciprocity exceeds c/b, and negative otherwise (Sec. II B). A less forgiving agent weights the positive contributions more heavily than a more forgiving agent does, so two agents at different values of q cannot both have zero gradient. The only possibility is a population concentrated at the single value of q where the degree of reciprocity equals c/b, that is, at GTFT. The Dirac mass at GTFT is a true fixed point of the full two-dimensional dynamics: for a population con-centrated at this point, both components of the gradient vanish, including the gradient in the p-direction. Both components vanish because GTFT has degree of reci-procity exactly c/b, so that an opponent at GTFT makes a co-player’s payoff independent of the co-player’s strat-egy (Sec. II B). The GTFT outcome is nearly socially optimal, as well. Consider another fixed point of the system, ALLD, where the uniform learning rate agents end up. Two ALLD agents playing one another will each have payoff near zero, (b − c)ε, while two GTFT agents will each 8have payoff b(1 − ε) − c. Although two ALLC agents have slightly higher payoffs (b − c)( 1 − ε), the difference is O(ε). GTFT maximizes the payoff of the population subject to being invasion-proof against less cooperative strategies [3]. ALLC, on the other hand, is vulnerable to invasion or gradient ascent exploitation. However, over the course of the agent-based sim-ulations, the population remains in a slowly moving transient state along this line. All gradients in the q-direction are positive for agents on the p = 1 − ε line when they first reach it. In the limit of small ε, there is a threshold qGTFT = 1 − c/b such that if the mean q

value of a population on p = 1 − ε exceeds qGTFT , then an agent at ALLC = (1 − ε, 1 − ε) will have negative gra-dient in the p-direction. The mechanism is that an agent facing forgiving opponents raises its payoff by lowering its own reciprocity. When the opponents an agent plays have forgiveness q above 1 − c/b, that agent can defect and, with high probability, not be punished, and if it is punished the opponent’s high forgiveness restores cooperation quickly. Equivalently, opponents on the re-ciprocal boundary with forgiveness above 1 − c/b − ε

have degree of reciprocity below c/b, the threshold iden-tified in Sec. II B. Indeed, in simulations, once the population ap-proaches a mean q value of 1 − c/b (here 1/2), multiple agents with high q values then move toward lower p

(Fig. 2). In response, most of the population lowers their q values, reducing the payoff from exploitation. Most agents who departed the p = 1 − ε axis return, except for one. There is a back and forth as this one agent becomes more or less exploitative as the rest of the population moves in response. This back and forth can be seen in Fig. 2 “Variable Learning Rates.” As mean q approaches 1 − c/b, the minimum value of reciprocity p becomes less than 1 − ε, immediately followed by a slight decrease in the mean forgiveness q.The population eventually reaches a distribution where the majority of agents are near q = 1 − c/b − ε, the stationary point. Given enough simulation time the population converges to GTFT. A long-term simulation of a reduced population sampled from the final state of our system suggests that the system is indeed slowly converging on GTFT (Fig. S6 of the Supplemental Material). Although GTFT is a stationary point, it is not lo-cally stable. Perturbing even one agent from GTFT can destabilize the population. When slightly increasing the 

q value of a population at GTFT, a population with uniform learning rates provably never returns to GTFT (see Sec. S6 of the Supplemental Material). In simula-tions this population reaches the q = ε axis (Fig. S7 of the Supplemental Material), from which Theorem 1 guarantees convergence to ALLD, since its degree of reciprocity remains below c/b. However, a population with variable learning rates, despite being locally desta-bilized, eventually recovers to GTFT through a transient. Similarly to the main experiment, the preservation of high p values by relatively slow learners stimulates the population’s return to the p = 1 − ε axis. This result is confirmed by perturbing random fractions of the popu-lation, with displacements having random magnitudes and directions (independent of other agents), over 500 simulations per learning rate regime: uniform learning rate populations never recover p = 1 − ε for any agent, but variable learning rate populations always recover 

p = 1 − ε for all agents (Fig. S8 of the Supplemental Material). 

E Robustness to population size and benefit-to-cost ratio 

The emergence of cooperation under variable learning rates is not particular to one population size or one payoff regime (Fig. S9 of the Supplemental Material). We varied the benefit-to-cost ratio b/c and the degree of heterogeneity in learning rates, and recorded the fraction of replicate populations that reached the coop-erative GTFT outcome, for populations of size N = 10, 100, and 1000. Three patterns emerge. First, coopera-tion becomes more frequent as the benefit-to-cost ratio increases, consistent with the threshold role of c/b in Theorem 1 and in the location of GTFT itself. Second, greater heterogeneity in learning rates shifts the bound-ary of cooperation toward smaller b/c: populations with more variable learners can sustain cooperation in games where less variable populations cannot. The learning rates are distributed according to a symmetric Beta dis-tribution with parameter α: ηi ∼ Beta (α, α), shifted and scaled onto the range [0.01, 0.39 ]. As − log α increases, the distribution of learning rates increases in variance, going from a point mass at η = 0.2 to a uniform distri-bution on [0.01, 0.39 ] to a two-point distribution with equal probability mass at 0.01 and 0.39. Although some 

b/c ratios cannot sustain cooperation with any param-eter α, some intermediate b/c ratios can only sustain cooperation with sufficiently high learning rate variance. Third, the transition sharpens with population size. In small populations ( N = 10) outcomes are mixed, with small-to-intermediate fractions of replicates reaching co-operation across much of the parameter range; in large populations ( N = 1000) the outcome is nearly determin-istic, with a sharp boundary in the (b/c, heterogeneity )

plane separating populations that reach ALLD from populations that reach GTFT. This is consistent with our analysis: the fate of the population is determined by the distribution of reciprocity on entering the transient unforgiving state, and in larger populations that distri-bution concentrates around its expectation, so replicate 9populations behave alike. 

# IV Discussion 

We have studied a population of selfish, myopic learn-ers who update reactive IPD strategies by simultaneous gradient ascent on their payoffs against the whole popu-lation. The fate of such a population can hinge on a sin-gle naturalistic parameter: variation among individuals in their rates of learning. With uniform learning rates the population converges to unconditional defection, whereas with variable rates it converges to a stable dis-tribution of generous tit-for-tat strategies and sustains nearly socially optimal payoffs. Along the way, every population passes through a transient unforgiving state in which forgiveness has collapsed and the distribu-tion of reciprocity determines the long-term outcome. We proved criteria on this distribution that guarantee convergence to ALLD or to TFT (Theorem 1), and we proved that GTFT is the only stationary distribution on the reciprocal boundary of the strategy space (Theo-rem 2). These results are robust to population size and benefit-to-cost ratio, with the transition between defec-tion and cooperation sharpening as the population size grows (Fig. S9 of the Supplemental Material). Our results identify a condition under which gradi-ent ascent solves a social dilemma, and so they speak di-rectly to the literature on optimization and multi-agent learning. Gradient methods are effective across a wide range of optimization and learning problems [20, 30], and yet strict gradient ascent typically fails on social dilemmas such as the IPD, where pairs of learners mak-ing selfish updates converge to mutual defection [10, 11, 18, 19]. Existing remedies modify the learner: agents may weight group payoffs in their gradients [22], an-ticipate the learning of their opponents [23], or adapt their learning rate to their performance [21]. We found a way to make gradient ascent succeed that requires no engineering at all: place the learners in a popula-tion and let their learning rates vary. The agents remain purely selfish and myopic; only the composition of the population changes. Heterogeneity in learning rates is not the only way to avoid the large basin of attraction to ALLD. Random-ness in the learning process can have a similar effect. In our simulations, a population with uniform learn-ing rates also converges to a GTFT-like distribution un-der gradient ascent perturbed by small isotropic noise (Fig. S10 of the Supplemental Material), although an isolated pair of noisy learners still converges to mutual defection (Fig. S11 of the Supplemental Material). Noise of this kind could enter through the strategy update directly, or through agents estimating their gradient from a finite sample of opponents rather than the whole population. The value of variable learning rates is there-fore not that they are the unique cause of cooperation, but that they are a more mechanistic and biologically realistic one. Variation in learning rates generates no new strategies and posits nothing beyond a fixed, in-trinsic trait that differs across individuals, the kind of variation organisms are known to exhibit. Variation in learning rates therefore preserves the diversity already present in the population: slow learners with high reci-procity survive the collapse of forgiveness with their reciprocity intact, and they leave the population hold-ing the high-reciprocity strategies that the later move to GTFT requires. This contrast is sharpest against evolutionary game theory on a finite set of IPD strategies. When a pop-ulation of ALLD, TFT, and ALLC strategies evolves by payoff-biased imitation with rare mutation, it never settles into cooperation. The population instead cycles indefinitely between cooperative and defecting phases: tit-for-tat permits the rise of generous players, then un-conditional cooperators, who are then exploited by de-fectors, and the population collapses back toward ALLD before the cycle repeats [4, 6, 16, 17]. Stable, persistent cooperation is not an outcome of this dynamic. Learning in a population with variation in learning rates succeeds where this imitation dynamic fails. The two differ in mechanism, in that our agents compute a local payoff gradient over a continuous strategy space rather than copying successful neighbors from a finite menu. The feedback we observe near GTFT, where an occasional agent moves toward exploitation and the population’s forgiveness decreases in response, is a damped pertur-bation around this stable outcome, not a recurrence of the cooperative and defecting phases. Closer to our setting, McAvoy et al. [24] showed that selfish gradient learners can reach social optima when updates occur through rare, random pairwise encounters in a large population. Their mechanism is different from ours: optimality there arises from the ephemerality of interactions, whereas our agents all update simultaneously against the entire population, and optimality arises from heterogeneity in learning rates. The two models nevertheless agree on a broader point, namely that the population context can reverse the dysfunction of selfish learning in pairs. Several limitations suggest directions for future work. Our agents compute gradients of their payoff against the entire population; a more realistic learner would sample a subset of opponents, introducing stochasticity whose effect on whether the population reaches ALLD or GTFT is unknown. We restricted atten-tion to reactive strategies; the full space of memory-one strategies doubles the dimension and includes strate-gies, such as extortionate ones, that may alter the 10 transient unforgiving state. The continuous-time limit of our population dynamics is a nonlocal transport equation (Sec. S1.2 of the Supplemental Material), and a rigorous treatment of its well-posedness and long-term behavior, including mass accumulation on the bound-ary, remains open. Finally, our agents’ learning rates are fixed for life; learning rates that decay over time, as in stochastic approximation [20, 21], would likely stabilize the slow transient near GTFT into true convergence. Our results also hint at broader implications for multi-agent learning. The distribution of learning rates (or other microscopic parameters) in a population might determine which collective outcomes become accessi-ble, and which are resilient. Coordination problems, in particular, might benefit from this heterogeneity. Mis-matches are costly, so fast learners might abandon opti-mal strategies, but slower learners could maintain them long enough for others to relearn them. Preserving strategic diversity through heterogeneity might lead to more optimal outcomes for multiple agents across a broad class of games and tasks. Individuals differ in how quickly they learn, both across and within species, and that variation is well documented [31–33]. Variation in learning rates might also be thought of as a measure of one’s reactivity to a situation: how quickly and how much should they act upon information at their disposal? Should they change their entire strategy, or wait for more to play out? It is similarly naturalistic that this trait might vary in a population. Our results show that this mundane form of variation is sufficient to produce stable and nearly optimal cooperation in a population of entirely selfish learners, with no genetic predisposition to altruism, no foresight, and no randomness in their updates. Variable learning rates cannot create high reciprocity where none exists; what they do is preserve the strategic diversity a population starts with, long enough for reciprocity to produce cooperation. Diversity in how individuals learn, not only in what they do, may thus be an under-appreciated resource for the emergence of cooperation.

# S5 Long-term experiment (Fig. S6) 

Directly evaluating the population gradient requires N2 pairwise calculations at each learning step. # S6 No recovery to GTFT under uniform learning rates 

We now consider a population with uniform learning rates in which every agent is displaced from GTFT to the same strategy. If the displacement lowers the degree of reciprocity below c/b, the population cannot recover to GTFT, provided the learning rate satisfies the bound below. This conclusion follows from the full discrete update rule, including projection, and does not require the displacement to be infinitesimal.
