# Decentralized Bandit Learning of Markov Perfect Equilibria ∗Felix Höfer † H. Mete Soner ‡

## October 14, 2026 

Abstract 

We study decentralized bandit learning in discounted infinite-horizon, general-sum Markov games with continuous actions. Agents observe the public state, but only their own realized rewards, and use simultaneous perturbation stochastic approximation to update their actions. Under strong monotonicity of the negative running-reward gradients and sufficiently small time steps, we prove that the fully decentralized procedure converges almost surely to the unique stationary Markov perfect equilibrium. We obtain an O(n−1/2) convergence rate and present a natural extension to episodic finite-horizon learning. 

Key words: multi-agent reinforcement learning; decentralized learning; bandit learning; stochastic approximation; Markov perfect equilibrium. 

Mathematics Subject Classifications: 91A15, 91A25, 62L20, 93E35. 

# 1 Introduction 

We consider a discounted infinite-horizon general-sum Markov game. Agents construct strategies based only on observations of the current state, their own realized action, and their own realized reward. They do not know the objectives of the other agents and need not even be aware of their existence. We develop a fully decentralized, payoff-based learning procedure and prove its convergence to the unique stationary Markov perfect equilibrium (MPE). Learning Nash equilibria is a classical and difficult problem. Among the classical multi-agent reinforcement-learning (MARL) algorithms are Littman’s minimax-Q [10], Hu and Wellman’s Nash-Q [9], and Bowling and Veloso’s variable-learning-rate method [3]. In general games, however, even natural learning dynamics may not converge, and global convergence guarantees rely on additional structure, such as potential games or weakly acyclic games. In this paper, we establish conditions under which dynamic equilibria can be learned in both stationary infinite-horizon and finite-horizon Markov games. Our structural condition is of a control-theoretic kind: we impose strong monotonicity of the players’ negative running reward gradients, which is equivalent to strong concavity of running rewards if they are independent of other players’ actions. In the finite-horizon setting, Höfer, Soner, and Yılmaz [8] showed that this condition ensures the uniqueness of the dynamic MPE for sufficiently small time steps, and Höfer 

et al. [7] use it to prove geometric convergence of model-based iterative schemes. In our algorithm, agents update their strategies by stochastic gradient ascent, with the required gradient estimated by simultaneous perturbation stochastic approximation [16, 17] from their private observations. A perturbed action, the realized reward, and an estimate of the discounted continuation value yield a one-point estimate of each agent’s pseudo-gradient. The continuation values are unknown and must be estimated as learning proceeds, adding a difficulty specific to the dynamic setting. Under

Zhang and Mu [22] also study payoff-based equilibrium learning in discounted games by playing repeated terminating trials to estimate policy gradients. In contrast, our algorithm uses a single uninterrupted trajectory of the original game to learn both policies and continuation values simultaneously, and our policy and value updates do not require separate timescales. The recent paper of Zhang and Mazumdar [23] establishes convergence of learning dynamics in discounted Markov games to stationary risk-averse quantal-response equilibria, but requires that agents observe opponents’ actions. By comparison, we learn ordinary MPE in continuous-action games from private bandit feedback alone. The closest static counterparts are the papers of Bravo, Leslie, and Mertikopoulos [4] and Tatarenko and Kamgarpour [18]. Both study continuous-action monotone games in which agents learn from realized payoffs: [18] uses a distributed payoff-based procedure with coupling constraints, while [4] analyzes no-regret bandit learning. With slightly differing assumptions and guarantees, they provide natural static benchmarks for our work. In the strongly monotone static setting, Drusvyatskiy, Fazel, and Ratliff [6] improve the rate obtained by [4], see also the convergence results of Ba et al. [2]; we obtain the same order of mean-squared error in our dynamic setting. Our dynamic setting introduces several additional layers. The target is an MPE rather than the Nash equilibrium of a static game, and the agents’ objectives depend on continuation values which must be learned along with their policies. At the same time, the observations come from a single trajectory: actions affect future states, and the states visited change as the policies evolve. The analysis must therefore control the policy and value errors together, while preserving the needed monotone structure. Mertikopoulos and Zhou [12] provide a complementary static comparison. They work under variational stability without requiring concavity, but use noisy first-order feedback (i.e., an oracle providing noisy observations of the payoff gradient) rather than bandit feedback. They prove global almost-sure convergence under global stability and local convergence with high probability under local stability. In our setting, the quantitative bound arising from the concavity assumption gives global convergence from arbitrary initialization and helps control the error from the evolving continuation estimates. For Markov games, Arslan and Yüksel [1] and Yongacoglu, Arslan, and Yüksel [20, 21] give decentralized 

Q-learning results for finite-action weakly acyclic and common-interest settings, see Ozdaglar et al. [13] for an overview. Their methods use exploration phases and comparisons among finitely many actions. Sayin et al. [15] study decentralized learning in two-agent zero-sum games, while Maheshwari et al. [11] exploit the Markov-potential structure. Chen and Li [5] estimate gradients of an overall policy-performance criterion from trajectories in general-sum games. In our continuous-action general-sum setting, each agent estimates the gradient of its Bellman operator with respect to its own action from a private one-step observation. The error due to the unknown continuation value is treated as a vanishing stochastic-approximation bias and controlled through a contraction of the coupled policy and value errors. Our updates and numerical sequences are synchronized across agents. Unsynchronized decentralized 

Q-learning has recently been studied [21], building on the classical asynchronous stochastic approximation and Q-learning of Tsitsiklis [19]. Motivated by [21], a natural extension of our approach would allow agent-dependent clocks and step sizes. We consider both finite-horizon and infinite-horizon discounted criteria and obtain similar results in the two settings. To avoid repetition, we first describe the game and algorithm in the infinite-horizon setting. Subsection 2.4 introduces episodic finite-horizon learning, and Section 8 establishes its convergence.

# 3 Illustrative example 

In this section, we consider a discounted dynamic service game inspired by the strategic queueing model of Zhong, Gopalakrishnan, and Ward [24]. Here, the agents are service providers who choose their processing rates. For example, they may operate computing servers that process jobs arriving at a shared queue. Providers receive contractual payments for the processing capacity they supply, pay operating costs, and receive standby payments when idle. All rewards are net monetary payments observed by the providers. Each provider’s processing rate affects the shared queue and thereby the assignment of future jobs. These interactions give rise to a non-cooperative dynamic game. 

State and actions. There are N ≥ 2 service providers (agents), each operating one server, and a finite total capacity of jobs k ≥ N , including jobs in service. With this convention, at most k − N jobs can wait. A job is assigned uniformly among idle servers, waits if all servers are busy and space remains, and otherwise leaves. Waiting jobs enter service in order of arrival. Decision periods are indexed by 

t = 0 , 1, . . . , with time step h > 0. The public state is Xt = ( ℓt, S t), where ℓt counts waiting jobs and 

St ⊆ [N ] records the busy servers. Formally, we let the state space be 

X = ( {0} × 2[N ]) ∪ ({1, . . . , k − N } × { [N ]}),

where 2 [N ] denotes the set of all subsets of [ N ]. When not idle, after observing Xt, agent i chooses its service rate αit ∈ [α, α ], where 0 < α < α < ∞.
