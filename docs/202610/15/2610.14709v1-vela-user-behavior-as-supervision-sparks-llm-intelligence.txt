# Vela: User Behavior as Supervision Sparks LLM Intelligence 

AllSpark Team 

Large language models are increasingly deployed in real-world applications, from personal as-sistants that take actions based on users’ long-term behavior histories to recommendation and advertising systems that predict what users will do. These applications demand the user dimen-sion of LLM intelligence: the ability to infer a person’s preferences, intent, and likely future actions from weeks to years of user behavior data. Yet current models remain limited in this user dimen-sion of intelligence: they are trained on static text or synthetic data, neither of which captures how user behavior evolves over time. Real-world user behavior data, by contrast, is already available at scale on content platforms and in personal-assistant agents. These records capture sequences of user actions, making the user’s actual behavior a natural supervisory signal with the potential to improve LLM intelligence. To this end, we propose user behavior as supervision , a paradigm in which the training signal for LLMs comes from the user’s real behavior in content platforms. We further introduce Vela , a framework that operationalizes this paradigm by converting real user be-havior data into trainable tasks and trajectories. From the anonymized behavior records of 100,000 users, Vela generates 100,000 tasks across 91 real-world task templates. Difficulty-based filtering and trajectory acceptance criteria jointly retain approximately 75,000 tasks with selected training trajectories. Fine-tuning Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on these training trajectories improves performance over the base model by up to 11.5 points on user-related tasks and by an average of 4 points on general benchmarks. Scaling experiments further show that training on more user behavior data improves user-related performance. Reinforcement learning after fine-tuning outperforms rein-forcement learning from the base model by up to 7.7 points on user-related tasks. These gains suggest that using real-world user behavior as supervision can improve not only user-centered capabilities but also the broader intelligence of LLMs.

As foundation models grow more capable, they are increasingly deployed in real-world applications, from personal assistants that take actions based on users’ long-term behavior histories, such as OpenClaw [23] and WorkBuddy 1, to recommendation and advertising systems that predict what users will do [2, 36]. Such scenarios share three properties: (1) individualized: decisions concern one person, not the average user; (2) long-horizon: they span weeks or even years of behavior; (3) evidence-bound: every judgment must be grounded in the user’s behavior history. Together, these properties define the user dimension of LLM intelli-gence: the ability to infer an individual’s preferences, intent, and likely future actions from real-world user behavior data. Understanding users from their behavior histories requires models to learn from context, identify relevant signals amid noise, integrate evidence over time, and track changes in preferences. However, current LLMs remain limited in this user dimension of intelligence. Common training sources include web pages, textbooks, code [24, 13], and synthetic data [1]. These sources may contain user-related content, but such content may not capture how individual users’ behavior evolves over time. Thus, training on these sources alone is insufficient to develop the user dimension of LLM intelligence. Recent evaluations show that LLMs with stronger general capabilities do not always perform better in user-facing applications [9]. LLMs also struggle to reliably complete tasks based on real user needs [11]. Even when behavior his-tories are provided, models can drift toward an average-user profile [4], lose accuracy as histories lengthen [12], and struggle with personalization [35]. Real-world user behavior data, by contrast, is already available at scale on content platforms and in personal-assistant agents [29, 40, 34]. These records capture sequences of user actions and their context over weeks to years: what the system presented, how the user responded, and how the user’s behavior evolved over time. Given a user’s behavior history, the actions the user actually takes afterward provide a natural supervisory signal with the potential to improve LLM intelligence. As public human-generated text becomes a potential bottleneck for LLM scaling [28], user behavior data offers a large, still-growing source of training signals. The challenge is to convert heterogeneous behavior records into diverse tasks with well-defined supervision. Prior work has focused on converting user behavior data into training sig-nals for specific tasks. For example, P5 unifies multiple recommendation tasks under a language modeling objective [8], while RLPF uses prediction feedback to train LLMs to produce user summaries [33]. How-ever, these studies focus on specific applications, leaving open whether training on user behavior data can improve performance across broader real-world user-related scenarios and enhance general LLM capabili-ties. This motivates three research questions: (1) how to convert raw user behavior data into trainable tasks and trajectories; (2) to what extent user behavior data improves user-related intelligence; and (3) whether training on user behavior data strengthens or harms general LLM capabilities. To this end, we propose user behavior as supervision , a paradigm that trains LLMs to understand users from their behavior histories using supervision derived from users’ observed behavior. We further introduce Vela, a framework that operationalizes this paradigm by converting raw user behavior data from a large so-cial media platform into trainable tasks and trajectories. Using anonymized behavior records from 100,000 users, Vela generates 100,000 tasks across 91 real-world task templates. Difficulty-based filtering and tra-jectory acceptance criteria jointly retain approximately 75,000 tasks. The selected trajectories form two training corpora: Vela-Instruct and Vela-RFT. Fine-tuning Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on the training trajectories in Vela-Instruct and Vela-RFT yields two models, Vela-35B-A3B and Vela-35B-A3B-RFT, respectively. To evaluate user-related performance and general capabilities, we use three groups of benchmarks: (1) Ve-laBench: 982 held-out evaluation tasks drawn from the same 91 task templates; (2) user-related out-of-distribution (OOD) benchmarks: PurchaseIntent, ProfileSummary, PersonaBench, AdRecommendation; (3) 

general OOD benchmarks: AIME 2026, GPQA-Diamond [21], IFBench [17], CL-bench [7], CL-bench Life [6]. Vela-35B-A3B improves over the base model by 11.5 points on VelaBench and by up to 11.2 points on user-related OOD benchmarks. The average improvement on general OOD benchmarks is 4 points. Scal-ing experiments further show that training on more user behavior data improves user-related performance. Reinforcement learning after fine-tuning outperforms reinforcement learning from the base model by 7.7 points on both VelaBench and ProfileSummary. These gains suggest that using real-world user behavior as supervision can improve not only user-centered capabilities but also the broader intelligence of LLMs.

## 2 Vela: User Behavior as Supervision 

This section describes the Vela framework and evaluation setup. We first describe the user behavior data (Section 2.1), followed by task synthesis under user behavior as supervision (Section 2.2) and difficulty-based filtering and trajectory generation (Section 2.3). We then introduce the evaluation benchmarks (Sec-tion 2.4). 

2.1 Real-world user behavior data 

Real-world user behavior data consists of timestamped sequences of actions taken by users on digital plat-forms. This study is based on a large-scale corpus of anonymized user behavior from a social media plat-form. The corpus contains behavior histories from 100,000 users, with each history spanning several weeks to several years. Before task construction, we remove user profile information and direct personal iden-tifiers, including names, contact details, and user account IDs, from the behavior records. Any behavior record containing personally identifiable information is also excluded. The resulting anonymized histo-ries are used for task generation and model training. We organize each user’s records into six behavior categories: (1) Views : browsing or watching content; (2) Searches : searching for content or products; (3) 

Purchases : purchasing products; (4) Posts : creating and publishing content; (5) Follows : following other accounts; and (6) Dislikes : hiding posts to express negative feedback. Illustrative examples of behavior records are provided in Figure 4 and Appendix A. 

2.2 User behavior as supervision 

As illustrated in Figure 2, under user behavior as supervision, each task combines multiple questions grounded in a single user’s behavior history. The questions span two families: Predictive questions evalu-ated against observed future behavior, and Rubric questions evaluated using task-specific criteria. 

Task formulation. A user’s behavior history is a sequence of timestamped events, H = ( e1, . . . , eT ), ordered chronologically. Each event belongs to one of the six behavior categories defined in Section 2.1. A cutoff index t divides the sequence into a visible history , H<t = ( e1, . . . , et−1), and a hidden future , H≥t =(et, . . . , eT ). A task Q = ( q1, . . . , qk) combines k questions about the user. Question templates. We design 91 question tem-plates across the six behavior categories, cover-ing four question types grouped into two fami-lies (Figure 3). Predictive templates comprise Ob-jective and Open-prediction questions, whose ref-erence answers are extracted from the hidden fu-ture H≥t using deterministic rules. Rubric tem-plates comprise Open and Attribution questions, whose answers are evaluated using task-specific criteria. (1) Objective questions provide the model with the visible history H<t and a candidate list, and ask it to select the items or entities involved in the user’s behavior in the hidden future H≥t

(e.g. the products they will buy). The list contains one or more ground-truth answers and distractors sampled from a pool of other users. (2) Open-prediction questions provide the model with the visible history H<t and ask it to predict the user’s behavior in the hidden future H≥t in free-form text, such as naming the products they will buy, without a candidate list. (3) Open questions ask the model to summarize the user’s attributes, interests, or language style based on H<t. Because these summaries have no single correct answer, they are evaluated against a task-specific rubric grounded in the visible history. (4) Attribution questions provide the model with the visible history H<t and an observed user behavior from the hidden future H≥t, and ask it to explain that be-havior based on H<t. A task-specific rubric evaluates how well the explanation is supported by the visible history. We call this paradigm user behavior as supervision: observed behavior provides the grounding for task construction and supervision. 

Metrics. Predictive questions are evaluated by comparing the LLM’s predicted answers with ground-truth answers derived from the hidden future H≥t. Let Yi denote the ground-truth answer set and ˆ Yi the LLM’s predicted answer set for question qi. For Objective questions, the selected candidates are matched ex-actly against the ground-truth answers. For Open-prediction questions, an LLM-based scorer receives the ground-truth answers and the evaluated LLM’s free-form response. The scorer extracts the predicted items and maps semantically equivalent predictions to their corresponding ground-truth items before computing set-F1:

Task synthesis. We divide each user’s behavior history into segments with randomly sampled lengths between 72K and 120K tokens, measured using the Qwen3.6-35B-A3B tokenizer. Each behavior record is kept intact during segmentation. Within each segment, approximately the first two-thirds of the tokens form the visible history H<t, and the remainder forms the hidden future H≥t. This division also preserves complete behavior records. For each segment, we select templates whose required behavior categories are present. For the same future behavior event, we randomly select either a Predictive template or an Attribution template. Including both would reveal the prediction target through the Attribution question, allowing the model to answer the Predictive question without inferring future behavior from the visible history. Predictive questions are constructed using predefined rules: Objective questions include candidate lists, while Open-prediction questions require free-form answers without candidates. For Open questions, an LLM-based composer uses the selected template and visible history H<t to generate a concrete question and its task-specific rubric. For Attribution questions, the composer additionally uses an observed user behavior from the hidden future H≥t to construct a question that asks the model to explain that behavior based on H<t, together with a corresponding rubric. The resulting questions are combined into one task Q

for each segment. Figure 4 illustrates this process with an illustrative example. In total, this yields 100,000 candidate tasks from 100,000 users. Both the composer and scorer use Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B with role-specific prompts (Appendix C). 2.3 Difficulty-based filtering and trajectory generation 

As illustrated in part (3) of Figure 2, training trajectories are constructed in two stages: candidate tasks are first filtered by difficulty, and trajectories are then generated and selected for the retained tasks. 

Difficulty-based filtering. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B generates four responses to each task with thinking disabled. The scorer evaluates each response, and the mean score is used to estimate task difficulty. Tasks with mean scores in [0.2, 0.8 ] are retained, focusing training on tasks of intermediate difficulty. 

Trajectory generation. For each retained task, each generation model produces four training trajectories. Only the highest-scoring trajectory is considered for inclusion. This trajectory is accepted only if its score exceeds the mean score obtained during difficulty filtering; otherwise, no trajectory from that model is retained for the task. We use Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B at the xhigh thinking level and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B with thinking enabled to generate Vela-Instruct and Vela-RFT, respectively. Vela-RFT contains trajectories gener-ated by Qwen3.6-35B-A3B itself. For Vela-Instruct, difficulty-based filtering and the trajectory acceptance criterion jointly retain approximately 75,000 of the 100,000 candidate tasks, with one training trajectory per retained task. Vela-RFT is constructed on a smaller scale and contains 9,375 training samples.

User-related OOD. We use four independently constructed benchmarks based on real user behavior to evaluate LLM capabilities in real-world user-facing scenarios. These benchmarks cover purchase intent prediction, profile summarization, persona understanding, and ad recommendation. Their inputs, outputs, and metrics are described below, with examples in Appendix D: (1) PurchaseIntent : given a user’s history and a set of candidate items, rank the candidate items by how likely the user is to purchase them, evaluated by recall@20 against ground-truth purchases; (2) ProfileSummary : given a user’s history, generate a profile and interest summary in a structured format, evaluated using rubrics for the user profile, core interests, and inferred interests; (3) PersonaBench : given a user’s history, answer a multiple-choice question about the user’s attributes or purchase behavior, scored by macro-F1; (4) AdRecommendation : given a user’s profile and recent search queries, generate search queries to retrieve ads relevant to the user. An LLM-based scorer evaluates the generated queries against rubrics for inferred intent and subsequent ad interactions. 

General OOD. We evaluate general capabilities using five benchmarks: AIME 2026 [14] for mathematics, GPQA-Diamond [21] for science, IFBench [17] for instruction following, and CL-bench [7] and CL-bench Life [6] for context learning. Among these benchmarks, PurchaseIntent, PersonaBench, AIME 2026, GPQA-Diamond, and IFBench use rule-based evaluation. Outputs requiring semantic matching or rubric-based evaluation in VelaBench, Pro-fileSummary, AdRecommendation, CL-bench, and CL-bench Life are assessed by the scorer, Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B, at the xhigh thinking level. The evaluated models use thinking mode, with the highest supported thinking level where configurable.

3.2 User behavior as supervision improves user-related intelligence 

Table 1 compares Vela-35B-A3B and Vela-35B-A3B-RFT with the baseline models across ten benchmarks. Vela-35B-A3B outperforms Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on all five user-related benchmarks. On VelaBench, Vela-35B-A3B scores 63.0, exceeding the base model by 11.5 points. With only 35B total parameters, Vela-35B-A3B outperforms larger DeepSeek-V4-Flash and Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B on this benchmark. Across the four user-related OOD benchmarks, Vela-35B-A3B improves over the base model by 6.2 points on average, with the largest gain of 11.2 points on AdRecommendation. Vela-35B-A3B-RFT also improves over the base model, by 2.0 points on VelaBench and 1.6 points on average across the user-related OOD benchmarks. These results suggest that user behavior as supervision improves user-related performance both on VelaBench and in real-world user-facing scenarios. 

3.3 User behavior as supervision benefits general LLM intelligence 

As shown in Table 1, Vela-35B-A3B outperforms Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on all five general OOD benchmarks, with an average gain of 4.0 points. The largest gains are on IFBench and CL-bench Life, with improve-ments of 6.7 and 5.6 points, respectively. Together, these results show that the benefits of user behavior as supervision extend beyond user-related tasks to general LLM capabilities.

## 4 Analysis 

4.1 User behavior as supervision provides a stronger foundation for downstream tasks 

We further investigate whether user behavior as supervision provides a stronger foundation for down-stream tasks. Reinforcement learning is commonly applied after supervised fine-tuning in post-training pipelines. We therefore investigate whether the stronger foundation provided by user behavior as supervi-sion leads to better performance after reinforcement learning. We apply GSPO [39] with the same configu-ration (Appendix E) to Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and Vela-35B-A3B. For the VelaBench experiment, we randomly sample 2,000 tasks from the training set for RL. For the ProfileSummary experiment, we use 2,330 training examples from the same task domain as ProfileSummary. For these experiments, Vela-35B-A3B denotes the checkpoint trained on 18.8K Vela-Instruct examples. We select this checkpoint because it achieves the high-est overall score across all ten benchmarks among the data scales evaluated in Section 3.4. Each resulting model is evaluated on the benchmark corresponding to its RL training domain. Figure 6 reports the results. On VelaBench, RL initialized from Vela-35B-A3B reaches 66.2, compared with 58.5 when initialized from Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. On ProfileSummary, the corresponding scores are 37.6 and 29.9. With the same RL configuration, initialization from Vela-35B-A3B yields scores 7.7 points higher on both benchmarks. On both benchmarks, RL from the base model remains below the SFT checkpoint be-fore RL, while RL from the SFT checkpoint further improves performance. These results show that user behavior as supervision provides a stronger foundation for reinforcement learning, leading to better final performance under the same RL configuration. 

4.2 Difficulty-based filtering improves user-related intelligence 

To investigate the contribution of difficulty-based filtering (Section 2.3), we compare models fine-tuned on training examples constructed with and without this filtering step. We conduct the ablation at half the full training scale, using 37.5K examples and the corresponding checkpoint from Section 3.4 as the filtered baseline. The variant without filtering draws tasks randomly from the 100,000 candidates without applying the difficulty threshold. Both variants use the same trajectory generation procedure, trajectory acceptance criterion, and fine-tuning settings, with 37.5K final training examples each. As shown in Table 2, difficulty-based filtering improves performance on all four user-related OOD benchmarks, with an average gain of 1.1 points, while VelaBench performance remains unchanged at 62.4. ## 5 Related Work 

Recent work evaluates how well LLMs remember, understand, and respond to individual users over time. LongMemEval [32] examines information retention and reasoning across long conversational histories, while PrefEval [37] tests whether models infer and follow explicit and implicit user preferences. Person-aMem [10] focuses on evolving user profiles and personalized responses, and Memora [27] examines how agents update memory and avoid relying on outdated information. ATM-Bench [15] extends personalized memory evaluation to multiple modalities and sources, including questions that require resolving personal references and combining evidence. Behavior prediction benchmarks further connect user understanding to observed behavior: OPeRA [31] captures online shopping sessions with observations, personas, ratio-nales, and actions; FineRob [12] evaluates fine-grained social media behavior prediction from accumulated histories; and OmniBehavior [4] studies long-horizon behavior across scenarios. Our work complements these efforts by using real-world behavior histories as supervision for a broad set of user-related tasks. Learning from user histories has also been explored through user representations, recommendation, and behavior simulation. User-LLM [16] encodes interaction histories into user embeddings that condition LLM responses, while P5 [8] expresses multiple recommendation tasks within a unified text-to-text framework. RLPF [33] uses prediction feedback to optimize user summaries for downstream tasks. DEEPER [3] sim-ilarly uses discrepancies between predicted and observed behavior to refine dynamic personas. Beyond user representations, Customer-R1 [30] uses reinforcement learning to improve personalized shopping be-havior simulation, and the OM-CoT framework accompanying FineRob [12] fine-tunes models for behavior prediction with long-term memory. The source of training signals also matters: work on recursive training with model-generated data identifies risks of losing information from the original data distribution [22]. Vela combines tasks and trajectories with supervision grounded in real user behavior, using observed fu-ture behavior to derive predictive ground truth. We study this supervision across a broad task collection and multiple data scales, evaluating its effects on user-related performance, general LLM capabilities, and subsequent reinforcement learning. 

## 6 Conclusion 

User behavior as supervision derives training signals from users’ observed behavior, and Vela turns real-world behavior histories into training tasks and trajectories. Experiments demonstrate gains in both user-related and general LLM capabilities, further improvements with data scaling, and a stronger foundation for downstream tasks through reinforcement learning. These findings establish real-world user behavior as a scalable source of supervision and motivate extending this approach to more real-world tasks and scenarios.

## C Composer and Scorer Prompts 

C.1 Composer System Prompt 

English 

You are the Task Composer. Given a selected template and the supplied behavior records, construct zero or one valid question with its hidden scoring specification. Return strictly valid JSON, without Markdown or explanatory text outside the JSON object. The request contains the frozen visible history user_behaviors , corresponding to H<t, and a template already selected by the program. Do not repeat template routing or introduce a task outside the selected template. If the evidence does not satisfy the template’s requirements, return an empty candidate list. Use the template’s task definition, eligibility conditions, permitted evidence, scoring requirements, and failure conditions to instantiate the question. Preserve the solver-facing seed instruction, including its task objective, analysis steps, output fields, and restrictions. Do not copy template-editing headings or Composer-only instructions into the question. Place the task instruction first, followed by instance-specific materials or a disclosed outcome, and then the output requirements. If the seed instruction already specifies the output format, integrate any additional instance-specific constraints into that format rather than adding a second output specification. Do not duplicate the full visible history or the final model input inside the candidate. For Open questions, use only the visible history. Construct the question and a task-specific rubric without producing a unique reference answer, gold , or gold_items . The answer must be evaluated against the question requirements and visible evidence, not against a summary written by the Composer. For Attribution questions, the request additionally supplies observed future behavior in future_user_behaviors ,corresponding to H≥t. Use the relevant observed behavior as the disclosed outcome to be explained. Include the outcome information required by the template in the question. The explanation must be supported by the visible history; do not use undisclosed future information as evidence. When a disclosed outcome requires a content record, include its relevant title, text, and available image or video descriptions. When the template requires a complete exposure list, preserve its order and distinguish recorded engagement from exposure alone. Skip the candidate if required materials are missing. Do not invent entities, events, conflicts, or missing context. Do not upgrade weaker behavior into a stronger outcome, such as treating a click as a purchase. Keep uncertainty and alternative explanations when the evidence does not support a unique conclusion. Do not reveal hidden scoring answers, rubric weights, or scoring anchors in the question. Derive the rubric from the complete template and the evidence available for this instance. Use non-overlapping, independently assessable criteria, typically four to seven. Each criterion must identify a concrete requirement and define full_credit , partial_credit , and zero_credit . Assign equal weight 1/ m to each of the m criteria.
