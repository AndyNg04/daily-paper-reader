ABSTRACT
Wake words face an inherent trade-off: higher sensitivity reduces
missed commands but increases accidental activations.
In co-
located augmented reality (AR), the system must also determine
whether the user is speaking to the assistant or to a nearby person.
We introduce a new perspective on this problem: combining gaze-
based address with an embodied assistant, visible listening status,
and turn management across activation, continued interaction, and
release. In our implementation, users look at an assistant anchored
in the scene and see activation progress and listening status on its
body. Sustained gaze opens a local interaction channel; speech and
playback keep it open when attention returns to the task; and inac-
tivity closes it. We evaluated this design with 25 participants. After
selecting the assistant’s placement and dwell duration, each partic-
ipant worked with a partner to plan a trip while using the assis-
tant. The study recorded 500 interaction outcomes, including 496
assistant-directed utterance attempts. Gaze acquisition completed
before speech for 490 of these attempts (98.8%): 467 began while
the channel remained open, whereas 23 began after it had been re-
leased. The other 6 attempts began before acquisition completed.
Among 102 reviewed attempts in which gaze left after acquisition
but before speech, the configured retention policy kept the chan-
nel open for 79 and released it before 23. The results show that
an embodied target with visible status can support clear entry into
an assistant interaction while revealing a different problem at re-
lease: after users look back to their work, they may not see that
the assistant has stopped listening. We contribute the implemented
gaze-invocation design and design implications for communicating

INTRODUCTION
Intelligent assistants need a reliable way to tell whether nearby
speech is meant for them, which is a recurring source of break-
down in everyday use [36, 7, 27]. Wake words provide an explicit
signal, but no single sensitivity setting avoids both failure modes:
more permissive detection catches more intended commands and
also admits more background or conversational speech; stricter de-
tection reduces accidental triggers and misses more intended com-
mands [30]. When users work with an AR assistant and another
person at the same time, their speech may be directed to either one
while they handle physical materials [5, 9].
Existing systems address parts of this ambiguity through spatial
and multimodal cues. Tama uses gaze toward an embodied smart-
speaker head, while Look and Talk and Look to Wake use sustained
gaze toward AR assistants [29, 46, 39]. Persistent Assistant com-
bines gaze and pinch to connect repeated queries [11]. Other ap-
proaches infer whether an HMD wearer is addressing another per-
son or combine gaze, pointing, and dialogue history to resolve spo-
ken references after a trigger phrase [45, 26]. These systems show
that gaze, embodiment, and multimodal context can clarify activa-
tion, addressee selection, or reference. However, gaze-invocation
studies primarily examine whether and how an interaction starts,
while addressee and reference systems solve adjacent classification
problems. They do not jointly examine how a visibly embodied
assistant should remain available after activation, decide when the
interaction has ended, and communicate that transition while the
wearer is collaborating with another person.
This gap matters because activation and release occur under dif-
ferent visual conditions. An embodied assistant anchored in the
physical scene gives users a stable target to look at and a place

where the system can display its status. During activation, the user
is already looking at that display. Afterward, people often look back
to documents or toward a collaborator while planning what to say
[5, 14, 4, 8]. Requiring continuous gaze would interrupt this behav-
ior, but once gaze moves elsewhere, status displayed only on the
assistant may no longer be seen. A gaze-invocation method there-
fore needs to manage not only entry but also continued availability
and communication of release.
We address this need by separating three stages of the inter-
action: sustained gaze opens the channel (acquisition); speech
and assistant playback keep it open while gaze moves elsewhere
(retention); and a period without relevant activity closes it (re-
lease). Because looking alone is ambiguous—the classical Midas-
touch problem—we use dwell to filter shorter incidental looks
[20, 24, 31]. A short dwell responds quickly but is easier to trigger
accidentally; a long dwell reduces incidental activation but delays
access [28, 12, 19]. Our design therefore lets each participant se-
lect the dwell duration and displays activation progress and readi-
ness on the assistant. Opening status is easy to see because the user
is looking at the assistant; closing status may be missed because
release usually occurs after attention has moved away. Figure 1
summarizes this motivation and the acquisition–retention–release
lifecycle.
We implemented this design on HoloLens 2 and evaluated it with
25 participants. The first three tasks established assistant place-
ment, measured gaze-to-speech timing, and let participants choose
their dwell duration. Participants then used the configured assistant
during a co-located travel-planning task with a collaborator. Of 496
assistant-directed utterance attempts, only 6 were Premature speech
events in which users spoke before gaze acquisition completed and
before the ready indicator appeared. A further 23 were post-release
events: acquisition had completed earlier, but users spoke after the
assistant had returned to Idle. First-person video, a visible gaze
cursor, and assistant status cues made these transitions observable
during critical incident analysis [13].
The paper makes three contributions:
• An embodied gaze-invocation design in which the assistant
is both the gaze target and the status display.
Participant-
selected dwell opens the interaction channel, speech and play-
back retain it, and inactivity releases it (Sections 3 and 4).
• An implemented HoloLens 2 system that keeps interaction
control on the headset while maintaining a persistent con-
nection to the conversational service, with participant-specific
placement and dwell calibration (Sections 4 and 5).
• New empirical findings throughout the invocation life-
cycle.
Our collaborative evaluation reveals that gaze be-
havior and status visibility differ between acquisition and
release, a pattern that activation-only evaluations overlook.
These findings provide lifecycle-level design insights for
gaze-invocation systems (Sections 6 and 7).

RELATED WORK
2.1
Wake-Word Invocation and Other Address Cues
Always-on voice interfaces use keyword spotting to decide when to
process subsequent speech. Increasing sensitivity reduces missed
commands but accepts more background speech; stricter detection
reduces accidental triggers but misses more intended commands
[30]. In shared environments, the system must also decide whether
speech addresses the device or another person [7, 27]. Wake-word
invocation therefore combines speech detection with addressee se-
lection.
Gaze provides a spatial cue for addressing an assistant. Tama
combines gaze with an embodied smart-speaker head and compares
it with a conventional speech-agent interface [29]. Its video analysis
identified failures before users established gaze and failures caused
by looking away; the wake-word condition produced more suc-
cessful interactions, while mutual-gaze feedback improved the gaze
condition. Look and Talk uses sustained gaze toward an AR avatar
and includes a small exploratory wake-word comparison [46]. It
reports early findings on naturalness, unobtrusiveness, dwell dura-
tion, and activation feedback. Look to Wake uses sustained gaze to
invoke an assistant without a spoken wake word [39]. These studies
primarily examine whether gaze starts an interaction and how users
experience that trigger. Our work follows the interaction after acti-
vation, including when the channel stays open, closes, and must be
opened again while the user looks elsewhere.
Related systems study other parts of assistant interaction in
shared settings. Speak-to-shoulder detects which person a head-
mounted display (HMD) wearer is addressing and reports detector
accuracy and false triggers on real data [45]. GazePointAR com-
bines gaze, pointing, and conversation history to resolve spoken
references, while a trigger phrase still starts the assistant interaction
[26]. Persistent Assistant uses gaze and pinch to connect repeated
queries and combines haptic and speech feedback [11]. These sys-
tems show how spatial cues, context, and multimodal feedback can
clarify an addressee or referent. Our study begins with gaze-based
selection of the embodied assistant and follows how visible status
and turn management support the interaction through retention and
release.
The present design brings together gaze toward a visible assis-
tant, status displayed on that assistant, and control of an open in-
teraction channel. Acquisition, retention, and release describe how
this specific gaze interaction starts, continues, and ends. The col-
laborative evaluation follows all three stages while users move their
attention between the assistant, task materials, and a collaborator.
2.2
Gaze and Embodiment as Spatial Address
GazePointAR illustrates the referential use of gaze in wearable AR:
it combines gaze, pointing, and conversation history to resolve spo-
ken pronouns [26]. This determines what the user is referring to.
Assistant invocation must also determine who the user is address-
ing.
Natural looking and intentional gaze input use the same signal,
creating the Midas-touch problem [20, 24, 31]. Common solutions
include dwell or fixation, gaze gestures or pursuit, and multimodal
confirmation [28, 19, 12, 32, 34]. Shorter dwell speeds activation
and admits more incidental looks; longer dwell reduces accidental
activation but delays intended access [28, 12]. Adaptive dwell can
vary the threshold with inferred intent, attention, or task context,
but a changing trigger time can weaken the user’s sense of control
[33]. GazeIntent users still preferred static dwell in some tasks [33],
while a focus-adaptive EyeBCI condition produced more fatigue
and frustration than fixed-dwell conditions [6]. We therefore let
each participant select a dwell duration and hold it constant during
the collaborative evaluation.
An embodied AR assistant provides a stable gaze target and a
place to display feedback. Prior work shows that agent form affects
perception and task fit [10, 22, 43, 37, 15, 38], and nonverbal cues
communicate attention and coordination [42, 3]. Status displayed
on the agent is easy to see during gaze activation because the user
is already looking at it. Peripheral, audio, and haptic cues provide
additional ways to communicate changes after attention moves else-
where [25, 34].
2.3
Conversational Coordination and Control of the As-
sistant’s Turn
Conversation and human–robot interaction (HRI) research use gaze
for addressee selection, floor management, and turn allocation
[5, 4, 1].
Situated dialogue systems similarly combine gaze,
2


speech, timing, feedback, and joint attention to manage participa-
tion [9, 2, 40, 41, 3]. Embodied agents can mediate collaborative
decisions [16], while social virtual-reality (VR) systems use visual
and spatial-audio cues to direct attention toward a potential human
speaker [25]. This research shows that turn-taking depends on gaze,
speech, feedback, and timing. Our design applies these principles
to gaze activation for an embodied AR assistant: speech and play-
back keep an open channel available, inactivity closes it, and the
assistant displays the controller’s current state.
3
GAZE INVOCATION WITH VISIBLE ASSISTANT STATUS
3.1
Design Requirements
The target scenario combines three demands. First, users need a
deliberate, spatial way to address the assistant without confusing
intentional selection with incidental looking. Second, they must re-
main free to inspect materials or look toward a collaborator after
selecting the assistant. Third, they need to know whether the assis-
tant is still available for speech. These demands lead to three design
requirements: a stable embodied gaze target with an adjustable ac-
tivation dwell; retention that does not require continuous gaze; and
visible feedback for activation, listening, response activity, and re-
lease. Together, these requirements treat invocation as a lifecycle
rather than a single trigger.
3.2
Interaction Lifecycle
Figure 2 follows one interaction from gaze acquisition to release.
A local controller combines gaze, speech activity reported by the
conversational service, and response playback on the headset. It has
four states: Idle, Gaze arming, Eligible, and Release timing. The in-
teraction channel is closed in Idle and Gaze arming and open in Eli-
gible and Release timing. In this paper, acquisition denotes opening
the channel through sustained gaze, retention denotes keeping that
channel open as attention moves elsewhere, and release denotes re-
turning it to Idle.
Acquisition.
From Idle, gaze entry starts the Gaze arming state
and its timer Tdwell.
Reaching the threshold opens the channel
and moves the controller to Eligible; looking away before the
threshold returns it to Idle. Dwell filters shorter incidental looks
associated with the Midas-touch problem, but its duration deter-
mines the trade-off between responsiveness and accidental activa-
tion [28, 19, 12, 32]. The threshold is therefore adjustable rather
than fixed as an intrinsic property of the interaction technique.
Retention.
Direct gaze keeps the controller in Eligible. When
gaze leaves, the controller enters Release timing but keeps the chan-
nel open. If neither speech nor response playback has occurred
since acquisition, gaze departure starts the leave delay Tleave. Af-
ter speech or playback, activity itself postpones release; once both
have ceased, the controller waits Tfollow and then Tleave. New speech
activity or playback restarts this post-activity wait. Returning gaze
moves the controller back to Eligible and pauses the pending re-
lease countdown; if gaze leaves again, the countdown resumes.
This policy lets gaze establish the addressee without requiring
continuous eye contact during speech. People routinely avert gaze
while planning an utterance or consulting shared material, and cog-
nitive difficulty increases gaze aversion [14, 8, 4]. Tama likewise
documented listening failures when users looked toward notes or
ceased maintaining gaze [29]. Speech and playback therefore re-
tain an already-open channel but cannot open one themselves, con-
sistent with work showing that gaze, speech, feedback, and timing
jointly organize turns and joint attention [40, 41, 3].
Release.
The channel closes only when the applicable release
countdown completes with gaze absent and no new activity. The
controller then returns to Idle, preventing subsequent speech to the
collaborator from being treated as assistant-directed. Acquisition
and release consequently occur under different attention conditions:
the user necessarily sees the assistant during gaze acquisition but
may be looking elsewhere when release occurs.
3.3
Embodied Target and Visible Status
The embodied assistant connects spatial address and system feed-
back. It gives gaze a stable target and places activation progress
and channel status on the addressee itself [10, 22, 43, 37, 42]. The
assistant is anchored at a user-adjustable location beside the work
area. Users can reposition it through direct pinch or gaze-assisted
pinch interaction, and it continuously rotates around its vertical axis
to face the wearer. Figure 3 shows the visible cues across one acti-
vation cycle.
Figure 3: Embodied feedback during one activation cycle. From left
to right: the resting cue, dwell cue, listening-ready cue, and animated
ellipsis. The ellipsis appears during assistant-audio playback within
the existing controller states.
Character form, scale, and orientation.
The deployed assis-
tant is a stylized, female-presenting humanoid approximately 20 cm
tall. Its rig supplied legible idle, attentive, greeting, and response
poses; the miniature scale limited competition with the collabora-
tor and tabletop materials. Prior work shows that AR agent form
and scale affect perception and task fit [43, 37, 15, 38]. Facing
the wearer keeps its body cues and gaze target visible; orientation
also supports attention and coordination in embodied interaction
[1, 42, 3].
State feedback.
Each controller state selects a visible cue.
Idle uses the resting cue. Gaze arming moves the character into
an attentive pose and displays dwell progress. Entering Eligible
triggers a brief wave and a listening-ready glow, which remains vis-
ible through Eligible and Release timing. Response playback adds
an animated ellipsis without introducing another controller state.
The assistant therefore presents activation progress, channel-open
status, and response activity at the same location used for spatial
address.
Colocating target and status makes state changes readily in-
spectable during acquisition because the user is already looking at
the assistant. It also creates a visibility boundary: during reten-
tion and release, attention may have returned to the materials or
collaborator. Prior work examines peripheral, audio, and nonver-
bal cues for maintaining awareness beyond the current visual fo-
cus [17, 18, 23, 3]. Our evaluation therefore follows the interaction
through release rather than treating successful activation as the end-
point.

4.1
Runtime Deployment and Data Paths
We implemented the system as a Unity 2022.3.62f1 application for
Microsoft HoloLens 2. Gaze sensing, interaction control, media
gating, embodied feedback, and response playback all run on the
headset. The headset captures the wearer’s microphone audio and
optional camera frames, while a remote conversational service pro-
cesses admitted speech and context and generates responses. A
persistent WebSocket session carries mono pulse-code-modulation
(PCM) audio and optional camera frames to the service, and re-
turns service activity events and response audio. The interaction
controller decides locally whether to admit media, before any user
media enters the remote service.
A separate control path connects a companion application on
the workstation to the headset.
It carries runtime configuration
and explicit activation or deactivation commands, while a telemetry
path returns structured controller records. User media travels only
between the headset and conversational service; it does not pass
through the workstation. This separation supports live configura-
tion and logging without placing the workstation in the user-media
path.
4.2
Local Interaction Control and Media Gating
The headset queries the Mixed Reality Toolkit 3 (MRTK3) eye-
gaze ray once per Unity update and raycasts it against a capsule
collider that follows the assistant’s visible body. At the deployed
0.15 scale, the collider measured approximately 0.27 m high with
a 0.045-m radius. The controller records gaze entry when the ray
begins intersecting the assistant and gaze departure when it ceases;
a ray miss is treated as departure. The local interaction controller
accumulates activation dwell and applies the acquisition, retention,
and release transitions in Figure 2. It controls the current embodied
cue and whether the conversational media path is open.
The conversational service session remains connected while the
controller opens and closes a local media gate. A closed gate sup-
presses outgoing microphone audio and optional camera frames,
discards newly received response audio, stops playback, and clears
the playback buffer. Reopening the gate makes the existing ses-
sion available for a later engagement. While the channel is open,
recent speech activity reported by the service and local response
playback or buffering postpone release after gaze departure. Outgo-
ing microphone packets are withheld during response playback to
4


reduce acoustic feedback into the service; consequently, this proto-
type does not support user barge-in during assistant playback. To-
gether, these mechanisms implement the controller states without
coupling channel opening and closing to network reconnection.
The service reports detected speech through asynchronous input-
transcription callbacks, and the controller treats speech activity as
current for 1.5 s after the most recent callback. Figure 4 contrasts
the two release paths. If neither a callback nor playback has oc-
curred since acquisition, gaze departure starts Tleave directly. Af-
ter speech or playback, release waits until both the speech-activity
window and playback have ended, then applies Tfollow followed by
Tleave. With gaze continuously absent and no new activity, the ear-
liest return to Idle after a final callback is therefore approximately
1.5+Tfollow +Tleave seconds; ongoing playback extends this inter-
val.
4.3
Runtime Configuration and Service Interface
Through the companion application, experimenters can update spa-
tial offsets, gaze-following thresholds, display settings, activation
dwell, and the leave wait. They can also start or stop a runtime
stage, explicitly deactivate the assistant, and enable or disable gaze
processing. Parameter changes take effect without rebuilding or
restarting the headset application.
The headset communication layer presents the local controller
with a stable set of operations: maintain a session, admit or block
media, expose recent speech and playback activity, and deliver re-
sponse audio. Service-specific connection and message details re-
main within this layer.

EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND CALIBRATION
Before the collaborative evaluation, three tasks set the assistant lo-
cation and activation dwell used by each participant. Short dwell
speeds activation but admits more incidental looks; long dwell fil-
ters those looks but delays access [28, 19, 12, 31, 35]. Figure 5
summarizes the complete procedure and the configuration or evi-
dence carried from each stage to the next.
5.1
Participants, Ethics, and Procedure
We recruited a 7-person engineering pilot to refine an earlier imple-
mentation and a formal cohort of 25 participants for the reported
study. All pilot data are excluded from the analyses and figures.
The study protocol was reviewed and approved through the au-
thors’ institutional ethics process, and all participants provided in-
formed consent covering participation, first-person recording, voice
processing, and de-identified research use. Formal cohort study
logs use de-identified identifiers (IDs). The retained recruitment
record was audited against the local participant register to link those
IDs before aggregation. Formal participants were 23–36 years old
(mean 27.0, standard deviation (SD) 3.6); 14 identified as men, 10
as women, and 1 preferred not to report gender. Education levels
were bachelor’s (3), master’s (14), and doctoral (8). Vision was
normal without correction for 6 participants, corrected with glasses
for 14, and corrected with contact lenses for 5. Prior experience
included mobile AR for 20 participants, a VR headset for 22, and
a head-worn AR or mixed-reality (MR) device for 12. Preferred
study languages were Chinese (17), Japanese (7), and English (1);
task materials were localized.
Every formal participant completed the sequence in Figure 5 in
the same order.
5.2
Apparatus and Experimental Configuration
Participants completed the HoloLens 2 eye calibration before the
study. The formal tasks used the prototype described in Section 4
with ByteDance Doubao Realtime Dialogue over WebSocket. The
headset captured 16-kHz, 16-bit mono PCM audio in 1024-sample
chunks; camera transmission to the service was disabled. The head-
set used wearer-focused microphone processing, but the study did
not independently measure leakage of collaborator speech into cap-
ture. Character style, scale, and orientation policy remained con-
stant across the formal tasks.
The companion workstation applied the parameters selected
during Tasks 1–3 and received placement, gaze-to-speech tim-
ing, dwell, and controller records. For study instrumentation, the
HoloLens displayed a persistent gaze cursor—a red heart with a
small green crosshair-like ring. It followed the gaze locus and re-
mained distinct from the assistant’s state cues. First-person record-
ing preserved this cursor, the assistant cues, and the surrounding ac-
tivity. Task 4 used each participant’s final dwell value. The follow-
up and leave waits were fixed at 1.0 s for every session; the con-
troller’s 1.5-s speech-activity window remained unchanged. Thus
all participants experienced the same release timing after calibra-
tion.
5.3
Task 1: Assistant Placement
Task 1 let participants place the assistant where it remained easy to
address without obstructing the collaborative work area; Figure 6
shows the final selections. Audio interaction was disabled, while
dwell and listening-ready cues remained available for familiariza-
tion. A fixed 0.8 s dwell provided the same placement condition for
all participants; this value lies within durations examined in prior
gaze-input studies [28, 19, 12, 32]. Participants worked with a lap-
top and paper materials, repositioned the assistant, and confirmed
a location. The system recorded the center of its rendered mesh
bounds relative to the headset. Participants could revise the place-
ment during Task 2.
For analysis, we retained the last confirmed placement for each
participant.
Final placements were relatively consistent in dis-
tance and varied in horizontal direction: distance had mean/median
0.685/0.68 m (interquartile range (IQR) 0.64–0.74 m), azimuth had
mean/median 2.9◦/8.3◦(IQR −19.1◦to 20.2◦), and elevation had
mean/median −20.3◦/−19.9◦(IQR −29.1◦to −15.0◦). Most par-
ticipants placed the assistant below eye level and near the work ma-
terials, with greater horizontal than distance variation. This pattern
supports an initial location near the materials while preserving hor-
izontal adjustment for the user.
5.4
Task 2: Gaze-to-Speech Timing
Task 2 measured the interval from gaze onset to speech onset. The
assistant was force-activated so that visible state changes would not
cue when participants began speaking. Participants received read-
ing materials and were asked to interact with the assistant. Their
only instruction was to look at the assistant before speaking; they
chose when to speak and what to ask. The controller timestamped
gaze onset and, after the conversational service reported speech,
searched backward through a 20-Hz history of local microphone
root-mean-square (RMS) values to estimate the earlier speech on-
set.
The detector retained 10 s of pre-gain RMS values, required a
local peak of at least 0.02, and treated values below 0.01 as silence.
Its 3.0 s initial lookback adapted after 3 service notifications to the
recent 80th-percentile delay plus 0.7 s, bounded to 1.5–8.0 s and the
available history. We selected these thresholds during development
by comparing the stored waveforms with the corresponding audi-
ble utterances. The resulting onset is therefore a locally calibrated
estimate rather than the later service notification.
The controller stored a measurement only when the estimated
gaze-to-speech interval was within 100–3000 ms. The formal logs
contained 178 retained measurements: 23 participants contributed
7, 1 contributed 8, and 1 contributed 9. To give each participant
equal weight, the distribution analysis used the first 7 retained
measurements per participant, yielding 175 observations.

Task 3: Dwell Selection
At the start of Task 3, the runtime used all retained Task 2 mea-
surements then available for that participant (7–9 measurements).
To reduce the influence of an unusually short or long interval on
the starting estimate, it removed the fastest and slowest values, av-
eraged the remainder, rounded to the nearest 100 ms, and clamped
the result to 100–2000 ms. The resulting starting dwell had mean
1088 ms, median 1100 ms, and range 500–2000 ms. Participants
then tried the gaze trigger and adjusted this starting value in 100-
ms steps using the embodied target and cues, a process consistent
with prior gaze-dwell calibration [28, 19, 12, 35]. The selected
value was fixed for Task 4. Final dwell ranged 100–1800 ms (mean
748.0, SD 543.2, median 800, IQR 300–1200 ms; Figure 8). Of the
25 participants, 19 reduced the starting value, 5 increased it, and 1
retained it; the median change was −400 ms. The calculated value
served only as an initial setting for adjustment and was not treated
as an optimized dwell threshold.

COLLABORATIVE INTERACTION TEST AND EVENT ANAL-
YSIS
6.1
Collaborative Planning Task
Task 4 evaluated the configured assistant in a collaborative tabletop
travel-planning activity. Each participant worked with a researcher
acting as the collaborator. The researcher gave no instructions or
hints about assistant use and did not attempt to produce particu-
lar events; they conversed naturally as the participant’s planning
partner. Together they consulted printed materials, discussed con-
straints, queried the assistant, and recorded their decisions. Gaze
and speech moved between several plausible targets, as in multi-
party turn coordination [5, 9, 3, 25]. The assistant was visible to
the participant but not to the collaborator, who followed the same
planning prompt and did not operate the interface.
Each participant used the dwell selected in Task 3. Reading,
writing, and conversation repeatedly shifted attention and speech
among the assistant, materials, and collaborator, testing the con-
figured interaction during natural task activity.
A staff member
recorded events in chronological order, and the task ended when
the live counter reached 20 records. All formal logs contain in-
dices 1–20 for each participant. The resulting fixed sample con-
tains 496 intended assistant-directed utterance attempts and four
other channel-open events. Continuous first-person recording pre-
served the surrounding context. After the 20th record, participants
answered open-ended questions about the interaction and possible
improvements.
6.2
Event Recording and Critical-Incident Coding
We combined fixed event recording with critical-incident review.
Critical Incident Technique focuses analysis on concrete episodes
that reveal requirements for effective activity [13]. HCI studies of
communication systems similarly combine observation with post-
session accounts to derive design requirements [21], while first-
person video preserves the temporal relation among gaze, speech,
body movement, and system feedback [44].
During Task 4, a staff member outside the participant’s view en-
tered a live marker for each recorded event while watching the first-
person perspective. This staff member was not the collaborator.
The runtime interface provided 3 provisional categories: success-
ful activation, failed activation, and apparent unintended activation.
These markers established the chronological event sample but did
not determine the final classifications.
Two researchers subsequently reviewed every complete first-
person recording and reached consensus on the final code. Table 1
shows how the live markers were resolved through this review. The
visible gaze cursor and assistant cues allowed the researchers to re-
construct gaze entry and departure, system state, speech onset, and
intended addressee; participants clarified ambiguous addressee in-
tent when needed. Every chronological record ultimately received
one of the fixed terms in Table 2.

6.3
Outcomes Across Acquisition, Retention, and Re-
lease
Figure 9 and Table 2 summarize the fixed sample of 500 recorded
outcomes. It comprised 496 assistant-directed utterance attempts
and 4 other channel-open events. Of the 496 intended attempts, 467
(94.2%) were Ready at speech onset; the other 29 began while the
channel was closed. Gaze acquisition completed before speech in
490 attempts (98.8%): the 467 ready attempts and 23 post-release
events. The remaining 6 were Premature speech events in which
speech began before dwell completed. Thus, 98.8% describes ac-
quisition completed before speech, not ASR accuracy, response
quality, or task success.
The 6 premature-speech events came from 6 participants. The 23
post-release events comprised 3 Post-release initial question and 20
Post-release follow-up events and spanned 16 participants. In each,
the assistant had previously activated and returned to Idle while
the participant looked elsewhere; the participant then addressed the
assistant without reactivating it. Altogether, 17 participants con-
tributed at least 1 intended utterance that began while the channel
was closed. Four participants contributed 19 intended attempts and
1 other channel-open event; the other 21 contributed 20 intended at-
tempts. Across participants, the median proportion of intended at-
tempts that were ready at speech onset was 95% (range 85–100%).
Consensus video review identified 102 intended attempts in
which gaze left after acquisition but before speech. The configured
retention policy kept the channel open for 79 (77.5%); the other 23
(22.5%) were the post-release events defined above. This percent-
age is conditional on those 102 reviewed gaze-departure sequences,
rather than a global retention-success rate.
The remaining 4 outcomes comprised 1 Accidental activation
and 3 Over-retention events. These counts distinguish an acciden-
tal opening from a channel that remained open as speech shifted
to the collaborator. Because the fixed 20-event sample does not
enumerate all incidental looks or all collaborator-directed utterance
opportunities, these counts are not false-activation or over-retention
probabilities.
6.4
Post-Study Interview and Willingness
Immediately after Task 4, participants rated their willingness to use
gaze-based invocation, setting aside HoloLens weight and response
quality. Of the 25 participants, 10 selected strongly willing, 11
somewhat willing, and 4 neutral; none selected an unwilling option.
Open-ended material was used as descriptive context rather than as
a systematically coded qualitative dataset, so we make no preva-
lence claims from it. Comments described gaze-based invocation as
convenient and activation status as clear, including one description
of the activated and non-activated states as “very conspicuous” (au-
thors’ translation). Other comments mentioned needing to look at
task materials and learning a gaze-then-speak habit. One participant
8


reported that input stopped before an utterance ended; because this
statement was not aligned to a specific runtime record, we treat it
as an illustrative perception rather than a separately counted event.

DISCUSSION
7.1
Acquisition through an Embodied Assistant with
Visible Status
Among the 496 assistant-directed utterance attempts, gaze acquisi-
tion completed before speech in 490 (98.8%); the other 6 (1.2%)
were Premature speech events. This is a within-system measure of
whether dwell completed before speech. It is not a measure of re-
sponse correctness or task success, and the study did not include a
wake-word condition from which to infer relative modality perfor-
mance.
Participants looked toward the intended addressee, saw acquisi-
tion progress and readiness on the same body, and then spoke. De-
brief comments included descriptions of activation status as clear
and conspicuous. Together, deliberate gaze and an explicit visual
state change demonstrate the feasibility of using gaze as a spatial
activation signal while visible status lets users verify that the assis-
tant is ready.
Embodiment served a functional role: the anchored character
served as addressee, interaction target, and status display, turning
a hidden detector decision into a visible interaction. Participant-
specific dwell changes the required gaze duration while preserving
the same action and feedback across users.
7.2
Retention, Release, and Later Intended Speech
Within the 102 reviewed cases in which gaze left after acquisition
but before speech, the configured retention policy kept the channel
open for 79 (77.5%), letting participants inspect materials or for-
mulate a question without continuous fixation. The other 23 were
post-release events. In 3 Post-release initial question events, par-
ticipants activated the assistant and then looked at the materials to
prepare their first question; the assistant returned to Idle before they
spoke. In 20 Post-release follow-up events, participants had com-
pleted an assistant-directed turn and were preparing the next ques-
tion when the assistant returned to Idle. These events spanned 16
participants. Because all 102 reviewed events share the same order
of acquisition, gaze departure, and speech, they show how the con-
figured timing handled continued interaction and when it ended too
soon.
The opening and closing states differ in what the user can see.
During acquisition, the wearer looks at the embodied character,
where its attentive pose, wave, glow, and the gaze cursor show the
interaction state. After attention returns to materials or a collabo-
rator, the cursor follows the new gaze location while the assistant’s
status cue remains on the assistant. The cue therefore becomes spa-
tially separated from the wearer’s current focus. Retention is con-
sequently valuable because it lets users formulate language while
leaving gaze free to move. After release, users may attempt the
next turn while believing the assistant remains active because its
status remains at the prior gaze location.
Video confirmed the complete activation–release sequence for
every post-release event. The resulting design question is how to
preserve natural attention shifts while communicating that the as-
sistant has stopped listening when the user is looking elsewhere.
7.3
Design Lessons for Gaze-Activated AR Assistants
The first lesson is that embodiment serves two functions. In optical
see-through AR, one character anchored in the physical scene can
be both a spatial addressee target and the place where status is dis-
played, consistent with work on embodied form and nonverbal co-
ordination [10, 22, 43, 37, 42, 3]. The observed acquisition pattern
supports colocating address and status at the attended target. Once
attention moves, however, release feedback should reach the user
through a peripheral visual, audio, or haptic channel [17, 23, 25].
The second lesson is to separate opening the channel from keep-
ing it open. Gaze establishes the addressee, while speech and play-
back keep the channel open as visual attention returns to the task
[40, 41, 3]. This retention supports natural planning behavior with-
out requiring every utterance to begin under direct gaze, as demon-
strated by the 79 events in which gaze left before speech while the
channel remained open. Release then deserves an explicit cue avail-
able beyond the assistant’s body, such as a short spatial sound, pe-
ripheral marker, or wearable haptic.
The third lesson is to expose and evaluate each controller transi-
tion. Participant-selected dwell accommodates speed–error prefer-
ences, while fixed release waits make the closing threshold explicit
and inspectable [28, 12]. Future systems can test adaptive retention
using speaker-aware audio processing together with gaze location,
head orientation, and turn cues. Evaluations can replay critical inci-
dents and count all relevant opportunities, preserving the transition
that preceded each outcome rather than reducing the interaction to
one activation total.
7.4
Scope and Limitations
Task 4 recorded the first 20 chronologically marked events for each
participant, comprising assistant-directed utterance attempts and
other channel-open events. This fixed sampling gives each par-
ticipant equal event weight, but it does not enumerate every inci-
dental gaze opportunity or every period of non-addressed speech.
The accidental-activation and over-retention findings are therefore
counts within these 500 records rather than time-normalized error
rates.
The study evaluated one integrated configuration without a
wake-word or component-ablation condition.
It can locate out-
comes within acquisition, retention, and release, but cannot es-
tablish superiority over wake words or isolate the causal contri-
butions of embodiment, visible status, personalized dwell, and re-
tention timing. The follow-up and leave waits were fixed across
participants, making release timing comparable but leaving param-
eter optimization for future work. The prototype also withheld mi-
crophone packets during assistant playback, so the findings do not
cover barge-in interaction.
Task 2 retained only gaze-to-speech estimates between 100 and
3000 ms; rejected episodes were not logged, so their number can-
not be reconstructed. The calculated dwell served only as a starting
point for explicit participant adjustment, not as a validated opti-
mum. In addition, the visible gaze cursor supported review but may
itself have influenced gaze behavior and awareness of system state.
The study recorded 20 events per participant in a single session.
This short exposure cannot show learning effects or how gaze and
turn-taking habits change through repeated use. One participant
expected interaction to become easier after learning the gaze-then-
speak sequence. Longer deployments can examine whether users
adapt their timing or become more aware of release.
All participants were pursuing or had completed a bachelor’s de-
gree, and most had prior extended reality (XR) experience, includ-
ing mobile AR (20), VR headsets (22), or head-worn AR/MR (12).
Users with less formal education or no prior XR exposure may de-
velop different interaction strategies.
Finally, the gaze cursor, assistant cues, and synchronized diag-
nostics supported reconstruction of the recorded event histories.
The two researchers reached consensus rather than producing in-
dependent reliability codes, so no inter-rater reliability statistic is
claimed. Participant clarification supported ambiguous addressee
judgments, while open-ended debriefs were used only as illustra-
tive context. The collaborative tabletop planning task, HoloLens 2
implementation, and one conversational service define the current
boundary of generalization.

CONCLUSION
We examined gaze-based interaction with an embodied AR assis-
tant whose status was visible to the user. Users opened the inter-
action by looking at the assistant and seeing its ready state on the
same body. Speech and playback then kept the channel open as
attention returned to the task. Participant-selected placement and
dwell, together with the collaborative test, showed that this design
provided a consistent entry into assistant interaction within the eval-
uated configuration.
Following the interaction beyond activation revealed an equally
important challenge at release. While planning what to say, users
sometimes looked back to task materials and could no longer see the
assistant’s status. Most intended utterances with a closed channel
occurred after an earlier activation and release, rather than during
the initial gaze trigger. The resulting design lesson spans the full
interaction lifecycle: embodied visible status clarifies entry, reten-
tion supports natural shifts of visual attention, and release needs
cues that remain perceptible after attention moves elsewhere. Our
implementation and findings provide concrete guidance for future
gaze-activated assistants.
