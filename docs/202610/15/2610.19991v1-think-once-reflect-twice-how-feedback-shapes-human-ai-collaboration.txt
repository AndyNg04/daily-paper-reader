# ABSTRACT 

As artificial intelligence (AI) increasingly supports human decision-making in high-stakes domains, designing effective human-AI collaboration becomes critical. Successful collaboration requires humans to reflect on decision-making processes and adjust their strategies accordingly. Current research typically investigates interventions, such as explainable AI, that target reflection during the decision-making process (reflection-in-action). Less attention has been given to retrospective examination of decisions (reflection-on-action). This work explores reflection-on-action by studying the impact of feedback on reliance behavior and human-AI team performance. Specifically, we investigate feedback interventions targeting overrides of AI advice through a behavioral study (N=244). We find that such feedback influences reflection-on-action, while unidirectional feedback risks reducing subsequent reflection-in-action. Furthermore, we identify opposing shifts in reliance behavior that leave overall human-AI team performance unchanged but show context-dependent effects. Balanced feedback did not show these risks, preserving both concurrent and retrospective reflection, offering insights for designing for appropriate reliance on AI. 

Keywords Human-AI Collaboration · Reflection · Appropriate Reliance · Artificial Intelligence · Feedback 

# 1 Introduction 

Envision you are collaborating with an artificial intelligence (AI) as a loan officer to evaluate the creditworthiness of loan applicants. When the AI advised rejection but you favored approval (or vice versa), who would you rely on? How would your reliance on the AI shift after receiving feedback on the outcomes of decisions you overrode? 

AI systems increasingly support human decision-making across domains [34, 58], including high-stakes applications such as medical diagnosis [79], legal advice [87], and loan approval [57]. In these applications, humans no longer decide alone but collaborate with an AI system that advises them. Besides understanding the task and its domain, they therefore also need to understand the AI advice and develop strategies for when to follow it. When this collaboration fails, the people affected by the decisions bear the consequences, for example, for their health in medical diagnosis [2], their liberty in recidivism decisions [3], and their access to credit in loan decisions [62]. These high-stakes consequences underscore the need for humans to develop collaboration strategies with AI systems that enable appropriate reliance, i.e., the ability to effectively complement one’s own judgment with available AI advice

[65, 67]. If humans blindly rely on AI advice, they risk systematic overreliance [13, 64] and deskilling [50, 52]; if they ignore AI advice, they miss out on potential improvements [68]. Developing effective collaboration strategies to achieve appropriate reliance requires reflection . In general terms, reflection is the purposeful and critical analysis of one’s own experiences, knowledge, and actions, aimed at a deeper understanding that informs future action [11, 69]. In human-AI collaboration, this analysis extends to the advice of the AI: it helps humans recognize systematic errors, their own and the AI’s, and learn from them [20]. Research on reflection distinguishes between two equally important types of reflection that occur at two different points in time: reflection-in-action that occurs during decision-making, as deliberate reasoning about the case and the advice at hand, and reflection-on-action that occurs retrospectively, targeting past decision-making and the lessons drawn from its outcomes [69]. Research in human-AI collaboration has so far mainly focused on reflection-in-action: for instance, researchers have investigated whether explanations [45, 71] or uncertainty displays [36, 88] along with AI advice can support humans in critically assessing AI advice. While reflection-in-action can support understanding of specific advice [14, 67] and may even enable implicit learning of effective collaboration strategies over time [48, 82], reflection-on-action is equally important to calibrate collaboration strategies. Reflection-on-action can build on what reflection-in-action lacks, the outcomes of past decisions. In real-world decision-making, whether a decision was correct is unknown when it is made [28] and becomes apparent only later, once its outcome can be observed, often after a substantial delay. For example, a loan officer might approve a loan though the AI advised rejection, thereby overriding the AI advice. Later, the loan officer would likely learn whether the loan was repaid and could then retrospectively reflect on whether and why their own judgment or the AI advice was correct. Such feedback on past overrides provides information on whether (and why) the user’s overrides of AI advice were successful (i.e., led to better outcomes) or unsuccessful (i.e., led to worse outcomes). Feedback on past overrides can be applied to any situation in which, after a decision is made, its actual outcome can be observed. Feedback in general has been attributed an important role in HCI literature [54]. For instance, Förster et al. [27] and Spitzer et al. [72] investigated the effectiveness of feedback provided through AI on human learning. Ma et al. [48] explored the impact of calibration-status feedback, i.e., feedback indicating whether the accuracy of human decisions matches their confidence, on reliance. However, the role of feedback on past overrides, i.e., on whether disagreeing with the AI advice led to superior outcomes, has so far been overlooked. We aim to address this important gap by posing the following research questions (RQ): 

RQ1 : How does feedback on the outcomes of past overrides of AI advice affect human reflection? 

RQ2 : How does feedback on the outcomes of past overrides of AI advice affect subsequent reliance behavior and human-AI team performance? To address these questions, we conducted a behavioral experiment in which participants evaluated loan applications with the support of an AI system over three rounds. Drawing on theory on reflection [69], reliance behavior [67], and feedback effectiveness [30, 51], we designed interventions that provided participants with systematic feedback on their (un)successful overrides of AI advice after the first two rounds. Specifically, participants received feedback on past overrides of AI advice that led to (i) correct decisions (successful), (ii) incorrect decisions (unsuccessful), or (iii) both correct and incorrect decisions (balanced), or (iv) they received no feedback in our control condition. Our findings indicate that feedback on past overrides can induce reflection-on-action. However, for our unidirectional feedback conditions (only successful or unsuccessful overrides), we find lower subsequent reflection-in-action, sig-nificantly so after unsuccessful overrides. These cognitive changes accompany changes in reliance behavior, where feedback on successful overrides is associated with marginally lower adherence to AI advice, while feedback on unsuccessful overrides is associated with more adherence to incorrect AI advice. Although these behavioral changes do not translate into significant changes in overall human-AI team performance, subgroup analysis reveals context-dependent differences. Our findings make three important contributions: (1) We address a critical gap in the literature by introducing interventions that allow the investigation of reflection-on-action in human-AI collaboration, complementing the growing body of work that predominantly focuses on reflection-in-action. (2) Drawing on reliance theory, we translate established frameworks into feedback interventions on past overrides that provide systematic information about past collaboration outcomes and that move explanations from the moment of decision to the review of past overrides. (3) We show that feedback on past overrides can increase reflection-on-action and is associated with changes in reliance behavior, indicating that the two forms of reflection interact and that selective feedback can itself miscalibrate reliance, insights that inform future human-AI collaboration system design. 2Think Once, Reflect Twice A P REPRINT 

# 2 Foundations and Related Work 

Reliance and Reflection in Human-AI Collaboration. Research on human-AI collaboration has extensively in-vestigated the reliance behavior of humans in AI-assisted decision-making scenarios [65, 67, 68]. Ideally, humans selectively follow AI advice when it is correct and override it when it is incorrect. However, a substantial body of work has documented the persistent problem of overreliance, where humans systematically follow AI advice even when their independent judgment would yield superior outcomes [32, 77]. Conversely, underreliance occurs when humans systematically dismiss and override AI advice, even in situations where such advice would improve decision quality. Ma et al. [48] show that such inappropriate reliance often emerges from a mismatch between humans’ self-confidence and their perception of the AI’s competence. Thus, calibration of humans’ perception of their own abilities and the AI’s abilities is essential for appropriate reliance [48]. A key mechanism towards appropriate reliance is reflective practice in decision-making [13, 47]. An important distinction is the timing of reflection within the decision-making process. Schön [69] distinguishes between reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action . Reflection-in-action is a situational, real-time form of reflection that occurs during the decision-making process and before a decision has been made, enabling immediate reframing and adjustment of cognitive approaches. In contrast, reflection-on-action refers to the retrospective examination of decision-making, in which individuals analyze their actions after completing a task, thereby enabling the reframing and adjustment of cognitive approaches for future decisions. Reflective practice in human-AI collaboration has gained growing attention in literature [1, 10]. For instance, Taudien et al. [75] highlight the critical role of metacognition, describing humans’ ability to reflect on their own cognitive approaches, including perception and decision-making. Ideally, reflection leads to calibration of humans’ perception of their own abilities and the AI’s abilities and, accordingly, adjustment of reliance behavior [82]. However, even if humans exhibit a high degree of metacognition, they can still be prone to cognitive shortcuts, which can lead to poor decisions [31]. Thus, HCI research has explored how interactive systems, such as those used in human-AI collaboration, can prompt reflection, demonstrating the need for deliberate “breakdowns” (i.e., situations of surprise) and support of “inquiry” to trigger reflection [9, 18, 25]. Abdel-Karim et al. [1] have brought forward the idea that AI can serve as a reflection partner, proposing a grounded, theory-informed model of AI-induced reflection. 

Reflection Mechanisms in Human-AI Collaboration. So far, research has examined initial mechanisms that trigger reflection in human-AI collaboration, as shown in Table 1. Researchers have predominantly used experimental studies to investigate mechanisms such as explanations and confidence displays alongside AI advice. Findings suggest that the provision of confidence or uncertainty displays can improve trust calibration and have positive effects on humans’ reliance behavior [83, 88]. Researchers have also identified positive effects of explanations on trust and confidence calibration [5], especially when they were applied in combination with other mechanisms, such as cognitive forcing interventions and case-specific confidence information [13, 29, 83, 88]. Critically, the framing and presentation of uncertainty [36] and explanations [19, 29] can influence their effectiveness. Existing literature also indicated that the impact of reflection mechanisms is highly dependent on contextual and individual factors. Bansal et al. [6] highlight mental models as a key human factor and investigate the effect of key properties of AI and tasks on mental models. In the study by Spitzer et al. [73], humans’ cognitive styles and verbal ability moderated how effectively they processed explanations and reflected on AI advice. Accordingly, researchers emphasize the importance of communication style [19, 47]. For instance, deliberative AI—enabling humans to discuss with the AI—was found more effective for appropriate reliance than explainable AI [47]. Similarly, Danry et al. [19] found that explanations that are framed as questions have a positive effect. While updates on the side of the AI model in human-AI teams, as well, can trigger reflection, Bansal et al. [7] claim the need for compatibility of updates with mental models. Ma et al. [48] specifically studied mechanisms for confidence calibration using different interventions, such as asking for opposite arguments, thinking in bets, and calibration status feedback. They find that all three interventions have a positive influence on human performance and reduce under-reliance. In sum, the literature emphasizes the critical role of reflection mechanisms, primarily the application of explainable AI and confidence displays, in calibrating humans’ perception of their own abilities and AI’s abilities and adjusting their reliance behavior. Most existing studies examine mechanisms that take effect alongside AI advice before a decision is made, which Schön [69] call reflection-in-action. The few studies that also address reflection-on-action examine calibration-status feedback on humans’ confidence [48] or updates of the AI model [7], and neither gives humans feedback on the outcomes of their own overrides of AI advice. So far, the role of reflection-on-action, i.e., the retrospective evaluation of decision-making in collaboration with the AI, remains underexplored in human-AI collaboration research. In particular, feedback on past overrides as a potential reflection mechanism influencing reliance behavior has so far been neglected. # 3 Theoretical Development 

Reflection has been established as a cornerstone for effective learning and performance improvement across do-mains [69]. In education, reflection enhances learning outcomes by helping students identify knowledge gaps and adjust learning strategies [30]. In professional contexts, reflection enables practitioners to recognize patterns in their decision-making and calibrate their expertise over time [81]. The effectiveness of reflection stems from its ability to activate metacognitive processes that help individuals recognize and correct systematic biases in their reasoning [24]. These benefits of reflection extend to human-AI collaboration, where users must critically evaluate both their own judgment and AI advice to make effective decisions. Prior work supports this evaluation during decision-making, for instance through explanations [46, 71] and cognitive forcing functions [13]. We instead target reflection-on-action, which becomes possible once the ground truth of past decisions is available. We assume that feedback on past overrides is a critical component for effective reflection-on-action, as it provides information on whether and why past decision-making was successful [30, 51]. In human-AI collaboration, cognitive approaches mainly refer to humans’ collaboration strategies, i.e., strategies humans develop about whether they should adhere to AI advice or override it. Thus, reflection-on-action translates into reflection on when and why humans successfully or unsuccessfully override AI advice, with the aim of learning from errors for future collaboration [76]. We assume that providing feedback on past overrides, which reveals when and why AI advice was correct or incorrect compared to the human’s decision, facilitates such cognitive engagement. Therefore, we formulate our first hypothesis: 

Hypothesis 1: Feedback on past overrides, revealing when and why the AI diverged from the human’s decision, will increase reflection-on-action. 

Organizational [56] and psychological research [40] suggest that feedback can improve human performance when communicated appropriately. Research demonstrates that feedback enhances individuals’ awareness of decision consequences in complex settings, enabling them to identify and adopt more efficient alternatives [15]. In collaborative decision-making contexts, such as human-AI collaboration, this mechanism translates into how individuals calibrate their reliance on different information sources, such as AI advice, based on the outcomes of past decision-making [4]. When individuals receive information about the consequences of adhering to or overriding advice, they update their beliefs about when to rely on their own judgment versus external input. We thus expect that feedback interventions also translate into behavioral changes in collaborative decision-making settings [55]. We propose that feedback on past overrides can influence reliance calibration by providing information about when and why AI advice was correct or incorrect compared to the human’s judgment. Specifically, feedback on unsuccessful overrides should increase reliance by revealing missed opportunities, while feedback on successful overrides should decrease reliance by reinforcing appropriate skepticism, leading to our second hypothesis: 

Hypothesis 2: Humans who receive feedback on their unsuccessful past overrides of AI advice will show increased reliance on AI advice, while humans who receive feedback on their successful past overrides of AI advice will show lower reliance on AI advice. 

Appropriate reliance behavior directly translates into improved human-AI team performance, as better calibrated collaboration strategies enable such teams to leverage their complementary strengths [33, 65]. If feedback on past overrides successfully improves reliance calibration, we should observe corresponding performance improvements. Meta-analyses provide evidence that feedback interventions improve performance when they focus on task-relevant processes rather than self-evaluation [40]. Educational research further confirms that feedback highlighting both successful and unsuccessful decision patterns enables learners to develop more accurate mental models of task requirements [70]. We assume that, in human-AI collaboration, feedback on unsuccessful overrides will decrease 5Think Once, Reflect Twice A P REPRINT 

missed opportunities, while feedback on successful overrides will reduce the adoption of mistakes from AI advice in future decisions. Combining both assumptions, feedback on past overrides that contains both humans’ successful and unsuccessful overrides (i.e., balanced feedback) should result in even higher performance gains. Accordingly, we hypothesize: 

Hypothesis 3: Humans who receive feedback on their past overrides of the AI advice (feedback on successful overrides, feedback on unsuccessful overrides, and balanced feedback) achieve higher human-AI team performance, with balanced feedback leading to the highest performance gains. 

# 4 Experimental Design 

Our aim is to provide initial insights into the underexplored area of how feedback on past overrides of AI advice affects human-AI collaboration. To this end, we conduct an IRB-approved behavioral experiment examining both subjective and objective variables on reflection, reliance behavior, and human-AI team performance. For our experiment, we instantiated an AI system and an XAI method to generate AI advice and explanations based on real data on loan applications. We systematically manipulate the feedback on past overrides, resulting in four experimental groups: two groups receiving unidirectional feedback (either successful overrides or unsuccessful overrides), one group receiving balanced feedback (successful and unsuccessful overrides), and a control group receiving no feedback. The study follows a convergent mixed-methods design with quantitative priority [23]. The experiment tests our hypotheses, and the open-ended responses, collected in the same post-task questionnaire, serve to explain and extend the quantitative results. 

Task and Dataset. Following established practice in HCI research on human-AI collaboration, we selected loan approval as our experimental task [38, 78, 86], specifically the Home Credit Default Risk dataset from Kaggle [41]. The task involves evaluating multiple complex factors, such as income, credit history, and debt-to-income ratios, that benefit from algorithmic support while remaining accessible to participants even without extensive domain training. Based on feature importance rankings from a decision tree trained on the full dataset, we select eight key features: demographic characteristics (gender, marital status, education, number of dependents), financial indicators (household income, loan amount, loan duration), and credit history.

Explanations. Each AI recommendation came with an explanation that listed the three features contributing most to it according to SHAP [46]. We presented a ranked list without numerical values to keep the explanations accessible to participants without a technical background [16], and an informal pretest with ten people outside the study confirmed that the rankings conveyed the AI’s reasoning. Since our focus is on feedback on past overrides, the explanation format was identical across conditions. 

Treatments. We employ a between-subjects design with four conditions across three rounds. In the Control condition, participants complete all 18 loan approval decisions without receiving any feedback about their performance or overrides of AI advice. The three feedback conditions provide systematic feedback on past overrides of AI advice after rounds 1 and 2, highlighting instances where participants disagreed with the AI advice and whether the final decision was correct or incorrect. Specifically, there are two unidirectional feedback conditions: in the Successful condition, feedback shows those instances of the previous round where participants disagreed with the AI but made the correct decision according to ground truth; in the Unsuccessful condition, feedback shows those instances of the previous round where participants disagreed with the AI but made incorrect decisions. The Balanced condition combines both types of feedback, showing participants all instances of the previous round where participants successfully or unsuccessfully overrode the AI advice. Participants in each feedback condition were explicitly informed about the selective nature of their feedback during the introduction phase. We present all feedback on past overrides as a brief summary after the first and second rounds, showing, for each instance, the original case details, AI advice, SHAP-based explanation, participant decision, and ground truth. Figure 1b visualizes the feedback interfaces for the different Treatments. We restrict the feedback to overrides and thus to two of the four cases of the reliance taxonomy. An override is the only case in which the participant’s judgment and the AI advice diverge, so its outcome shows which of the two was right and makes the participant’s decision to disagree with the AI the object of reflection. Under adherence, the participant’s decision coincides with the AI advice, and its outcome shows whether the AI was right but not whether the participant’s own judgment would have differed. We discuss the consequences of omitting adherence outcomes in Section 6.2. 

Study Procedure. As shown in Figure 2, the experiment starts with a welcome and task introduction where participants receive detailed instructions about the loan approval decision-making task. Participants then complete a cognitive reflection test to assess their tendency to engage in reflective thinking [26], followed by pre-task measurements of perceived self-efficacy and perceived AI efficacy [74] and task familiarity. Next, participants are randomly allocated to one of the four conditions and receive an introduction to the feedback interface (for participants in treatment conditions only). Following this, all participants work through a single practice example that demonstrates the interface, including the case details and AI advice, along with a SHAP-based explanation to ensure familiarity with the task. In addition, this practice example serves as a comprehension check. The main experimental phase consists of three rounds of six randomly selected loan approval decisions each. To ensure ecological validity, we delivered feedback in rounds to simulate the natural delay between a decision and its outcome. Upon completion of all three rounds, participants are 7Think Once, Reflect Twice A P REPRINT 

asked to complete a post-questionnaire. We distribute four attention checks across the study procedure, including one within the task instances and three within questionnaire items, all requiring participants to select specified responses. Feedback is generated from participants’ own overrides, so participants who override the AI more often also receive more feedback (Section D). 

Measurement. To assess how feedback on past overrides affects reflection, we measure reflection-in-action through system 2 thinking [53], and we measure retrospective reflection-on-action through perceived learning from errors [61] in the post-task questionnaire. We measure both constructs after the task, since concurrent approaches such as think-aloud alter the behavior they are meant to record. We therefore selected the scale of Novak and Hoffman [53], which was developed for retrospective self-assessment of whole task episodes. Since both scales rest on participants’ own perception, we complement them with two objective anchors, i.e., the cognitive reflection test before the task and page-level timestamps (see Section B). We operationalize reflection-on-action as learning from the outcomes of past decisions and measure it with the Error Orientation Questionnaire of Rybowiak et al. [61], an established instrument whose items are framed around recognizing and learning from errors. We retained the item wording apart from the reference to the task. Its error framing may understate reflection-on-action under feedback on successful overrides (Section 6.4). To assess how feedback on past overrides affects reliance and human-AI team performance, we measure the accuracy of decisions by comparing participants’ decisions and AI advice with ground truth labels from the dataset. We operationalize reliance as observed behavior based on Schoeffer et al. [67]. Therefore, we compare a participant’s final decision with the AI advice for every decision. This yields adherence when the two coincide and an override when they diverge. Crossing this with the correctness of the AI advice results in four cases: correct adherence, detrimental adherence, corrective overriding, and detrimental overriding. We report reliance as the share of decisions in which a participant adhered to the AI advice, and our feedback on past overrides draws on the two overriding cases of this taxonomy. We refer to the accuracy of the final decisions as human-AI team performance, following research on AI-assisted decision-making [8, 65]. The term describes the joint outcome of a human deciding with AI advice and does not presuppose that participants perceive the AI as a teammate. As control variables, we used the cognitive reflection test, perceived self-efficacy and AI efficacy, as well as task familiarity. Since the self-efficacy and AI-efficacy items are repeated after the task, we can also compute how both beliefs changed over the course of the task (see Section 5.2.5). Furthermore, we collected socio-demographic data in the post-task questionnaire as control variables. An overview of variables, including all items per construct, is provided in Table 16 in the Appendix. 

Qualitative Analysis. Our qualitative analysis pursued two complementary aims: a confirmatory one, triangulating our quantitative findings, and an exploratory one, shedding light on the participants’ reasoning and reflection processes. To that end, we asked open-ended questions in the post-task questionnaire addressing the decision cues participants drew on and the effects the feedback had on their beliefs and behavior, adapting questions from He et al. [31]. Table 16 in the Appendix lists the questions. We analyzed the responses using codebook thematic analysis [12]. We developed a two-facet codebook through a hybrid deductive–inductive process [22]. Seed codes for the effects facet were derived from the theoretical constructs underlying our experimental measures (reliance, AI-efficacy, self-efficacy, reflection-in-action, and reflection-on-action) and were then refined and extended inductively. The decision cues facet was coded purely inductively, without a predefined template. Given the brevity of open-ended responses, we treated each participant’s full response to a question as the unit of coding, allowing multiple codes per response; codes were then grouped into themes. Two authors independently coded a stratified subset of 108 responses (20% of the corpus) using the final codebook. As responses can carry multiple themes, we assessed agreement with Krippendorff’s multi-valued 

α (mvα ) [43], which extends Krippendorff’s α [42] to units coded with sets of values. On the independent codings, agreement at the theme level was mvα = .80 . Per-theme agreement (binary Krippendorff’s α) ranged from .73 to .89. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. The remaining responses were coded in an LLM-assisted setup with Claude (Opus 5.5), where all final codes were verified by one author. We report inter-rater reliability between both human coders and the LLM-assisted coding using Krippendorff’s multi-valued α, yielding mvα Coder 1−LLM = .84 

and mvα Coder 2−LLM = .76 , respectively. We then used the coding in two complementary ways. First, we quantified code and theme prevalences for effects [17] and compared them across conditions to examine whether the qualitative patterns converged with our quantitative findings. Second, we analyzed the themes qualitatively to surface patterns not anticipated by our hypotheses. 

Participants. We recruited participants through Prolific.co who were fluent in English, US residents, and maintained a high approval rating on the platform. We excluded participants who failed more than one attention check, the comprehension check, or always followed the AI advice without deviation, indicating no task engagement (n=6). The final sample consisted of 244 participants equally distributed over the four conditions. Participants completed the study in a median time of 16.41 minutes and received a base payment of $2.25 and a bonus payment of five cents per correct decision, resulting in an average payout of $2.76. The final sample consists of 49.6% male and 50.4% female participants with an average age of 44.09 years. # 5 Results 

Section 5.1 examines how feedback on past overrides affects reflection-on-action and reflection-in-action ( RQ1 ). Section 5.2 turns to reliance and human-AI team performance ( RQ2 ), separates both by the correctness of the AI advice, and tests whether changes in perceived AI efficacy and self-efficacy mediate the reliance shifts. Section 5.3 reports the themes in participants’ open-ended responses, and Section 5.4 uses them to explain results that the quantitative analysis leaves open. 

5.1 Reflective Processes: In-Action and On-Action 

To understand how our feedback on past overrides influences participants’ reflection ( RQ1 ), we first examine whether they successfully activate the intended cognitive process of retrospective reflection-on-action. Additionally, as the feedback on past overrides may also influence reflection during decision-making, we measure participants’ reflection-in-action. When shown their own errors, participants revise their view of the AI more readily than their view of themselves. 

Decreased perceptions of the AI’s efficacy are reported most frequently in the successful condition ( “[...] the AI was not always correct and couldn[‘]t be fully relied on” , P1451), and increased perceptions most frequently in the unsuccessful condition ( “The information made me have more confidence in the AI’s decisions” , P1414). This symmetry follows from the feedback, since feedback on successful overrides displays cases in which the AI was wrong and the human was correct, whereas feedback on unsuccessful overrides displays cases in which the AI was correct and the human was wrong. Perceived self-efficacy shows no such symmetry as participants frequently report increased self-efficacy after successful overrides, but reports of decreased self-efficacy remain rare in all conditions, including the unsuccessful condition. A stable self-efficacy, however, does not imply growing confidence in one’s own judgment, because participants may come to distrust the AI for particular cases without trusting themselves more. These findings complement our quantitative results, in which only change in perceived AI efficacy mediates the effect of feedback on reliance (Section 5.2.5).

6.1 Feedback on Past Overrides Raises Reflection-on-Action but Can Narrow Reflection-in-Action 

Our findings indicate that feedback on past overrides induces reflection-on-action (partially supporting H1), with participants in the balanced and unsuccessful feedback conditions reporting enhanced learning from errors. This pattern follows the reasoning behind H1, according to which feedback that reveals when and why the AI diverged from one’s own decision provides the material for learning from errors [30, 51]. Feedback on successful overrides displays no errors of the participant, which is consistent with its smaller and non-significant increase. At the same time, unidirectional feedback is associated with lower reflection-in-action during subsequent decisions (significant for unsuccessful, marginal for successful feedback). Sensemaking theory provides a possible explanation for this pattern [84], which our hypotheses did not anticipate. Effective learning requires receiving information that challenges existing beliefs, creating psychological tension that ultimately results in adaptations of mental models that inform decision-making [21, 39, 60]. In this context, unidirectional explanatory feedback provides unambiguous signals about collaboration effectiveness that may have eliminated this cognitive dissonance. When participants consistently see evidence that they were wrong (unsuccessful feedback) or consistently right (successful feedback), compared to the AI, they may develop simplified collaboration heuristics instead of maintaining critical evaluation of each decision, reducing reflection-in-action during subsequent decisions. Balanced feedback, however, may maintain this productive cognitive dissonance required for sensemaking by presenting both successful and unsuccessful override outcomes. This 16 Think Once, Reflect Twice A P REPRINT 

balanced evidence, therefore, may preserve the psychological tension necessary for sustained reflection-in-action while still activating retrospective reflection-on-action processes. The open-ended responses indicate that participants engaged less with feedback that showed only the AI’s errors. Participants who received feedback on successful overrides least often referred to the individual cases shown to them, whereas participants with balanced feedback referred mainly to the cases they had gotten wrong. This difference fits the non-significant increase in reflection-on-action under successful feedback. Yet participants reflected on more than errors, describing improved understanding of the AI and the development of their decision strategy alongside a better understanding of the task. For reflection-in-action, the responses agree with the scale under feedback on unsuccessful overrides, where reports of a more deliberate approach are no more frequent than under balanced feedback. After successful overrides, participants report a more deliberate approach most often and describe weighing their own reasoning against the AI as often as analyzing cases more carefully. Feedback on successful overrides thus appears to direct deliberation toward particular cases in which the AI may err, which a scale of effortful processing across the whole task does not register as higher reflection-in-action. These findings advance HCI research on human-AI collaboration and reflection, which has so far focused mainly on reflection-in-action [13, 19, 29, 47, 88]. We show that feedback on past overrides can increase reflection-on-action and that a mechanism targeting one form of reflection also changes the other. Schön [69] conceives of reflection-on-action as preparation for future reflection-in-action, whereas our findings suggest that this coupling can also turn negative, consistent with retrospective feedback replacing case-by-case deliberation with a simplified heuristic. Unidirectional feedback may thus remove the breakdowns that reflection requires [9, 25, 84], whereas balanced feedback preserves them. Reflection interventions therefore need to be evaluated for both forms of reflection. For designers, this interaction matters because feedback in practice is often selective, for instance in credit lending, where repayment is only observed for approved loans and a review of a loan officer’s overrides can therefore only cover overrides that led to an approval. However, system designers should be aware that feedback restricted in this way can raise reflection-on-action and at the same time lower reflection-in-action. Where the available outcomes allow it, a balanced review, showing an officer both the loans approved against the assessment that were repaid and those that defaulted, may avoid this narrowing. 

6.2 Unidirectional Feedback Shifts Reliance Through Different Mechanisms 

Under the two unidirectional conditions, adherence shifts in opposite directions, as H2 predicts. It is lower under feedback on successful overrides (marginally, p = 0 .064 ) and descriptively higher under feedback on unsuccessful overrides ( p = 0 .118 ), and the two conditions differ from each other by a medium effect ( d = 0 .59 ). H2 rests on the reasoning that feedback on past overrides informs users about when to rely on their own judgment and when on the AI [4, 55]. The mediation analyses support a belief update of this kind for feedback on unsuccessful overrides only, so the two shifts are symmetric in the behavioral data but asymmetric in their mechanism. Feedback on unsuccessful overrides shifts adherence upward through what participants believe about the AI. The indirect effect through change in perceived AI efficacy explains the association in full and leaves nothing for a direct path, although adherence itself increases only descriptively and the total effect does not reach significance. This is the kind of competence calibration Ma et al. [48] describe, but here it calibrates toward the wrong target, since the feedback reflects a biased sample of cases. By construction, participants see only those cases in which they overrode and the AI turned out to be right, so they update accurately on a sample that misrepresents the AI. Every case a participant saw showed the AI outperforming their own judgment, which leaves increased adherence as close to the only update the feedback permits. The condition therefore shows that participants update on the sample of cases they are shown, and not that they derive a reliance strategy through reflection. The consequence is a decision-maker who follows the AI more often, including when the AI is wrong, which is the pattern the context-dependent analysis isolates ( β = −0.83 ,

p = 0 .03 ). Feedback on successful overrides works differently, and its direct effect on reliance survives a belief-mediated path that runs the other way. Compared with the control condition, perceived AI efficacy declines less under feedback on successful overrides, descriptively also under balanced feedback, and increases under feedback on unsuccessful overrides, and participants with a more positive change in perceived AI efficacy adhere more to the AI advice. The mediated path therefore pushes adherence up while the direct path pushes it down, so the reduction in adherence occurs against the belief participants form about the AI and not through it. Perceived self-efficacy does not differ between conditions, which speaks against a general rise in confidence in one’s own judgment as the driver of the behavior. What the direct path consists of remains open, because a null indirect effect speaks against the two global beliefs in our questionnaire but not against every belief a participant might revise. Bansal et al. [6] describe users’ mental models of the AI’s error boundary, that is, beliefs about the kinds of cases in which the AI errs, and feedback that displays only errors of the AI may revise such a model locally. 17 Think Once, Reflect Twice A P REPRINT 

The open-ended responses agree with these mechanisms, since participants’ accounts of their reliance shift in the same directions as their behavior, with reduced reliance reported most often after successful overrides and increased reliance most often after unsuccessful overrides. When shown their own errors, participants revised their view of the AI more readily than their view of themselves. A more favorable view of the AI was reported most often after unsuccessful overrides, whereas reports of lower confidence in oneself were nearly absent, although this feedback displayed precisely the cases in which participants erred. This pattern fits the full mediation through perceived AI efficacy. After successful overrides, participants most often reported increased confidence in themselves. Many of them described it in relation to the AI and tied it to particular kinds of cases, such as applicants whose marital status the AI weighted too heavily. The general self-efficacy scale does not ask about such case-bound confidence, which may explain why it registers no change. These accounts point to a local revision of the AI’s error boundary, although confirming it quantitatively requires case-level measures of perceived AI competence that our design does not provide. Prior research on appropriate reliance traces miscalibrated reliance to two sources, the human, for example through self-confidence [48, 75] or an illusion of competence [31], and the AI, for example through model updates that are incompatible with users’ mental models [7]. Our findings extend this view by pointing to feedback as a third source, since feedback on unsuccessful overrides miscalibrated reliance while the AI remained unchanged. Compatibility, which Bansal et al. [7] establish for model updates, is thus also a property of feedback. The findings also qualify explanations that attribute appropriate reliance to the match between humans’ confidence in themselves and in the AI [48, 82], because reliance under feedback on successful overrides shifted against the measured belief about the AI. For explainable AI, they add a dimension to the design space, which has so far focused on the content [19, 29] and format [16, 36] of explanations, namely the selection of which cases are fed back. With the explanation of each case held constant, different selections led to different effects on reflection and reliance. For designers, the selection of feedback cases is thus a decision with consequences for reliance. Feedback restricted to overrides keeps the review short but hands users a biased sample and omits the most frequent error, following incorrect AI advice. Feedback on unsuccessful adherence shows the AI failing where the user deferred to it and should lower adherence for the same reason that feedback on unsuccessful overrides raises it, so a review of every error of the human-AI team may correct this bias while covering only about a third of decisions. 

6.3 More Reflection Does Not Mean Better Reliance Decisions 

The reliance shifts do not translate into changes in human-AI team performance. H3 assumed that feedback on past overrides improves performance by calibrating reliance, so that users follow the AI when its advice is correct and override it when it is wrong [65]. However, the context-dependent analysis (subset by AI advice correctness) points to a mechanism behind these patterns, where opposing effects among varying AI accuracies are buried on a global level. When AI advice is correct, participants receiving feedback about successful overrides decrease the human-AI team performance, marginally ( p = 0 .095 ). Conversely, when AI advice is incorrect, participants receiving feedback about unsuccessful overrides achieve lower human-AI team performance. Neither unidirectional condition thus shows the pattern H3 presupposed, more adherence to correct and less adherence to incorrect advice. These opposing effects partially mask each other on a global level, which may explain the limited global performance improvements that led to the rejection of our third hypothesis (H3) on human-AI team performance. Balanced feedback shows neither error pattern and the highest adjusted performance, marginally above control ( p = 0 .068 ), which is the direction H3 predicted for feedback that combines successful and unsuccessful overrides. These findings on reliance patterns together with the dissonance of perceived increases in reflection-on-action and lowered reflection-in-action point to the risk of an illusion of learning when unidirectional feedback is provided. While participants report higher reflection-on-action, they simultaneously report lower concurrent reflection-in-action, consistent with simplified reliance strategies, as adherence was descriptively higher under feedback on unsuccessful overrides and marginally lower under feedback on successful overrides. This is consistent with the context-dependent analyses, which show decreased performance. Our results point to this risk most clearly under feedback on unsuccessful overrides, where both shifts in reflection are significant. The illusion of learning extends prior literature that has highlighted the risk arising when perceived understanding does not align with objective understanding [16, 85] to feedback on past overrides. Our performance results suggest that activating reflection-on-action alone may be insufficient, since its effectiveness appears contingent on maintaining concurrent reflection-in-action. The open-ended responses help explain why higher reflection-on-action did not lead to better reliance decisions. Participants most often reported lessons about the task, such as why they had misjudged a case or how much weight the applicants’ attributes deserve, and few reported a strategy for when to follow or override the AI. A better model of the task does not indicate which advice to override, so reported reflection-on-action need not translate into better reliance decisions. Participants who received feedback on unsuccessful overrides also most often described the influence of the 18 Think Once, Reflect Twice A P REPRINT 

feedback as marginal, although this condition shows the clearest effects on reflection and on adherence to incorrect AI advice. Feedback can thus shape reliance in ways that users do not attribute to it. Explainable AI research has studied explanations as support for evaluating a single piece of advice before a decision and has assessed them by their effect on that decision [45, 63, 71]. We extend this research by moving explanations into feedback on past overrides, after the outcome is known, where they no longer answer whether to follow a given piece of advice but show where the AI’s errors lie relative to one’s own. Unlike explanations in AI-provided feedback on task performance [27, 72], they are meant to support learning about the collaboration itself across cases [6, 35]. Our participants drew more lessons about the task than about the AI from them, which marks the gap between what such explanations offer and what users take from them. Schoeffer et al. [66, 68] argue that reliance and accuracy are interdependent. We extend this argument to feedback interventions, whose opposing effects on cases with correct and incorrect AI advice can cancel out in the aggregate, which implies that intervention studies should report effects separately by the correctness of the AI advice. Our approach operationalizes the existing framework for analyzing reliance behavior of Schoeffer et al. [67] by translating it into an actionable feedback intervention for participants. Since participants in our study drew lessons mostly about the task, designers may need to make the reliance decision itself the object of such feedback, for example by summarizing in which kinds of cases the user’s overrides succeeded and in which they failed. How feedback can support both forms of reflection without producing an illusion of learning is an open question for future research. 

6.4 Limitations and Future Research Reflection-in-action is measured retrospectively. Participants reported on System 2 thinking after completing the task, so the measure records how they recall processing the decisions and not the processing itself. Both reflection measures were collected at the same point in time, so the temporal distinction between the two constructs is not mirrored in their measurement. Time spent on the decision pages and the pre-task cognitive reflection test both correlate with System 2 thinking and not with perceived learning from errors (Section B), which is the extent of the independent evidence available in this design. Participants’ own reports and the measured effects also do not always align, since participants who received feedback on unsuccessful overrides most often reported that the feedback influenced them only marginally, although this condition shows the clearest effects on reflection-in-action and on adherence to incorrect AI advice. Process-tracing measures such as think-aloud protocols or eye-tracking capture concurrent processing directly, and a laboratory replication using them would test whether the reduction in reflection-in-action under unidirectional feedback appears outside self-report. 

Measurement considerations for reflection-on-action. Our measure of retrospective reflection focused on error-based learning through items explicitly referencing mistakes (e.g., “recognizing my errors,” “learning from errors”). That wording fits the conditions whose feedback displayed unsuccessful overrides, and it plausibly understates retrospective reflection in the successful condition, where the feedback displayed correct overrides. A participant who analyzed in depth why their own judgment outperformed the AI engaged in reflection-on-action and would still rate error-oriented items low, because the experience contained no recognizable errors. The intermediate adjusted mean of the successful condition ( μ = 4 .52 ), between control ( μ = 4 .21 ) and the two error-viewing conditions ( μ = 4 .68 ), is consistent with this reading without establishing it. The open-ended responses support it. Participants who received feedback on successful overrides reported an improved understanding of the AI (29.5%) and the development of a decision strategy (31.1%) about as often as participants in the other feedback conditions, but an improved understanding of the task, which the error-oriented items address most directly, less often (36.1% compared with 50.8% and 63.9%). A measure covering both valences of retrospective learning would separate a genuine gradient of engagement from an artifact of item wording. 

Feedback volume is endogenous to the behavior we study. Feedback on overrides can only be delivered for cases in which a participant overrode the AI, so the number of feedback instances a participant received is a function of their own override rate, which is the very behavior the intervention is meant to shift. Including feedback exposure as a covariate does not fully resolve this, because that covariate is itself downstream of the disposition it is meant to control for. Since control participants receive no feedback by design, we examine feedback volume within the three feedback conditions. There, feedback count predicts none of the outcomes, and including it leaves the differences between feedback conditions in reliance unchanged (Section D). Participants who received more feedback did not report more learning from errors, which speaks against the amount of feedback as the driver of the reflection-on-action effect, although the design cannot vary the amount independently of participants’ behavior. The dependency is structural. Any system that delivers feedback on overrides faces it, and a user who rarely overrides generates little material to reflect on. Future designs could enforce a minimum number of feedback cases or extend feedback to adherence cases. 

Our findings are bounded by the conditions of our study. Our study examined reflection-on-action under specific conditions, namely moderate AI accuracy (62.5%), novice participants without domain expertise, brief collaboration 19 Think Once, Reflect Twice A P REPRINT 

periods of 18 decisions over approximately 15 minutes, and SHAP-based feature importance explanations. Domain expertise shapes how individuals process and integrate AI advice and explanations [50, 89], with experts drawing on prior knowledge and more developed mental models that enable more selective reliance than the novices in our study [6, 35]. AI accuracy shapes how trust forms and how it evolves during collaboration [37]. Our moderate-performing system likely created different learning dynamics than participants would experience with either highly accurate or poor-performing AI. The explanations we provided further shaped this dynamic, as feature importance approaches like SHAP, which we presented as ranked feature lists, support particular cognitive processes that may interact differently with retrospective feedback on past overrides than alternative explanation types such as counterfactual explanations [29] or uncertainty and confidence information [36, 88]. Trust calibration and reliance strategies typically require extended interaction periods to fully develop and stabilize [37]. These contextual factors may help explain why reflection-on-action, while increased under balanced and unsuccessful feedback, did not translate into performance improvements. The temporal constraints of our design may have been insufficient for participants to develop and apply nuanced mental models based on retrospective feedback on past overrides. Our controlled experiment with a simplified loan approval task may not capture the complexity and stakes of real-world human-AI collaboration applications. Future research should therefore examine feedback on past overrides and reflection-on-action in field studies with domain experts using real-world AI systems, particularly investigating how long-term collaboration development unfolds in actual work environments where consequences extend beyond experimental participation. Longitudinal studies with repeated feedback over more rounds could also distinguish whether the effects of feedback on past overrides accumulate or reflect anchoring on the most recent feedback, which our three rounds could not separate. 

# 7 Conclusion 

This work introduces feedback on past overrides as a mechanism for reflection-on-action in human-AI collaboration, which research has so far addressed mainly through mechanisms for reflection-in-action such as explainable AI. Our findings show that both forms of reflection interact, since feedback that raises reflection after a decision can lower reflection during subsequent decisions. They also show that the selection of feedback cases matters, because feedback restricted to one type of override can itself miscalibrate reliance. Finally, more reflection does not by itself lead to better reliance decisions, since participants drew lessons mostly about the task and rarely about when to rely on the AI. Feedback that shows both successful and unsuccessful overrides avoided these pitfalls in our study, and designing feedback that makes the reliance decision itself the object of reflection remains a task for future research.

# B Validation of Reflection Measures 

Our two reflection measures—perceived learning from errors (reflection-on-action) and System 2 thinking (reflection-in-action)—are both self-reported in the post-task questionnaire. This appendix reports the analyses establishing that they are empirically distinct and that each is anchored by evidence collected independently of self-report. 

Distinctness of the two measures. Table 9 reports the correlation between the two measures within each condition. In the control condition, which receives no feedback and is therefore free of treatment effects, the association is small and not significant. The correlation is substantially larger in the unsuccessful condition, where the feedback made errors salient and cognitive effort and perceived learning from errors became coupled. This is a treatment-induced association rather than evidence of construct overlap at baseline. # C Longitudinal Analysis Across Rounds 

The main text analyzes round 3, after full feedback exposure. This appendix reports the round 2 results and a model that tests whether the treatment effect differs between rounds. 

Round 2. Replicating the round 3 ANCOVA specification on round 2 reliance, with the number of feedback instances shown after round 1 as the feedback covariate, yields no significant main effect of treatment condition ( F (3 , 235) = 1 .66 ,

p = 0 .176 ), and feedback count does not predict reliance ( p = 0 .812 ). In the planned contrasts, participants receiving feedback on successful overrides already adhere less to AI advice than control participants ( ∆ = −0.078 , p = 0 .021 ,one-sided, d = 0 .42 ), while the unsuccessful ( p = 0 .832 , one-sided) and balanced ( p = 0 .527 , two-sided) conditions do not differ from control. The reduction in adherence under successful feedback thus appears after the first wave of feedback, whereas the increase under unsuccessful feedback emerges only by round 3, in line with the unsuccessful ×

round term in the model below. 

Treatment × Round. We estimate a mixed-effects logistic regression on individual decisions in rounds 2 and 3, with treatment condition, round, and their interaction as fixed effects, round 1 reliance, cognitive reflection degree, and pre-task AI and self-efficacy as covariates, and random intercepts for participants (Table 11). Control and round 2 serve as reference levels. The Treatment × Round interaction is not significant ( χ2(3) = 5 .45 , p = 0 .142 ), and neither is the treatment term at the round 2 reference level ( χ2(3) = 4 .10 , p = 0 .251 ). Among the individual interaction terms, only the unsuccessful condition approaches significance ( b = 0 .459 , SE = 0 .243 , p = 0 .058 ), in the direction of an effect that grows from round 2 to round 3. The model neither establishes that the treatment effect is constant across rounds nor that it changes. 

Unadjusted model. A simpler specification without covariates, estimated on all three rounds, reaches the same conclusion about the interaction ( χ2(6) = 9 .88 , p = 0 .130 ). We report no treatment main effect from either interaction model. Under treatment contrasts, a Type III main effect is evaluated at the reference level of the other factor, so in the three-round model that term contrasts conditions in round 1, before any feedback was delivered. Reliance in round 1 does differ across conditions ( F (3 , 240) = 3 .09 , p = 0 .028 ), and every model in this paper adjusts for it. # D Robustness to Feedback Frequency 

Feedback is generated from participants’ own overrides, so the number of feedback instances each participant received is determined by their behavior and not by the design. Feedback volume differs across conditions in both waves (p < 0.001 ). Participants in the control condition receive no feedback by design, so their feedback count is zero, and a model that includes the count across all four conditions largely re-codes the contrast with control instead of separating the kind of feedback from its amount [49, 59]. We therefore assess the role of feedback volume within the three feedback conditions ( n = 183 ) and re-estimate the round 3 ANCOVA for each outcome with and without the exact number of feedback instances a participant received as a covariate (Table 12). Both specifications include cognitive reflection degree, pre-task AI and self-efficacy, and round 1 reliance (round 1 performance for the performance outcome), so the columns are comparable with each other but not with the models in Section 5, which include the control condition.
