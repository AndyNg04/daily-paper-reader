ABSTRACT
Nurse scheduling is a complex optimization problem in which nurses are assigned to shifts to meet
staffing demands while complying with working-time limits, qualification requirements, and other
operational and regulatory constraints [1]. Artificial intelligence is increasingly being used to support
this process by forecasting future staffing needs and providing advice on how to improve shift schedul-
ing. Effective human-AI collaboration in nurse scheduling requires users to understand, interpret,
and appropriately act on AI forecasts and advice. However, it remains insufficiently understood
how healthcare professionals use transparency mechanisms like uncertainty communication and AI
advice explanations when making staffing decisions. Applying a human-centered design approach,
we iteratively developed a prototype decision support system for nurse scheduling and evaluated it
through a mixed-methods user study with expert users. Using thematic analysis, questionnaire data,

1
Introduction
Healthcare systems worldwide are facing increasing challenges due to demographic change, rising patient numbers, and
persistent shortages in qualified healthcare professionals [2]. Nurse scheduling refers to the process of assigning nurses
to shifts over a planning horizon of typically four to ten weeks, while satisfying a range of requirements, including
legal and contractual regulations such as minimum rest periods, minimum staffing levels, as well as qualification
requirements [1]. This makes nurse scheduling a particularly complex and time-consuming [2, 3, 4] yet critical
organizational decision-making task with high impact on nurse job satisfaction [5, 4], patient satisfaction [6], and the
overall quality of hospital care [4, 7]. AI-supported systems offer a promising avenue to address these challenges by
helping planners construct schedules that better align nursing capacity with patient care demand [8, 9, 10, 11]. However,
the effectiveness of decision support systems (DSS) depends not only on predictive or optimization performance but
also on how users perceive and interact with AI-generated recommendations [12]. If recommendations are insufficiently
transparent, users may ignore useful suggestions or over-rely on them without appropriate scrutiny [8]. Particularly in
safety-critical domains such as healthcare, users remain responsible for final decisions and therefore require sufficient
information to critically assess AI-generated recommendations. Human-centered AI research has emphasized fostering
effective human–AI collaboration through the design of AI systems that promote appropriate reliance and transparency
[13, 14, 15].
Two complementary transparency mechanisms have emerged as promising directions for supporting users of AI-
supported DSS. The first is explainable AI (XAI), which aims to increase the transparency of AI advice by communicat-
ing why a particular piece of advice has been made. Explanations can help users assess the plausibility of AI advice
and increase confidence in AI-supported decisions [12]. The second mechanism is the communication of prediction
uncertainty. Predictions generated by AI systems inherently contain uncertainty arising from incomplete information,
changing conditions, or variability in the data. Nevertheless, many DSS omit uncertainty information in their interfaces,
potentially encouraging users to overestimate the system’s reliability. Communicating uncertainty has the potential to

support users in interpreting AI outputs more realistically, enabling more informed decision-making and better trust
calibration.
However, XAI and uncertainty communication come with their own pitfalls [16] and limitations [17]. For instance,
explanations are not human-understandable [18], fail to address trust or bias mitigation [18, 19], and ultimately can
lead to overtrust or mistrust [20]. Similarly, uncertainty information introduces additional cognitive demands and
may reduce usability if presented inappropriately [21]. Existing research has reported mixed findings regarding how
uncertainty visualizations affect user understanding, trust, and decision quality [22, 13, 23, 24].
Although both mechanisms are widely recognized as important design principles for trustworthy AI, they have largely
been investigated separately. Further, empirical evidence from realistic healthcare planning user studies involving
domain experts remains limited. In particular, there is little understanding of how these mechanisms impact the
acceptance and use of AI-supported nurse scheduling.
To address this gap, we conduct a mixed-methods user study including eye tracking, evaluating an AI-supported nurse
scheduling prototype we developed within a research project for clinical workforce planning. The study investigates
two complementary interface features. First, we examine how well visualizations of predictive uncertainty for time
series forecasts of care demand and staff absences are understood and how they influence decision confidence, task
performance, and usability. Second, we evaluate how explanations accompanying AI-generated nurse scheduling advice
influence acceptance, trust, decision confidence, and user experience. The study aims to answer the following research
questions:
RQ1: How well is the uncertainty information understood, and to what extent do brief explanations of how to interpret
the uncertainty information improve understanding and interpretation?
RQ2: How does uncertainty visualization influence users’ confidence in decision-making?
RQ3: How do explanations of AI recommendations influence users’ acceptance and their confidence in decision-
making?
Contribution Statement:
Our contributions to Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) [25][ch. 1.2] are:
1. Design and evaluation of an AI-supported nurse scheduling prototype: We designed and evaluated
an interactive AI-supported nurse scheduling prototype that combines time series forecasts, uncertainty
communication, visualizations of historical forecast performance, and explainable AI advice to investigate
how different forms of AI information support shift-planning decisions.
2. Empirical study of uncertainty communication and explainable AI in shift planning: We conducted a
mixed-methods user study with nurses and shift planners, showing that prediction intervals for uncertainty
communication were often misunderstood, while AI explanations improved perceived utility, but did not
significantly affect advice acceptance or decision confidence.
3. Design implications for AI-supported nurse scheduling: Based on these findings, we derived design
implications to support comprehensible uncertainty communication, concise explanations, calibrated trust,
human oversight, and AI-supported socially aware nurse scheduling.
By combining quantitative measures with qualitative insights from healthcare professionals, our work contributes
empirical evidence on the design of intelligent user interfaces for human-centered AI-supported clinical decision
support.

Related Work
2.1
Clinical Decision Support for Nurse Scheduling
Nursing workforce planning involves determining and deploying the number and qualifications of nurses required
to ensure safe patient care [26]. This process is shaped by external factors – such as legal frameworks – and internal
operational demands. Regulatory care demand assessment frameworks like Germany’s Pflegepersonalregelung 2.0
(PPR) [27] or, more generally, patient-to-nurse ratios, specify the minimum number of required nursing staff per shift.
In most hospital wards, shift plans are prepared on a monthly basis, usually four to ten weeks in advance starting with
coverage requirements such as staff qualifications and staffing demand across early, late, and night shifts [28]. This
process, called nurse scheduling, is still often carried out manually by ward managers, usually with the support of

planning tools. However, manual planning is particularly challenging because it must take into account staff preferences,
internal requirements and patient volume [9], among others. Notably, tensions between scheduling for patient care needs
and optimizing staff needs have been identified [29]. Operational shift planning still relies heavily on expert knowledge
and current indicators, while future developments and anticipated changes in care demand are often neglected, leading
to overly uniform staffing estimates. AI forecasts can equip shift planners with valuable foresight into future staffing
demands [30]. Several studies have therefore examined the prediction of care demand, for example on the basis of
patient admissions or bed occupancy [31, 32, 33], or staff absences [34].
A small number of studies have evaluated AI-supported scheduling tools in real-world settings. Shia et al. [12] developed
an AI-supported scheduling DSS that automatically generates complete shift plans containing a workload prediction
component with SHAP-based explanations [35]. Deployed across 156 nurses, the system achieved substantial reductions
in scheduling time and errors, and nurse engagement with the explanations was positively associated with satisfaction,
underscoring the importance of transparency for adoption. Renggli et al. [36] conducted focus group interviews with
21 nurses and supervisors and found that while a majority saw potential for improved efficiency and fairness, 38%
expressed concerns about AI reliability and loss of human oversight. This tension between efficiency gains and the
desire for human control motivates the design of DSS that augment rather than replace human judgment, which was a
core principle in the development of the system evaluated in this study. Moreover, we build upon the concerns about AI
reliability and transparency by incorporating AI uncertainty and explainable AI. To the best of our knowledge, no study
has examined these aspects in a real-world nurse scheduling context through a dedicated user study.
2.2
Transparency Mechanisms for Human-AI Decision-Making
AI-supported clinical DSS often suffer from the opacity of the underlying AI model, a challenge commonly referred to as
the “black-box” problem [37]. This lack of transparency can give rise to well-documented problems in human-computer
interaction, such as over-reliance or under-reliance on the system [38], as well as anchoring and confirmation bias [39].
To address these challenges, different transparency mechanisms—in particular explainable AI (see Section 2.2.1) and
uncertainty communication (see Section 2.2.2)—have emerged as helpful tools for achieving trust [40] and appropriate
reliance [20] and thereby improving human-AI decision-making.
2.2.1
Explainable AI
Explainable AI (XAI) has emerged as a research field to increase transparency of AI models by providing explanations
that simplify AI outputs for human understanding [41]. Although XAI is being used more often in applications and
software for end users, human-centered evaluations remain relatively limited and rarely incorporate insights from
cognitive or social sciences [42, 43]. For this reason, human-centered explainable AI (HCXAI) has emerged as an
approach that places users and their desiderata at the center of the design process [44, 45, 46]. It extends beyond the
technical generation of explanations by considering human values and desiderata [47], interpersonal dynamics, and
the broader social context [43] in which AI systems are used. This also includes who needs an explanation, why it is
needed and in which context it is used, because different social groups interpret explainability in different ways and use
it for different purposes [48, 44]. Consequently, HCXAI emphasizes the joint examination of technical explainability
and human factors, including how users perceive, understand, and evaluate AI-generated explanations.
2.2.2
Communicating Uncertainty in AI Systems
Uncertainty is an inherent property of machine learning predictions and is commonly decomposed into two types [49].
Aleatoric uncertainty reflects irreducible variability in the data arising from stochasticity or ambiguity. Epistemic
uncertainty, or model uncertainty, stems from limitations in the model’s knowledge, for instance, due to insufficient
training data. Predictive uncertainty (or prediction uncertainty) integrates both sources. Common approaches for
quantifying predictive uncertainty include Deep Ensembles [50] and Monte Carlo Dropout [51], both of which produce a
distribution of predictions for a given input from which statistical uncertainty estimates—such as variance or prediction
intervals—can be derived.
Communicating uncertainty to end-users remains a significant challenge, owing to variability in statistical literacy and
the inherent complexity of uncertainty representations such as variances, probabilities, and prediction intervals [21].
Several recent studies have examined how uncertainty information shapes user behavior in AI-supported decision-
making [23, 24, 13, 52]. Cao et al. [13] investigated the effect of uncertainty presentation format on appropriate reliance
in a skin cancer screening task. They found that expressing model uncertainty in a frequency format enabled users to
adjust their reliance more appropriately and reduced the influence of confirmation bias. Their results further indicated
that age and prior familiarity with statistics modulated the quality of human-AI collaborative decisions. Nourani
et al. [53] documented the design of a clinical decision support interface for intensive care unit risk prediction, with a

2.3
Research Gap
The preceding review reveals two compounding gaps in the existing literature. First, while XAI and uncertainty
communication have each been recognized as important transparency mechanisms for AI-supported DSS, empirical
user studies evaluating their respective effects on understanding, trust, and decision-making in realistic nurse scheduling
settings with domain experts remain scarce. While the work of Prabhudesai et al. [22] on uncertainty communication is
most closely related to our study, their evaluation was conducted with lay users on a hypothetical house price prediction
task, leaving open the question of how uncertainty communication affects expert users in real-world professional
settings. Moreover, the understandability of uncertainty visualizations themselves, as opposed to their downstream
effects on decision-making, has received comparatively little attention in the existing literature. Regarding XAI in a
nurse scheduling context, we identified one study that evaluated explanations of workload prediction [12]. However,
their evaluated system differs substantially from ours: rather than generating complete shift plans autonomously, our
system provides advice on which shifts to change, preserving planner control and judgment and placing greater emphasis
on human autonomy and oversight.
Implications for this study.
These gaps motivate a mixed-methods user study with healthcare professionals, evaluating
an AI-supported nurse scheduling prototype that incorporates both uncertainty visualization and XAI explanations
as separate interface features. The XAI component targets shift plan advice, providing explanations for suggested
shift changes while leaving final decisions to the planner. Using complementary quantitative and qualitative methods
including eye tracking, structured questionnaires, and thematic analysis of think-aloud data, we investigate the effect of
XAI and uncertainty communication on understanding, decision confidence, trust, and usability.
3
Prototype Implementation
Applying a human-centered design approach based on the principles of ISO 9241-210:2019 [54], we iteratively
developed a web-based decision support prototype integrating AI forecasts and optimization-based scheduling advice
as part of a larger research project on AI-supported nurse scheduling. The prototype was evaluated with domain experts
and practicing shift planners and is fully functional, although it contains partially mocked data, as detailed below.
3.1
Time Series Forecasts
The first functionality provides time series forecasts of (a) future care demand and (b) unplanned staff absences for a
hospital ward over the next 30 days, informing shift planners of shifts with potentially inadequate staffing. The two
targets are defined as follows:
Care demand is operationalized via patient acuity scores under the German PPR regulation, quantifying individual
care requirements in minutes per day. Total care demand per time step is the sum of acuity scores across all
ward patients.
Staff absences are predicted from historical records of unplanned absences (e.g., sick leave), as distinct from planned
absences such as scheduled vacation.
Both targets are forecast as hourly time series over a horizon of 720 hours (30 days) using a TSMixer model [55], a
deep learning architecture designed for long-term time series forecasting. Hourly outputs are aggregated into the three
most prevalent shift types at the study site (early, late, night; each 8 hours), following a co-design session with an
experienced shift planner. Care demand forecasts, expressed in PPR minutes, were converted to nursing staff equivalents
assuming 480 minutes of capacity per nurse per shift. As the source ward employs more nurses than typical study wards,
forecast outputs were scaled down proportionally to represent a smaller, anonymized ward. Prediction uncertainty was
communicated via 95% prediction intervals, which were constructed manually to ensure relevance to study tasks.

or understaffing (Figure 1 (1)), and provides three additional views per shift: a combined overlay of both forecasts with

3.2
AI Advice on Nurse Scheduling
The second functionality provides data-driven advice for improving an existing shift plan by proposing targeted
modifications that better align it with forecast care demand and staff absences, and regulatory requirements.
AI advice is generated by a rule-based heuristic that considers the current shift plan, predicted care demand and staff
absences, and regulatory scheduling constraints (e.g., no more than five consecutive working days). The heuristic
minimizes a penalty function over staffing deviations and constraint violations by iteratively proposing single-shift
changes; for example, reassigning a nurse from an early shift to a day off when that shift is forecast to be overstaffed.
The underlying shift plan was adapted from a real ward schedule: non-standard shift types were reassigned to early,
late, or night shifts, and the plan was reduced in scale to a ward of 22 nurses with fictitious names. Forecast and shift
plan data are fully synchronized to ensure internal consistency.
Advice is presented as an enumerated list and highlighted within the shift plan view, enabling planners to assess the
advice in context (see Figure 2). Each advice item is accompanied by a concise natural language explanation derived
from the penalty component most reduced by the proposed change (e.g., “reduction of overstaffing”). Planners retain
full agency since each advice item can be individually accepted or declined. Existing regulatory violations are also
listed and highlighted.
4
Study Design and Execution
We conducted a within-subject study comparing multiple variants of the AI-supported nurse scheduling prototype
from Section 3. The within-subject design allowed us to compare participants’ interactions and evaluations across the
different design variants. During the interaction, participants wore eye tracking glasses to record their visual attention
and were asked to think aloud. Before the experimental tasks, participants watched a video tutorial introducing the basic
functionalities and interaction principles of the prototype. To avoid bias, the video included placeholder information for
design elements that varied depending on the design variant.
The study comprised two blocks focusing on different aspects of AI-supported decision-making: (1) Uncertainty
communication in time series forecasts, which examined how well uncertainty information was understood (RQ1)
and how uncertainty visualizations influenced users’ decision confidence (RQ2); and (2) AI advice for shift plan
improvements, which examined how explanations of AI advice influenced user acceptance and confidence in decision-
making (RQ3).

4.1
Study Setting
Data collection was carried out in a prepared room in the hospital. Care was taken to ensure an environment with as
few distractions as possible. The study was conducted via a main screen on which the tutorial, the answering of the
questions, and the actual prototype of the personnel planning software were displayed. The participant was seated in
front of the main screen and interacted with the system using a mouse. The interviewer used a laptop on the left side
for technical purposes, but it was not part of the participant interface. For tasks not involving eye tracking, the glasses
were placed on the table. Microsoft Teams was running in the background to record the thoughts verbalized during the
interaction with the system and while answering the questionnaires. The study design is shown in the Figure 6.
4.2
Participants
An on-site study was conducted at a hospital with 16 participants (age: M = 40.1 years, SD = 12.0) ranging in age from
28 to 64 years.. We made sure that we included a balanced number of male and female participants. However, we
do not report the exact numbers for reasons of traceability. 12 participants held (assistant) ward manager roles (also
called shift planners in the following) and therefore had frequent prior contact with nurse scheduling tools (self-rated
experience with similar tools: M(SD) = 4.3(1.2); with nurse scheduling: M(SD) = 4.8(0.39)). Four participants were
registered nurses and are therefore directly affected by the nurse scheduling process (self-rated experience with tools:
M(SD) = 1.5(1.0); with shift scheduling: M(SD) = 2.5(1.3)).
4.3
Thematic Analysis
We analyzed the interview data using inductive thematic analysis. After transcribing the interviews, we extracted
relevant excerpts from each participant. These excerpts served as the basis for an initial round of open coding. In
collaborative sessions, our research team — four Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), Artificial intelligence (AI), and
Requirements Engineering researchers — discussed and iteratively refined emerging codes. Through this process,
we developed a preliminary codebook capturing recurring patterns across participants. We subsequently refined the
codebook through repeated discussion. To support the development of higher-level themes, we iteratively organized
the excerpts into affinity-based clusters according to their conceptual similarities, following an affinity-diagramming-
inspired process [59].
To assess robustness, we reserved a subset of interviews for independent coding by all four researchers and calculated
inter-rater reliability using Krippendorf’s α, applying a threshold of > 0.67 [60]. Initial scores fell below this threshold
(0.436–0.62). After revising the codebook, scores remained insufficient (0.420–0.546). We therefore conducted a joint
coding session to reconcile perspectives, which led to a simplified codebook and shared understanding. All remaining
interviews were subsequently coded by at least two researchers according to the final codebook (see appendix). Excerpts
assigned to the codes uncertainty communication, system explanation of uncertainty visualization, XAI AI advice, and
other XAI were additionally annotated with sentiment (positive, neutral, or negative) toward these design elements.

5.5.2
Uncertainty Visualization: Actionability and Decision Confidence (RQ2)
Beyond interpretation difficulties, uncertainty communication also shaped participants’ willingness to engage with the
forecast view and their confidence in acting on it.
Actionability Gap.
Even when the uncertainty visualization was broadly understood, participants questioned what to
do with the information. P7 raised: “And the other question is, what do I do with the uncertainty??” The perceived
utility of the uncertainty visualization was experience-dependent: P10 noted that it offered limited value to experienced
planners (“Let’s be honest, as a shift planner, it’s not much use to me if the program says that it’s uncertain”), while P2
suggested it would be especially useful during onboarding (“Especially at the start, one would definitely make use of
this view frequently.”). Where actionability was articulated, it connected to domain knowledge (DK): P10 described
how “high uncertainty on absence days would prompt planning a personnel buffer”.
Demand for XAI and Explanations of Uncertainty.
Several participants (n = 9) expressed a desire for explanatory
information in the time series forecast designs. P2 articulated this clearly: “So why that particular date, 5 January? It
would help to have an explanation as to why the system chose that day.” This finding suggests that the demand for XAI
extends beyond the AI advice in the shift plan to the forecast functionality. P1 also expressed a desire to understand
why uncertainty varies across days (P1: “It makes me wonder why, on some days, it is possible to calculate exactly
when an absence will occur and on others it is not?”).
5.5.3
Explainability of AI Advice: Acceptance and Decision Confidence (RQ3)
XAI AI Advice (XAI-Adv) was among the most frequently coded theme in the optimization task block (XAI-Adv:
n = 38). The sentiment of excerpts labeled with XAI-Adv and other XAI (O-XAI) is predominantly positive with 32
positive, four negative, and thirteen neutral statements. XAI-Adv co-occurred most strongly with pragmatic quality
(PQ: n = 21), trust (n = 16), and domain knowledge (DK: n = 13), indicating that explanations were evaluated at the
intersection of usability, trust, and professional context.
Positive Reception and Increased Willingness to Use.
Most participants (n = 11) who actively engaged with the
explanations found them meaningful and useful, and several (n = 5) reported increased willingness to act on the AI
advice. Participants valued knowing the rationale behind an advice item, particularly when it aligned with or extended
their domain knowledge. P1 noted that explanations would be especially valuable for less experienced shift planners.
Cognitive Overload and Salience.
Only three participants raised negative points regarding the explanations, all
related to information overload (e.g., P16: "I think there’s far too much text again"). A few participants did not notice
the explanations at all during the interaction (P4: “No” when asked whether they had seen the explanations), pointing
to a salience problem in the current design. This tension between transparency and cognitive economy was illustrated
by P7’s preference for the version without explanations: “Less is more.”
5.5.4
Trust and Decision Confidence
Trust merged as a prominent cross-cutting theme, co-occurring frequently with both XAI AI Advice (XAI-Adv) and
uncertainty communication (UC).
Stakes-Driven Distrust.
A recurring source of distrust was the perceived risk of acting on incorrect AI outputs
in the high-stakes professional context (n = 3). One participant articulated how blind reliance on the system could

propagate planning errors that ultimately affect patient safety. This pattern illustrates how professional responsibility
amplifies distrust: the perceived cost of an AI error is not merely operational inconvenience but potential harm to
patients and nursing staff. P8, in contrast, expressed unconditional trust: “I’m not skeptical at all. Harmful or adverse
consequences? I don’t think so. I trust the system.”
Explainability as a Trust Enabler.
Several participants reported increased trust when explanations accompanied AI

Learning Curve and Growing Confidence.
Repeated interaction with the system reduced initial confusion and
increased decision confidence. P4 noted: “But the good thing is, by the third time, I was already feeling a little more
confident.” P8 similarly reported that initial confusion about AI advice resolved with use: “At first, I didn’t understand
it—not even this AI advice. Now I get how it’s supposed to work.”
5.5.5
Human-AI Collaboration
Across both task blocks, participants consistently framed the tool’s role as assistive rather than autonomous. In
the co-occurrence analysis, human-AI collaboration (HAI) appeared frequently alongside domain knowledge (DK)
(Figure 12), indicating that views on collaboration were tightly coupled to professional expertise and awareness of
social dimensions that AI cannot fully capture.
Human Oversight as Professional Necessity.
Participants repeatedly described AI advice as a starting point rather
than a means to fully automate scheduling (e.g., P5: “Well, I really think that it’s better to build a basic structure and
then make adjustments [with the AI] than to have a finished plan [created by the AI].”). Oversight was not framed as
distrust per se, but as a professional necessity given the social complexity of nurse scheduling: P5: “Several colleagues
have a lot of wishes [. . . ], so I think it’s important for a human to take another look at it”, and P4: “Of course, a human
always has to double-check. Especially in nursing.”
Efficiency Benefits and Workflow Integration.
Participants acknowledged the efficiency benefits of AI assistance
for time-consuming tasks, e.g. P4: “I think the AI takes a few seconds, and you’ll need—well, if you really sit down
and do it—a whole day, a day and a half.” Several participants (n = 8) explicitly endorsed an AI-supported workflow,
provided that individual preferences and fairness constraints could be incorporated into the optimization (e.g., P6: “If,
for example, the AI could also include personal data as rule violations [. . . ], I would actually think that would be really
cool.”). Overall, participants viewed AI as an extension under human supervision rather than a full delegation of tasks.

Discussion
6.1
How well is the uncertainty information understood, and to what extent do brief explanations of how to
interpret the uncertainty information improve understanding and interpretation? (RQ1)
Findings: Uncertainty communicated as prediction intervals was poorly understood. Participants systematically
confused the uncertainty band width with the peak or trough. Brief in-interface explanations were insufficient to
remedy misinterpretation.
Across all data sources, the findings converge on a clear picture: uncertainty visualized as prediction intervals is poorly
understood by professional nurses and shift planners, and brief explanations of the visualization are insufficient to
remedy this.
Objective task performance was lowest precisely for the uncertainty-specific questions (TSF-Q3 and TSF-Q5), with
mean F1-scores well below those for basic forecast comprehension tasks. The think-aloud data reveal the dominant
misinterpretation pattern: participants identified days of highest or lowest uncertainty by the peak or trough of the band
rather than by its width. These errors persisted even after participants had read the explanatory note on how to interpret
the visualization. This finding is inconsistent with prior work where users understood prediction interval visualizations
without prior explanation [61, 62].

Participants also struggled to act on uncertainty information. Even when they understood the visualization, they
questioned both the source of that variability and its practical implications for shift planning. Positive task performance
on Question TSF-Q1, TSF-Q2, and TSF-Q4 (identifying days requiring action and identifying days with low care
demand or high staff absences) confirms that participants could reliably extract actionable signals from the forecasts;
the difficulty was specific to the uncertainty layer, not to the forecast as a whole.
6.2
How does uncertainty visualization influence users’ confidence in decision-making? (RQ2)
Findings: Uncertainty visualization significantly reduced decision confidence (F(2, 21.36) = 5.68, p = .011).
Participants reported higher confidence without uncertainty visualization than with it.
Uncertainty communication had a measurable negative influence on decision confidence (F(2, 21.36) = 5.68, p =
.011). Participants reported significantly higher confidence in design TSF w/o UV (forecast without uncertainty) than in
design TSF w/ UV (forecast with uncertainty). This finding stands in contrast to prior work reporting beneficial effects
of prediction interval visualizations on decision confidence and advice utilization [62, 61, 63]. A plausible explanation
lies in the specialized user group and domain context, as prior work confirms that such effects are highly domain and
visualization dependent [64].
Design TSF w/ UV + PH, which added historical forecast performance, did not significantly improve decision confidence
or trust over either design TSF w/o UV or design TSF w/ UV. UEQ-S scores did not differ significantly across the
three TSF designs, suggesting that overall user experience was not critically harmed by communicating uncertainty; the
frustration was specific to the uncertainty layer rather than the tool as a whole.
Notably, objective task performance was not significantly related to decision confidence (b = −1.41, p = .232),
meaning differences in confidence between designs cannot be explained by differences in task performance, and
confidence was not a reliable indicator of decision quality.
6.3
How do explanations of AI recommendations influence users’ acceptance and their confidence in
decision-making? (RQ3)
Findings: Explanations were subjectively valued and shifted visual attention toward the explanation area, yet
did not significantly change acceptance rates or decision confidence.
The findings reveal an important divergence between subjective attitudes and objective behavior. On the subjective side,
qualitative data indicate that explanations were broadly welcomed. Several participants (n = 4) explicitly connected
explanations to greater willingness to act on AI advice and higher trust. Trust and perceived explainability were strongly
correlated (r = .71, p < .001), and qualitative data confirm that explanations were a mechanism by which initial
mistrust was reduced (see Section 5.5.4). Eye-tracking data provide corroborating behavioral evidence: in design
AI Advice w/ Expl, participants allocated significantly more fixation time to the explanation area (AOI1; rrb = .68,
Holm-adjusted p = .037) and correspondingly less to the shift plan area (AOI3; rrb = −.78, Holm-adjusted p = .020).
On the objective side, however, design AI Advice w/ Expl did not produce significantly higher questionnaire scores
(UEQ-S, XAI scale, TiA), nor did it change the number of accepted advice items or decision confidence. A negative
association between decision confidence and acceptance rate was present in both designs, and the interaction effect was
not significant, providing no reliable evidence that explanations changed this relationship.
The eye tracking pattern suggests that explanations substituted for rather than supplemented independent verification:
attention shifted toward the explanation and away from the shift plan on which the decision was made. Together with
unchanged acceptance rates, this indicates a shallow, heuristic mode of engagement in which the explanation served as
a reassurance cue rather than as evidence to be evaluated. Such engagement is consistent with the effect of “placebic
explanations” [65], derived from “placebic information” [66], where the mere presence of a justification raises perceived
trust independently of its content, and it is precisely the mode that cognitive forcing designs are intended to disrupt [67].
The salience problem identified in the qualitative data is a likely contributing factor: Three participants missed the
explanations entirely, and others (n = 2) found them too text-heavy to engage with fully. This suggests that the current
implementation failed to make explanations sufficiently salient and concise to reliably influence decision behavior.

6.4
Reflection of Practical and Societal Impact
Trust calibration in a safety-critical domain.
In the cross-sectional correlational analysis, perceived explainability
was the strongest predictor of trust in automation (r = .71, p < .001), stronger than pragmatic quality (r = .67),
hedonic quality (r = .31), or experience-related variables. Pragmatic quality, in turn, was strongly associated with both
trust and XAI ratings (r = .67 for both), suggesting that trust is built primarily through the functional experience of
the tool. The qualitative data add a longitudinal dimension to this picture: participants described trust and decision
confidence as developing over time through repeated interaction, consistent with process models of human-automation
trust that distinguish between initial calibration and experience-based recalibration [e.g., 68]. The finding that digital-
tool experience — but not scheduling experience — was positively associated with trust ratings suggests that familiarity
with similar automated systems, rather than domain expertise, shapes the starting point of this calibration process.
The high-stakes nature of nurse scheduling amplifies both the importance and the difficulty of appropriate trust
calibration. Four participants articulated heightened awareness of the consequences of AI errors for staff and patients, a
healthy response in this domain. However, growing familiarity with the tool may also carry risks: some participants
acknowledged that repeated interaction could lead to increasing trust, potentially leading to overtrust and thus in
overreliance. One participant even mentioned to place unconditional trust in the system (see Section 5.5.4).
Fairness in AI-assisted scheduling.
Deploying AI-assisted scheduling in hospitals carries implications that extend
beyond usability and decision support effectiveness. In a profession already characterized by high workload and power
imbalances [26, 5, 29], nurses risk being systematically disadvantaged by optimization systems that treat all inputs
as equivalent. Our qualitative data — consistent with prior work [8, 12] — reveal that participants want scheduling
systems to incorporate and fairly weigh individual staff wishes. Yet treating all preferences equally does not necessarily
yield a fair schedule [69]: fairness may additionally require equitable distributions of preference satisfaction, workload,
and accumulated overtime across staff. Future deployments should therefore allow such contextual inputs to be captured,
made accessible, and subjected to transparent human review, while maintaining data privacy.
A related risk concerns how explanations shape fairness perceptions. Prior research shows that XAI can increase
perceived fairness even when the underlying system is unfair, and that explanations can foster trust independently of
their actual content [19]. In our context, explanations accompanying scheduling advice could lead planners and nurses
to perceive a schedule as more equitable than it actually is, a form of miscalibrated fairness perception that may mask
rather than surface problematic optimization behavior. As Deck et al. [19] argue, the goal of XAI in high-stakes settings
should be to support calibrated fairness perceptions rather than merely positive ones. Designed inclusively, such a
system could actively reduce, rather than strengthen, the inequities that already arise in manual scheduling.
Risks of institutional misuse.
AI-optimized scheduling outputs may also be appropriated at an institutional level in
unintended ways. P9 raised the concern that HR could use optimized schedules to justify staff reductions — and while
they assumed this would not occur, the risk is plausible. If AI outputs are taken to demonstrate that demand can be met
with fewer nurses, decision-makers may cut positions, compromising patient safety and staff wellbeing [29]. These
outcomes are in direct contradiction to the intended purpose of the system.
Human oversight and participatory design.
Participants consistently framed human-AI collaboration as augmenta-
tion under human oversight, not as a lack of trust, but as a professional and ethical necessity given the social complexity
of shift scheduling (see Section 5.5.5). This is consistent with prior qualitative evidence that nurses and other health-
care professionals view AI decision support as an aid to, rather than a substitute for, clinical judgment [70, 71, 36].
Scheduling algorithms should support, not replace, the professional judgment and negotiation between staff that this
task inherently requires. We therefore view participatory design — engaging not only direct users but also affected
parties such as nurses — as a prerequisite for responsible deployment.
6.5
Design Implications
Based on the results and discussion from the studied nurse scheduling use case, we derived the following design
implications for AI-based decision support systems, particularly in planning and scheduling contexts:
Design Implication 1: Replace static prediction intervals with interaction-first uncertainty representations.
Given the persistent misinterpretation of prediction interval bands and the negatively considered user experience
with respect to the uncertainty visualization (see Section 6.1), designers should consider alternative uncertainty
representations that are more compatible with non-expert chart literacy, such as color-encoded risk categories, textual
warnings of high forecast uncertainty above a certain threshold, or progressive disclosure designs in which uncertainty
detail is revealed only on demand to reduce cognitive overload.

Design Implication 2: Use examples to clarify uncertainty communication.
Given that the misinterpretation errors
persisted even when participants had read the explanatory note on how to interpret the visualization (refer to Section 6.1),
interfaces with uncertainty communication should use clear examples to demonstrate how the information should be
interpreted.
Design Implication 3: Embed actionable guidance directly in the uncertainty visualization.
Because participants
understood uncertainty information better when it was linked to concrete planning actions (e.g., scheduling a personnel
buffer on high-uncertainty days) (see Section 6.1, and Section 6.2), the visualization should surface explicit recommen-
dations rather than leaving the translation to the user. This could take the form of contextual annotations that suggest
specific planning responses when uncertainty exceeds a defined threshold. This reframing connects the uncertainty
display to the planner’s existing workflow and domain knowledge, reducing the cognitive gap between perceiving and
acting on uncertainty.
Design Implication 4: Prioritize salience and conciseness over completeness in AI advice explanations.
The
finding that some participants missed the explanations entirely, while others found them cognitively overwhelming
(Section 6.3), points to a fundamental design challenge: the current textual format placed the full cognitive burden on
the user. Explanations should be redesigned for progressive disclosure: a brief, prominent rationale by default, with
fuller detail available on demand, preferably directly in the shift plan where the decision is made. Visual cues such as
color coding, iconography, or inline highlighting could improve salience without increasing text density.
Design Implication 5: Extend explanatory information to forecast visualizations.
Participants spontaneously
requested explanations not only for the AI advice but also for the time series forecasts, asking why specific days were
flagged and why uncertainty varied across the planning horizon. This demand is currently unmet in the design. Future
iterations should provide on-demand explanations for forecast outputs, linking high uncertainty or high forecast values
to identifiable drivers such as historical patterns, known staffing constraints, or seasonal effects.
Design Implication 6: Design for calibrated trust, not maximum trust.
Given that the perceived costs of AI errors
in nurse scheduling can extend to harm to patients, and given that our findings suggest that some users tend to have an
unconditional trust in the AI’s advice (e.g., Section 6.4), the system should actively support appropriate skepticism
alongside positive engagement. Therefore, the goal should not be to maximize trust, but to support calibrated trust to
achieve appropriate reliance [20] — actively encouraging ongoing scrutiny rather than passive acceptance. This could
include transparent communication of the AI’s known limitations and historical accuracy, explicit options for human
review and override, and verification mechanisms that enable planners to understand the consequences of accepted
advice.
Design Implication 7: Design AI scheduling tools as collaborative tools with explicit options for manual override.
The user interface should make it clear at every stage that the AI provides suggestions rather than making decisions,
and that final decision authority remains with the human user. The options available to the user for modifying and
reordering advice items, and for explaining why they have accepted or rejected them, should be clearly visible and easy
to use. This helps ensure that the AI remains a decision-support tool while preserving human control and allowing users
to incorporate their workflow requirements and domain knowledge into the final scheduling decision.
Design Implication 8: Incorporate staff preferences and fairness constraints as optimization inputs.
To move
toward human–AI collaboration that users perceive as useful and fair, future systems should provide structured
mechanisms for planners and nurses to specify individual staff preferences, workload equity constraints, and other
social considerations as optimization inputs rather than post-hoc corrections, thereby incorporating users’ domain
knowledge and situated needs directly into the planning process.
6.6
Study Limitations
Some limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample consisted of only 16 participants,
limiting statistical power and generalizability. However, the study involved a specialized and difficult-to-recruit target
group and was conducted in a real-world hospital setting with actual hospital staff. It therefore prioritized context-
specific expert perspectives over a larger sample. Nevertheless, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and
validated in larger, multi-site studies.
Second, uncertainty was visualized exclusively through prediction intervals. Consequently, the findings may not
generalize to other uncertainty-visualization approaches, such as frequency-based representations or hypothetical
outcome plots.

Although participants were recruited from a single hospital, we deliberately included individuals with varying levels
of experience and aimed for a balanced distribution across genders and age groups. Nevertheless, differences in
organizational practices and workflows may limit transferability to other clinical settings.
Finally, the mock data did not always correspond to the actual ward sizes of the participants or to specific organizational
rules, such as the maximum number of consecutive working days. These inconsistencies may have caused confusion
and reduced the ecological validity of the study. Care was taken, however, to ensure that the simulated data resembled
realistic data. The use of real data from one of the hospital’s wards might have led to increased cognitive load due to the
large number of staff members.
7
Conclusion
This paper presented a mixed-methods user study with expert users investigating the role of explainable AI (XAI) and
uncertainty communication in a clinical decision support system for AI-supported nurse scheduling. Using thematic
analysis of think-aloud interviews, structured questionnaires, and eye tracking data collected from 16 healthcare
professionals in a real-world hospital setting, we examined how XAI and uncertainty communication affect usability,
understandability, decision confidence, trust, and human–AI collaboration, with a particular HCI focus on how the
design and presentation of AI outputs shape users’ attention, interpretation, reliance, and ability to use system outputs
to inform concrete planning decisions.
With respect to uncertainty communication, we found that prediction interval visualizations were widely misinterpreted:
participants focused on the peak or trough of the interval rather than its width as the indicator of uncertainty magnitude.
Critically, adding uncertainty visualization led to a statistically significant decrease in decision confidence compared to
the design without it. This suggests that exposing uncertainty alone is insufficient and may be counterproductive without
carefully designed scaffolding. An actionability gap emerged as a recurring challenge: even when participants broadly
understood the visualization, they struggled to translate uncertainty information into concrete planning decisions.
Regarding the explainability of AI advice, explanations were received predominantly positively and emerged as the
most frequently discussed theme. Eye tracking data confirmed that explanations directed visual attention more strongly
toward the AI advice itself, and participants reported greater trust and willingness to act on AI suggestions when
explanations were present. However, a tension between transparency and cognitive load was evident: several participants
found the explanations information-dense, and a subset did not notice them at all, pointing to a salience problem in
the current design. These findings highlight that providing explanations is not sufficient in itself: their placement,
timing, visual salience, and level of detail are critical interaction design choices that determine whether explanations
can effectively support human decision making.
Taken together, these findings yield eight design implications: uncertainty should be communicated in simplified,
example-based, and action-oriented formats; explanations should be concise, visually salient, and progressively
disclosed; AI interfaces should support calibrated trust and appropriate reliance while providing explicit mechanisms
for human review and override; and scheduling systems should incorporate staff preferences and fairness constraints as
optimization inputs rather than post-hoc corrections. More broadly, our findings emphasize the importance of designing
AI decision support as an interactive process of collaboration rather than as a one-way communication of predictions.
Effective human–AI collaboration requires interfaces that preserve human agency, support critical assessment of AI
outputs, and connect AI-generated information to users’ existing workflows and domain knowledge.
Future work should address several open challenges. Alternative uncertainty representations, such as frequency-based
formats or hypothetical outcome plots, should be explored to better support intuitive interpretation. Additionally, future
work should investigate design and interaction approaches for explaining prediction uncertainty in ways that improve
both understandability and actionability. Further research should explore how AI scheduling tools can account for
fairness across staff and examine how repeated interaction affects trust calibration and the risk of automation bias over
time. Finally, the tension between explanation transparency and cognitive load calls for adaptive and personalized XAI
interfaces that tailor explanation depth and salience to individual user needs and expertise levels. Longitudinal and
in-situ evaluations would further help determine how these interaction patterns evolve with experience and how AI
decision support becomes integrated into everyday work practices.
