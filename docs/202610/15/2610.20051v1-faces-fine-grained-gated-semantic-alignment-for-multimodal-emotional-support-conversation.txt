Abstract
In recent years, with advances in multimodal in-
teraction technologies and the growing demand
for intelligent emotional support, Multimodal
Emotional Support Conversation (MESC) has
emerged as an important task. However, exist-
ing systems still suffer from shallow responses
and inappropriate support strategies due to
weak semantic feedback, cross-dimension re-
ward compensation, and coarse reasoning su-
pervision. To address these challenges, we
propose FACES, a fine-grained gated seman-
tic alignment framework for MESC. Specifi-
cally, FACES integrates multi-source rewards
to construct multidimensional semantic and
functional alignment signals, employs a non-
compensatory gated fusion mechanism to re-
duce cross-dimension reward offset, and lever-
ages structured Chain-of-Thought (CoT) an-
chors to perform token-level credit assignment.
Under a controlled response-length protocol,
FACES improves the average MLLM-based
evaluation score from 3.61 to 4.04 and achieves
an 80.22% pairwise win rate over SOTA. Hu-
man evaluation under the same protocol yields
a 75.3% preference rate.
Results on three
external datasets further support transferabil-
ity.
The code and dataset are available at
https://github.com/OCT-ARR/arr.

Introduction
Multimodal Emotional Support Conversation
(MESC) (Chu et al., 2025) extends ESC (Liu et al.,
2021) by generating empathetic, appropriate, and
supportive responses from dialogue context and
multimodal cues such as facial expressions, pos-
tures, and interaction context (Chaubey et al., 2026;
Lu et al., 2025). Beyond producing a superficially
natural response, MESC requires models to infer
users’ emotions, underlying causes, and support

Correct emotion recognition alone does not ensure
appropriate support (Liu et al., 2021; Wang et al.,
2024a). In Figure 1, the baseline identifies anger

ing and supportive action.
To better understand users and select support
strategies, prior work has advanced MESC through
dataset construction (Chu et al., 2025), multimodal
modeling (Le et al., 2026), and reasoning-based
training for support generation (Wang et al., 2025a).
Complementary work personalizes multimodal sup-
port through memory (Tu et al., 2025) and links

emotion recognition and cause inference to sup-
port generation (Tu et al., 2026). Reasoning-based
methods use structured CoT (Wei et al., 2022) to
make explicit how inferences about user emotions
and underlying causes guide support strategy selec-
tion. In particular, FIRES (Wang et al., 2025a) in-
troduces flexible thinking and shows that reinforce-
ment learning (RL) objectives improve structured
reasoning and final response quality in MESC.
Despite this progress, existing RL objectives
for MESC still face three limitations in translat-
ing intermediate understanding into effective sup-
port. First, surface-overlap metrics such as BLEU
and ROUGE-L (Papineni et al., 2002; Lin, 2004)
poorly capture cross-modal grounding, semantic
consistency, and supportive intent, providing weak
feedback on whether a response meets the user’s
support needs. Second, additive reward fusion can
let strong understanding scores mask a poor sup-
port strategy or ineffective response (Van Moffaert
et al., 2013). Accurate emotion or situation under-
standing alone cannot compensate for these support
failures. Third, existing MESC methods relying on
completion-level rewards (Wang et al., 2025a) lack
stage-specific credit for visual grounding, emotion
and situation understanding, strategy planning, or
response generation. This coarse feedback makes it
difficult to identify and correct the reasoning errors
underlying an inappropriate support response.
To address these issues, we propose FACES,
an MESC framework that uses gated multidimen-
sional rewards to guide fine-grained token up-
dates. Specifically, FACES uses CLIP (Radford
et al., 2021) for visual perception, MiniLM (Wang
et al., 2020) for emotion understanding, and a
multimodal large language model (MLLM) judge
(Zheng et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024) for situa-
tion understanding, support strategy, and response
outcome. After a format-and-outcome warm-up,
non-compensatory gated reward fusion limits com-
pensation for weak strategy or response quality.
Reasoning-stage markers route dimension-specific
token-level advantages to the corresponding CoT
and response spans, jointly optimizing intermediate
reasoning and final response generation.
Our main contributions are as follows:
• We propose a multi-source semantic reward
scheme that provides more reliable semantic-
level supervision than surface-overlap metrics.
• We introduce format-and-outcome warm-up
with non-compensatory gated reward fusion to
reduce cross-dimension compensation.
• We propose fine-grained semantic alignment re-
wards for structured CoT and outcome genera-
tion, aligning dimension-specific feedback with
token spans to jointly optimize intermediate rea-
soning and the final response.
Under a 15-word response cap (cap-15) on
MESC-FT, FACES raises the average MLLM-
based evaluation score from 3.61 to 4.04, achieves
an 80.22% pairwise win rate, and obtains a 75.3%
human win rate over the SOTA baseline FIRES.
Results on MELD-ESC300, StickerConv, and
AvaMERG support cross-dataset transferability.

Related Work
2.1
MESC Research
Compared with traditional ESC (Liu et al., 2021),
MESC uses multimodal cues alongside dialogue,
providing additional evidence about the help-
seeker’s emotion and situation when words are
ambiguous (Chu et al., 2025; Park et al., 2024).
Two representative approaches have emerged. The
first fuses multimodal signals to infer emotion
and guide support generation, through sequential
emotion–strategy–response modeling (Chu et al.,
2025) or psychologically informed optimization
(Le et al., 2026). The second makes intermediate
decisions explicit, using flexible reasoning over
visual context, emotion, situation, and strategy
(Wang et al., 2025a), or appraisal-guided reasoning
and retrieval (Chu et al., 2026). But remaining chal-
lenges include weak semantic feedback, compen-
sation across reward dimensions, and insufficient
supervision for intermediate reasoning; these are
the problems FACES aims to address.
2.2
Reward Evaluation and Fusion
Reward evaluation and fusion aim to build reli-
able supervision and coordinate multiple objectives.
Preference-based learning can optimize a policy
through a learned reward model (Christiano et al.,
2017; Ouyang et al., 2022; Stiennon et al., 2020;
Bai et al., 2022). Later work develops psychology-
grounded, multi-perspective reward models (Wang
et al., 2026a). MultiMood (Le et al., 2026) com-
bines psychological criteria with PPO (Schulman
et al., 2017) and GRPO (Shao et al., 2024); G-Eval
(Liu et al., 2023), Prometheus (Kim et al., 2024),
PandaLM (Wang et al., 2024b), STICKERCONV
(Zhang et al., 2024), and related studies (Zhao et al.,
2024) demonstrate the utility of model-based eval-
2


uation. However, linear fusion can allow strong
dimensions to mask weak ones (Van Moffaert et al.,
2013), motivating non-compensatory reward fusion
in multimodal emotional support conversations.
2.3
Fine-grained Reward Optimization
Fine-grained reward optimization moves beyond
outcome-only feedback by supervising intermedi-
ate reasoning steps or local response segments.
Prior work shows that process-level feedback
improves reasoning reliability (Lightman et al.,
2024), and dense local signals are useful for mul-
timodal alignment (Yu et al., 2024).
In ESC,
FIRES optimizes flexible multimodal reasoning via
completion-level rewards; RLVER derives emotion
rewards from simulated users (Wang et al., 2025a,
2026b). Other methods provide finer-grained su-
pervision at different levels: MindChat-R0 applies
GRPO to structured empathetic reasoning; AFlow
supervises dialogue prefixes; PEER trains a unified
process–outcome reward model (She et al., 2025;
Zou et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025c). In MESC,
FACES uses non-compensatory fusion for heteroge-
neous dimension-level signals, routing their group-
relative advantages to aligned token spans.

Methodology

optimizes structured candidates through the fine-
grained reward workflow in Figure 2, following
supervised fine-tuning and a format-and-outcome

4.2
Main Results
4.2.1
Automatic Evaluation
Table 1 shows that FACES leads on METEOR,
Distinct-3, and DIFF-EPITOME, while FIRES
leads on BLEU-2 and ROUGE-L and Gemini-2.5-
Flash-Lite on BERTScore-F1. Although FACES
trails FIRES on BLEU-2 and ROUGE-L, these
metrics emphasize lexical overlap, and a support-
ive response need not closely match the refer-
ence wording. In contrast, its gains over FIRES
on METEOR, BERTScore-F1, Distinct-3, and
DIFF-EPITOME suggest stronger semantic cor-
respondence, greater response diversity, and better
support-strategy alignment. This pattern is con-
sistent with semantic rewards favoring meaning
over wording, while strategy and outcome gates
discourage plausible but unhelpful replies.
4.2.2
MLLM Evaluation
To complement automatic metrics, we assess sup-
portive quality with the MLLM judge using di-
alogue context and visual evidence. For brevity
and symmetry, we remove reasoning traces and
prefix-truncate FACES and baseline responses to
15 words; greedy decoding reduces sampling ran-
domness. Details are in Appendix C.1.
Under length control, the judge scores Empa-
thy, Multimodal Appropriateness, and Context-

5
Conclusion
We present FACES, a fine-grained gated semantic
alignment framework for MESC. It addresses weak
semantic supervision, compensatory reward fusion,
and coarse credit assignment through multidimen-
sional rewards, non-compensatory gating, and span-
aligned token-level advantages.
On MESC-FT,
FACES improves several automatic metrics and is
preferred to FIRES in matched cap-15 MLLM and
human evaluations. This preference advantage per-
sists under an independent GLM judge, while ab-
lations support the contributions of the three main
designs. Evaluations on three external datasets pro-
vide further evidence of transfer without additional
training. These findings highlight fine-grained se-
8


mantic alignment with non-compensatory gating
as a promising direction for MESC generation.
Limitations
Although FACES shows consistent gains on mul-
timodal emotional support generation, this study
still leaves room for further extensions in personal-
ization and evaluation. It focuses on instance-level
semantic alignment and therefore does not explic-
itly model user-specific preferences. Moreover, our
experimental setup prioritizes reproducibility and
controlled comparison by relying on automatic met-
rics, MLLM-based assessment, pairwise human
preference evaluation, and keyframe-based visual
inputs. While these design choices are sufficient to
establish the effectiveness of FACES in the present
setting, future work could further strengthen the
framework through finer-grained human evaluation,
more diverse annotators, and richer multimodal
signals such as vocal prosody and physiological
signals in realistic support settings.
Ethical Considerations
This study uses the MESC-FT dataset and con-
structs the MELD-ESC300 external generalization
test set from publicly available MELD/EMOTyDA
resources. All data are used solely for research
purposes, and we strictly comply with the licens-
ing, citation, and redistribution requirements of the
original datasets. For released code, datasets, or de-
rived resources, we avoid redistributing raw audio,
video, or image materials without proper authoriza-
tion; instead, we provide guidance for obtaining
the original datasets, along with data processing
scripts and reproduction instructions.
This work focuses on multimodal emotional
support conversation generation, but the proposed
model remains a research prototype. It should not
be used as a tool for clinical diagnosis, psychother-
apy, or crisis intervention without professional su-
pervision.
The model may inherit biases from
the training data and foundation models, which
could lead to inappropriate judgments in emotion
recognition, support strategy selection, or response
generation. For acute psychological crises, per-
sonal safety risks, or other scenarios requiring pro-
fessional intervention, any real-world deployment
should incorporate human oversight, safety screen-
ing, and professional referral protocols to deter-
mine whether and how the system should respond.

B
Prompt Templates
This section reports the prompt templates used in
FACES. The prompt design follows three prin-
ciples. First, training-time reward construction
and post-hoc evaluation use separate prompt fam-
ilies because they serve different purposes: the
former provides optimization signals for the pol-
icy, whereas the latter measures the quality of fixed
model outputs under independently specified eval-
uation criteria. Second, for every scoring task, we
use three semantically equivalent prompt variants
that preserve the same input fields, scoring criteria,
score range, and output schema while varying their
surface realization. This design reduces sensitivity
to any single prompt formulation without chang-
ing the underlying evaluation objective. Third, all
prompts require machine-readable JSON outputs,
enabling deterministic parsing and consistent ag-
gregation across instances.
For training-time reward scoring, Table 14
presents one representative template. For post-hoc
MLLM evaluation, we similarly use three variants
for each pointwise metric and pairwise preference
setting. The shared evaluation rules and represen-
tative templates are reported in Table 15, Table 16,
Table 17, and Table 18. The complete prompt vari-
ants used in the experiments, together with the
corresponding parsing and aggregation code, are
available in the code repository.
B.1
Training-time Reward Prompts
The training-time MLLM reward prompts are used
to score situation understanding, support strategy,
and response outcome during reward construction.
Candidate reasoning and response spans, together
with their corresponding reference annotations, are
dynamically inserted through placeholders such as
{context} and {candidate_response}. The judge
assesses semantic consistency, functional align-
ment, and contextual appropriateness, while penal-
izing unnecessary verbosity, and returns structured
scores from 1 to 5 for the three dimensions. Visual
perception and emotion understanding are evalu-
ated separately using CLIP and MiniLM rather than
the MLLM prompt. Scores obtained from the three
prompt variants are normalized and then averaged
before being used in reward fusion.
B.2
MLLM Evaluation Prompts
For automatic MLLM evaluation, we use comple-
mentary pointwise scoring and blind pairwise pref-
erence comparison. Pointwise evaluation measures
each response independently along Empathy, Mul-
timodal Appropriateness, and Context–Response
Consistency, whereas pairwise evaluation asks the
judge to select the better overall emotional-support
response or return a tie. The former provides in-
terpretable dimension-level measurements, while
the latter captures holistic quality when differ-
ent strengths and weaknesses must be considered
jointly. In pairwise comparisons, model identities
are hidden, and both candidates are evaluated using
the same dialogue context and visual evidence.
Unlike training-time reward scoring, post-hoc
MLLM judges receive no reference situation, strat-
egy, or response annotations. Consequently, a can-
didate can be assessed for contextual appropriate-
ness without requiring its wording or support strat-
egy to match a single dataset reference.
For each pointwise metric, we apply three se-
mantically equivalent prompt variants and average
the resulting scores. Pairwise evaluation uses the
same number of prompt variants together with bidi-
rectional candidate swapping and majority voting,
reducing sensitivity to prompt wording and presen-
tation order. For the length-controlled evaluations
reported in the main results and Appendix C, all
think-related fields and think-specific criteria are
removed from the judge input and prompt. The
judge therefore evaluates only the visible, length-
controlled final responses rather than explicit rea-
soning traces. All prompts require structured JSON
outputs; invalid or unparsable pairwise outputs are
conservatively treated as ties. Table 15 summa-
rizes the shared evaluation rules, Table 16 defines
the pointwise dimensions, and Tables 17 and 18
present the representative prompts.

C.1
Controlled Views
We consider a response-only view without a word
limit and three length-controlled views. In the
response-only no-cap view, the judge sees the di-
alogue context, attached images, and the final re-
sponse only; the prompt excludes the think sec-

used. This view shows the same preference trend,
but it may still be affected by response-length dif-
ferences. Therefore, we focus the reported con-
trolled results on the length-controlled cap-30/cap-
25/cap-15 views. In these views, each candidate
response is prefix-truncated to at most N words,
where N ∈{30, 25, 15}.

C.3
Statistical Significance
To assess whether the observed preference advan-
tages in the controlled pairwise evaluation are sta-
tistically reliable, we further perform significance
testing on the main comparisons. For each dataset
and each reported length-controlled view, we ap-
ply a two-sided exact binomial sign test over deci-
sive (i.e., non-tie) comparisons only, and estimate
95% confidence intervals for the FACES prefer-
ence share via bootstrap resampling. This analysis
does not introduce any new evaluation criterion;
rather, it provides a statistical summary, including
confidence intervals, of the majority-vote pairwise
results reported in the main text and appendix.
The results show that FACES remains signifi-
cantly preferred over the baseline under all reported
length-controlled views on both the MESC-FT test
set and MELD-ESC300. Notably, even under the
most conservative cap-15 condition, where explicit
reasoning traces are hidden and the judge can ac-
cess only length-truncated final responses, FACES
still attains a decisive preference share of 87.2%
on the MESC-FT test set (95% CI: 83.9%–90.4%,
p ≈2.0 × 10−57) and 86.5% on MELD-ESC300
(95% CI: 81.9%–91.2%, p ≈3.1 × 10−29). These
results suggest that the observed preference gains
are not merely driven by visible CoT content or
longer responses, but remain robust under substan-
tially restricted judging conditions.

Hyperparameter Sensitivity Analysis
We vary τv, γ, m, and λ individually to assess
FACES sensitivity. These set visual reward spar-
sity, the minimum scale for positive advantages,
the number of final tokens receiving full advantage,
and the weight of earlier tokens, respectively. Ap-
pendix A gives the fixed training, decoding, and
evaluation settings. Defaults and sensitivity results
use the MESC-FT validation set; we compare end-
point anchoring with linear and exponential decay
using validation rewards and MESC-FT test-set
response evaluations.
Table 32 shows moderate robustness to changes
in the visual threshold τv. Lower thresholds make
visual rewards more permissive and slightly im-
prove lexical overlap but weaken semantic discrim-
ination; higher thresholds make rewards sparse and
reduce semantic and MLLM performance. The de-
fault τv = 0.60 gives the best DIFF-E. and MLLM
average, though not every metric.
Table 33 shows that FACES is stable around the
default gate floor. Since gi is dynamically com-
puted from γ and Ri/Rmax, we analyze γ rather
than treating gi as an independent hyperparame-
ter. When γ is too small, positive local advantages
are overly suppressed. As γ increases, the gate
becomes weaker. Setting γ = 1.00 improves B-2
and R-L but worsens DIFF-E. and the MLLM aver-
age, supporting the use of the gate to limit positive
updates for candidates whose total rewards are low
relative to the best candidate in the same group.
Table 34 analyzes the endpoint-anchored credit-
assignment parameters on the held-out validation
set. A shorter anchor window or a smaller λ over-
emphasizes segment endings and weakens supervi-
sion for earlier reasoning tokens. A larger anchor
window is still stable but slightly less balanced.
When λ = 1.00, all tokens in a segment receive the
same advantage, which improves lexical-overlap
metrics but reduces semantic and MLLM-based
quality. The default setting (m = 5, λ = 0.80)
provides a good balance between endpoint empha-
sis and full-span supervision.

External Dataset
We use MESC-FT as the sole training set because
its multimodal emotional support setting and su-
pervision structure align with the structured reason-
ing and multidimensional reward design of FACES.
Adding other datasets during training would change
both the amount and distribution of training data,
making it difficult to distinguish the effect of the
method from the effect of additional data. Accord-
ingly, we reserve MELD-ESC300, StickerConv,
and AvaMERG for cross-dataset evaluation without
additional training. This choice follows ESC eval-
uations on datasets not strictly dedicated to ESC:
FIRES evaluates on an ESC-oriented EMOTyDA
subset (Wang et al., 2025a), while straQ* evaluates
on EmpatheticDialogues (Wang et al., 2025b).

These statistics make the composition of the bench-
mark explicit and reproducible. We also conduct an
expert audit of all 300 samples to assess emotional
need, response relevance, supportive function, and
overall validity.
A sample is considered valid only if the pre-
ceding context conveys an emotional need and the
target response is relevant and plausibly support-
ive. The same three annotators described in Ap-
pendix D.1 independently audited all samples, with
the final label for each criterion determined by ma-
jority vote. The non-negative target-emotion fil-
ter is intended to exclude clearly negative replies,
rather than to serve as a proxy for empathy. As
shown in Table 38, 274 of the 300 samples pass
the overall validity criterion, yielding validity rates
of 91.3% overall, 92.8% for the strict subset, and
88.0% for the relaxed subset. For example, when
Phoebe expresses anger about a bad date, Mon-
ica responds, “Don’t worry Phoebe, you’re gonna
meet someone.” In another case, Joey criticizes
himself for being unable to finish his writing, and
Ross responds, “I’ll help you.” These examples
illustrate that the filtering procedure captures a neg-
ative context followed by a relevant and plausibly
supportive response, rather than treating the target
emotion label as evidence of empathy. Overall, the
benchmark centers on mildly supportive or non-
negative target replies while still covering a modest
range of more explicitly positive feedback.
We emphasize that MELD-ESC300 is derived
from scripted TV dialogue rather than profession-
ally curated emotional-support data, so it is in-
tended as a controlled external generalization test
set rather than a replacement for dedicated ESC
benchmarks. By combining recent negative-affect
context, neutral-to-positive target replies, and vi-
sual evidence from the immediately preceding
turn, it provides a practical approximation of cross-
domain emotional-support scenarios for evaluating
transfer beyond the MESC-FT test set.

G.4
Additional External Generalization
Beyond MELD-ESC300, we evaluate transfer to
StickerConv and AvaMERG without additional
training. Table 41 reports the blind pairwise re-
sults, while Table 42 reports the corresponding au-
tomatic evaluation. We sample 300 test instances
from each dataset with seed 42. StickerConv uses
textual sticker cues in the dialogue context, while
AvaMERG uses dialogue text and five uniformly
sampled facial frames per instance, with audio ex-
cluded. For each dataset, responses are generated
greedily and evaluated using the same blind pair-
wise protocol as the main experiments: three se-
mantically equivalent judge prompts, bidirectional
candidate swapping, prefix truncation under cap-15
and cap-25 settings, and majority voting. Statisti-
cal significance is assessed with a two-sided exact
binomial sign test over non-tied comparisons, and
confidence intervals are estimated using 5,000 boot-
strap resamples.
The automatic results are complementary rather
than uniformly one-sided.

H
Case Study
To complement the controlled pairwise results, we
provide qualitative examples that illustrate where
FACES improves over the baseline and where it can
still fail. Across the success cases, FIRES often
paraphrases the user’s surface expression, whereas
FACES more consistently follows a chain of spe-
cific emotion labeling, causal situation interpreta-
tion, and targeted exploration. Under aggressive
cap-15 truncation, the higher tie rate may reflect the
removal of explanatory and strategy-related con-
tent, but the FIRES win rate remains low, suggest-
ing that truncation reduces separability rather than
reversing the preference. The failure case illus-
trates an important boundary: once the visual cue
is misread, the error can propagate through situ-
ation inference and strategy selection. In panel
(F) of Figure 4, Mona holds a photograph, which
FIRES correctly describes as a picture taken of her.
FACES, however, mistakes it for a phone and re-
sponds as though she were showing something to
Ross and Joey. The result is a fluent but visually
ungrounded follow-up. Gated reward fusion can
suppress poor final behavior during training, but it
cannot fully repair incorrect upstream perception.
The figure compares the dialogue context, visual
cue, baseline response, FACES response, and a
brief analysis for six representative cases, cover-
ing in-domain success, length-controlled success,
cross-dataset transfer, and a failure case.
