## ABSTRACT 

In long-horizon tool-calling tasks, LLM agents can fail not only because of tool-use errors, but also because of ineffective interaction with users. Yet existing benchmarks largely assume cooperative and behaviorally stable users, leaving the effect of emotional variation on agent reliability underexplored. We introduce 

τ -Emotion-Bench , a ten-domain benchmark for evaluating tool-calling agents under controlled emotion-conditioned user interactions while preserving the un-derlying task objective. To scale both benchmark construction and agent training, we introduce Tracer , a scalable agentic data-generation pipeline that constructs grounded, policy-valid tasks and collects trajectories through an interactive agent– user–environment harness. By allowing dissatisfied users to terminate inefficient interactions, the harness provides an interaction-aware signal that filters trajectories with excessive user dependence and favors effective tool use. Experiments with two user simulators show that frontier agents remain unreliable, with substantial gaps between average task success and consistent task completion. Tracer-generated supervision consistently improves Qwen3 models across scales, generalizes to unseen domains, and transfers to τ -Bench and τ -Trait. Behavioral analyses further show that higher task success is associated with a better balance between tool execution and necessary user follow-up. Overall, τ -Emotion-Bench and Tracer provide a scalable framework for evaluating agent reliability under diverse user behaviors and improving tool-calling agents through interaction-aware supervision.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models have rapidly evolved into agents capable of planning (Zhu et al., 2025), calling external tools, maintaining long-term memory (Uddin et al., 2026), and completing long-horizon tasks in interactive environments (Wang et al., 2024). Recent benchmarks have made these evaluations increasingly realistic by introducing complex tool dependencies, stateful environments, and multi-turn user interactions (Yao et al., 2024; He et al., 2025b; Yu et al., 2026b; Barres et al., 2025). In parallel, recent agent-training methods generate large collections of successful trajectories from executable environments (Li et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026; Saeidi et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026b). However, both evaluation and data generation still largely rely on terminal task success as the main signal of agent quality (Saeidi et al., 2026c). A trajectory may eventually solve the task while repeatedly asking unnecessary questions, requesting information that could be obtained through tools, or relying excessively on the user instead of interacting with the environment (Mishra et al., 2025). Such trajectories receive positive task reward despite exhibiting undesirable behavior for user-facing agents. This exposes an important limitation of current agent harnesses, which measure whether the task is completed but provide little signal about whether the interaction itself is efficient. We introduce τ -Emotion-Bench and Tracer to address this gap through an emotion-conditioned agent–user–environment harness. τ -Emotion-Bench contains 580 executable tasks across ten domains and systematically varies user behavior through five emotion families and fine-grained behavioral attributes while preserving the underlying task objective. Tracer constructs these tasks through grounded, policy-valid tool workflows and uses the same harness to generate training trajectories (See Figure 2). The key difference from conventional trajectory collection is that the user simulator contributes an additional interaction-aware reward signal. When an agent repeatedly asks unnecessary questions or fails to make sufficient progress, the user may become dissatisfied and terminate the interaction, preventing the trajectory from receiving a successful reward even if the task could eventually have been completed. Consequently, Tracer filters out successful but interaction-inefficient behavior and favors trajectories in which the agent asks only for information that is genuinely unavailable while relying more heavily on tools and the environment. This produces training data with a better balance between necessary user interaction and autonomous task execution. Our experiments show that τ -Emotion-Bench remains challenging even for strong frontier models. With GLM-5.2 as the emotion-conditioned user simulator, the best evaluated model, GLM-5.3, reaches 78.6% Avg@3 but only 66.5% average Pass^3. Under Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 as the user simulator, the strongest model, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, reaches 55.9% Avg@3 and only 32.2% average Pass^3. These results leave substantial room for improvement in reliable task completion across both evaluation settings. Importantly, the gains from Tracer supervision remain substantial across model scales. Under GLM-5.2, Tracer-High improves the corresponding Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, 2Under review. and 8B base models by 17.1–25.2 percentage points in Avg@3, with Tracer-High-8B reaching 54.7%. Tracer-Low, trained on only 1,498 trajectories from four domains, generalizes to six unseen domains and achieves the strongest average Pass^3 among the compared agent-training methods at all three model scales. The learned behavior also transfers to τ -Bench (Yao et al., 2024) and τ -Trait (He et al., 2025a), which use different tasks and user simulators. Our behavioral analyses further show that higher task success is associated with a better balance between tool execution and conversational follow-up, while policy violations and unnecessary interaction remain important failure modes. Together, these results show that emotion-conditioned interaction can serve not only as a stress test for agent reliability, but also as a useful reward signal for collecting more effective agent-training trajectories. Our main contributions are summarized as follows. • τ -Emotion-Bench. We introduce a ten-domain benchmark and emotion-conditioned agent– user–environment harness for evaluating tool-calling agents under diverse user behaviors while preserving the underlying task objective. • Interaction-aware trajectory generation. We introduce a scalable pipeline for generat-ing grounded agentic tasks and an interaction-aware reward signal that filters inefficient trajectories with excessive user dependence while favoring effective tool use. • Generalizable agent training. We show that Tracer consistently improves Qwen3 models across scales and transfers to unseen domains, external benchmarks, and different user simulators with a relatively small training set. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

Interactive Tool-Use and User Simulation. Agent benchmarks have increasingly shifted from isolated function calls toward stateful interaction among agents, users, and executable environments. ToolTalk, ToolSandbox, and τ -Bench introduce conversational tool use, user simulation, and policy-constrained multi-turn interaction (Farn & Shin, 2023; Lu et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2024), while 

τ 2-Bench, VitaBench, and WildToolBench further increase interaction complexity through shared environments, ambiguous requests, shifting intent, and conversational transitions (Barres et al., 2025; He et al., 2025b; Yu et al., 2026a). More recent work studies variation in user behavior, with τ -Trait controlling traits such as impatience and confusion and RealUserSim grounding user behavior in real conversational profiles (He et al., 2025a; Zhu et al., 2026a). Related affective benchmarks, including EIBench and TEA-Bench, focus on emotion management and emotional support (Zhu et al., 2026b; Sui et al., 2026). However, these works do not systematically measure how controlled emotional variation affects task-oriented tool use while preserving the same executable task and success conditions. As summarized in Table 1, τ -Emotion-Bench fills this gap by varying emotion and behavioral expression while keeping the underlying task and environment fixed. 

Synthetic Environments and Agent Training. A parallel line of work scales agent training through synthetic environments and executable trajectories. VULCAN constructs deterministic tool-use environments from API specifications and collects executable multi-step trajectories (Saeidi 3Under review. et al., 2026a), while EnvScaler, Agent World Model, and Simia generate stateful environments and training data for supervised and reinforcement learning (Song et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025). EigenData and SPADE further improve synthetic data generation through executable verification and adaptive environment construction (Gao et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). However, these methods primarily assess trajectories through task success, verification, or environment difficulty. In contrast, Tracer introduces an interaction-aware signal through the emotion-conditioned user harness, where unnecessary dependence on the user can lead to dissatisfaction and terminate the trajectory. Consequently, Tracer favors successful trajectories that balance necessary clarification with autonomous tool and environment interaction rather than task completion alone. 

## 3 τ -E MOTION -B ENCH 

3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

Following τ -Bench (Yao et al., 2024), we formulate each task as a partially observable Markov decision process (S, A, O, T, R, U ), where S is the state space, A is the agent action space, O is the observation space, T is the transition function, R is the task reward, and U is the space of user instructions. The agent interacts with both an executable environment and a simulated user. Accordingly, the action space contains tool calls and natural-language responses, while observations contain tool outputs and user messages. 

τ -Emotion-Bench extends this interaction with an emotion condition e ∈ E . Given a task instruction 

u ∈ U and the interaction history ht, the user response at turn t is sampled according to 

ut ∼ πuser (· | u, e, h t) .

The task instruction, environment, and task objective remain unchanged across emotion conditions. The emotion condition is fixed within an episode, while its expression evolves with the interaction history. The simulator can therefore react differently to successful progress, repeated clarification, unnecessary requests, or other interaction behavior without changing the underlying task. In addition to producing natural-language responses, the user can terminate the episode with either 

###STOP### or ###UNSATISFIED### . The former indicates that the user considers the interac-tion complete, following the termination mechanism of τ -Bench, while the latter indicates that the accumulated interaction has become sufficiently unsatisfactory to continue. An episode therefore ends when the task interaction terminates normally, when the user becomes unsatisfied, or when the maximum interaction horizon is reached. If ###UNSATISFIED### is emitted before success-ful task completion, the trajectory receives −1 reward. This extension preserves the original task objective while allowing the interaction itself to affect whether a trajectory is considered successful. 3.2 TASK CONSTRUCTION 

We construct grounded and executable tasks across ten tool-use domains, following the general interactive tool-use setup represented by τ -Bench, τ -Trait, and ToolOrchestra (Yao et al., 2024; He et al., 2025a; Su et al., 2025). Our construction separates the generation of valid tool workflows from their grounding in concrete environment states. For each domain, we construct a directed tool graph 

G = ( V, E ) following the dependency-based formulation of VULCAN (Saeidi et al., 2026a). Each node v ∈ V corresponds to a task-relevant tool, while an edge (vi, v j ) ∈ E indicates that vj can validly follow vi according to the tool interfaces and domain policy. Candidate edges are inferred from tool definitions, execution behavior, and policy constraints, and the graph is manually verified before task generation. We then sample valid paths from G to obtain workflows of varying complexity and compose multiple paths when constructing multi-intent tasks. Detailed sampling configurations are provided in Appendix A, with additional environment details in Appendix D. A sampled workflow defines the sequence of operations needed to complete a task, but leaves the specific entities and arguments unspecified. The grounding agent fills in these details using the executable environment, producing a valid reference trajectory. The preference agent then turns the grounded task into a natural user request and adds preferences that are feasible without changing the task’s intended outcome. Separately, the feeling generator uses the user context and requested 4Under review. actions to describe the user’s likely emotional state. It does not access the environment database, so the generated description cannot rely on information that would be unavailable to the user. 3.3 EMOTION MODELING AND USER SIMULATION 

We next map the task-specific feeling generated in Section 3.2 to a controlled emotion condition. Our emotion space follows the Atlas of Emotions (Ekman, 2016) and contains five families with 22 fine-grained emotions. These are Anger (annoyance , frustration , argumentativeness , bitterness ,

exasperation ), Fear (anxiety , nervousness , dread , desperation ), Disgust (dislike , aversion , distaste ,

repugnance ), Sadness (disappointment , discouragement , helplessness , hopelessness , sorrow ), and 

Enjoyment (relief , pride , peace , excitement ). We further combine each emotion with three behavioral dimensions covering politeness, urgency, and trust, yielding 594 conditions that capture both what the user feels and how that feeling is expressed. To map each free-form task feeling into this space, we use natural-language anchors and a multi-head classifier over the fine-grained emotion and behavioral attributes, then use semantic similarity to the anchors in the classifier’s learned embedding space to select the final condition. The corresponding natural-language instruction is provided to the user simulator rather than the discrete labels. Further details of the taxonomy, anchor construction, and mapping procedure are provided in Appendix A.1. Given the selected emotion instruction, the user simulator generates each response conditioned on the task and the evolving conversation history. Rather than enforcing a fixed surface style, the instruction acts as a behavioral constraint, allowing the same emotion condition to be expressed differently as the interaction develops. This provides controlled variation in user behavior without scripting individual turns. The same interaction mechanism is used during Tracer trajectory collection, where the simulator’s responses provide an additional signal for selecting trajectories beyond environment-level task success. 3.4 TASK VERIFICATION AND DATA COMPOSITION

We verify generated tasks before including them in the benchmark. A task must specify a concrete objective, provide sufficient information for ex-ecution, admit an executable reference trajectory, and remain consistent with the corresponding do-main policy. Reference trajectories are validated through deterministic replay, while model-based adjudication is used only when the determinis-tic outcome is inconclusive. All 580 evaluation tasks are additionally reviewed manually under the same criteria, and this process identifies 101 tasks requiring correction before inclusion. As an additional solvability check, a human tool-calling agent successfully completes selected tasks on which the evaluated models fail across all trials. The final benchmark contains 70 evaluation tasks from each of Airline, Retail, Telecom, and Tele-health and 50 from each of School, Basketball, Restaurant, Travel, Movie, and Bank. We first describe the evaluated models, training baselines, and evaluation protocol, followed by in-domain, out-of-domain, and cross-benchmark results. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models. We evaluate a broad set of strong open-weight and proprietary LLM agents, covering both thinking and non-thinking configurations when available. Our evaluation includes Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025), Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507 (Yang et al., 2025), Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026), DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (Xu et al., 2026a), Gemma-4-31B-it (Team, 2026), GLM-5.3 (GLM-5-Team et al., 2026), Kimi-K2.7-Code (Team et al., 2025), MiniMax-M2.7, MiniMax-M3 (Chen et al., 2026) and Muse-Glimmer-30B (Meta Superintelligence Lab, 2026). To study whether Tracer-generated supervision can improve substantially smaller agents, we additionally fine-tune Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B under two training settings. 

Tracer-High is trained on 1,958 trajectories generated across all ten τ -Emotion-Bench domains, while Tracer-Low is trained on 1,498 trajectories from only Airline, Retail, Telecom, and Telehealth domains. This setup allows us to measure both the effectiveness of Tracer supervision and the extent to which the learned behavior transfers beyond the domains represented in the training data. Further hyperparameter details are included in Appendix B.1. 

Agent-Training Baselines. For out-of-domain evaluation, we compare Tracer-Low against ex-isting agent-training approaches applied to the same Qwen3 model scales whenever correspond-ing results are available. These include AReaL (Fu et al., 2026), EnvScaler (Song et al., 2026), Arctic-AWM (Wang et al., 2026), SPADE (Liu et al., 2026), Simia-Tau-SFT (Li et al., 2025), and Simia-Tau-SFT-RL. The comparison covers both supervised and reinforcement-learning-based agent training approaches and is particularly useful for evaluating whether Tracer’s comparatively small training set produces behaviors that transfer to unseen domains.

Frontier agents remain fragile under emotion-conditioned interactions. Figure 1 and Table 3 show that τ -Emotion-Bench remains challenging even for strong frontier models under Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 as the user simulator. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash performs best with 55.9% Avg@3, followed by GLM-5.3 and Kimi-K2.7-Code at 51.9% and 49.3%, respectively. More importantly, performance drops substantially when consistency across repeated trials is required, with the highest average Pass^3 reaching only 32.2%. Moreover, performance varies considerably across domains. For example, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash achieves 44.3% Pass^3 on Retail but only 17.1% on Telecom, while GLM-5.3 reaches 62.0% on Basketball but only 2.9% on Telecom. Overall, these results show that strong aggregate agent capability does not ensure reliable behavior under emotionally variable user interactions. We additionally evaluate the same models with GLM-5.2 as the emotion-conditioned user simulator. Although absolute performance increases, the benchmark remains unsaturated. The strongest model, GLM-5.3, reaches 78.6% Avg@3 but only 66.5% average Pass^3, leaving a substantial gap in consistent task completion. Moreover, performance remains highly uneven across domains, with GLM-5.3 reaching 94.3% Pass^3 on Retail but only 40.0% on Telecom. These results show that the reliability gap exposed by τ -Emotion-Bench persists under a second strong user simulator. Detailed results with GLM-5.2 are provided in Appendix C.4.

Empathy is associated with more follow-up. Figure 6 shows that the re-lationship between empathy and task success varies across emotions. Empa-thy is associated with lower success un-der Enjoyment across all model groups and under Fear for most groups, while its effect under Disgust varies substan-tially across models. Small gains also appear in several settings, particularly under Sadness. More consistently, em-pathetic responses are accompanied by a higher fraction of follow-up questions across every model group. Together with the previous analysis, this suggests that additional empathetic interaction can increase conversational overhead when it does not resolve uncertainty or advance the task. Overall, effective em-pathy should acknowledge the user’s emotional state without introducing un-necessary interaction. 

Emotion conditioning does not destabilize the user simulator. A potential confound in τ -Emotion-Bench is that emotional conditioning could cause the simulated user to deviate from its task instruction, making some apparent agent failures originate from the simulator. We therefore examine 6,961 failed trajectories, flag 449 user turns as potential hallucinations, and verify each against the original task instruction with an LLM judge (See Appendix C.1). Only 124 are confirmed as user errors, corresponding to 1.1% of failed trials and at most 1.4% for any emotion family. Moreover, Figure 7 shows that most emotion and domain combinations contain no hallucinations, with the few 10 Under review. elevated rates appearing as isolated cases. Although some annotation uncertainty may remain, these results suggest that emotion conditioning does not meaningfully compromise simulator reliability. See Appendices C and E for additional error analysis, task coverage analysis, and the human study. 

Training interaction patterns may help explain Tracer’s generalization. To investigate why Tracer-trained models generalize more effectively, we test the hypothesis that training trajectories which emphasize progress through tools while limiting unnecessary follow-up encourage agents to rely more on the environment and less on continued user interaction. Figure 8 compares this interaction pattern across Tracer-Low and Tracer-High and the publicly available training trajectories from two prior agent-training approaches, AReaL and Simia. Tool calls exceed follow-up actions in 76.2% of Tracer-Low and 78.9% of Tracer-High trajectories, compared with 55.6% for AReaL and only 22.0% for Simia. Conversely, 51.9% of Simia trajectories contain more follow-up actions than tool calls, compared with 12.8% for Tracer-Low and 10.6% for Tracer-High. This distribution is consistent with the interaction-aware signal used by Tracer, which is designed to discourage unnecessary user dependence during trajectory collection. Together, these observations support the hypothesis that the interaction-aware signal helps collect trajectories with a more effective balance between necessary user interaction and autonomous tool use. Since the datasets also differ in other aspects of their construction, this evidence remains correlational. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced τ -Emotion-Bench, a ten-domain benchmark for evaluating tool-calling agents under controlled emotion-conditioned user interactions. Our evaluation reveals substantial performance degradation and persistent policy failures when agents interact with emotionally variable users. We further proposed Tracer, a scalable approach for generating grounded agentic tasks and training trajectories through an emotion-conditioned agent–user–environment harness. Tracer-tuned models achieve strong performance across both in-domain and out-of-domain evaluations while using substantially less training data than prior agent-training methods. Our behavioral analysis suggests that these gains are linked to a more effective balance between tool execution and necessary user follow-up. This behavior is encouraged by the interaction-aware signal exposed by the harness, which favors efficient task progress rather than task completion alone. Overall, our findings highlight user behavior as an important dimension of agent reliability and show that interaction-aware data generation can provide efficient and transferable supervision for tool-calling agents. 

## LIMITATIONS 

Our study has two main limitations. First, we evaluate Tracer-generated data only through supervised fine-tuning, and its effectiveness as supervision for reinforcement learning remains unexplored. Second, our experiments focus on tool-calling agents. Extending τ -Emotion-Bench and Tracer to other agent settings, such as coding and web-based agents, is an interesting direction for future work. ## A DATA STATISTICS 

We construct ten emotion-conditioned tool-use benchmarks by reusing the customer-service environ-ments users, database schemas, and tool APIs released with τ -Bench, τ -Trait, and ToolOrchestra (Yao et al., 2024; He et al., 2025a; Su et al., 2025), without modification to the underlying environment logic. For each domain, we generate candidate tool dependency graphs using GPT-4.1, GPT-5.1, GPT-5.2 (Singh et al., 2025; Achiam et al., 2023), GLM-5.2, GLM-5.3, GLM-5.3-flash (GLM-5-Team et al., 2026), Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026), and Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025), and select the highest-quality graph per domain through manual review, before sampling tool traces over it. For each domain’s selected tool graph, we sample tool traces by composing 1–3 independent random walks (intents) of length 2–6 tool calls each into a single trace, retaining unique traces after deduplication. Per-domain unique trace counts range from 2,603 (Retail) to 8,175 (Airline), with Telecom, Telehealth, School, Basketball, Restaurant, Travel, Movie, and Bank yielding between 3,344 and 4,641 unique traces each, totaling 42,138 unique traces across the ten domains. We used a different set of traces for generating evaluation and training tasks. A.1 EMOTION MODELING DETAILS 

Our emotion taxonomy follows the Atlas of Emotions (Ekman, 2016) and contains 22 fine-grained emotions organized into five families. Anger contains annoyance, frustration, argumentativeness, bitterness, and exasperation. Fear contains anxiety, nervousness, dread, and desperation. Disgust con-tains dislike, aversion, distaste, and repugnance. Sadness contains disappointment, discouragement, helplessness, hopelessness, and sorrow. Enjoyment contains relief, pride, peace, and excitement. Each fine-grained emotion is combined with three independent behavioral dimensions. Polite-ness takes values from {warm , neutral , cold }, urgency from {low , moderate , high }, and trust from 

{trusting , cautious , skeptical }. Combining the 22 fine-grained emotions with these dimensions yields 594 emotion conditions. For each condition, we generate 10 natural-language emotion instructions with GPT-4.1, producing 5,940 anchor instructions that describe how the corresponding user should communicate. We embed these anchors with text-embedding-3-small and use them to train a multi-head classifier that predicts the fine-grained emotion together with politeness, urgency, and trust. Given the task-specific feeling generated during task construction, we project it into the classifier’s learned embedding space. We use similarity-weighted votes from the nearest anchor instructions in this space to select the final emotion condition. One of the 10 natural-language anchor instructions for that condition, chosen at random, is then provided to the user simulator rather than the discrete emotion and behavioral labels. A.2 EVALUATION DATA 

We initially generate 2,266 candidate evaluation tasks from sampled tool traces using the methodology described in Section 3.2 and illustrated in Figure 2. We use Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025) for all generation agents in the Tracer pipeline. Tasks that pass the verification procedure described in Section 3.4 are then rolled out using Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct as both the tool-calling agent and the user simulator. This additional rollout stage allows us to observe how the generated tasks behave during interaction and identify cases requiring further modification. After this stage, 884 tasks are retained for manual review. During manual review, we make three main types of modifications. • Ground-truth edits. We revise 101 tasks by correcting reference parameters, adding missing ground-truth actions, or removing unnecessary actions to ensure that the intended solution is executable and consistent with the corresponding domain policy. • ID-swap edits. We identify 23 tasks containing internal identifiers such as payment_id 

that would not normally be known to the user. We replace these identifiers with user-accessible information, such as the last four digits of a payment card, while preserving the intended task. • Larger rewrites. We revise 23 tasks to introduce policy-relevant challenges that make the interaction more difficult while preserving task solvability. For example, some tasks involve 17 Under review. a user requesting an action on behalf of another person after authentication, requiring the agent to follow the domain policy rather than simply execute the user’s request. After these revisions, the final benchmark contains 580 evaluation tasks. Airline, Retail, Telecom, and Telehealth each contain 70 tasks, while School, Basketball, Restaurant, Travel, Movie, and Bank each contain 50 tasks. Each task is provided with its emotion instruction and, for controlled comparisons, a corresponding version without emotion conditioning. A.3 TRAINING DATA 

Training tasks are generated with the same pipeline as the evaluation tasks. In Airline, Retail, Telecom, and Telehealth, training and evaluation tasks are grounded in the same environment database. In School, Basketball, Restaurant, Travel, Movie, and Bank, the training and evaluation tasks each have their own database. In total, we generate 7,767 training tasks (Table 6). We roll out each training task once with Qwen3.8-27B in thinking mode as the agent and Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct as the emotion-conditioned user simulator, with a limit of 50 interaction steps. The agent’s reasoning is kept on every assistant turn. Rollouts that failed because of endpoint errors are discarded, leaving 7,048 trajectories. C.1 ERROR ANALYSIS 

We adapt the conversation-review judge of τ 2-Bench (Barres et al., 2025) and FAMA (Saeidi et al., 2026b) to analyze failed trajectories. For each failed trajectory (reward ̸ = 1 ) the judge receives the domain policy, the user simulator’s guidelines, the user instruction including its emotion instruction, the reference action sequence, and the full conversation with all tool calls and results. It returns every error it finds, each with its source (agent or user), a severity, one or more fault tags, the turn where it occurs, and a short justification. Agent errors are critical if they caused the failure or violated an important policy, and minor otherwise. We extend the judge with an emotional-outcome label that records whether the user abandoned the conversation and, if so, the dominant reason: dismissive tone, ignored emotional cues, procedural friction, or emotional neglect. We use Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct at temperature 0 as the judge and group its fault tags into five categories and fifteen fault types (Table 11). We apply it to eleven models: the Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B baselines, our Tracer-Low and Tracer-High models at the same scales, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash. Figure 11 aggregates the eight models other than Tracer-Low, yielding 6,961 trajectories with at least one identified fault, and decomposes these failures into five categories and fifteen fault types across domains. Overall, failures are concentrated in task execution rather than affective interaction. Missing or extra actions account for 25–36% of faults across domains, while incomplete resolution accounts for 20–29% and wrong tool calls for 14–27%. Misread intent is the most frequent individual fault overall at 15.9%, followed by incorrect action order at 13.7%, wrong tool arguments at 12.9%, and missed actions at 12.2%. In contrast, policy and interaction faults contribute only 4–10% in each domain, while emotional neglect remains below 1.7%. Thus, under emotion-conditioned interaction, agents fail primarily by misinterpreting the request or executing the required workflow incorrectly rather than by failing to acknowledge the user’s emotional state. 21 Under review. C.3 TASK COVERAGE 

Figure 13 reports, for each task, how many of the three trials are solved by at least one of the 18 evaluated models. Coverage is high across most domains, although the consistency of successful solutions varies substantially. Retail has 68 of 70 tasks solved in all three trials, followed by Airline and Telehealth with 63 of 70 each. Among the 50-task domains, Bank, School, Basketball, and Restaurant have 45–48 tasks solved in all three trials, while Travel and Movie have 36 and 34, respectively. Overall, only 15 tasks are never solved by any evaluated model across the three trials, including 1 Airline, 5 Telecom, 1 Telehealth, 2 Basketball, 1 Restaurant, 4 Travel, and 1 Movie task. To further verify that difficult tasks remain executable, Appendix E.1 reports human tool-calling trials under the same tools, policies, and emotion-conditioned user simulator. The successful human trials provide additional evidence that persistent model failures can arise from the difficulty of maintaining the correct workflow under emotionally variable interaction rather than invalid task construction, leaving substantial headroom for current agents. C.4 DETAILED RESULTS 

Tables 12, 13, and 14 report per-domain Pass^1, Pass^2, and Pass^3 on τ -Emotion-Bench. Table 12 covers the evaluated thinking and non-thinking models together with Tracer-Low and Tracer-High, Table 13 covers the Qwen3 base models and existing agent-training methods at the same scales, and Table 14 repeats the evaluation of Table 12 with GLM-5.2 instead of Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct as the user simulator. All averages below are unweighted means over the ten domains. 

Consistency across trials. Success drops sharply when a task must be solved in more than one trial. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash falls from 55.9% average Pass^1 to 32.2% Pass^3, GLM-5.3 from 51.9% to 31.7%, and Kimi-K2.7-Code from 49.3% to 26.1%, and most models lose 14–24 points between the two metrics. Even the strongest agents therefore solve only about a third of the tasks in all three trials. 

Variation across domains. Difficulty varies widely across domains, and the best model changes from one domain to the next. Travel and Movie are the hardest: no model exceeds 30.0% Pass^3 on either, and only DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and GLM-5.3 exceed 45% Pass^1 on Movie. Telecom is difficult for frontier models in particular, none of which exceeds 38.1% Pass^1 or 17.1% Pass^3; 24 Under review. GLM-5.3, for example, reaches 62.0% Pass^3 on Basketball but only 2.9% on Telecom. Individual models also show sharp domain-specific strengths: Muse-Glimmer-30B is the best model on School (64.0% Pass^3) but solves almost no Retail tasks (1.4% Pass^1), and Kimi-K2.7-Code leads on Bank with 52.0% Pass^3. 

Effect of Tracer training. Tracer-High improves over the corresponding Qwen3 base model at every scale, raising average Pass^1 from 6.0% to 25.7% at 1.7B, from 18.0% to 37.7% at 4B, and from 24.4% to 38.1% at 8B, and average Pass^3 from 1.1% to 10.4%, from 3.9% to 17.2%, and from 8.4% to 19.8%, respectively. Tracer-High-8B outperforms Qwen3-32B in both modes as well as Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking (36.0% Pass^1, 16.5% Pass^3). The Tracer models are strongest on Airline, Retail, Telecom, and Telehealth, the four domains shared by both training sets: Tracer-Low-8B achieves the best Telecom scores of all evaluated models (41.9%, 26.2%, and 20.0%), and Tracer-High-8B achieves the best Telehealth Pass^2 (39.5%) and ties DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash for the best Telehealth Pass^3 (31.4%). Comparing the two training settings isolates the effect of domain coverage. On these four domains, Tracer-Low-8B and Tracer-High-8B perform similarly (49.6% vs. 48.2% average Pass^1), whereas on the remaining six Tracer-High-8B reaches 31.3% compared with 20.7% for Tracer-Low-8B, with the largest differences on School (56.0% vs. 29.3%) and Bank (46.7% vs. 28.7%). Restaurant, Travel, and Movie remain difficult for all Tracer models, with Pass^3 of at most 12.0%. 

Comparison with agent-training methods. Table 13 shows that existing agent-training methods often fail to improve over the base model under emotion-conditioned users. AReaL and SPADE score below the corresponding base model in average Pass^1 at every scale, with SPADE reaching at most 3.5%, while Arctic-AWM, Simia-Tau-SFT, and Simia-Tau-SFT-RL stay within 3 points of the base model. EnvScaler is the strongest baseline, but its gains are concentrated on Airline, Retail, Telecom, and Telehealth: at 8B, it reaches 39.8% average Pass^1 on these four domains but only 20.8% on the other six. At every scale, Tracer-Low (Table 12) achieves a higher average Pass^3 than every method in Table 13 (7.2%, 13.0%, and 15.2% at 1.7B, 4B, and 8B, compared with 3.1%, 9.6%, and 11.8% for EnvScaler). On the six domains excluded from Tracer-Low’s training, Tracer-Low matches or exceeds EnvScaler in average Pass^1 (8.5% vs. 6.5%, 18.6% vs. 16.9%, and 20.7% vs. 20.8%) and exceeds it in average Pass^3 (2.7% vs. 0.7%, 7.7% vs. 3.3%, and 8.3% vs. 7.0%). 

Robustness to the user simulator. With GLM-5.2 as the user simulator (Table 14), every model scores higher than with Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct; for example, average Pass^1 rises from 55.9% to 77.5% for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, from 51.9% to 78.6% for GLM-5.3, and from 38.1% to 54.7% for Tracer-High-8B. The relative ordering is nevertheless largely preserved: GLM-5.3, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, Kimi-K2.7-Code, and Qwen3.8-27B are the four strongest models in average Pass^1 under both simulators. The gains from Tracer training also persist, with Tracer-High improving average Pass^1 over the base model by 25.2, 20.2, and 17.5 points and average Pass^3 by 15.4, 18.0, and 14.9 points at 1.7B, 4B, and 8B, respectively. As with the default simulator, Tracer-Low performs best on its four training domains, where Tracer-Low-8B reaches 76.1% average Pass^1 and the best Telecom Pass^1 of all models (75.2%), but drops to 27.1% on the other six. These results indicate that the user simulator affects absolute scores, but not the main conclusions drawn from τ -Emotion-Bench.
