## Abstract 

Traditional human-computer interaction models rely on domain-specific techniques to model target prediction; models designed for cursor interaction prediction fail to generalize to mobile interfaces and vice versa. We introduce a unifying framework grounded in thermodynamics, proposing that human interaction is composed of phases in thermodynamic equilibrium and non-equilibrium. To demonstrate this, we derive Fitts’ law and the proposed target prediction model from equilibrium thermodynamics by assigning kinetic and potential energies to a moving agent and target. Sub-sequently, we analyze the shortcomings of the prediction model and Fitts’ law in edge cases, such as predicting intent for large targets. This analysis demonstrates that large targets cannot be ac-curately modeled using equilibrium thermodynamics. The proposed model scales across interaction modalities without modification, requires zero training data, and evaluates in constant 𝒪 (1) time. Furthermore, we show that design properties such as the color of a button act as independent parameters that influence the attractive force exerted on an agent. Applied to live web prefetching tasks, the framework achieved an efficient Fetch:Click ratio of 1.37 and predicted the user’s target with an accuracy of 98.1%.

## 1 Introduction 

Human movement follows the path of least effort [48]. This behavior is rooted in the Principle of Least Action, a universal physical law, which suggests that a system will naturally take the path between two points that minimizes a total quantity. This enables modeling human movement using energy frameworks such as the Hamiltonian and Lagrangian. The theoretical foundation of Human-Computer Interaction is governed by models rooted in energy frameworks. Paul Fitts’ law [12] is directly derived from Claude Shannon’s theory of data trans-mission [42]. Crucially, data transmission works by reducing un-certainty. Shannon defined this reduction of uncertainty as Infor-mation Entropy. Jaynes [20] mathematically proved that Shannon’s information entropy formulation is identical to the Gibbs entropy arXiv:2608.07123v1 [cs.HC] 7 Aug 2026 Uzafir Ahmad Rafaq, Muaz Hassan, and Ali Muzaffar 

formula. Because the Gibbs distribution strictly describes systems in thermal equilibrium, the historical models of HCI are fundamentally thermodynamic in nature. Building upon this lineage, this study models HCI through its thermodynamic roots. It shows that, much like a physical ther-modynamic system, the complete lifecycle of human-computer interaction is composed of equilibrium and non-equilibrium phases. To further demonstrate, it recovers Fitts’ law from equilibrium sta-tistical mechanics, revealing that Fitts’ law is a thermodynamic model used to describe a system in equilibrium. Consequently, the well-documented limitations of Fitts’ law, such as for purely ballistic movements, can be understood as non-equilibrium failure modes. Target prediction models can be created to understand user in-tent for the equilibrium and non-equilibrium phases of interaction. Accurate prediction of user intent has applications in user interface design, such as making target icons sticky [44] or enlarging the target [30, 31]. Different interaction mediums approach the problem of predicting user intent separately. VR systems, mobile devices, and desktop cursors, each have their own unique methods to pre-dict the user’s target. These methods are limited to their specific interaction mediums: touch prediction models for phones do not function on desktops, and conversely, cursor-based models fail on mobile devices. Various modeling options have previously been adopted. Early methods relying on static kinematic and spatial heuristics [2, 3, 26, 36] are computationally lightweight, but do not account for variability and noise in human motor behavior. Data-driven paradigms, using neural networks [5, 27] and re-inforcement learning [46] account for the variability in human behavior by pattern matching. However, these models require mas-sive datasets and struggle to generalize across different devices without extensive retraining. In addition, they lack interpretability, where it is not possible to determine why a model made a particular decision. The data requirements of neural networks have been partially addressed by using biomechanical simulations [34] that use a virtual human to perform tasks and gather data. However, biomechanical simulation approaches require significant computational resources and remain domain-specific. Conversely, Optimal Control models [11, 23, 29, 47] successfully ground prediction in fundamental physics, but they demand com-putationally intense optimization that is impractical for real-time deployment on low-end devices. Therefore, there remains a need for a unified intent prediction model that combines the physical rigor of Optimal Control with the computational efficiency of static heuristics. To address this, this study introduces a model that supports both 1D mobile scrolling and 2D cursor interaction. The proposed model computes the prob-ability of interaction between the agent and the target from their kinetic and potential energies. Additionally, we demonstrate that the properties of the target, such as the color of a button, have an impact on the attractive force on the agent by modifying the potential difference between them. In order to apply the model to a practical use case, prefetching of website links is adopted as an evaluation criterion. Once the model establishes that a particular link will be clicked, it is prefetched. The model is evaluated in both 1D mobile scrolling and 2D cursor interaction. 

## 1.1 Aim, Objectives and Contributions 

1.1.1 Aim of the study. Develop and evaluate a probabilistic model to predict user interaction across both desktop and mobile inter-faces. 

1.1.2 Objectives of the study. 

(1) Formulate the model using statistical mechanics. (2) Implement the model in a working web system. (3) Evaluate prediction accuracy and prefetching efficiency. (4) Test whether target properties (button color) affect interac-tion accuracy. (5) Identify the model’s limitations and possible future work. 

1.1.3 Contributions of this study. 

(1) Present a model for equilibrium HCI that predicts user intent with high accuracy, works across dimensions (1D and 2D), does not require training data, and evaluates in constant 𝒪 (1) time, requiring simple lightweight multipli-cation to be computed. (2) Indicate that agents aim for the exact center of a target as a result of the harmonic potential difference being reduced, causing the agent to exhibit damped harmonic motion about the equilibrium position, therefore explaining why users overshoot and correct their trajectory. (3) Demonstrate that the endpoints are normally distributed in an equilibrium phase as a result of the harmonic potential difference being reduced in an agent-target system (4) Show that an equilibrium-only model has failure modes, suggesting that the full life cycle of interaction is comprised of distinct equilibrium and non-equilibrium phases. Simul-taneously, propose a diagnostic criterion for identifying when a system enters a non-equilibrium state. (5) Recover Fitts’ and Schmidt’s laws from equilibrium statisti-cal mechanics to indicate that they and related laws which originate from Claude Shannon’s Information Entropy are properties of systems in equilibrium. Since they are con-strained to the equilibrium phase, they cannot apply to the complete life cycle of human behavior, which is also composed of non-equilibrium phases. (6) Demonstrate that for an agent-target system in equilibrium, each parameter such as the color of a button, influences the attractive force towards the user by modifying the geometry of the potential field. (7) Show that each parameter that attracts an agent to a target is independent and additive. For example, the color of a button and its text label clarity independently influence the attractive force and are therefore additive properties. 

## 2 Related Work 

Target prediction can be approached from multiple different angles, such as with neural networks or control theory. The applications of successfully predicting a target are numerous and have a high impact on user experience. Thermodynamic Human-Computer Interaction 

## 2.1 Applications of Target Prediction 

Once the user’s intent to interact with a specific target has been successfully inferred, numerous practical applications arise, such as (1) Move candidate targets closer to the pointing location [40] (2) Restricting pointing to selectable targets [16] (3) Sticky Icons [44] (4) Enlarge the candidate targets [30, 31] (5) Expand the cursor’s interactive area [7, 15, 35] (6) Prefetching website links [37] The application of target prediction in this study is chosen as prefetching. The prefetching process involves a system that calls an API to fetch the contents of a page on a website before the user clicks on the link. This enhances the user experience by providing zero loading and instant navigation. This application is chosen be-cause of its high impact on economics and productivity. Miller [33] showed that a computer must respond in under 100 milliseconds to keep the user focused on their current task. If a system waits for a user to initiate a request before starting its computation, the sys-tem will feel slow. Small delays cause users to leave a website and decrease their overall engagement. Brutlag showed that adding just a few hundred milliseconds of delay significantly reduces user in-teractivity [6]. Similarly, Kohavi demonstrated that minor increases in latency cause measurable financial loss and user frustration in commercial systems [25]. In modern web development, prefetch-ing is often implemented using JavaScript libraries. For example, Quicklink [1] is a tool that automatically prefetches the URLs of links that are currently visible on the user’s screen. ForesightJS [43] takes a different approach by focusing on the trajectory of the cursor to predict which link is about to be clicked. 

## 2.2 Spatial & Kinematic Heuristics 

Spatial heuristics involve tracking the position of an agent, such as detecting the pointing finger in mid-air [2] using motion sensors and applying a probabilistic nearest neighbor algorithm to detect the target which will be selected. However, this inevitably results in one of the selectable targets being ranked higher constantly, despite the user having no intention of interacting with it, and this approach does not incorporate kinematic data. Another spatial heuristic approach is to measure the angle between the velocity vec-tor of the agent and the position vector of the target [36]. Although angle based heuristics successfully incorporate kinematic data, they suffer from inaccuracies when attempting to make predictions in cluttered interfaces, where a lot of targets are in close proximity. Further work on kinematic and spatial heuristics involves track-ing the peak movement velocity of the cursor and its direction [3, 14, 26]. ForesightJS [43] is a JavaScript library which is built upon these kinematic and spatial heuristics. It involves projecting the cursor velocity and direction forward in time to predict the end-point of interaction. All these kinematic and spatial heuristics help determine the endpoint of interaction but do not provide enough information on the click event itself. The Intermittent Click Plan-ning model [39] improves on this weakness by providing insight on when the click event occurs. A shared problem among all these heuristics is that they fail to account for the variability in human behavior. If an attempt is made to predict the landing position of a cursor based purely on kinematic and spatial data, then the fact that a user could abort the process mid-way is not accounted for. 

## 2.3 Data-Driven and Learning Paradigms 

Data-driven and learning paradigms expand on purely spatial and kinetic heuristics by introducing techniques such as neural net-works [5, 27] and reinforcement learning, including its use in biome-chanical simulation [8–10, 19, 32, 34, 38, 46]. Neural network based approaches work by learning from kinematic and spatial features, including the velocity of the agent, its distance from the target, the angle between the velocity vector of the agent and the position vector of the target, etc. This helps overcome the human usage vari-ability issues associated with purely kinematic and spatial heuristics by accounting for usage patterns. However, they fail to generalize to different tasks without requiring retraining. In addition, training neural networks requires a lot of human data and is a computa-tionally expensive task. In contrast, reinforcement learning frames interaction as a sequential decision-making problem aimed at max-imizing cumulative reward over time. These models are typically formalized using a Markov Decision Process (MDP), or a Partially Observable MDP (POMDP) when the agent only has partial sen-sory access to the environment, defined by a tuple consisting of a state space representing the environment, a set of permissible actions, a transition function dictating the probability of moving between states, a reward signal, and a discount factor. Unlike neu-ral network based approaches, reinforcement learning does not require pre-collected human data, since the agent instead learns by interacting with an environment. Biomechanical simulation builds on this by pairing reinforcement learning with a physiologically grounded musculoskeletal model and physics engine, which to-gether serve as the environment from which the agent learns. This addresses the data requirement and generalization issues of neural network based approaches, since the simulated human can generate unlimited training data across different tasks. Studies have shown that such simulations can closely replicate human performance metrics [32, 34], with the simulated human naturally following Fitts’ law [12] and the speed-accuracy tradeoff [45] as an emergent property of the simulation without being hard-coded. However, the inter-user variability in these simulations is typically smaller than observed human variability. The simulations simplify real physiol-ogy, are sensitive to the choice of hyper-parameters, and carry a high computational cost to train and run. 

## 2.4 Control-Theoretic and Analytical Models 

Control-Theoretic and analytical models ground prediction in fun-damental physics and motor-control theory rather than fitting pa-rameters to human data. The simplest of these, the Minimum Jerk model [13], assumes that human reaching movements minimize jerk (the rate of change of acceleration), producing smooth, bell-shaped velocity profiles without modeling feedback or noise. More sophis-ticated Optimal Control models frame movement as a closed-loop process in which a simulated user continuously corrects its actions based on noisy sensory feedback. Linear-Quadratic-Gaussian (LQG) control [11] models the body and interface as a single dynamical sys-tem with linear dynamics and a quadratic cost function, combining a Kalman filter to estimate hidden state (e.g., velocity) from noisy Uzafir Ahmad Rafaq, Muaz Hassan, and Ali Muzaffar 

observations, which lets it reproduce entire trajectory distributions rather than a single deterministic path. However, LQG’s reliance on linear dynamics and quadratic costs makes it unsuitable for re-alistic, nonlinear biomechanical models. Model Predictive Control (MPC) [23] addresses this by repeatedly solving a short-horizon optimization problem at each timestep rather than the entire move-ment at once, allowing nonlinear, physiologically grounded arm models to be used. Intermittent Control [29] instead assumes that users only correct their movement intermittently, when the discrep-ancy between perceived and intended cursor position grows large enough, more closely matching the discrete submovement structure observed in human trajectories. Most recently, Active Inference (AIF) models [24] have reframed the problem entirely: rather than hand-tuning a cost function, the agent acts to fulfil preferences over its own future perceptions while minimizing uncertainty, unifying perception, belief updating, and action into a single probabilistic loop. Notably, AIF models reproduce qualitatively different pointing behavior for targets of differing difficulty using a single fixed param-eter set, whereas the other Optimal Control approaches typically require separate retuning per target or user. While these models successfully ground prediction in fundamental physics and motor-control theory, they demand computationally intense optimization (e.g., solving thousands of sampled action rollouts per timestep in the case of AIF, or repeated short-horizon optimization in MPC) that is impractical for real-time deployment on low-end devices. 

## 3 Methodology 

To model human interaction, it is necessary to define the boundaries of the system being observed and identify the known parameters so that we can select an appropriate physics framework. For an information system, properties such as the position and ve-locity of a cursor and that of buttons are known, and since we want to model probabilities, statistical mechanics is a logical choice. Sta-tistical mechanics is in the domain of thermodynamics and before applying it, we must declare whether the system we are modeling for is in equilibrium or not. 

## 3.1 The Equilibrium Foundations of HCI 

Inherently, human-computer interaction is governed by a user’s noisy inputs and cognitive dynamics, so it should be a classic ex-ample of a non-equilibrium system. However, historically HCI and the broader computer science community have treated the human-computer system as if it were in equilibrium, often implicitly. Claude Shannon’s work on Information Theory [42] assumed that the sys-tem generating the signals is stable, closed, and not chaotically changing over time. Jaynes [20] proved that Shannon’s Information Entropy formula is identical to the Gibbs entropy formula. The Gibbs entropy formula describes a system that is in thermal equilib-rium. Paul Fitts’ [12] work, which is built on top of Shannon’s, also inherits that equilibrium assumption. To demonstrate this under-lying assumption, the subsequent sections recover Fitts’ law from equilibrium statistical mechanics. Despite its limitation being well documented, Fitts’ law serves as a robust approximation. Given the effectiveness of this approxima-tion, human-computer interaction is perhaps defined by phases of equilibrium and non-equilibrium. Using a falsification analysis, this study approximates the entire interaction as an equilibrium system to identify the inaccuracies that emerge in the resultant model and theorems. This will help to establish that while the equilibrium assumption might be true for certain phases, it does not describe the entire interaction.

The position and velocity of the cursor and buttons in any given instance of time represent a microstate. A successful interaction occurs when the system observes a microstate where an agent is at rest and its geometric boundary intersects the boundary of the target. ## 3.8 Bayesian Decision-Making 

The output of Equation (4) gives a probability output weighted against other links. However, this output alone does not provide enough information to make a rational decision on whether or not an action should be performed. Rather than assigning a heuristic parameter such as "Execute action if P > 0.5", the decision making process should be weighed against its cost and benefit, and not just the probability of an interaction occurring. Therefore, we utilize the following formulation: 

E[𝑈 ] = (𝜔 𝑔𝑎𝑖𝑛 · P𝑖 ) − 𝜔 𝑐𝑜𝑠𝑡 , 𝜔 𝑔𝑎𝑖𝑛 , 𝜔 𝑐𝑜𝑠𝑡 ∈ [ 0, 1] (57) This decision making framework helps determine the utility of an action that has a probability P𝑖 of occurring after weighing the expected gain 𝜔 𝑔𝑎𝑖𝑛 and cost 𝜔 𝑐𝑜𝑠𝑡 . It gives a simple parameter that suggests executing an action if there is any utility E[𝑈 ] > 0. We are going to utilize this decision making framework by applying it to prefetching tasks on a website for the evaluation of Theorem 1.

## 4 Evaluation 

We did not evaluate Theorem 2 as it is analogous to Schmidt’s well established law [41]. To evaluate Theorem 1 and Theorem 3, we recruited 30 participants (22 male, 8 female). Each participant took part in all three experiments. The participants had a mean age of 23.5 years (SD = 2.8, range: 19–32). All participants were daily users of mobile touchscreens and laptops. None of the participants reported having visual or motor impairments.

## 4.1 Evaluation for Theorem 1 

For evaluation of Theorem 1, the parameters of the links such as their color contrast and the clarity of the label, etc. were ignored. This resulted in Theorem 1 being reduced to Equation (21). This is because for the application of prefetching, we prioritized intent over UI aesthetics and let the Bayesian decision framework from Equation (57) penalize links by 𝜔 𝑐𝑜𝑠𝑡 . The weights of Equation (57) should ideally be determined using performance metrics from ser-vices such as Google Analytics. Pages that are viewed more often should get prefetched more often and therefore should have higher 

𝜔 𝑔𝑎𝑖𝑛 and lower 𝜔 𝑐𝑜𝑠𝑡 . This decision making process helps avoid prefetching pages that statistically have a lower chance of being visited according to usage metrics, thereby saving on compute cost. Usage metrics were not available as the website was a testbed and did not have real-world users, so all the links on the website were assigned 𝜔 𝑔𝑎𝑖𝑛 = 0.5 and 𝜔 𝑐𝑜𝑠𝑡 = 0.1. In order to simulate a real world environment with network delays and to enable participants to run the model on their own devices, we programmed Theorem 1 into an e-commerce clothing website and deployed it on Google Cloud Run using Docker. Each participant was instructed to use the website naturally and browse the products as if they intended to buy them. The website interface was designed to look professional and was built on NextJS for the single page app experience. Images of the website can be found in the appendix Figure 8. Additional optimization techniques were implemented to fully simulate a real world production e-commerce website experience, such as (1) CDNs for image delivery (2) Lazy loading parts of the page as the user scrolled down (3) Backend API caching using redis (4) Page swap loaders. Although in most cases this became redundant as the high prefetch hit rate eliminated loading delays Auto-scaling and load balancing were implemented to ensure that there was no server lag as a result of multiple participants doing the experiments at the same time. The website contained links in close proximity to each other, to test Theorem 1 in a cluttered interface. The website had 110 uploaded products and browsable categories for Men, Women, Girls, Boys, and Babies. To test the prediction accuracy of Theorem 1 against other heuristics, we chose two base-lines, ForesightJS and prefetch all visible links. Foresight functions as a spatial and kinematic heuristic baseline which projects the cursor trajectory forward to estimate the landing position, while fetch all visible links provides a standard maximum cost baseline. (1) Experiment 1. Cursor Prefetching. Each participant used the website on their own laptops, which included both Windows and Mac operating systems. In order to begin the experiment, the participants pressed the space key on their keyboards and then used the website naturally. The website prefetched links whenever E[𝑈 ] > 0 from Equation (57). Both ForesightJS and a fetch all visible links baseline were also running in the background. When the participants had Thermodynamic Human-Computer Interaction 

decided that their browsing session was complete, they pressed the space key again to export a CSV file that con-tained the usage metrics. All participants used their laptop touchpads to interact with the website. (2) Experiment 2. Mobile Prefetching. Each participant used the website on their own mobile phones, which included both Android and iOS operating systems. As soon as the website opened, they tapped a button on the website to begin the experiment and had to press the same button again to export the usage metrics as a CSV file. The website prefetched links whenever E[𝑈 ] > 0 from Equation (59). Since ForesightJS defaults to fetching visible links on a mobile screen, this experiment only had the fetch all visible links as a baseline comparison. The screen resolutions of the participants’ mobile devices and lap-tops varied, but Theorem 1 adapted without any special consider-ation or modifications. The exported usage metrics included: the normalized probabilities of the top five links at the moment of the click, and the unnormalized probability over the preceding 1.5 sec-onds (segmented into 30 ms time bins). The time of prefetch and additional information, such as if the clicked link was prefetched and what prefetched links were not clicked, were also reported for Theorem 1, ForesightJS and the fetch all visible baseline. 

## 4.2 Evaluation for Theorem 3 

Experiment 3 tests the distribution of endpoints as a result of parametrized target potential fields from Theorem 3. To perform the test in a controlled environment, we created a simple one-page website and participant testing was conducted sequentially on localhost using the evaluator’s laptop, a 14-inch MacBook M3 Pro. A mouse, Razer Viper Mini configured to 8000Hz was connected to the MacBook for the participants to use. Pictures of this testbed website are in the appendix Figure 9. To begin the experiment, participants had to click a start button on the website. The website had a rectangular area with a circular green point in the center. At any given moment, there would only be one target button in the rectangular area and the participant would have to click it. Upon clicking the target button, the user had to reset their mouse position by moving their cursor to the center and clicking the green circle. Upon clicking the green circle, a new target appeared at a random angle with a fixed center-to-center distance of 280px. We employed a 2 ×2 within-subjects experimental design. There were 4 categories of buttons (1) High Contrast Button with Clear Label (2) High Contrast Button with Ambiguous Label (3) Low Contrast Button with Clear Label (4) Low Contrast Button with Ambiguous Label The experiment was carried out in 4 phases. Each phase had one of these four categories of the buttons. The user had to click 20 buttons from each phase in order to progress to the next phase. With 4 phases, 20 buttons, and 30 participants, there was a total of 2400 recorded data points. The exported files contained the endpoint distribution 𝜎 𝑠𝑝𝑎𝑡𝑖𝑎𝑙 for each button category. For the clear label category, the text "Click" was present inside the buttons. For the ambiguous label, it was "#@?.5!(-". ## 6.1 Experiment Results Discussion 

At 1.5 seconds prior to the click, mobile interactions exhibited a higher probability than cursor-based interactions, as illustrated by the left subplot in Figure 4. This was due to users scrolling the screen and spending some time observing the newly appeared elements on the screen. Consequently, this caused the 1.5 seconds preceding the click event to have a higher probability. For interaction using a cursor, the probability at the 1.5 second mark was lower as a result of the participants’ cursors being sufficiently far from the center of the clicked link. The high probability at 𝑡 = 0 ms further justifies the harmonic potential having an equilibrium position on 𝑑 = 0, at the exact center of the links, towards which the users aimed for. The probability of cursor based interaction is not 1 at 𝑡 = 0 ms because of the distribution of endpoints around the center of the links. In the case of interaction on mobile, users were not aligning the center of the link with the hard-coded agent position from Equation (58), leading to a slightly lower probability at the 𝑡 = 0ms mark. Since users aimed for precise centers on cursor based interaction, the normalized probability was heavily in favor of the clicked link, which explains the 24.6 times domination over the second ranked link in the middle subplot of Figure 4. Since mobile based interaction used an unnormalized probability, the probability difference between the ranking of the links was much lower than on the desktop. The 98.1% accuracy of the proposed model as illustrated by the left subplot of Figure 5 was due to users sometimes not clicking near the center of extremely large section based links. Since the harmonic potential component has a low probability at the edges of huge targets, this resulted in the failed prefetch. Similarly, for mobile based interaction, the inaccuracy is a result of users sometimes clicking large links towards the edges of the screen, whose centers were far away from the hard-coded agent position, and in certain cases, outside the viewport. The parallel lines observed in the right subplot of Figure 7 demon-strate that the target parameters are independent of each other. This validates the assumption in Equation (40), where the coupled terms were set to zero. In general, label clarity had a greater impact on user inaccuracy than color contrast. This suggests that in the process of making contact, decoding and understanding textual information makes the user more inaccurate than differentiating colors. 

## 6.2 Non-equilibrium Systems 

An important finding of Figure 5 is that the model starts to break down when faced with large links. This was the case for the testbed e-commerce website (see Figure 8), resulting in 98.1% accuracy instead of 100%. This reveals a crucial detail about the boundary constraints of the proposed model, consistent with the known weak-ness of Fitts’ law for extremely large targets. Since the proposed model and Fitts’ law are both rooted in the equilibrium assumption and breakdown for large targets, these large targets must exist in a state of non-equilibrium, which requires the user to exit their state of equilibrium, in order to establish contact. Since equilib-rium demands a system to have an equal distribution of Energy, this condition breaks down for large targets where the harmonic potential Energy is significantly different at the edges than at the center. Consequently, the laws of HCI that can be recovered from an equilibrium assumption, including the proposed model, fail to accurately predict user intent for larger targets. Having analyzed the results and the limitations, it is almost obvious what boundary constraint must be defined to establish that a system is not in a state of equilibrium. Theorem 4. Instinctively, one might be tempted to engineer a special-case rule around this result, such as “IF ℒ > 0, 𝑉 > 0 AND 𝑇 = 0 THEN prefetch().” Such an approach, however, would reduce Theorem 4 to a narrow implementation heuristic. Many fundamental physical theories are formulated through Lagrangians. The Standard Model of particle physics, represented by 

ℒ𝑆𝑀 , is one prominent example. This raises a broader question: was thermodynamics, with particles, energy, and equilibrium, essential to the framework developed here, or was it one instance of a more general description of interaction? The results of this study do not establish that electrodynam-ics or other physical theories would produce identical interaction laws. However, they suggest that Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations may provide a broader foundation for modeling in-teraction beyond cursor movement and target acquisition. This includes human interaction with artificial intelligence and other systems. The deeper question is therefore whether computer sci-ence has an equivalent of ℒ𝑆𝑀 , a sufficiently general Lagrangian that describes the different domains of human interaction within a common mathematical framework. 

## 7 Limitations and Future Work 

Despite the limitations discussed below, the results provide consis-tent empirical support for the model across desktop, mobile, and controlled pointing experiments. Further studies with broader sam-ples and interfaces can establish how widely the thermodynamic framework generalises. 

## 7.1 Threats to Validity 

The study had 30 participants with a limited age range. The partici-pants also used different devices in Experiments 1 and 2. Learning or fatigue may have affected the results. The utility weights from Equations (57) and (59) were fixed and other values were not tested. Four participants were excluded from the final ANOVA shown in Figure 7 because they did not have valid trials in all four conditions of the 2 × 2 within subjects design. The evaluation used one website and one controlled pointing task. 

## 7.2 Model Limitations and Future Work 

There are no mathematical restrictions that prevent Theorem 1 from being applicable beyond the evaluated website and prefetching use case. It should be applicable to 3D use cases, such as AR/VR, or in 3D games to deduce where a character is moving. By extension, it is applicable in all interaction scenarios under the equilibrium constraint. However, this has yet to be fully evaluated. Although the laws of thermodynamics can explain why certain HCI laws and the proposed model fail for cases such as extremely large targets, they simultaneously expose a much more fundamental concern. All the approaches from the related work section attempt to create al-gorithms for the entire interaction life cycle without accounting for equilibrium and non-equilibrium energy landscapes. For example, a neural network that blindly fits parameters across both equilibrium and non-equilibrium states will be prone to inaccuracies. Future work must attempt to make heuristics and apply known HCI laws while acknowledging Theorem 4. For the state of non-equilibrium, there potentially exists a multitude of undiscovered principles. The laws describing Equilibrium and Non-equilibrium states coupled together would enable the establishment of a general interaction field theory which completely describes human interaction, pro-viding value to all the current mediums of interaction and to all of those which will exist in the future. 

## 8 Conclusion 

This study presented a unifying framework for equilibrium human-computer interaction grounded in statistical mechanics. By model-ing the agent-target system through a Hamiltonian energy formu-lation, we showed that digital targets behave as harmonic potential wells, that the resulting endpoint distributions are Gaussian, and Thermodynamic Human-Computer Interaction 

that Fitts’ and Schmidt’s laws emerge as direct consequences of a system’s potential difference being reduced in equilibrium. We further demonstrated that design parameters, such as color contrast and label clarity, act as independent additive modifiers on this po-tential field rather than requiring separate predictive mechanisms. The model was deployed on a production e-commerce testbed and validated across 1D mobile scrolling and 2D cursor interac-tion with 30 participants. It achieved a Fetch:Click ratio of 1.37 on desktop and 1.75 on mobile, outperforming ForesightJS and a viewport-based baseline while requiring no training data and evalu-ating in constant 𝑂 (1) time. A separate controlled study confirmed that label clarity and color contrast independently reduce endpoint standard deviation, consistent with the theoretical hypothesis that these parameters are additively separable. Critically, the model’s predictable failure on large targets is not merely a limitation but evidence for the underlying theory: equilibrium-based laws, including Fitts’ law itself, are bounded to the equilibrium phase of interaction. This reframes HCI heuristics as special cases of a broader thermodynamic picture, one that is only half complete. Coupling equilibrium and non-equilibrium de-scriptions is the natural next step toward a complete, general theory of interaction.
