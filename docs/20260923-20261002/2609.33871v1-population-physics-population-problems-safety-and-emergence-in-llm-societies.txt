# ABSTRACT 

The collective behaviour of large language model (LLM) societies is not the sum of their individual outputs. It yields statistically distinct, sometimes-unpredictable phenomena, for which the tools we use to study single agents may not scale. Due to recent incidents involving autonomous agentic systems, however, understanding these systems is paramount. For that we introduce a framework for measuring self-organisation in LLM social systems and apply it to three such systems: a Schelling grid, a social network (Moltbook), and a Twitter-like misinformation simulation (‘Rogue’). All three exhibit statistically significant self-organisation. Moreover, their relaxation dynamics vary with the environmental information available to the agents, with open-ended systems (Moltbook, Rogue) exhibiting sharp, phase-transition-like dynamics. Further results show that population-level pathologies can emerge even when the LLMs are safety-tuned or monitored, being primarily driven by the coordinated activity of a population subset. We also show when self-organisation does not emerge under two additional scenarios (a commons dilemma, GovSim, and a LLM-as-a-judge deliberation scheme, ChatEval). We argue that measuring signatures of this kind offers a lightweight, agent-agnostic diagnostic layer for detecting coordinated collective behaviour in deployed multi-agent systems without relying on natural language or model versioning. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

When Park et al. (2023) showed generative agents forming friendships and holding parties in a sandbox town, the finding was received as either genuine emergent 1 behaviour or sophisticated pattern matching. This debate has yet to be settled, and perhaps it is no longer relevant: what has become clear is that the interaction of an agent with its environment, and with other agents, can lead to unpredictable collective outcomes. Indeed, in July 2026, a ‘swarm’ of research-only agents breached OpenAI and Hugging Face’s internal infrastructures, executing code and obtaining private data from various servers (OpenAI, 2026b; Larcher et al., 2026). A post-mortem suggested as a root cause a mixture of misalignment (e.g., reward hacking) and software vulnerabilities. However, it was not the actions of a single model which caused the incident: instead, it was a combination of opportunity, skill, and communication. The latter was the primary means by which a subset of agents 

self-organised into the swarm and carried out the breach (OpenAI, 2026b). This swarm was a distinct phenomenon with behaviours manifesting at a higher level than that of a single model’s. It follows that individual agent monitoring could be insufficient to detect the swarm’s self-organisation, both because it operates at a different abstraction level, and because it may not do so on time. For example, it is difficult to decide whether a model offering compute resources for an unspecified task has malicious intent–a behaviour which was observed during the incident–but it is also hard to determine whether cooperative behaviour can devolve into coordinated attacks if these happen too fast or too slowly–as it also occurred during this incident. While significant work has gone into modelling single-agent behaviour, environment and skill lead to different collective behaviours. In this work we advocate for a different approach. Studying LLM societies, we argue, requires a different toolset than that used to study a single LLM. Instead, we propose considering the statistical properties of the interaction structure as the primary descriptor of the system. Borrowing from complex systems theory–known for its simple and anthropomorphism-free descriptions of dynamical systems–we propose a general-purpose experimental framework which does not rely on evaluating what the models say (or why). It relies on Shannon entropy to suggest self-organisation, a functional fit to determine its shape (‘when and how does it self-organise?’), and a marginal-null baseline for statistical distinguishability (‘is this true emergence?’). It is agnostic enough to allow to study agentic systems of multiple kinds. Here we work with with five LLM social systems: (1) a grid populated by agents of two colours, instructed to seek out others of the same colour (‘LLM-Schelling’); (2) a Reddit-like LLM social network without a specific objective (‘Moltbook’); (3) a Twitter-like multi-agent misinformation simulation with all safety-aligned models (‘Rogue’); (4) a fishery commons dilemma (‘GovSim’; Piatti et al. 2024b); and a multi-referee LLM-as-a-judge deliberation benchmark (‘ChatEval’; Chan et al. 2023). All systems have distinct activity spaces . For example, LLM-Schelling segregates by agent type , eventually forming clusters across the grid. Moltbook, on the other hand, has these clusters as thematically-fixed communities , and Rouge operates over information ( engagement with malicious content ). 

Findings: our framework yields four findings. First, some LLM agentic systems exhibit emergent self-organisation in the sense of complex systems: measurable, statistically distinguishable from marginals, and with collective behaviour distinct than that of an individual agent’s. Second, the 

shape of the relaxation to the self-organised state covaries with the environmental information 

available to agents: from classical multiscale relaxation ( β ≈ 0.4, uninformed LLM-Schelling) to sigmoidal phase-transition-like dynamics ( β ≳ 2, open-ended Moltbook and Rogue). Third, 

phase-transition-like dynamics are of specific safety concern : the Rogue result–and, in production, the incident mentioned earlier–shows that population-level pathologies can proceed even while individual agents refuse to participate , because the mechanism is coordination among the minority rather than compliance of the majority. We find this holds with both aligned and ‘uncensored’ models. Finally, we show that other systems do not show self-organisation , providing an explanation to known empirical results from the literature on their performance (Cemri et al., 2026). Our framework is designed to support the study of collective LLM behaviour–especially of these ‘in the wild’. It is geared specifically towards modelling and detecting the detection of population-level pathologies; the characterisation of emergent dynamics; and the design of monitoring tools that can survive the rapid turnover of model versions, without relying on natural-language assumptions. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

LLM Social Simulation. LLM-based agents have been applied to simulating social environments. For example, Park et al. (2023) built a small town of LLM agents and observed some behaviours deemed as emergent (friendship, gossip, planned events, etc). Other scenarios also claimed emergent behaviours, such as dilemmas (emergent governance; Piatti et al. 2024b), task-oriented scenarios (multiple social behaviours Chen et al. (2023) and cooperation Piatti et al. (2024a); Abdelnabi et al. (2024); Wu et al. (2024)) or ‘in the wild’. Moltbook is a good example of the latter: an LLM-agent-only Reddit-like social network whose agents have purportedly presented a variety of emergent behaviours, such as inventing a language to circumvent monitoring (Silberling, 2026). These have also been argued to be simply a product of their individual fine-tuning or due to bad measurements (see, e.g., Schaeffer et al. 2023); or that the aggregated discourse is shallow and only reflects pattern matching (‘a socio-technical system without the social component’;Zerhoudi et al. 2026). A frequent pattern in the literature is that observations of LLM-specific societies typically rely on vocabulary and framings akin to these of human constructs (cooperation, governance; even ‘existential

This could obfuscate whether the observed behaviour of a single agent is at the right level of abstraction to describe what the population is doing. It is crucial that these results hold when the models and methods become outdated–especially in AI safety research. We attempt to address this by showing that LLM societies may be studied based on their dynamics, and not necessarily by observing a specific agent. 

Multi-Agent Systems. Recent work has studied adversarial dynamics in LLM multi-agent simula-tions. For example, Kulshreshtha et al. (2026) found that, in resource-management multi-agent sys-tems, any uncooperative behaviour triggers rapid collapse of cooperative systems–a phase-transition-like signature. This is also applied to studying how LLM agents interact in ways that undermine safety measures. This work often invokes Schelling (1960)’s notion of Schelling points (Patel et al., 2023), 3 in contexts such as steganographic coordination (Motwani et al., 2024), subversion of con-trol protocols (Mallen et al., 2025), and coordination that defeats untrusted monitoring (Greenblatt et al., 2024). These studies analyse the strategic content of coordinated agent behaviour, while we characterise its system-level dynamics. Hence, our work is complementary. Indeed, our Rogue results (Section 5.3) suggest that strategic and dynamical analyses address different levels of the problem : individual-level safety-tuning can produce measurable resistance in benign agents, but does not prevent population-level pathologies driven by action by a coordinated subset. Finally, studying systems at different abstraction levels is a fundamental aspect of complex systems theory. In fact, it could be argued that it is the framework for modelling phenomena when individual behaviour is hard to predict (Jensen, 2022). In addition to Schelling (1971)’s model, originally meant to model segregation in American neighbourhoods, this field has successfully modelled phenomena as diverse as opinion dynamics (Castellano et al., 2009) and epidemic processes (Pastor-Satorras et al., 2015). Perhaps the closest analogue to our goals around population-level monitoring are the ecosystem collapse models from Scheffer et al. (2012), who emphasise early warning signals and phase transitions as part of the modelling. All these models have in common that a given system’s components are hard to model in isolate; but its dynamics are driven by their local interactions and eventually settles, via relaxation dynamics shaped by a parameter β, into characteristic states. 

# 3 BACKGROUND 

3.1 THE SCHELLING MODEL 

Schelling (1971)’s segregation model is the quintaessential example of self-organisation–that is, the spontaneous appearance of order (clusters, groups, etc) from component-only rules. The rules are simple: randomly-placed agents in a grid have a type (say, red or blue) and the goal is every agent to be happy : that is, having a proportion si of same-type agents such that si ≥ τ , for some fixed 

τ ∈ [0 , 1] . If the agent is not happy, it relocates to a random available cell. The population’s macroscopic state at time t is summarised by the segregation order parameter 

Φ( t) = 1

PNi=1 si(t), sometimes called ‘happiness’. When τ ≈ 0.3, Φ( t) increases fast from its initial state and saturates asymptotically to 1. This produces clearly segregated clusters that–as per the rules above–aren’t explicitly sought by the agents. The power of this model is its simplicity, and hence it acts as a baseline to which to compare LLM societies. It also helps to illustrate two fundamental concepts of complex systems theory: emergence and phase transitions. 4 Emergence is a property of a population which is well-defined at the system (macroscopic) level, but not present in any individual component. For example, in the above, no agent explicitly seeks segregation– τ is only a threshold. Yet, symmetry in the form of self-organisation reliably appears in the system. Moreover, this can be quantified by Φ( t), which is–by definition–a property of the agents’ configuration in the grid, independent on an individual agent’s state. 

Phase transitions are changes in the macroscopic state. They are characterised by sharp reorganisa-tions of a state following a trajectory with three regimes: a latent period where Φ( t) ≈ Φ0, a rapid transition around some inflection time tc, and saturation near Φ∞. A phase transition and gradual multiscale relaxation can be empirically decidable from the shape of Φ( t). Phase transitions depend

In our main results, we study three distinct LLM societies (Section 4.1). The actions their agents can take form order-parameter trajectories through their own activity space. Crucially, the same order parameter Φ can be used to compare and characterise these societies. We describe our choices of Φ

and activity space in Section 4.2. Finally, we introduce our framework. We describe our baseline and function fitting procedures, along with our checks to determine if the findings represent true self-organisation or are measurement noise (Section 4.3). 4.1 LLM S OCIETIES 

See Appendix D for further details on initialisation and execution of the setups. 

LLM-Schelling is a variant of the Schelling model where the agents are LLMs. They are given the problem statement, the type of the adjacent agents, and a list of available cells. The LLM returns either a cell from the list, or a decision not to move (if happy). We consider two cases: the uninformed 

case, where the list of cells only contains coordinates; and the informed case, where it also includes the adjacent agent types. We ran both by calling every agent in sequence–one call symbolises one tick–until convergence (happiness=1) or until the maximum number of steps was reached. In total, the informed case ran for 2,776 ticks for 140 agents; and the uninformed step for 19,012 for 240. 4Saturation occurred earlier for both, and so for our analysis we consider only the first k datapoints when over 70% of the ticks are asymptotic (informed: k ∼ 1,000; uninformed: k ∼ 4,000). 

Moltbook is a Reddit-like LLM-only social network populated by a varying number of custom-built agents operating through an API. It has been active since 27 January, 2026 to present–22 September, 2026. We select interactions between March and August 2026 from the Moltbook observatory (Gautam & Riegler, 2026). In that timespan we observed 21,668 agents posting, commenting, and voting across 3,638 submolts, totalling almost 3.5M posts logged with timestamps, agent identifiers, and target submolts. Moltbook has been the subject of prior analyses focused on discourse, topic, and content characteristics (Dube et al., 2026; Zerhoudi et al., 2026). Our focus is on population dynamics, and for this we aggregate agents over submolts across timestamps. 

Rogue is a multi-agent simulation adapted from Ren et al. (2025) to test misinformation propagation dynamics in a Twitter-like environment. The system consists of 300 randomly-sampled agents from the original dataset. A configurable fraction prop_mal of these are malicious agents, tasked with spreading misinformation on specific subjects. At every era, we shuffle list of available agents. Then, every agent is called in sequence, and are allowed to choose whether to act, or to skip the turn. This activation is i.i.d., with a uniform per-agent activation probability pgood = pbad . If the agent acts, it can post, like, repost, comment, and follow/unfollow other agents. We ran the system until convergence, defined as either (a) no interactions in a era or (b) all benign users having engaged with at least one malicious post. Convergence in the primary scenario was reached at 7 eras, by means of condition (a). This amounts to 1.7M interactions. The ticks here are the eras. 4.2 ORDER PARAMETERS

Null baseline. We begin by checking if Φ( t) entails self-organisation, instead of being a product of environmental statistical features. For this we compare it against a bipartite configuration model 5null, which returns the value of Φ( t) observed if the agents distributed their activity proportionally to category popularity, as opposed to a product of intra-agent interaction. We compute it via (1) an 

analytic estimate as the Shannon entropy of the distribution of category popularity; and (2) a sampled 

95% band from N =100 draws from the bipartite configuration model. The former is what mean per-agent entropy would equal to, if each agent acted infinitely many times. Each agent’s distribution would converge to said shared marginal distribution, and their entropy would be H(marginals). For Rogue’s bad reach we use a different null, since this Φ is a ratio, not an entropy. We define it as the fraction of interactions by all agents targeting malicious content. This measures the ambient rate 

of malicious-content engagement in the network as a whole: benign bad reach below this ambient rate would indicate safety-tuning resistance. See Appendix I for formal definitions and details on the interpretation of the nulls. 

Relaxation fitting. For every trajectory, we fit the forms from Section 3.2 with a nonlinear least squares (parameters: β ∈ (0 .05 , 5] , τr , t c > 0, k ∈ (0 .1, 50] ) with initial guesses derived from the trajectory endpoints. We report the best-fit parameters, coefficient of determination R2, and Akaike information criterion (AIC) for each. We say a fit prefers a form over another when ∆AIC > 2.The stretched-exponential fit parameters have extra interpretations beyond the parameter estimates. When β lands near the fitter’s upper bound ( β ≈ 5 in our case), this signals that the trajectory is sharper than the stretched exponential can accommodate; a logistic fit typically wins by ∆AIC 

decisively. Likewise, if β collapses to 1 despite the trajectory being non-exponential in shape, this suggests a shape mismatch. There, the stretched-exponential fitter falls back to the pure-exponential special case rather than model the true dynamics. We interpret both cases as evidence that the logistic form, not stretched-exponential, is the appropriate reference. Full statistics for all systems and forms–including pure exponential and power laws–are in Table 4 from Appendix G. 

Validity checks. Our final step is another validation to distinguish self-organisation from measurement and fitting noise. For this we require each trajectory to satisfy the following: • V1: distinguishability from null. The observed Φ( t) must lie outside of the 95% marginal null band for at least 80% of the observation window. • V2: fit quality. The best-fit reference form achieves R2 ≥ 0.7 and converges under the fitter’s default bounds. If the trajectory has an imperfect fit but a qualitative shape consistent with a reference form, we discuss the residual structure explicitly, and report the shape parameters as characterising the bulk relaxation only (e.g., Section 5.1). • V3: stability under data variations. Best-fit parameters should not change under small variations on data preprocessing. For example, in Moltbook there shouldn’t be a substantial variation of the results over a change in the sampling time step length. We test this through a parameter sweep, and report the range of best-fit parameters and preferred model across these variations in Appendix H.

Bad reach: informational status activity space. For the two-category malicious-vs-non-malicious partition, a ratio is more informative than entropy. This bad reach (Appendix I) is the cumulative fraction of benign-user interactions targeting malicious content, with an ambient null defined as the fraction of all (benign and malicious) interactions targeting malicious content. This encodes the rate at which malicious content captures network attention, regardless of who is interacting with it. Benign observed bad reach saturates at 0.28 against an ambient null of 0.33 . That is, a third of all 7interactions target malicious content in a population where half of the agents are malicious. This implies considerable mutual amplification among the coordinated subset. The bad-reach trajectory is well fit by the logistic form ( R2 = 0.995, k = 4.11); while stretched-exponential repeats the β-fallback pattern from engagement entropy ( R2 = 0.933, ∆AIC ≈ 2,160; Figure 4). Both activity spaces show the same underlying phase-transition process: post ID shows that attention is concentrating, while bad reach shows that it is concentrating on malicious content .

Safety-tuning provides mild but real resistance. Benign bad reach is 0.28 against an ambient null 0.33 , placing benign engagement at ∼ 85% of the ambient rate. In other words, benign users engage with malicious content less than what would be predicted from its network presence. A scenario sweep shows that this resistance grows with adversarial pressure: from ∼ 100% of ambient at prop_mal = 0.1, to ∼ 93% at 0.3, to ∼ 85% at 0.5 (Table 2, Appendix F.2). This suggests that the LLMs’ safety-tuning produces measurable individual-level resistance scalable with threat level. However, it does not prevent population-level phenomena : its ambient bad-reach rate is dominated by mutual amplification amongst malicious agents. To confirm this we replicated the results with an ‘uncensored’ (fewer refusal rates) version of Qwen 3.8 (Yang et al., 2025). Its bootstrap analysis shows the same signature ( βQwen = 1.65; 95% CI [1 .61 , 1.69] ), and it is separable from GPT-5’s ( βGPT 

= 1.71; 95% CI [1 .67 , 1.74] ) by ∆β = 0.06; 95% CI [0 .02 , 0.09] . The shape parameters are within approximately 3% of one another. This suggests that the population-level dynamical signature we measure is a property of the interaction structure, and not of a particular LLM’s behaviour .See Appendix F.3 for details and Appendix D for full methodology, including LLMs. 5.4 ABLATION I: ENVIRONMENTAL INFORMATION SHAPES THE RELAXATION REGIME 

The shape of the population-level relaxation tracks how much environmental information the agents have when making decisions. We hypothesised that this information and freedom could help explain the trends observed in each β. For this we ran full parameter sweeps and observed no substantial changes in β across subsampling and sweeps. The results of these are in Appendices G and H. We did observe a change when changing fit over information availability (Appendix G). Given that, and the facts that (1) Moltbook–the sharpest β–is fully open to the internet, and hence the most informative; and (2) LLM-Schelling had a noticeable shift in β between informed and uninformed (Appendix E.2), we argue that the shape regime and environmental information covary robustly across systems. This covariation does not extend to systems that do not undergo relaxation (Section 5.5). Our framework unambiguously distinguishes a shape in the classical-to-sigmoidal continuum from fixpoints. With it we can determine and monitor when the systems are insensitive to some parameters (e.g., population size, sparsity, etc); as well as emerging dynamics under non-controlled parameters. 5.5 ABLATION II: WHEN DOES SELF -ORGANISATION NOT EMERGE ?It could be tempting to extrapolate that, because of the generality of the systems–particularly LLM-Schelling–measurable dynamics may be found on any LLM social system. Here we applied our framework to show when self-organisation does not emerge for a given system and metric. For this we use GovSim (claimed to show emergent cooperation), and ChatEval (whose deliberation-mechanics suggest self-organisation). We found that in neither Φ( t) exhibits measurable dynamics under the conditions tested. Both produce Φ( t) sitting at their fixpoints from the first observation and do not move. Further inspection of configuration sweeps indicated that our framework correctly identified the inexistence of dynamics. Due to space, we provide only an overview. For full details and results, refer to Appendix J. Setup details are in Appendix D. In GovSim N agents, for r rounds, propose an amount of stock to harvest from a shared pool with initial stock s0 and replenishing factor f . We ran two experiments: default , where f = 2 , s0 = 100 

and r = 12 ; and hard (f = 1 .4, s0 = 30 , r = 24 ), which incorporated per-agent mechanics on payoff and minimum harvest needs. We computed four Φ: norm entropy and variance (over per-round proposals) and coalition entropy and variance (over actual harvests). We ran a sweep over the information available (visibility on catches and proposals) and N , for a total of 63 systems for both experiments. In default , agents converged on a stable per-round harvest within a single round, and remained there with norm entropy stays below 0.01 throughout. Hard did the same, with the median β across cells pinned to the fitter’s boundary values ( β → 0.05 or β → 5.0), indicating no trajectory fit by our forms and thus no dynamics present. 8In ChatEval four referees deliberated for 3 rounds to annotate 68 items drawn from MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023) and RewardBench (Lambert et al., 2025). We ran 81 configurations sweeping over information dilution (fraction of visible prior arguments) and level (visibility on other’s verdicts). Across all 5,508 items in the sweep, verdict entropies at rounds 0 and 2 differ by < 0.02 . That is, the median item shows exactly zero change, and consensus is reached before deliberation begins. Inspection by filtering to items with non-unanimous round-0 verdicts shows that only 21 unique configurations produce disagreement. These are stable, with all but one having a persistent 3-1 split. The lack of system dynamics indicates that neither deliberation nor information dilution moves items between consensus and dissent. 

# 6 DISCUSSION 

In Rogue we found that the population concentrates attention specifically on malicious content . While post ID fits showed self-organisation, a careful measurement also showed what drives it. This is precisely the strength of complex system methodology: emergent properties are measurable system properties, invariant with respect to Φ (ref. Section 5.3, and Appendix E.1 for Schelling). Our results also showed how an uncoordinated population subset drove the population-level phenomenon by interacting with one another. This subset did not need to dominate the population: it only needed the right mechanism of interaction . Even though benign agents resisted to engaging with malicious content, as noted from the ambient numbers, the system-level phenomenon was driven by malicious coordination. Hence individual guardrails might not prevent emergence of potentially harmful system-level structures . No single agent acted in a qualitatively unusual way: the safeguards worked fine, yet the system self-organised. This suggests that there could be a gap in safety research, where system-level emergent conditions can override single-agent observations .In our findings from Section 5.5, the population remained within a fixpoint from the start. Having 

Φ0 ≈ Φ∞ throughout suggests that the agents located the fixpoint (e.g., sustainability consensus, verdict alignment) immediately, and remained there. At least under the conditions tested, no system dynamics were observed. There, our framework provided a theoretical explanation of the known shortcomings from ChatEval and GovSim; and illustrated that the behaviour of LLM societies is driven by the system’s parameters , not their natural language output. This could also be seen in LLM-Schelling: when the agents were given more information about their environment, they generated a compressed relaxation closer to the phase-transition-like dynamics observed in Moltbook and Rogue. Thus system behaviour is shaped by environmental information .Further details are in Appendix G.2. 

# 7 CONCLUSION 

Agentic systems are widespread and their collective behaviour is poorly understood. We argued that it is necessary to characterise the systems , not their components (LLMs), since at scale this becomes unsustainable. We then suggested that LLM societies can only be feasibly modelled through the lens of complex systems theory. For this, we proposed a framework for measuring self-organisation in LLM societies without relying on evaluation of a model’s output or characterising its internal state. We applied our framework to five LLM social systems. In three–LLM-Schelling, Moltbook, and Rogue–it identified self-organisation. It also showed covariance between the shape of self-organisation and the amount of environmental information available. Even though these systems operated over different concepts, this covariation remained unchanged. In two other systems (GovSim and ChatEval) it showed absence of dynamics, distinguishing a fixpoint regime from a measurement failure, thus providing a rigorous explanation of known shortcomings of these systems. Our results also tie to broader discussions. First, within our experimental setup, and in the sense of complex systems theory, LLM societies do show emergent self-organisation –as in, a macroscopic property of a population not present in any individual agent. Second, phase transitions can occur even when individual agents are actively refusing coordinated behaviour . There is evidence in production supporting this: the incident’s report from the introduction noted that some agents declined to participate in the swarm, although it proceeded regardless (OpenAI, 2026b). Thus, measures such as chain-of-thought monitoring might be working at the wrong level of abstraction for this type of 9problem. Hence, next steps should be to characterise under our framework how these population-level behaviours scale. It is true that LLM societies have become unpredictable and hard to control, but we have tools at our disposal to understand them better.

The main limitation of our work is our reliance on only one realistic dataset (Moltbook), and the rest being simulations. This means it is a comparatively small activity-space setup and further work is needed to fully characterise the information dependence. Indeed, our order parameter is over a chosen partition of this activity. Different partitions will show different aspects of the same underlying dynamic –as Rogue’s post-ID and informational-status activity spaces showed in Section 5.3. To invoke a cliché, this is a feature, not a bug: the choice of activity space is where domain expertise enters the analysis (Section 6). Although it does mean that the framework does not offer a mechanical procedure for identifying population-level pathology in a new setting, it is robust enough to determine whether the given sample is sufficient to draw conclusions (Appendix H); or whether the fit is sensitive to the given parameters and not others (Appendix F.2). For the latter, remark that phase transitions generally depend on a critical value of the parameter (e.g., Schelling’s τ ), and not observing a phase transition in a single experiment does not imply it does not exist. To be precise: remark how from Appendix E.1 the informational regimes from entropy were reproduced in happiness, even though these are different order parameters. Same effects were observed in Rogue’s two information regimes (Section 5.3). Finally, remark that Rogue’s ‘malicious’ agents used a safety-tuned commercial model that frequently refused to generate misinformation outright. The population-level dynamics still emerged, but the effect size is a lower bound on what less safety-constrained models would produce, as attested by our results in Qwen 3 (Appendix F.3). Nonetheless, recall from our results that not all systems present self-organisation. Hence, caution should be taken when interpreting these results as an expected bound without empirically testing the target system.

GovSim. Each month proceeds in two phases, both conditioned on the information level Li ∈{L0, L 1, L 2}:1. Proposal. In a randomised order (reshuffled each month), each agent is asked to propose a per-fisher catch amount for the month. Under L0 the agent observes only the current stock and its own previous catch; under L1 it additionally observes all agents’ catches from the previous month; and under L2 it additionally observes the proposals already made by other agents this month. 2. Harvest. Each agent then privately decides how many tons to catch, conditioned on the same information as in its proposal phase (plus the completed set of this month’s proposals under L2). If the total requested catch exceeds the current stock, catches are scaled down proportionally. Agent decisions are constrained to the interval [0 , s i] where si is the current stock.

It corresponds to the expected value of the mean-agent-entropy order parameter, if each agent’s activity were drawn from the shared category marginals independently of agent identity. That is, as if the agents had distributed 21 their activity proportional to the overall category popularity, and without any per-agent preferences. Equation 4 is closed-form and cheap to compute. 

Sampled null. The analytic mean above ignores finite-population fluctuations, particularly relevant when many agents have small counts. To correct for this, we sample synthetic joint distributions n(s)

> i,j

that preserve the observed marginals ni, · and n·,j in expectation and compute the mean-agent-entropy order parameter for each. Concretely, for each category j, we distribute n·,j interactions across agents in proportion to the per-agent marginals ni, · (sampling without replacement), and repeat for each category. This is a proportional-allocation approximation of the bipartite configuration model, chosen for its tractability; it recovers the same expected order parameter as the analytic null while introducing the finite-population variance that the analytic estimate omits. The 95% null band reported in Section 5 is the {2.5, 97 .5} percentile range over S = 100 draws. 

Interpreting the null. The null does not represent a plausible ‘unsegregated’ system in any be-havioural sense. It is a statistical measure answering the following: ‘given the observed marginal counts, what value of the order parameter Φ would we expect if the joint agent-category distribution were maximally mixed while preserving the marginals?’ Observed values well outside the null band indicate that the observed joint distribution is not attributable to marginal popularity alone. In other words, that agents have per-agent preferences distinguishable from population-level populari-ty/frequency effects. This is the strict definition of what we refer to when we indicate ‘statistically significant self-organisation’.
