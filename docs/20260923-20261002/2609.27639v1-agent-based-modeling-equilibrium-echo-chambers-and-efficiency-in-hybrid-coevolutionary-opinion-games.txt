# Agent-based Modeling: Equilibrium, Echo Chambers, and Efficiency in Hybrid Coevolutionary Opinion Games 

Ming-Zhi Jiang * An-Tzi Teng † Jun-En Liu ‡ Po-An Chen § Yung-Ming Li ¶

Abstract 

The rise of echo chambers and polarization in online social networks poses fundamental questions about the efficiency and stability of collective opinion formation. Traditional numerical opinion models provide formal tools for analyzing social cost and equilibrium, but often simplify communication into predefined update rules. Meanwhile, LLM-driven agents capture richer language-based reasoning and memory, yet neither paradigm accounts for their joint social cost. We close this gap with the Hybrid Coevolutionary Opinion Game (H-COG) framework, integrat-ing cost-minimizing Friedkin–Johnsen agents and Phi-4 LLM-driven reasoning agents in a dynamically rewired social network. Agent beliefs are initialized from 5,199 real-world Reddit stance comments, scored on a continuous [−1, +1] scale via a fine-tuned RoBERTa regressor, and a mixing parameter α

controls the agent composition. Our first result is that the coevolving graph converges under LLM-driven updating as reliably as under an optimizing update rule with a known fixed point, which is what licenses reading a Price of Anarchy from it at all. On that basis, purely LLM-driven populations are roughly five times less efficient than purely analytical ones, and a decomposition of social cost locates the gap: language agents are displaced from their own prior positions rather than merely disagreeing with their neighbors. The same susceptibility makes them less polarized, so lower polarization here is a symptom of what makes them inefficient rather than evidence of better deliberation. The pattern holds across three initial topologies. 

Keywords: Opinion dynamics, Echo chambers, Price of Anarchy, Language models, Agent-based modeling 

# 1 Introduction 

Online platforms have turned the public square into a coevolving system: what a user reads shapes what they think, and what they think reshapes whom they follow. Echo chambers are the visible result. A population that mostly meets what already agrees with it polarizes further and carries false claims farther, which is why the phenomenon has become a societal concern. Explaining it means tracking opinions and ties together, not either alone. Two lines of work approach this, and each gives up something the other keeps. Analytical opinion mod-els supply explicit update rules and formal tools for equilibrium, social cost and the Price of Anarchy (PoA), among them DeGroot, Friedkin–Johnsen, bounded confidence and the coevolutionary opinion games [De-Groot, 1974, Friedkin and Johnsen, 1990, Bhawalkar et al., 2013]. We sweep nine mixing levels, α ∈ { 0, 0.125 , 0.25 , 0.375 , 0.5, 0.625 , 0.75 , 0.875 , 1}, across three initial topologies.

RQ1. How does the proportion of Type-C and Type-L agents affect social cost and the Price of Anarchy?

1.2 Contributions 

This study makes four contributions: 1. Hybrid coevolutionary framework. We introduce H-COG, which integrates cost-minimizing Type-C agents and LLM-driven Type-L agents in a common network whose opinions and connections coevolve. 2. Empirically grounded initialization. We construct a two-stage pipeline that extracts real-world stance distributions from Reddit using semantic clustering and a fine-tuned RoBERTa regressor, then initializes agents through stratified sampling. 3. 2.1 Opinion Dynamics and Coevolutionary Games 

Opinion dynamics begins with agents that hold a number and update it by averaging their neighbors. De-Groot formalized that process and showed that under mild conditions repeated averaging drives the whole network to consensus DeGroot [1974]. Friedkin and Johnsen refined it by separating the belief an agent holds privately from the one it expresses, which lets the system settle where disagreement survives instead of vanishing Friedkin and Johnsen [1990]. Bhawalkar et al. took the further step of letting the network move as well Bhawalkar et al. [2013]. In their coevolutionary opinion formation game each agent attaches to neighbors who think as it does and picks an expressed opinion that trades social pressure against private belief. That setup admits a formal Price of Anarchy (PoA) analysis: how far does a decentralized outcome fall short of what a central planner could reach? Chen et al. carried it to a wider class of directed graphs Chen et al. [2016]. The loss stays bounded so long as no single node is too influential. All of these models speak in numerical update rules. What they leave out is the reasoning, the memory and the meaning through which people actually form and state an opinion. 

2.2 LLM-based Agent Simulation for Echo Chambers 

Large language models opened a different route. Instead of following a fixed numerical rule, an agent can read text, reason about it and answer in kind, which makes its behavior far more expressive and far more sensitive to context. Park et al. showed that such agents produce believable social behavior in interactive settings, which laid the ground for using them at network scale Park et al. [2023]. The idea reached echo chambers quickly. Wang et al. built a language-based framework in which agents talk, revise what they believe by reasoning, and rewire on closeness of opinion Wang et al. [2025]. They found that these agents track observed polarization more closely than Friedkin–Johnsen or bounded-confidence models do. Gu et al. took a comparable route but grounded the simulation in real Twitter data, which let them check the framework against observed behavior and compare how well different LLMs capture echo-chamber dynamics Gu et al. [2025]. Together these studies show that language-based agents reach something analytical models cannot. Both, though, run a single kind of agent throughout, and both report descriptive indices such as polarization and modularity. Neither asks what the resulting equilibria cost. Whether reasoning in language leaves a population better or worse off is therefore still open.

# 4 Methodology 

4.1 Simulation Framework Overview 

Figure 1 summarizes the two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 converts raw Reddit comments into calibrated stance distributions for agent initialization. Comments are semantically clustered, scored with a fine-tuned RoBERTa regressor, and sampled by stance stratum to produce N = 50 agents whose intrinsic-opinion distribution follows the empirical population. Stage 2 runs H-COG: the sampled agents are assigned to Type-C and Type-L according to α, update their expressed opinions, and coevolve with the network un-til the stopping rule in Section 4.4.3 is met. At each step we record social cost and PoA, polarization, null-corrected modularity, and the structural convergence measures in Section 4.4.2.

We manipulate the initial topology using three graph families: Barab´ asi–Albert with preferential attachment 

m = 2 [Barab´ asi and Albert, 1999], Erd˝ os–R´ enyi [Erd˝ os and R´ enyi, 1959], and Watts–Strogatz [Watts and Strogatz, 1998]. Type-L agents use deterministic decoding (temperature = 0 ), and the vLLM server is initialized with a fixed seed.

4.4 Structural Convergence and Stopping Rule 

4.4.1 Convergence Criterion 

We define convergence operationally as entry into an attractor or a stable structural regime: the rate of graph change ceases to decrease, although individual edges need not stop changing. K-NN reconstructs each out-neighborhood from a continuous ranking, so a small perturbation near the cutoff can replace an edge. Adaptive networks may consequently settle into dynamic regimes rather than frozen configurations [Gross and Blasius, 2008]. Our criterion detects either an exactly recurring edge set or a plateau in the rate of structural change.

# 5 Equilibrium Analysis 

In stochastic coevolutionary opinion formation games, proving convergence to standard equilibrium con-cepts (such as Nash equilibria) is non-trivial. Because network topologies evolve through randomized rewiring, small shifts in expressed opinions may drastically alter global transition probabilities (Chen et al. [2026a,b]). By framing the hybrid population of analytical cost-minimizing agents ( NC ) and LLM generative agents (NL) as a multi-player general-sum Markov game, we apply Chen et al. 6.3 Statistical Analysis 

Variances are markedly unequal across α, with the standard deviation of PoA falling by two orders of magnitude between α = 0 and α = 1 , so comparisons between α levels use Welch’s t-test [Welch, 1947] with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control [Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995] over the family of adjacent-α

comparisons, and we report q-values throughout. Where the claim of interest is equivalence rather than difference, specifically for the comparison among initial topologies, we test for it directly with two one-sided tests [Schuirmann, 1987] against a margin fixed in advance at δ = 0 .2 PoA units. The margin is absolute, so its relative size varies across the range: 17.5% of PoA at α = 1 but 3.6% at α = 0 . We fixed it before seeing the data and report the consequence in Section 6.5.5. Comparisons between topics use paired tests, for the reason given in Section 6.1. All confidence intervals are reported at the 95% level and quantify variability across topologies and Type-C/Type-L assignments rather than across draws of the agent sample (Section 4.3.2).

6.5 Numerical Results 

6.5.1 Convergence of the interaction structure 

Whether the coevolving graph settles is an empirical question for both agent types, and it is not answered by the analytical properties of either. The Friedkin–Johnsen update has a known fixed point, but K-NN rewiring is a discontinuous map applied on top of it, so convergence of the opinion vector does not imply convergence of the interaction structure. For Type-L agents there is no guarantee at either level: positions are obtained by scoring generated text, and nothing in the update rule constrains the sequence of graphs to settle. That both modes of updating converge is therefore a result, and it is what licenses comparing converged states across α at all. Theorem 1 guarantees that one of two regimes is reached; what the grid establishes is which of them, and how often. 

Both pure populations converge, and to the same kind of attractor. Figure 3 summarizes settling be-havior across the grid. Neither pure population settles predominantly to an exact fixed point: both are clas-sified plateau in the large majority of runs, and the purely Type-L population reaches an exactly stationary edge set slightly more often than the purely Type-C one, whose opinion dynamics converge analytically. Under K-NN rewiring, then, convergence takes the form of a plateau in the rate of structural change rather than a frozen graph, and Type-L agents attain that state at least as reliably as the closed-form Type-C dy-namics. The two modes differ in how long they take and in the level at which they settle, examined next, but not in whether they settle or in what they settle into. 

Settling time is a non-monotone function of composition. Figure 3a shows tconv falling monotonically in α from 52.2 steps at α = 0 to 21.7 at α = 0 .875 , and then rising to 25.1 at α = 1 .0. A population that is almost entirely cost-minimizing settles faster than one that is entirely cost-minimizing. # 7 Discussions, Conclusions and Future Work 

7.1 Interpretation of Key Findings 

Language reasoning operates as a conformity mechanism. Social cost separates into disagreement with one’s neighbors and displacement from one’s own prior position, and the two behave differently as compo-sition changes. Displacement grows by more than an order of magnitude between the Type-C and Type-L extremes, and becomes the dominant share of the inefficiency in a purely Type-L population. The mecha-nism is where the anchor sits. In the Type-C cost function an agent’s prior position is a term in the objective, a restoring force that penalizes departure however uniform its neighborhood becomes. A Type-L agent re-ceives the same prior as a sentence in a prompt, and within the homogeneous neighborhoods that rewiring produces, reasoning over what its neighbors say moves it away from that prior rather than defending it. Under uniform peer pressure, language reasoning operates less like deliberation and more like repeated persuasion. 

Weaker polarization is a symptom, not an improvement. The structural result follows from the same property. A population that is readily talked out of its own positions does not hold camp boundaries, and camps that do not hold do not consolidate. Weaker polarization among Type-L agents is therefore not evidence of better deliberation; it is a by-product of what makes them inefficient. This is worth stating plainly, because polarization is the metric this literature reports, and read alone it points the wrong way: the composition that looks healthiest by that measure is the one furthest from what a planner could achieve, and the composition closest to the planner’s allocation is the most segregated one. Low polarization is not a free good. It is what a population looks like when its members do not hold their ground. 

Two features do not follow this pattern. Mixed populations settle into exactly stationary structures more often than either pure one. And adding a small minority of Type-C agents raises social cost on one of our two topics and not the other; since both topics share personas and stubbornness coefficients, this belongs to the shape of the stance distribution rather than to who the agents are. 7.2 Conclusions 

This work began from a tension between two literatures. Game-theoretic models of opinion formation buy their analytical results with a convex cost function and an update rule chosen to be tractable; agent simulations buy semantic realism by giving that up. The tension is usually described as one between rigor and realism, but the substantive question underneath is narrower: whether equilibrium analysis survives the substitution. A Price of Anarchy is a statement about equilibria, and it says nothing about a population that does not reach one. What we find is that it does survive. Theorem 1 guarantees that the hybrid dynamics reach either an approximate equilibrium or a regime of bounded long-run average cost, and the grid shows which: the coevolving graph enters an attractor under Type-L updating as reliably as under an update rule with a known fixed point, even though nothing in the construction guarantees it: the rewiring map is discontinuous, and an agent whose position is read off generated text has no closed form to converge to. Opinion dynamics driven by language are, in this respect, well-behaved. That is what allows the efficiency gap we report to be read as a difference between two equilibria rather than between two snapshots, and what allows an agent that reasons in sentences to be placed in the same analytical frame as one that minimizes a quadratic cost. 

7.3 Limitations and Future Work 

1. All Type-L agents here are instances of a single model. Whether the efficiency gap is a property of language-based opinion updating in general, or of this model’s particular disposition to be persuaded, is not settled by our design. The decomposition above is the instrument that would settle it, and applying it across models is the most direct extension of this work. 2. The population is a stratified sample of N = 50 agents whose intrinsic opinions, personas, and stubbornness coefficients are fixed within each topic. Our confidence intervals therefore quantify variability across topologies and agent-type assignments but not across draws of the sample, and the two topics, which share personas by construction, are not independent replicates. 21 3. Statistical equivalence among the three initial topologies is established only for the purely Type-C population. Elsewhere no significant difference is found, but run-to-run variability at low mixing ratios exceeds what the pre-registered margin can resolve; confirming equivalence across the range would require a relative margin or more seeds. 4. The community structure is subject to the resolution limit of modularity maximization at this network size, which our null model does not correct. The bias runs toward merging communities that should remain distinct, so the reported values are conservative with respect to the compartmentalization they demonstrate.
