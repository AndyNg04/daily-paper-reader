Abstract 

This paper develops a theory of exploratory adaptation under AI-assisted optimization. The central argument is that the long-run adaptive effects of AI systems depend critically on how predictive assistance interacts with exploratory responsiveness itself. We formalize this mechanism using a dynamical framework in which cognitive, institutional, and technological systems evolve over rugged epistemic landscapes characterized by multiple locally reinforced configurations. A central state variable in the model is adaptive responsiveness, which measures the capacity of a system to traverse unfamiliar conceptual and institutional trajectories under changing conditions. Under convergent predictive regimes, AI systems substitute for exploratory engagement, reducing adaptive responsiveness and generating metastable trapping, hysteresis, premature convergence, and exploration-collapse dynamics in which systems become locally effi-cient but globally rigid. The framework also identifies contrasting exploration-enhancing regimes in which AI systems amplify exploratory search, conceptual traversal, and adaptive mobility. The effective substitution parameter is therefore responsiveness-dependent: systems possessing weak exploratory routines are more vulnerable to exploratory substitution, whereas systems already possessing high adaptive responsiveness may use AI assistance to expand exploratory mobility across rugged landscapes. The long-run adaptive effects of AI consequently depend not only on AI capability itself, but also on institutional structure, developmental context, and the architecture of human-machine interaction. 

# 1 Introduction 

Recent advances in artificial intelligence have substantially increased the ability of computational systems to provide prediction, recommendation, and task-specific guidance across a wide range of economic and cognitive activities [1, 2, 3, 4]. In education, programming, scientific research, and professional decision-making, AI systems increasingly reduce the cost of acquiring information, navigating uncertainty, and generating acceptable solutions. These developments are widely viewed as a major source of productivity growth and economic transformation [5, 6, 7]. At the same time, a growing literature suggests that systems improving short-run performance may also weaken the exploratory processes through which adaptive flexibility develops. Research on cognitive offloading 1

shows that external systems reducing immediate cognitive demand can weaken independent memory formation, metacognitive engagement, and exploratory reasoning [8, 9, 10, 11]. Experimental and theoretical work on AI assistance similarly finds that systems improving local productivity may simultaneously reduce skill formation and adaptive learning [12, 13, 14]. In collaborative knowledge environments, generative AI adoption has also been associated with declining public knowledge sharing and reduced contribution activity [15, 16], while recursive dependence on AI-generated outputs may reduce informational diversity itself [17]. These findings point toward a broader economic problem. Many socially valuable forms of adaptation emerge through decentralized exploratory activity that individual agents do not fully internalize. Scientific discovery, institutional adaptation, apprenticeship, collective learning, and open-ended inquiry all depend on costly processes of experimentation, disagreement, revision, un-certainty navigation, and publicly observable problem-solving. Such exploratory activity generates positive externalities because it contributes not only to the production of knowledge, but also to the preservation of adaptive responsiveness itself. The central argument of this paper is that the long-run adaptive effects of AI systems depend critically on how predictive assistance interacts with exploratory responsiveness. Systems possess-ing weakly developed exploratory routines may experience AI assistance primarily as a substitute for exploratory engagement, generating declining responsiveness and increasing rigidity over time. By contrast, systems already possessing high adaptive responsiveness may use AI assistance to ex-pand exploratory variance, conceptual traversal, and adaptive mobility. The framework therefore distinguishes between convergent predictive systems that compress exploratory responsiveness and exploration-enhancing systems that amplify it. The central concern of the paper is consequently not AI assistance in general, but the conditions under which optimization compresses or preserves exploratory mobility across cognitive and institutional landscapes. This perspective relates closely to recent work by Acemoglu, Kong, and Ozdaglar [18], who study how agentic AI systems may reduce incentives for costly human learning while weakening the accumulation of collective knowledge. In their framework, human learning generates informational externalities because individual effort contributes to a socially shared stock of useful knowledge. As AI systems increasingly substitute for human effort, equilibrium incentives for learning decline, potentially producing what the authors describe as knowledge collapse. The present paper builds on this insight but extends the analysis from knowledge accumulation to adaptive mobility itself. Knowledge accumulation alone does not guarantee continued adaptation. Societies may possess substantial informational resources while simultaneously losing the capacity to experiment, revise, and traverse unfamiliar conceptual or institutional trajectories under changing conditions. By introducing adaptive responsiveness as an endogenous state variable evolving over rugged epistemic landscapes, the present framework studies how optimization reshapes the exploratory mobility required for long-run adaptation itself. To formalize this idea, we study collective cognition as a dynamical system evolving over a rugged epistemic landscape. Let xt ∈ L denote the state of a cognitive, institutional, or behavioral system, where L is a high-dimensional space of possible configurations. The geometry of the 2landscape is summarized by an effective potential function 

V : L → R.

The function V (x) should not be interpreted as a literal utility function or consciously opti-mized objective. Instead, it summarizes the effective stabilization pressures acting on the system. These stabilization pressures emerge through institutional reinforcement, coordination effects, tech-nological standardization, learning dynamics, social expectations, and repeated adaptive interac-tion. Lower values of V (x) correspond to states that are relatively predictable, internally coherent, socially reinforced, institutionally stabilized, and low in coordination tension. Higher values cor-respond to states characterized by uncertainty, instability, novelty, unresolved contradiction, or cognitive and institutional strain. Empirically, movement across the landscape can be understood as movement across problem formulations, organizational routines, technological standards, or scientific domains. The barriers separating local basins therefore represent the frictions constraining movement across alternative configurations. In technological environments, steep barriers may reflect the financial and orga-nizational costs of abandoning established standards. In scientific environments, they may reflect the cognitive and professional costs associated with traversing distant epistemic regions. More generally, such barriers may correspond to coordination costs, retraining burdens, cognitive strain, institutional switching costs, legitimacy pressures, or accumulated infrastructure dependence. Re-cent evidence on scientific “pivot penalties” provides one observable analogue of such barriers: researchers moving too far from established trajectories face measurable traversal costs, consistent with the interpretation of rugged epistemic landscapes as locally reinforced knowledge structures [19]. This interpretation connects naturally to institutional theories emphasizing how stable rules, routines, shared belief systems, and social norms reduce uncertainty and structure adaptive be-havior [20, 21, 22]. In this sense, locally stable regions of the landscape correspond not only to cognitive attractors, but also to institutionally reinforced configurations that economize on co-ordination and uncertainty. The resulting landscape is generally rugged and jagged rather than convex because adaptive systems often contain multiple locally reinforced configurations separated by barriers of varying steepness and uneven traversal difficulty [23]. Arthur [24] argues that self-reinforcing economic mechanisms can generate multiple locally stable outcomes through increasing returns, coordination effects, adaptive expectations, and learning dynamics. Early fluctuations may then push systems toward particular trajectories that gradually become locked in through positive feedback processes. Attractors in this framework therefore represent locally stabilized cognitive, institutional, or technological configurations toward which adaptive systems repeatedly converge. Scientific paradigms, organizational routines, technological standards, and dominant explanatory systems may all func-tion as attractors when reinforcement and coordination effects stabilize particular trajectories over time. Recent large-scale analyses of scientific and technological research document substantial “pivot penalties” when researchers shift too far from established trajectories, consistent with rugged epistemic landscapes and locally reinforced knowledge structures [19]. This interpretation also con-3nects to Bourdieu’s conception of social fields as structured relational spaces in which established positions, routines, and forms of capital generate persistent trajectories and reproduce locally sta-ble orders over time [25, 26]. More broadly, the framework connects naturally to work on complex adaptive systems emphasizing nonlinear feedbacks, path dependence, and emergent system-level organization [27]. Social and institutional systems may therefore become locally stable while re-maining globally fragile because adaptation across rugged landscapes requires continued exploratory mobility. Existing work on AI and economic performance largely focuses on improvements in prediction, information processing, productivity, and local task performance [5, 3]. The model naturally generates several phenomena increasingly relevant in AI-mediated environments, including underproduction of exploratory activity, metastable trapping, hysteresis, premature convergence, attractor rigidity, and exploration-collapse transitions. Greater landscape ruggedness amplifies these effects because systems confronting fragmented, uncertain, or rapidly changing environments require higher responsiveness to avoid becoming trapped near locally stable but globally suboptimal configurations. The framework also connects several previously distinct literatures. Research in educational psychology shows that productive struggle and desirable difficulty improve long-run learning and transfer [31, 32]. The present paper proposes a unified economic framework connecting these phenomena through the dynamics of exploratory responsiveness. More broadly, the paper contributes to a growing literature studying how AI systems reshape the production of knowledge, expertise, and human capital [36, 37, 38]. Existing approaches pri-marily focus on informational substitution and labor displacement. The present framework instead emphasizes a distinct channel: predictive systems may alter the equilibrium level of socially valuable exploration required for long-run adaptive capacity. The remainder of the paper develops the formal model, characterizes its dynamical structure, derives conditions for rigidity and exploration-collapse transitions, and studies how reductions in exploratory activity reshape the long-run adaptive ca-pacity of collective intelligence systems. 

# 2 A Model of Exploratory Adaptation 

This section develops the formal framework linking exploratory activity, adaptive responsiveness, and AI-assisted optimization. The central premise is that long-run adaptation depends not only on the accumulation of information, but also on the preservation of exploratory mobility across cognitive and institutional landscapes. Systems confronting uncertain or rapidly changing environ-ments must repeatedly traverse unfamiliar regions of epistemic and institutional space rather than remain confined near existing attractors. Exploratory activity therefore generates socially valuable adaptive capacity because experimentation, exploratory deliberation, uncertainty navigation, and publicly observable reasoning sustain the mobility required for long-run adaptation. We represent local adaptive motion using gradient-like dynamics:

(5) The gradient term captures stabilizing pressure toward nearby locally coherent configurations, while the stochastic term captures exploratory perturbations generated through experimentation, decentralized search, disagreement, uncertainty navigation, exploratory variation, and stochastic adaptation [39, 34, 40]. This specification does not assume globally rational optimization. Rather, it reflects the general tendency of adaptive systems to reinforce locally stable trajectories while exploration generates perturbations capable of escaping local attractors. The central state variable in the framework is adaptive responsiveness z(t) ∈ [0 , 1], which measures the effective mobility of the system across epistemic space. High responsiveness permits continued exploration and recon-figuration under changing conditions, while low responsiveness generates rigidity and confinement near existing attractors. The distinction is important because systems may possess substantial accumulated knowledge while simultaneously losing the capacity to adapt effectively to unfamiliar environments. A firm can become highly efficient within an existing production regime while losing the ability to respond to technological disruption, while scientific communities may accumulate 7extensive knowledge while becoming increasingly conservative in exploratory behavior. Exploration consequently plays a critical role because deterministic stabilization dynamics alone drive systems toward nearby local minima. Productive struggle, exploratory search, and publicly observable reasoning therefore sustain both exploratory variance and adaptive responsiveness itself. This interpretation connects naturally to research in educational psychology and cognitive science emphasizing the importance of desirable difficulty, productive failure, exploratory engagement, and uncertainty navigation for long-run adaptive flexibility [31, 32, 33]. Related evidence from cognitive offloading shows that systems reducing immediate cognitive demand can weaken independent mem-ory formation and exploratory reasoning [8, 9, 11]. # 3 Dynamics of Exploratory Responsiveness 

This section studies the dynamic implications of AI-assisted optimization for exploratory respon-siveness and long-run adaptive capacity. The results develop progressively. We begin by charac-terizing the baseline effect of AI assistance on responsiveness. We then show how sufficiently large reductions in exploratory activity can generate responsiveness collapse, metastable trapping, and hysteresis. The analysis concludes by showing how decentralized optimization may underprovide the exploratory activity required to sustain long-run adaptive responsiveness in rugged epistemic environments. Unless otherwise stated, the numerical illustrations use normalized responsiveness dynamics with parameter values chosen to illustrate the qualitative mechanisms of the framework rather than empirical calibration. Simulations use bounded responsiveness z(t) ∈ [0 , 1], baseline exploratory engagement s0 = 1, responsiveness regeneration parameter η = 2 .5, stabilization parameter ρ = 1 .0, AI substitution intensity α = 0 .65, and nonlinear responsiveness parameter γ = 0 .75. Rugged epistemic landscapes are generated using stylized effective potential functions of the form 

V (x) = Vglobal (x) + Vlocal (x),

where Vglobal (x) captures broad stabilizing structure and Vlocal (x) generates irregular local basins, localized perturbations, and uneven traversal barriers. The resulting topology produces multiple lo-cally reinforced configurations separated by barriers of varying steepness and curvature, allowing the simulations to illustrate metastable trapping, exploratory collapse, hysteresis, and path-dependent adaptive dynamics. Specific functional forms and numerical landscape specifications used in the simulations are reported in Appendix A. The qualitative results are robust to moderate parameter variation and alternative rugged landscape specifications. The central mechanism linking these results is straightforward. AI systems improve local per-formance by reducing costly exploratory effort. However, exploratory activity also regenerates the adaptive responsiveness required for long-run mobility across epistemic and institutional land-10 scapes. As exploratory engagement declines, systems may therefore become increasingly efficient locally while simultaneously becoming increasingly rigid globally.

Proposition 3. For sufficiently low levels of adaptive responsiveness, the probability of escape from local attractors declines sharply, increasing metastable trapping and path dependence. 

The proposition follows from the interaction between declining responsiveness and rugged land-scape geometry. Local minima that were previously traversable become effectively absorbing states once adaptive mobility deteriorates. Exploration no longer generates sufficiently large perturbations to overcome the coordination, institutional, or cognitive barriers separating competing regions of the landscape. ## 3.7 Empirical Interpretation and Measurement 

Although the framework is stylized, its central variables admit natural empirical interpretations. The model is not intended as a calibrated description of any single institutional or cognitive system. Rather, it provides a dynamical structure linking exploratory activity, adaptive responsiveness, in-stitutional structure, and AI-assisted optimization. The framework consequently generates observ-able implications that may be examined empirically across organizational, scientific, educational, and collaborative knowledge environments. The central quantities in the model correspond to different aspects of exploratory behavior and adaptive mobility. Exploratory activity st captures observable engagement in experimentation, uncertainty navigation, publicly visible reasoning, exploratory search, disagreement, and adaptive variation. Adaptive responsiveness z(t) captures a more latent capability: the retained ability of a system to traverse unfamiliar conceptual or institutional regions under changing conditions. Landscape ruggedness reflects the complexity, fragmentation, and instability of the surrounding adaptive environment, while the effective substitution parameter

In the framework, exploratory systems generate high variance because trajectories repeatedly traverse un-familiar regions of the landscape. Observable exploratory behavior may therefore appear as elevated experimentation rates, branching structures, reversibility, disagreement, or exploratory diversity. In software development environments, exploratory activity may be reflected in the variance and entropy of code modifications, branching behavior, exploratory commits, or the frequency of dis-25 carded developmental trajectories before and after AI integration. Highly exploratory workflows generate irregular and decentralized search patterns, while strongly optimized workflows converge more rapidly toward standardized local solutions. Entropy-based measures may provide especially useful proxies because exploratory systems tend to generate greater diversity and unpredictability in adaptive trajectories. In collaborative knowl-edge environments, exploratory engagement may therefore be approximated through the entropy of editing patterns, semantic diversity of contributions, branching variance in discussion networks, or the ratio of original drafting activity relative to post-hoc optimization and editing. The framework predicts that strongly substitutive AI systems ( α > 0) should gradually reduce such exploratory variance over time, while exploration-enhancing systems ( α < 0) may instead preserve or amplify exploratory diversity by increasing conceptual traversal and adversarial variation. Adaptive responsiveness z(t) represents a more latent capability because it concerns retained exploratory mobility rather than immediately observable output. Empirically, responsiveness may therefore be inferred indirectly through performance under domain shifts, unfamiliar environments, or unexpected perturbations. Systems with high responsiveness should retain greater capacity to adapt when landscapes change, while systems with low responsiveness should exhibit increasing rigidity, adaptation lag, narrowing exploratory search, and local trapping. One possible empirical proxy concerns cross-domain traversal costs. Recent work documenting substantial “pivot penal-ties” when researchers move across distant scientific domains suggests that exploratory mobility may become increasingly constrained as specialization deepens [19]. Similar dynamics may appear in organizational environments. Firms exhibiting strong local optimization may continue per-forming efficiently within established technological regimes while experiencing increasing difficulty adapting to rapidly changing environments. The historical difficulties experienced by Nokia during the smartphone transition illustrate how highly optimized organizational trajectories may remain locally functional while adaptive responsiveness to emerging technological landscapes deteriorates [44, 23]. The framework also generates measurable implications for responsiveness collapse and metastable trapping. Systems approaching low-responsiveness regimes should exhibit declining exploratory variance, increasing convergence toward standardized routines, reduced willingness to traverse un-familiar conceptual regions, and growing sensitivity to environmental shocks. Organizations may display increasing adaptation delays under novel market conditions. Scientific systems may ex-hibit narrowing conceptual diversity and declining exploratory mobility across fields. Educational systems heavily optimized around predictive assistance may exhibit reduced independent problem-solving persistence under unfamiliar tasks. The hysteresis dynamics similarly imply that collapse and recovery should follow asymmetric trajectories. Systems experiencing prolonged periods of exploratory decline may therefore fail to recover rapidly even after optimization pressure weakens. Collaborative knowledge environments may again provide useful empirical settings. If publicly observable exploratory routines, mentorship pipelines, and decentralized participation deteriorate after generative AI diffusion, contribution recovery may remain persistently weak even if reliance on AI systems later declines. The responsiveness dependence of α also generates a broader empirical research agenda centered 26 on institutional structure and AI architecture themselves. Systems optimized primarily around convergence, recommendation, and immediate answer generation correspond to environments with strongly positive values of α, where AI systems substitute for exploratory engagement. Such sys-tems should reduce exploratory variance and increase convergence toward existing local attractors. By contrast, exploration-enhancing systems may generate low or negative values of α by lowering the cost of traversing unfamiliar conceptual regions. Open-ended simulation systems, adversar-ial research assistants, Socratic tutoring architectures, and generative scientific tools designed to produce anomalous hypotheses or unexpected cross-domain connections may therefore preserve or amplify exploratory mobility rather than compress it. The framework consequently predicts that systems optimized for convergent prediction and systems optimized for exploratory variance should generate fundamentally different long-run adaptive dynamics even when both improve short-run performance. More broadly, the responsiveness dynamics evolve continuously over time, whereas many ob-servable exploratory behaviors occur discretely and episodically. Exploratory activity may there-fore be interpreted as a latent continuous process generated through the accumulation of dis-crete exploratory events such as anomalous code commits, exploratory edits, branching structures, public reasoning actions, unresolved discussion threads, or experimentally divergent trajectories [34, 39, 40]. One possible approach is to model exploratory activity as a rolling time integral or exponentially weighted moving average of exploratory event intensity. More generally, discrete exploratory actions may be interpreted as realizations of an underlying stochastic arrival process whose intensity reflects latent exploratory engagement [49]. Under this interpretation, observable exploratory events repre-sent partial manifestations of a deeper adaptive process rather than direct one-to-one measurements of responsiveness itself. The responsiveness parameters themselves also admit empirical interpretation. The regenera-tion parameter η may capture the effectiveness with which exploratory activity translates into fu-ture adaptive mobility, while the erosion parameter ρ captures the speed with which routinization, dependency, institutional lock-in, or excessive predictive optimization degrade exploratory respon-siveness once exploratory engagement declines. The nonlinear parameter γ governs the persistence of exploratory regeneration itself and may therefore reflect the extent to which future adaptation depends on accumulated exploratory capacity, institutional memory, or established exploratory rou-tines. More generally, the empirical challenge is not merely measuring information production, but measuring whether systems retain the exploratory responsiveness required to traverse unfamiliar conceptual and institutional landscapes under changing conditions. 

# 4 Discussion and Conclusion 

This paper develops a theory of exploratory adaptation under AI-assisted optimization. The cen-tral argument is not that AI systems inevitably reduce human adaptability, but that the long-run adaptive effects of AI depend critically on how predictive assistance interacts with exploratory 27 responsiveness itself. Exploratory activity generates socially valuable adaptive responsiveness be-cause experimentation, uncertainty navigation, disagreement, publicly observable reasoning, and self-guided problem solving regenerate the mobility required to traverse rugged cognitive and in-stitutional landscapes under changing conditions. Systems confronting uncertainty, fragmentation, and rapid structural change therefore require more than accumulated information. They require sufficient responsiveness to escape local attractors, revise existing routines, and traverse unfamiliar conceptual regions. The framework studies these dynamics through the interaction between exploratory activity, adaptive responsiveness, institutional structure, and AI architecture. In convergent predictive regimes characterized by strongly positive values of α, AI assistance substitutes for exploratory engagement by reducing the need for independent search, deliberation, and uncertainty navigation. Under such conditions, increasing optimization lowers exploratory activity, gradually reducing adap-tive responsiveness and increasing rigidity over time. The resulting dynamics generate metastable trapping, hysteresis, premature convergence, and exploration-collapse transitions in which systems remain locally efficient while becoming globally brittle. Rugged epistemic landscapes amplify these effects because fragmented and rapidly changing environments require sustained exploratory mo-bility to avoid becoming trapped near locally reinforced configurations. The framework, however, also identifies a contrasting regime that is largely absent from existing automation-oriented literatures. The effective substitution parameter is itself endogenous: 

α = α(z, I, A),

where z denotes the existing responsiveness stock, I denotes institutional structure, and A captures the architecture of AI assistance. The same AI system may therefore operate as a substitute in one environment and as a complement in another. Systems possessing weakly developed exploratory routines and institutions optimized around frictionless prediction tend to generate positive values of α, making AI assistance increasingly substitutive over time. By contrast, systems already pos-sessing high adaptive responsiveness, combined with institutions preserving exploratory drafting, adversarial review, decentralized experimentation, and public reasoning, may reduce α and under some conditions generate α < 0. This distinction is central because it implies that exploration collapse is not an inevitable consequence of AI assistance itself. Exploration-enhancing systems may instead preserve or ex-pand adaptive mobility by lowering the cost of conceptual traversal, exposing agents to anomalous hypotheses, facilitating adversarial reasoning, or enabling movement across previously inaccessible epistemic regions. Socratic tutoring systems, adversarial research assistants, open-ended simulation environments, and generative scientific tools capable of translating across disciplinary boundaries may therefore strengthen exploratory responsiveness rather than compress it. In highly rugged environments characterized by strong traversal barriers, such systems may increase the capacity of organizations, scientific communities, and educational systems to adapt under changing conditions. The framework consequently differs from existing literatures emphasizing informational sub-stitution, labor displacement, or knowledge collapse alone. Existing work on cognitive offloading demonstrates that external systems reducing immediate cognitive demand can weaken independent 28 memory formation and exploratory reasoning [8, 9, 11]. Research on AI assistance similarly shows that predictive systems improving local productivity may simultaneously reduce skill formation and adaptive learning [12, 13]. Collaborative knowledge systems further document reductions in public knowledge generation following generative AI diffusion [15, 16], while recursive dependence on AI-generated outputs may reduce informational diversity itself [17]. The present paper builds on these concerns but extends the analysis from knowledge production to exploratory mobility itself. The relevant question is not only whether societies continue producing information, but whether they preserve the responsiveness required to traverse unfamiliar conceptual and institutional landscapes when environments change. The framework also differs from standard optimization models in economics and machine learn-ing. Conventional approaches typically treat exploration as an exogenous parameter or temporary inefficiency. Here, exploratory responsiveness evolves endogenously and may either deteriorate or strengthen depending on the interaction between AI architecture, institutional structure, and ex-isting responsiveness. This creates the possibility of multiple adaptive regimes. Under strongly convergent predictive dynamics, systems may gradually approach low-responsiveness equilibria characterized by high local throughput but weak adaptive resilience. Under exploration-enhancing regimes, by contrast, AI assistance may preserve or expand exploratory mobility by strengthening the exploratory processes through which responsiveness itself is regenerated. These results have important implications for institutional design. If exploratory activity gen-erates socially valuable responsiveness externalities, then environments optimized purely around immediate efficiency may become dynamically fragile over time. Educational systems emphasiz-ing productive struggle, scientific systems preserving methodological pluralism, and organizations maintaining decentralized experimentation may therefore possess long-run adaptive advantages even when they appear locally less efficient in the short run. The relevant distinction is therefore not between AI and non-AI systems, but between institutions and architectures that compress exploratory mobility and those that preserve or expand it. Organizations may preserve adaptive responsiveness by maintaining structured forms of productive friction within highly optimized envi-ronments. Educational systems can require active reasoning before automated assistance. Scientific institutions can preserve decentralized experimentation, adversarial review, and publicly observable reasoning processes. Firms operating in rapidly changing environments can maintain exploratory conceptual development before predictive optimization. Preserving exploratory responsiveness therefore becomes an institutional design problem rather than merely a technological one. Systems optimized exclusively around short-run throughput may gradually erode the exploratory routines, disagreement structures, and adaptive perturbations re-quired for long-run mobility across rugged environments. The architecture of AI-assisted systems consequently matters fundamentally for long-run adaptation. Convergent predictive systems op-timized for immediate answer generation may compress exploratory variance and accelerate local trapping, while exploration-enhancing systems may expand adaptive mobility by lowering the cost of traversing unfamiliar conceptual regions. The long-run adaptive effects of AI therefore depend fundamentally on whether institutions and AI architectures preserve the exploratory responsiveness required for continued collective adaptation. 29 The paper has several limitations. The framework intentionally abstracts from strategic inter-action, heterogeneous agents, endogenous network formation, and political economy considerations. The responsiveness dynamics are stylized and designed to isolate the core mechanism linking op-timization and exploratory adaptation. The model also does not distinguish formally between different AI architectures, although the analysis suggests that institutional structure and workflow design critically shape whether AI systems become substitutive or exploration-enhancing. These limitations point toward several important directions for future research, including heterogeneous responsiveness dynamics across agents and institutions, endogenous landscape formation, network structure and collective learning, strategic coordination, and political economy feedbacks under AI-assisted optimization. This framework generates empirically testable predictions regarding exploratory variance, domain-shift adaptation, public reasoning activity, and responsiveness recovery following periods of intensive AI assistance. The central question is no longer only how AI affects productivity, labor demand, or information acquisition. Increasingly, the relevant question concerns how predictive systems reshape the long-run geometry of exploration itself. By introducing adaptive responsiveness as an endogenous and socially underprovided stock generated through exploratory activity, and by linking AI-assisted optimization to rugged epistemic landscapes, metastable trapping, hysteresis, and exploration-enhancing regimes, the framework provides a unified account of how systems may either lose or preserve the adaptive mobility required for long-run collective adaptation. 30 References

This appendix provides additional details on the responsiveness dynamics and numerical illustra-tions used throughout the paper. The purpose is not to calibrate the framework empirically, but to clarify how the equations and landscape structure encode the central mechanism of the model. Exploratory activity regenerates adaptive responsiveness, while reduced exploration and stabiliza-tion pressures gradually erode the mobility required for long-run traversal across rugged epistemic and institutional landscapes. 

## A.1 Socioeconomic Interpretations of Rugged Landscapes 

The baseline framework uses stylized rugged landscapes to represent the interaction between sta-bilization pressures and exploratory mobility. The purpose is not to model any single cognitive, organizational, or institutional environment directly, but to characterize the general adaptive ge-ometry governing movement across multiple locally reinforced configurations. Nevertheless, the effective potential function V (x) may be interpreted more concretely as a composite representation of socioeconomic and cognitive frictions constraining movement across alternative trajectories. One possible decomposition is 

V (x) = λC C(x) + λK K(x) + λI I(x), (13) where C(x) captures coordination costs associated with deviating from established standards, rou-tines, or conventions, K(x) captures cognitive strain or learning effort associated with unfamiliar conceptual structures, and I(x) captures institutional switching costs generated by accumulated infrastructure, organizational routines, legitimacy constraints, or path dependence. The weights 

λC , λ K , λ I ≥ 0 determine the relative importance of each friction in shaping the effective landscape. Under this interpretation, local minima correspond to configurations minimizing combined co-ordination, cognitive, and institutional strain. Steep barriers separating basins represent retraining burdens, organizational switching costs, legitimacy pressures, accumulated infrastructure depen-dence, or cognitive effort associated with traversing unfamiliar conceptual regions. Ruggedness therefore emerges when multiple stabilization mechanisms reinforce competing local configurations simultaneously. In technological environments, ruggedness may arise through network externalities, installed infrastructure, retraining costs, and standardization pressures. The historical competition between VHS and Sony Betamax illustrates how early coordination advantages can generate self-reinforcing barriers that progressively constrain alternative trajectories [24]. In scientific environments, rugged-ness may reflect cognitive specialization, disciplinary boundaries, and professional incentives that increase the difficulty of traversing distant epistemic regions. Recent evidence documenting sub-stantial “pivot penalties” in scientific research is consistent with this interpretation [19]. In orga-nizational environments, ruggedness may arise from legacy workflows, accumulated routines, man-agerial incentives, compliance structures, and the costs of retraining personnel around alternative 35 practices. This interpretation is not intended to replace the general potential function used in the main text. Rather, it clarifies how the abstract geometry of V (x) can be grounded in empirically mean-ingful frictions. Different empirical settings may place different weights on coordination costs, cognitive strain, and institutional switching barriers, while the general adaptive mechanism re-mains unchanged: stabilization pressures pull systems toward nearby low-strain configurations, while adaptive responsiveness determines whether systems retain sufficient mobility to traverse the barriers separating alternative regions of the landscape.

The numerical illustrations use stylized rugged landscapes designed to generate multiple locally stable basins and uneven traversal barriers consistent with the general framework developed in the main text. The simulations are intended to illustrate exploratory traversal, metastable trap-ping, responsiveness collapse, and hysteresis rather than to calibrate any particular institutional or cognitive environment. The effective potential function used in the numerical simulations is given by

Alternative specifications producing comparable rugged-ness generate qualitatively similar trapping, collapse, and hysteresis dynamics. 

## A.3 Bounded Responsiveness Dynamics 

The main text normalizes adaptive responsiveness so that 

z(t) ∈ [0 , 1] .

The lower bound z = 0 represents complete rigidity. The upper bound z = 1 represents maximal exploratory responsiveness. This normalization is useful because responsiveness is interpreted as a stock of adaptive mobility rather than an unbounded quantity. The responsiveness law is ˙z(t) = ηz (t)γ st(1 − z(t)) − ρz (t)(1 − st).

The first term captures responsiveness regeneration through exploratory activity. The factor 

st means that responsiveness increases only when the system engages in exploratory activity. The factor (1 − z(t)) imposes saturation. When responsiveness is already high, the marginal increase in responsiveness becomes small. This prevents the model from generating unbounded responsiveness. 36 The second term captures responsiveness erosion. When exploratory activity is low, stabiliza-tion, routinization, learned dependence, and institutional lock-in reduce responsiveness. The term 

z(t) implies that erosion is proportional to the existing stock of responsiveness. A system with no remaining responsiveness cannot lose further responsiveness. The parameter γ ∈ (0 , 1] governs nonlinear regeneration. It determines how existing respon-siveness affects the capacity to regenerate future responsiveness. When γ = 1, responsiveness regeneration is proportional to the current responsiveness stock. When γ < 1, regeneration re-mains active even at relatively low responsiveness levels. This produces smooth but persistent recovery dynamics and allows collapse and recovery paths to differ.

## A.10 Interpretation of Numerical Illustrations 

The numerical figures are illustrative rather than calibrated. They are designed to show the qualita-tive implications of the model. The common parameter values are chosen to make the mechanisms visually transparent. The key qualitative patterns are robust to moderate parameter variation. The figures illustrate five linked mechanisms. First, AI assistance reduces responsiveness by substituting for exploratory activity. Second, sufficiently large reductions in responsiveness push the system toward a low-mobility collapse region. Third, low responsiveness increases trapping on rugged landscapes. Fourth, nonlinear responsiveness dynamics generate hysteresis because recovery differs from collapse. Fifth, decentralized adoption can underprovide socially valuable exploration because private agents do not fully internalize responsiveness externalities. Together, these mechanisms formalize the main claim of the paper. AI-assisted optimization may improve local performance while reducing the exploratory responsiveness required for long-run collective adaptation. 41
