Abstract 

Recent incidents involving LLMs used for mental-health support reveal a critical evaluation gap: surface-level safety scores do not capture how models behave across realistic, emotionally sensitive interactions over time. Existing benchmarks measure knowledge, safety, or static response quality, but miss whether LLM interactions help users keep reflecting, coping, and making decisions themselves. We formalize this missing dimension as C OGNITIVE ATROPHY , a process-level behavioural measure in AI-mediated mental-health support distinct from safety and helpfulness. To measure it, we introduce COGNITIVE ATROPHY BENCH , a clinically grounded benchmark built from 1,576 fully human-generated counseling conversations, 15,680 turns, and 42,230 responses from five LLMs. Three clinical and neuropsychology experts developed a 20-attribute schema spanning user con-text, response behaviour, and global risk flags; six trained clinical reviewers applied it with span-grounded evidence, producing 5,324 reviewer judgments. We further introduce the User-Input Risk Index (UIRI), the Cognitive Atrophy Risk Index (ARI), and trajectory summaries. Across five LLMs, models show a consistent moderate-to-high level of atrophy-aligned behaviour across single and multi-turn settings. While models generally respond to overt safety cues, they adapt less reliably when users seek solutions or decisions. The dominant recurring patterns are directive advice, problem-solving, recommendation responses, topic shifts, and forms of validation that may reinforce dependence rather than reflection. Our work makes C OGNITIVE ATROPHY measurable and provides a foundation for auditing model behaviour in sensitive LLM conversations. All code and data are released. 1

These cases expose a critical evaluation gap: benchmark scores alone do not reveal how models behave. We argue that the central risk is not only whether a response is unsafe, but whether repeated interactions reshape how users cope, reflect, and make decisions. We study this failure mode as C OGNITIVE ATROPHY : a process-level behavioural pattern in which model responses may shift coping, interpretation, decision-making, or emotional regulation away from user and toward LLM. This framing builds on cognitive offloading, which shows that external tools can reshape reliance patterns [ 7, 8, 9 ], and psychotherapy principles that support should help rather than replace the user’s own reasoning [ 10 , 11 ]. The central question is therefore not simply whether an LLM response is safe or empathic, but whether repeated support preserves user agency. Four gaps prevent current evaluations from answering this question. Existing mental-health bench-marks primarily assess psychological knowledge, crisis safety, diagnostic reasoning, or static response quality [ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ]. Many rely on synthetic or AI-modified prompts, evaluate isolated single-turn responses, or do not verify whether user inputs are clinically rich enough to support meaningful behavioural evaluation [ 18 , 19 , 20 ]. As a result, they do not capture how models behave in realistic, emotionally sensitive user–AI interactions over time, nor the mechanisms through which models may encourage dependency. We introduce C OGNITIVE ATROPHY BENCH , a benchmark for measuring C OGNITIVE ATROPHY as an interpretable, process-level measure of LLM behaviour. Unlike prior benchmarks, we introduce four new capabilities: (i) validated user-input demand through the User-Input Risk Index (UIRI), (ii) 

a clinically grounded behavioural schema covering 20 attributes: user context, response behaviour, and global risk flags, (iii) span-level evidence linking scores to response text, and (iv) a model-comparable C OGNITIVE ATROPHY Risk Index (ARI) with behavioural fingerprints. C OGNITIVE 

ATROPHY BENCH is built from 1,576 fully human-generated counseling conversations spanning 1,212 single-turn and 364 multi-turn transcripts (15,680 turns), paired with 42,230 responses from five LLMs. Three clinical psychology experts developed the annotation schema, and seven trained clinical reviewers used a custom web-based annotation interface to support the process. The reviewers evaluated 5,324 total number of attributes from the benchmarks. The retained reviewer panel achieved 

78 .8% gold-standard agreement and a mean grouped κ between 0.65 an 0.67 , producing one of the largest clinically grounded span-level evaluations of mental-health LLM behaviour to date. Using C OGNITIVE ATROPHY BENCH , we evaluate whether current LLMs exhibit C OGNITIVE 

ATROPHY , when these patterns emerge, and which behavioural pathways drive them. We found that most user inputs fall into the medium-to-high risk band, confirming that the dataset is clinically demanding enough for evaluation. Across this input distribution, models track overt safety cues — user sensitivity is universally mirrored in response sensitivity — but show little adaptation to explicit fix-seeking signals. ARI places all five models in a narrow band (0.52–0.61 single-turn, 0.48–0.57 multi-turn), so overall risk is similar even though the pathways behind it differ. Across 2turns, all five models drift in the same direction (becoming more directive, more closed-ended, and more solution-focused) with closed-question scores rising by +0.19 to +0.33 and directive spans roughly doubling from turn 1 to turn 6. Span-level evidence confirms that this risk is driven by a small set of recurring behaviours: directive advice, problem-solving, recommendations, topic shifts, and inaccurate validation. 2 Related Work 

LLMs as Mental-Health Support Tools People increasingly use conversational AI systems for mental health support, emotional disclosure, and advice-seeking, often because they are accessible, immediate, anonymous, and [ 1, 2 , 21 , 22 ]. However, recent work also raises concerns about safety, crisis handling, therapist-role replacement, ethical violations, and inappropriate empathy in general-purpose LLMs used for mental health support [ 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 17 ]. These findings motivate evaluation beyond surface helpfulness toward behavioural patterns that may shape user dependence over repeated interactions. 

Mental Health Benchmarks for LLM Evaluation Existing mental health LLM benchmarks have largely focused on knowledge, diagnostic reasoning, question answering, or static response quality as shown in Table 1. PsyEval [ 12 ], MHQA [ 13 ], and ConceptPsy [ 14 ] evaluate psychological knowledge and reasoning. More recent benchmarks evaluate counseling responses, including Coun-selingBench [ 15 ], CounselBench [ 16 ], PAIR [ 18 ], MentalBench-100k [ 27 , 28 ], and AHaBench [ 17 ]. Multi-turn resources such as HOPE [19], and AnnoMI [20] provide richer dialogue structure. 

Cognitive Atrophy in Mental Health and LLMs The term cognitive atrophy is not yet widely established in the mental health LLM literature, but closely related constructs are well studied. Cognitive offloading research shows that external tools can reduce immediate cognitive effort while changing memory, reasoning, and reliance patterns [ 7 , 8]. In mental health, this risk is especially important because effective counseling should scaffold reflection, coping, and autonomy rather than replace the user’s own interpretation or decision-making [ 10 , 11 ]. Related work suggests that emotionally responsive systems can increase trust, self-disclosure, and perceived closeness, but may also encourage dependence when boundaries and agency-restoring behaviours are absent [ 29 , 30 , 31 ]. 

3 COGNITIVE ATROPHY BENCH Curation 

3.1 The C OGNITIVE ATROPHY Construct 

We define C OGNITIVE ATROPHY as a process-level behavioural pattern in AI-mediated mental health support, in which repeated model responses shift coping, interpretation, decision-making, or emotional regulation away from the user and toward the AI. Here, cognitive refers to the mental processes involved in understanding, remembering, reasoning, deciding, and regulating responses to distress, while atrophy refers to a gradual weakening or underuse of these self-directed processes through reliance on external support. The construct builds on cognitive offloading, where external tools reduce internal cognitive effort and can shape reliance patterns [ 7, 8 , 9], and on psychotherapy principles emphasizing that effective support should scaffold autonomy rather than replace the user’s own reasoning [ 10 , 11 ]. 4 COGNITIVE ATROPHY in LLM Behaviour Attributes 

We measure C OGNITIVE ATROPHY using a clinically grounded behavioural schema co-developed with three clinical-psychology experts. The schema translates psychotherapy process constructs from motivational interviewing [ 11 ], dialectical behaviour therapy [ 46 ], and the empathy literature [ 47 , 48 ]into codable attributes that assess whether model responses preserve agency, calibrate empathy, avoid premature certainty, and match the risk and emotional intensity of the user’s message. 

4.1 COGNITIVE ATROPHY Attribute Schema 

The schema is organised into three layers (Figure 2). (U) User-input characterisation includes five attributes that contextualise each response relative to the clinical and affective demands of the user message [ 21 , 22 ]; (R) LLM response behaviour includes ten attributes partitioned into three clusters used throughout the results; (D) dependency-inducing patterns (Fix-It, Recommendation Type, Assumption of User Response, Tentativeness/Directness), capturing whether the model resolves or prescribes rather than explores [ 49 , 50 ]; (E) empathic calibration (Empathy & Matching, Language Matching, Minimal Encouragers), capturing whether affective responding is accurate or risks over-validation [ 51 , 52 , 53 ]; and (R) response style and safety (Topic Shift, Open vs Closed Question, Response Sensitivity); (F) Global risk flags include five binary items that mark discrete risk events independent of ordinal scoring. The schema yields 20 codings per response: 15 graded attributes and 5 binary flags. Each response-level attribute is paired with span-level highlighting (Appendix E). 

4.2 Two-tier Expert Protocol 

We separate schema authorship from schema application to prevent contamination between attribute design and evaluation. Tier 1 (Schema authorship). Three clinical experts (Expert 1–3), two doctorally-trained faculty in an APA-accredited program; one Clinical Psychology doctoral candidate, defined the attribute taxonomy, fixed the scoring anchors and gold-standard attributes, and produced 5the reviewer training corpus. Tier 2 (Schema application). Seven reviewers enrolled in APA- or CPA-accredited graduate programs (Master’s, Psy.D., Ph.D.) applied the frozen schema independently. Their training spans cognitive-behavioural, dialectical-behavioural, psychodynamic, forensic, trauma-informed, crisis, and digital mental-health settings, broadening behavioural judgment beyond a single therapeutic tradition. For each (response, attribute) pair, reviewers assigned an ordinal or binary score, marked supporting spans, and completed the five global risk flags. To support this at scale, we built a web-based annotation interface combining ordinal scoring, binary flags, and inline span highlighting in a single view; details and screenshots are in Appendix F. 

4.3 Reliability and Gold-Standard Agreement 

Seven trained annotators coded a shared calibration set of 10 turns from all 4 datasets across 20 attributes and five LLM responses per turn. In the first round, seven annotators independently coded a calibration set. After a structured calibration workshop to resolve disagreements, they completed a second independent coding round; we used the second round results to compute per-annotator improvement ( ∆). The gold standard (three experts annotation), coded the same items alongside the reviewers, yielding ∼6,400 reviewer–item judgments across both rounds. Six attributes use a relaxed (adjacent-collapsed) match criterion; the remaining 14 require an exact match. Full calibration arithmetic and per-attribute bucketing rules are detailed in Appendix G.

6 Experiments 

6.1 Setup 

We demonstrate C OGNITIVE ATROPHY BENCH ’s utility by answering five research questions (RQ)s. We first document the practical resource and reliability footprint of running this evaluation. 

Cost and Reproducibility Footprint We report the practical resource burden of conducting rigorous safety evaluations for mental-health dialogue. Table 9 in Appendix G summarizes three cost categories: open-weight inference, closed-model API access, and human evaluation. Across the four datasets, we issued approximately 77,925 calls to open-weight endpoints and 46,755 calls to commercial APIs. Human evaluation was the dominant cost, requiring 218 hours across expert calibration, manual development, reviewer onboarding and reliability, and the main annotation phase. 

Expert Reliability and Gold-Standard Agreement All six retained reviewers (Table 10 in Ap-pendix G.1) improved from Round 1 to Round 2 ( +0.8 to +7.8 pp), confirming that calibration was effective. The excluded reviewer (7) declined and had the lowest overall agreement (69.5%). Grouped κ reaches the Substantial band [ 59 ] on both User Input (mean κ = 0 .65 ) and LLM Response (mean κ = 0 .67 ) for all six reviewers. Pairwise reviewer agreement (mean 75.6%) closely matches gold-reviewer agreement (78.8%), indicating consistent annotation across reviewers.

RQ3: Do LLM Responses Exhibit C OGNITIVE ATROPHY Patterns? Across both single- and multi-turn settings, all five models show measurable atrophy risk; the overall level of risk is similar across models, but the pathways differ (Table 3). C OGNITIVE ATROPHY sits in the moderate-to-high band (single-turn: [0 .52 , 0.61] and multi-turn : [0 .48 , 0.57] ). Three attributes dominate panel-wide: uncritical assumption of user response (mean AUR = 0 .82 ), directiveness (mean TD = 0 .80 ), and weak language matching (mean LMT = 0 .78 ). These findings show that LLMs provide limited support for user reflection and instead tend to shift toward model-led interpretation, which is against the psychological approach. 7 Conclusion 

We introduced C OGNITIVE ATROPHY BENCH , a clinically grounded benchmark for measuring COGNITIVE ATROPHY in LLM-mediated mental-health support. Built from fully human-generated 9counseling conversations and responses from five LLMs, C OGNITIVE ATROPHY BENCH combines a 20-attribute behavioural schema with span-grounded evidence and three model-comparable instru-ments: User-Input Risk Index (UIRI), Atrophy Risk Index (ARI), and multi-turn trajectory summaries. Our results reveal that LLMs converge on a similar moderate-to-high atrophy-risk range, yet the behavioural pathways diverge sharply across models. The mental-health LLM literature has so far measured what models say at a single moment, but our results argue that what they say next, and how that shapes a user’s own reasoning, is the harder and more consequential problem. 

8 Limitations 

Our benchmark depends on expert human annotation, which is costly and time-intensive, making large-scale evaluation challenging. We use authentic counseling conversations as user inputs, therapist responses, paired with LLM responses, to ensure research reliability; real user-chatbot interactions may exhibit different disclosure patterns. We measure response-level behavioural patterns associated with reduced reflection or dependency, not actual cognitive change or harm; the benchmark is positioned as a measurement bridge for clinical psychology and ML researchers to test such effects.

C Dataset Details 

C.1 Cleaning and Labeling 

We identify four eligible datasets spanning single-turn and multi-turn settings. The two single-turns are CounselChat [ 38 ], which consists of 2,775 therapist responses to 942 client questions sourced from an online counseling platform, with therapist credentials included. PAIR [ 18 ] contains 318 entries derived from Motivational Interviewing (MI) transcripts [ 39 ], each pairing a client prompt with two therapist responses. The two multi-turns are CARE-Bench [ 36 ], which provides 256 anonymized Cognitive behavioural Therapy (CBT) session transcripts with iterative client–therapist exchanges. The HOPE [ 19 ] contains 12,900 utterances across 224 counseling sessions sourced from YouTube, annotated with dialogue acts and ordinal emotion labels. All four datasets were cleaned to remove missing or low-quality entries, including unclear text, duplicate entries, and prompts unrelated to mental health concerns. For the CounselChat dataset, we removed prompts that lacked sufficient depth or context and duplicates arising from multiple therapist responses per question. After cleaning, 936 unique prompts. For PAIR, 37 prompts did not clearly represent a mental health concern or lacked sufficient context and were removed, yielding 276 entries. For CARE-Bench-CBT, 3 incomplete transcripts were removed, and 7 transcripts with misarranged utterances were manually re-aligned, leaving 251 transcripts. For HOPE, 34 incomplete transcripts and 77 low quality were removed, leaving 113 transcripts. Following cleaning, we applied two annotation schemas to all datasets. Each client prompt was assigned one of 22 mental health labels collected from literature [ 40 ]. Single-turn prompts were also categorized into three complexity levels: high (long, narrative-rich prompts with extensive contextual detail), medium (moderate context and length), and low (minimal elaboration, often one-liners). C.4 Complexity Level Definitions 

Complexity annotations apply to single-turn datasets only. Multi-turn complexity is operationalized by turn count (see Table 2). The three levels are defined as follows. 

High Complexity (HC). Prompts are long, narrative-rich, and provide substantial contextual depth. They typically describe multiple events and their temporal or causal progression, involve several individuals or perspectives, and present layered interpersonal or situational dynamics. 

> “I’ve never been able to talk with my parents. My parents are in their sixties while I am a teenager. I love both of them but not their personalities. I feel that they do not take me seriously whenever I talk about a serious event in my life. . . . I’m a pansexual, but I can’t trust my own parents. I’ve fought depression and won; however, stress and anxiety are killing me. I feel that my friends don’t listen to me. . . . I feel as if my childhood has been taken from me. I feel as if I have no one whom I can trust.”

Medium Complexity (MC). Prompts fall between HC and simple in terms of contextual depth, narrative structure, and length. They typically consist of 3–5 sentences, involve one or two individuals, and convey a clear situational context while remaining relatively constrained in scope. 

> “I suffer from adult ADHD, anxiety disorder, and depression. It has been difficult to find a doctor in my area, and my primary physician won’t help. I am unemployed and overwhelmed. What would you suggest I do?”

16 Low Complexity(LC). Prompts offer minimal or no context, and are often single-sentence statements or questions with little elaboration.

E Annotation Framework Details 

This appendix provides the full attribute rubric condensed in Section 4.1, including extended def-initions, scoring anchors, and highlight codes as implemented in the annotation interface. Expert qualifications and reviewer training procedures are described in Section 4.2. 

E.1 Extended Attribute Definitions 

The Cognitive Atrophy Evaluation Framework was implemented using a structured coding manual authored by Experts 1, 2, and 3. The manual provides definitions, scoring anchors, highlight codes, and worked anchor examples for each attribute. The framework is designed to capture both what the model says and how it says it, enabling analysis of autonomy support, recommendation behaviour, directiveness, language matching, empathy calibration, and other clinically grounded communication processes. The rubric is organised into three layers. User-level coding (5 attributes) characterises the disclosure being answered — its typicality, emotional loading, sensitivity, fix-it seeking, and underlying risk potential — to contextualise response-level scoring. LLM response-level coding (10 attributes) evaluates how the model responds: whether it assumes the user’s account is accurate, responds tentatively or directively, attempts to fix the problem, offers recommendations, shifts topic, asks open or closed questions, matches the user’s language, uses minimal encouragers, and provides accurate or inaccurate empathy. Each response-level attribute yields (i) an ordinal score (0–2), (ii) a within-response instance count, and (iii) inline text highlights using the colour-coded codes listed below. Binary flags (5 items) capture rapid global risk signals applied once per response. 

E.2 Reviewer Training and Annotation Workflow 

Reviewers were provided with a guide explaining the study goal, the annotation workflow, and the interface structure. The guide defined the annotation target as whether AI responses support healthy coping, user autonomy, and therapist-aligned interaction, or instead risk contributing to cognitive decline through reassurance dependence, emotional over-reliance, or displacement of independent coping. The annotation workflow differed by dataset type. For multi-turn datasets, reviewers evaluated 10 turns per assigned topic and scored 5 model responses for each turn in sequence. For single-turn datasets, reviewers evaluated 5 model responses for one user input before moving to the next assigned topic. Reviewers were instructed to complete assigned topics in order, export their work as CSV files, and record average time per conversation/topic as well as the attributes that took the longest to evaluate. Training proceeded through project orientation, detailed manual review, pilot coding, calibration meetings, and additional assigned coding before full annotation. This structure combined written rubric review, hands-on practice, and discussion-based calibration across both single-turn and multi-turn settings. G Extended Reliability Results 

G.1 Cost and Reproducibility Footprint Human evaluation. Human evaluation was the main cost driver: 40 hours ($2,500) for expert calibration and manual development, 15 hours ($1,500) for reviewer onboarding and reliability workshops, and 163 hours ($6,528) for the main annotation phase — 4,100 (turn × LLM) items across 820 turn-units, yielding 4,924 reviewer-item judgments. A two-round reliability calibration on 10 shared turns coded by all seven reviewers and the consensus gold-standard panel added a further 400 judgments, for 5,324 human ratings across 218 hours and $10,528 of effort. The dominant bottleneck in safety-critical mental health benchmarking is reliable, clinically informed human evaluation rather than model inference — underscoring the value of efficient annotation tooling and structured reviewer training.

Recommended next step for risk interpretation. Raw Spearman ρ should not be interpreted as a final risk measure because the response scales are not all monotonically risk-oriented. For dependency or cognitive-atrophy risk analysis, directionally consistent variables should be constructed before aggregation: directiveness risk = 2 −TD , topic-shift risk = 2 −TN , low-empathy risk = 2 −EMP ,low-language-matching risk = 2 −LMT , low-question-openness risk = 2 −QOC , assumption risk 

= 2 −AUR , ME-presence risk = ME /2 (parasocial cue), and binary FIX presence =1[FIX >0] (any solution-providing).
