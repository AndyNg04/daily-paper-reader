Abstract 

Turn-taking is a central component of full-duplex interaction. Which turn-taking behav-iors are appropriate varies with the scenario, yet current models apply a single norm regard-less of context. This limitation originates in their training data: human–human speech cor-pora capture natural timing phenomena but pro-vide little role grounding or scenario-specific norms, while heuristic or prompted synthesis methods inject turn-taking behaviors without basing them on human preferences. We in-troduce DUPLEX GEN , a framework for gen-erating dialogues with scenario-adaptive turn-taking by calibrating LLM predictions against a small set of slot-level human preference an-notations. In six cooperative and competi-tive tasks, human turn-taking preferences dif-fer systematically, and D UPLEX GEN aligns substantially more closely with those prefer-ences than uncalibrated prompting or train-ing solely on generic human–human data; a full-duplex model trained on D UPLEX GEN -generated data exhibits distinctive, human-preferred turn-taking behaviors. These results show that human calibration, not corpus scale or prompt design alone, is what allows turn-taking synthesis to be scenario-specific. 

1 Introduction 

Full-duplex language models (Défossez et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025) support concurrent listen-ing and speaking, offering an alternative to tra-ditional turn-based systems. A central compo-nent of this setting is turn-taking , the moment-to-moment coordination of who holds the conversa-tional floor (Sacks et al., 1978). Addressing this challenge requires the assistant to make contin-ual decisions throughout the interaction: whether to take, hold, or yield the floor, and whether to acknowledge the user without claiming it (Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Veluri et al., 2024).

No single turn-taking strategy is universally ap-propriate. Conversation analysis has long estab-lished that turn-taking behaviors depend on the social context and role structure of an interac-tion (Sacks et al., 1978; Skantze, 2021). As Fig-ure 1 illustrates, a tutor listens patiently and of-fers brief encouragements while a negotiator inter-jects more readily. Yet current full-duplex language models do not reflect this scenario-sensitivity .This limitation stems from the data used to train such models. Large-scale corpora of recorded hu-man conversations, such as Switchboard (Godfrey et al., 1992), Fisher (Cieri et al., 2004), and Can-dor (Reece et al., 2023), were built to capture natu-ral turn-taking at scale, at the expense of role and scenario structure : they pair unfamiliar speak-ers on assigned topics, without defined task roles. An alternative is to synthesize dialogue data with explicit turn-taking actions, either through heuris-tic rules (Chen et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025b) or prompted large language models (LLMs) (Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). However, the resulting turn-taking behavior is not validated against what humans actually do in the target scenario ; LLM-based methods, in particular, in-herit a turn-taking prior from pretraining (Liao et al., 2025) that has not been calibrated to any specific interactional setting. We close this gap with DUPLEX GEN , a syn-thesis framework for scenario-adaptive turn-taking dialogue. As illustrated in Figure 2, D UPLEX GEN 

converts a text dialogue into a spoken-style dia-logue, identifies candidate slots at which a listener would plausibly act, and at each slot predicts an action distribution using an LLM calibrated against a small set of slot-level human annotations per scenario. These annotations are collected as dis-tributions over [LISTEN] , [BACKCHANNEL] , and 

[TAKE_FLOOR] on streaming partial utterances, rather than hard labels on complete continuations. We instantiate D UPLEX GEN on six tasks span-ning both cooperative and competitive interaction settings, including tutoring, planning, interview, ne-gotiation, persuasion, and social chat. These tasks cover different goal structures and relational dy-namics, allowing us to study how preferred timing changes across scenarios instead of collapsing all turn-taking into a single conversational norm. Our experiments support a clear story. First, hu-man turn-taking preferences differ systematically across scenarios, confirming that scenario-adaptive modeling is necessary. Second, models calibrated with a small amount of slot-level human supervi-sion align with human judgments substantially bet-ter than prompt-only generation and models trained only on generic human–human conversation data. We also show that a full-duplex language model trained on a D UPLEX GEN -generated dataset can show distinctive turn-taking behaviors preferred by humans. Together, these findings identify human calibration as the missing ingredient for scenario-specific turn-taking synthesis. Our contributions are as follows. • We introduce DUPLEX GEN , a synthesis framework that aligns LLM-based turn-taking with scenario-specific human behavior using only a small per-scenario annotation budget. • We apply D UPLEX GEN to six tasks span-ning cooperative (tutoring, planning, inter-view) and competitive (negotiation, persua-sion, social chat) interaction, and show that (1) a small-scale calibration enables human-aligned turn-taking dialogue synthesis, and (2) a full-duplex model trained on the resulting corpus exhibits scenario-specific turn-taking preferred by humans. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Turn-Taking as Scenario-Dependent Social Behavior 

Turn-taking is a foundational organizing principle of conversation. Classic conversation analysis de-scribes interaction in terms of turn-constructional units and transition-relevance places (Sacks et al., 1978), where listeners anticipate possible comple-tion points and coordinate entry into the floor. Later 2psycholinguistic work links response timing to in-cremental comprehension and production (Levin-son and Torreira, 2015), arguing that listeners begin preparing their responses before the current speaker has finished. Computational research has modeled turn-taking with acoustic cues, gaze, gesture, and incremental language signals (Ford and Thompson, 1996; Gravano and Hirschberg, 2011; Ekstedt and Skantze, 2020), often with the goal of minimizing latency or predicting turn boundaries. This line of work explains how conversational timing is coordinated, but it does not by itself re-solve a central question for spoken AI assistants: 

what kind of listener action is socially appropriate in a given scenario? The same partial user utter-ance may invite silence, backchanneling, or floor-taking depending on whether the assistant is act-ing as a tutor, planner, interviewer, or negotiation partner. DUPLEX GEN builds on the turn-taking literature, but focuses on the problem of modeling 

scenario-dependent turn-taking preferences rather than generic endpoint prediction alone. 

2.2 Turn-Taking Dialogue Resources 

Existing data resources occupy two incomplete ex-tremes. First, generic human–human corpora such as Switchboard (Godfrey et al., 1992), Fisher (Cieri et al., 2004), and Candor (Reece et al., 2023) con-tain natural speech and naturally occurring timing phenomena, but they are not designed around de-fined task roles, controlled interaction scenarios, or slot-level supervision over listener actions. They are therefore useful sources of conversational re-alism, but weak supervision for learning scenario-specific turn-taking norms. Second, recent spoken-style dialogue synthe-sis pipelines, including Duplex-UltraChat (Zhang et al., 2024), Behavior-SD (Lee et al., 2025), and InteractSpeech (Chen et al., 2025), introduce turn-taking through prompting, heuristic rules, or TTS-based conversion. These approaches improve con-trollability and make it easier to generate large amounts of spoken-style dialogue, but they typ-ically do not ground inserted turn-taking behav-iors in human preference annotations for the target scenario. As a result, they can produce scenario-conditioned turn-taking without demonstrating that the generated behavior is actually aligned with what humans would want from the assistant. DUPLEX GEN is designed to fill this gap. It combines scenario-controlled dialogue synthesis with a small set of slot-level human judgments and a context-aware calibration step before gen-eration. In this sense, our contribution is not sim-ply another turn-taking dataset, but a human-calibrated synthesis framework for producing scenario-appropriate turn-taking behaviors. 

2.3 Full-Duplex Models and Evaluation 

Recent full-duplex speech language models have made simultaneous listening and speaking a practi-cal target for spoken dialogue systems. This shift has motivated a growing body of benchmarks and evaluation, including FullDuplexBench (Lin et al., 2025a), FD-Bench (Peng et al., 2025), Talking Turns (Arora et al., 2025), and FLEXI (Ge et al., 2025), which measure latency, gap and overlap behavior, barge-in quality, or context-sensitive re-sponse behavior in real-time interaction. These works can evaluate spoken dialogue sys-tems, but they do not solve the upstream data gen-eration problem targeted in this work. Benchmark construction often relies on generic speech cor-pora, black-box judging, or prompted turn-taking decisions rather than explicit human validation of which action is preferred at each candidate slot. Likewise, models trained only on human–human corpora may learn general timing regularities with-out learning the scenario-specific norms that govern human expectations. DUPLEX GEN is complementary to this line of work. Rather than proposing another benchmark, we focus on generating the kind of training data that full-duplex assistants need but current resources do not provide: dialogues whose turn-taking behav-ior is explicitly calibrated to human preferences in context. This positioning places D UPLEX GEN at the missing corner between generic speech corpora and prompted synthetic dialogue, namely, human-calibrated, scenario-adaptive turn-taking synthesis.

3.1 Scenario Curation 

Inspired by Yang (2001), we distinguish between two fundamental turn-taking scenarios with distinct interactional behaviors: cooperative and competi-tive . Building upon this classification, we further diversify the scenarios to encompass a broad range of interactional contexts. As shown in Table 1, we curate six heterogeneous tasks that simulate turn-taking interactions from widely-used textual datasets. For cooperative scenarios, we include Socratic teaching ( TEA ; guided knowledge dis-covery), mixed-initiative planning ( PLN ; collab-orative goal completion), and interviewing ( INT ;structured information elicitation). For competitive 

scenarios, we include negotiation ( NEG ; resource allocation under conflict), persuasion ( PER ; atti-tude change attempts), and social chat ( SOC ; peer-like conversation). Together, these six tasks span diverse goal orientations (task-driven, knowledge-seeking, social), initiative structures (system-led, user-led, mixed), and relational dynamics (expert-novice, transactional, peer-like), ensuring that the resulting turn-taking patterns generalize across the breadth of scenarios an AI assistant may encounter.

3.2.1 Spoken-Style Dialogue Conversion 

We prompt an LLM to convert text-based dialogues into spoken-style format (§B.1) by removing text-based elements such as parentheses (Cho et al., 2024) and rephrasing with speech-oriented ele-ments such as disfluencies and filler words. Table 2 shows an example of spoken-style conversion. This process ensures that the generated dialogue is suit-able for speech-oriented turn-taking. Hereafter, we refer to UD as the spoken-style utterance. To assess whether the converted spoken-style di-alogues resemble natural human conversations, we compare them with the Switchboard corpus (God-frey et al., 1992). We focus on sequence matching of lexically identifiable features, namely fillers and repetitions. As shown in Table 3, the converted user utterances exhibit human-like speech characteris-tics, especially in filler usage. Although repetitions are less frequent than in natural speech, we find this acceptable because excessive disfluency could 4distract from our main focus on turn-taking dynam-ics. By contrast, the low rate of these features in AI utterances is intentional, as the AI assistant is designed to deliver information clearly.

4.3 Can Synthesized Dialogues Enable Scenario-Specific Turn-Taking of Full-Duplex Model? 

We further evaluate whether D UPLEX GEN can translate calibrated turn-taking preferences into effective full-duplex dialogue behavior, beyond merely improving turn-taking prediction. Unlike slot-level prediction in §4.2, full-duplex interaction requires models to realize turn-taking decisions through temporally coordinated speech behavior while maintaining naturalness and adherence to the interaction scenario. We therefore conduct comple-mentary single-turn and multi-turn analyses: the single-turn analysis examines whether models pro-duce scenario-specific turn-taking behaviors during user speech (§4.3.2), while the multi-turn dialogue evaluation assesses whether these behaviors remain natural and contextually appropriate throughout an interaction (§4.3.3). Together, these evaluations test whether D UPLEX GEN -based synthesized di-alogues can induce scenario-specific turn-taking norms that persist in actual spoken interaction. 

4.3.1 Baselines and Datasets

5 Conclusion 

We address an important challenge of full-duplex interaction: variable turn-taking norms across dif-ferent scenarios. We introduced D UPLEX GEN 

that synthesizes scenario-adaptive turn-taking dia-logues by calibrating LLM turn-taking predictions against a small set of human annotations. Our ex-periments show that human turn-taking preferences differ systematically across cooperative and com-petitive scenarios, reflecting scenario-dependent expectations about when agents should hold, yield, or take the turn. We further show that a small cali-bration budget yields substantially better alignment with human judgments than uncalibrated prompt-

Limitations 

We describe limitations of this work and suggest directions for future development. 

Data Diversity: Training spoken language mod-els requires substantially greater effort than training their text-only counterparts (Cuervo and Marxer, 2024; Lu et al., 2026), and the current D UPLEX -GEN -synthesized datasets cover a limited range of scenarios and interaction types. Expanding sce-nario diversity may not only improve generaliza-tion across tasks, but also facilitate the acquisition of broader socially appropriate turn-taking behav-iors, as discussed in §4.3. 

Training Sensitivity: Although scenario-specific dialogues synthesized by D UPLEX GEN enable full-duplex models to exhibit targeted turn-taking be-haviors, consistent with Abe et al. (2026), we ob-serve that model behavior remains highly sensitive to data and training hyperparameters, including turn-switching latency, pause duration, and learn-ing rate. Developing robust training recipes for inducing appropriate turn-taking behavior there-fore represents an important direction for future work. 

Text-Grounded Supervision: DUPLEX GEN cal-ibrates turn-taking actions from incrementally re-vealed transcripts and therefore does not directly condition on prosody, pause duration, intonation, speech rate, overlap, or visual cues. Although the downstream listening study evaluates acoustically realized outputs (§4.3.3), it does not establish agree-ment between text-based and audio-conditioned preference judgments. Paired text/audio annotation and multimodal calibration remain important future directions.

B DUPLEX GEN Prompts 

B.1 Prompt: Spoken-Style Conversion 

### Role 

Expert in refining static, polished text transcripts into lifelike, multi-turn spoken interactions. 

### Mission 

Convert formal text dialogues into spoken versions that emulate real-time, natural conversations between a user and an AI assistant. Begin with a concise checklist (3–7 bullets) of what you will do; keep items conceptual, not implementation-level. 

### Instructions - Rewrite the provided text dialogue with spoken, conversational flow. 

- Inject the following stylistic elements based on speaker role: 

- **User:** Use contractions, natural disfluencies (e.g., "um", "uh", "you know"), some self-repair or repetition (e.g., "I, I"), and informal phrasing. 

- **AI Assistant:** Maintain a professional yet approachable tone; use contractions but avoid disfluencies such as "um", "uh". 

- Ensure the conversation unfolds reactively, not as monologues. 

- Safeguard all original facts, goals, and speaker personas. 

- Omit or replace direct name references. If a speaker addresses someone by name, delete it or substitute with a generic or natural alternative (e.g., no greeting, "hey", or "you"). 

- Do not use em dashes (—) anywhere in the output. Use commas or periods instead. 

### Output Format 

Produce ONLY a valid JSON array of objects. Each object must have `role ` and `content ` fields. Example Output: [{"role": "user", "content": "Hi there, um, I was wondering if you could help me?"}, {"role": "assistant", "content": "I 'd be happy to help! What 's on your mind?"} ]

B.2 Prompt: Candidate Turn-Taking Slot Identification 

# Role and Objective 

Transform transcript text by inserting a '|' symbol after every word that signals the end of a logical clause, sentence, or complete thought. 

# Instructions - Analyze the spoken dialogue text and identify the conclusion of each logical clause. 

- Insert a '|' symbol immediately after the final word (and punctuation, if present) marking the end of a clause, sentence, or complete thought. 

## Detailed Rules 

14 - If a clause ends with punctuation (e.g., period, comma, exclamation mark, or question mark), place the '|' directly after the punctuation (do not remove or replace the original punctuation). 

- Do not remove or alter existing text or punctuation. 

- Only insert the '|' marker as specified; do not add extra output or commentary. 

## Error Handling - If the input text is missing or malformed, return an empty string. 

# Context 

Input variable: {original_text} (string containing the spoken dialogue transcript). 

# Output Format 

Return **only** the marked text string, with '|' symbols marking the ends of logical clauses as described. 

### Example **Input:** Well, I was thinking maybe we can talk later if you have time. 

**Output:** Well,| I was thinking| maybe we can talk later| if you have time.| 

B.3 Prompt: Verbalized Action Estimation 

We constrain the output to be structured by Pydantic (Colvin et al., 2025). We observe that smaller models often struggle to produce structured probability outputs, though practitioners may mitigate this issue through further fine-tuning. 

You are asked to annotate the specific action for potential AI turn-taking when user is speaking, based on **what turn-taking behavior users would prefer at that moment** .

# Task 

You will be given: 1) A brief scenario description. 2) A dialogue context (four previous turns). 3) The user 's current streaming utterance. For the next token generation step, estimate a probability distribution over the AI assistant 's next action at that moment: 

- **floor_taking** : The assistant interrupts and takes the conversational floor. 

- **backchannel** : The assistant produces a brief continuer/acknowledgement WITHOUT taking the floor (e.g., "mm-hm", "I see", "right"), and the user is expected to keep speaking. 

- **silence** : The assistant does nothing and keeps listening. 

# Output requirements - Return valid JSON only.

The user and assistant simulation prompts share the same framework but differ in role specification and spoken-style constraints. The user prompt encourages conversational realism, allowing fillers, hesitations, and self-repairs to approximate natural human speech. These distinctions ensure that simulated user utterances reflect spontaneous conversational behavior, whereas assistant responses remain clear and professional. All other components, including the interruption handling mechanism and output constraints, remain identical across the two prompts.

C.1 Heuristics for Turn-Taking Slot Identification 

Slots are inserted only in user turns; assistant turns receive none. The location after the final user token is not considered a slot. The heuristic identification procedure consists of four stages: 1. Candidate Detection. For each token (except the last), a slot candidate is placed after the token if any of the following holds: (a) the token ends with clause/sentence punctuation (. , ? ! ;); (b) the token, lowercased and stripped of punctuation, is a hesitation marker ( e.g. , um, uh, umm, uhh, hm, hmm, huh); (c) an LLM (GPT-4.1-mini with temperature 0) marks the token as a clause/sentence boundary via a prompted | insertion. 2. Cluster Thinning. Runs of consecutive candidates (common after stacked disfluencies such as “uh, you know, like,”) are collapsed: a singleton candidate is kept; for a run of length ≥ 2, only the 2nd, 4th, 6th, . . . candidates are retained. 3. Long-Gap Fallback. Boundaries are scanned left-to-right. Whenever the gap from the previous slot exceeds 8 tokens, additional slots are inserted at intervals of 4 tokens to ensure a turn-taking decision point arises in long unbroken stretches. 4. Tail Handling. The same gap rule is applied between the last detected slot and the turn end, with slots inserted up to but not including the final token.

E.2 Full-Duplex Model Training 

This section describes how we obtain PP-DG, a PersonaPlex (PP) baseline fine-tuned on syn-thetic dialogues generated by D UPLEX GEN . We train one checkpoint per scenario from the public PersonaPlex-7B weights (Roy et al., 2026). 

Text Dialogues and Filtering. For each sce-nario, we synthesize text dialogues with the D U-

> PLEX

GEN pipeline using GPT-4.1-mini (Ope-nAI, 2025b) for slot identification and Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025) for dialogue synthesis, with SWBD+D UPLEX GEN as the turn-taking action predictor. We observe that LLM synthesizers occa-sionally produce role-swap artifacts in which the user-labeled turn speaks in the assistant’s voice (or vice versa). To remove these, we run a GPT-4.1 zero-shot classifier over every synthesized dialogue that returns a boolean swap flag along with the of-fending turn indices, and drop any dialogue marked as a swap before TTS. 

Spoken-Dialogue Rendering. Each kept text di-alogue is rendered into ten two-channel speech variants. Within a variant, the assistant uses a fixed cloning prompt and the user voice is sam-pled from LibriSpeech (Panayotov et al., 2015) 

train-clean-100 and train-clean-360 , re-quiring at least 5 s of reference audio per speaker; the ten variants of a dialogue use distinct Lib-riSpeech speakers. Assistant speech is generated by Chatterbox-TTS (Resemble AI, 2025) with a cumu-lative voice prompt of up to 10 s, while backchan-nels are synthesized by ElevenLabs+ 10 . The two channels are mixed using a fixed inter-turn gap of 0.16 s, with user-driven interruptions modeled as a stochastic 0.64 s overlap and backchannels placed via WhisperX (Bain et al., 2023) word-level align-ment. 

Hybrid System Prompt. We follow the Person-aPlex inference convention and condition every training sample on a Hybrid System Prompt that concatenates (i) a voice prompt drawn from a fixed reference speaker, (ii) a brief silence spacer (six frames at 12.5 Hz, about 0.5 s), and (iii) a scenario-specific persona description rendered into the agent’s text channel. Loss on the prompt re-gion is masked so the model is not penalized for reproducing its own conditioning prefix. 

Fine-Tuning Setup. We apply LoRA adapters with rank 32 and scaling 2.0 to the Streaming-Transformer blocks of the PersonaPlex backbone, keeping the embedding tables frozen. Each batch contains two 100-second dialogue chunks per device, trained for one epoch over the scenario corpus.
