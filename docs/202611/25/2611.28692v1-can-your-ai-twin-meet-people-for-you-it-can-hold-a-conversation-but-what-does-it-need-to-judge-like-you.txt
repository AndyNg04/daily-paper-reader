Abstract
Should your AI twin go meet people on your behalf? We
built LLM twins of 710 real people from their other conver-
sations in the CANDOR corpus, sent them to meet each other
in 5,680 simulated first conversations, and checked every re-
hearsal against what the real people said after meeting for real
(355 held-out, participant-disjoint pairs, against baselines that
include each person’s rating history). The rehearsals work: a
twin’s conversation predicts real liking (ρ = 0.277), carries
information that questionnaires and rating history do not have
(partial ρ = 0.16 [0.05, 0.26]), and recovers most of what the
same reader gets from the real conversation (0.321). Swap con-
trols show that this signal travels through the conversation, not
through the persona card. What,then, does atwin needto judge
like you? Not your voice: making twins talk more like their
owners, with person-specific activation steering and a LoRA
trained on their own speech, did not help in a pre-specified
test and lowered accuracy in an exploratory second round
(−0.183 [−0.294, −0.066]). Nor simply your stated ratings:
a persona-prompted twin that holds the conversation does not
yet carry its owner’s judgment, and when told its owner’s
past ratings it anchors on them instead of weighing them.
Learning helps: a small appraisal head trained on other real
conversations, which combines the twin’s post-call answers
with its owner’s rating history, lifts rehearsal-based prediction
above the history baseline out of fold (+0.059 [0.001, 0.115],
against +0.022 without the twin’s answers; pre-specified pri-
mary contrast +0.056 [−0.001, 0.111]). We propose an attri-
bution protocol for twin-based social simulation and identify
learned appraisal as the component twins need next.

Introduction
Digital twins built from large language models are moving
from answering surveys to acting in social settings. Models
conditioned on people’s backgrounds reproduce survey sub-
groups and classic experiments (Argyle et al. 2023; Aher,
Arriaga, and Kalai 2023), and agents grounded in a per-
son’s interviews, answers or profile reproduce much of their
owner’s survey behavior (Park et al. 2024; Toubia et al. 2025;
Chen et al. 2025b). The next step is to let them act for us.
In social matching, the twins of two candidates meet first,
and the pairs whose twins hit it off are recommended to meet
in person: twin architectures for social pairing report agree-
ment with real speed-dating decisions (Ye et al. 2025), and
deployed platforms let twins converse on their users’ behalf
to estimate compatibility (Ye et al. 2026). What has not been
established is where in the pipeline that signal lives: in the
simulated conversation, or in the twin’s judgment of it. Twins
are usually validated on whether they answer questionnaires
like their owners or sound human, and neither check separates
these two steps. Separating them is the attribution problem
of LLM social simulation.
We run that test. In the CANDOR corpus (Reece et al.
2023), 1,456 people had 1,656 unscripted video calls of at
least 25 minutes with strangers and afterwards rated how
much they liked their partner. Because many people took
part in several calls, we build each person’s twin only from
their other calls, let the twins of a held-out pair meet, and
compare each twin’s liking with what the two real people
answered. Every held-out comparison uses 355 pairs that
share no participants and is made against non-LLM baselines
built from questionnaires and each person’s rating history.
Each experiment gives the twin one kind of information:
the encounter, its owner’s persona card, speaking style (pre-
specified) or past ratings.
Figure 1 shows the design; we make three points:
• Twin rehearsals carry real social signal. A base twin’s
simulated conversation predicts real liking beyond ques-
tionnaires and rating history and recovers most of what
the same reader extracts from the real call; swap controls
place this signal in the conversation (§4.1).
• Sounding like the owner is not the lever. Person-specific
steering and a LoRA trained on the owner’s speech did
not help in a pre-specified 2×2 test and lowered accuracy
in an exploratory second round (§4.2). We document six
failure modes of persona steering and twin readouts that
standard checks miss (§4.3).
• Judgment is the next step. At the person level, persona-
prompted twins do not yet reliably tell who liked whom
more, and they echo a stated rating history rather than
weighing it; a first learned appraisal head already helps at
the pair level (§4.4). We propose an attribution protocol
and name learned appraisal the next design target (§5).

Digital twins of individuals.
Simulations grounded in
two-hour interviews and surveys match individuals’ Gen-
eral Social Survey answers 72.8% of the time, against 79.5%
for the people themselves two weeks later (Park et al. 2024);
twin panels with 500 answers per person are public (Toubia
et al. 2025). Across 164 outcomes, twin and human answers
correlate at 0.20 on average, with insufficient individuation
(Peng et al. 2025); persona variables explain under 10% of
annotation variance (Hu and Collier 2024), LLM stand-ins
flatten identity groups (Wang, Morgenstern, and Dickerson
2025), models cannot play personas that perform worse than
themselves (Kumar et al. 2025), more than half of value-
conditioned personas fail to express their profile from the
outset (Atif, Saha, and Choudhury 2026), and personas drift
over multi-turn dialogue unless trained for consistency with
multi-turn RL (Abdulhai et al. 2025). We add an interac-
tive, dyadic outcome, where the twin’s decision depends on
a partner it has never met.
Steering and personalization.
Persona or trait directions
can be read, added or trained in activation space (Rimsky
et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2025a; Cao et al. 2024), and adapters
(Hu et al. 2022) can imitate a person’s style. They are usu-
ally evaluated with Big-Five questionnaires (Goldberg 1992),
yet strong steering pushes free text into repetition (Braun,
Eickhoff, and Bahrainian 2026), role directions need per-
role steering strengths (Song et al. 2026), the advantage of
feature- and probe-based steering over prompting depends
on the prompting strategy (Fan et al. 2026), and personality
LoRAs role-play no better than prompting (Krabbe and Shi
2026); we evaluate both on social outcomes.
Predicting attraction and judging conversations.
In
speed dating, the largest share of the variance in desire is
specific to the pair, and self-reports collected before the
dates could not predict it (Joel, Eastwick, and Finkel 2017);
romantic evaluations are more unique to the pair than con-
sensual (Eastwick and Hunt 2014), so matching before peo-
ple meet is unlikely to explain more than a small part of
long-term outcomes (Finkel et al. 2012), and people under-
estimate how much a new partner likes them (Boothby et al.
2018). LLMs reading speed-dating transcripts predict match-
ing only modestly (Matz et al. 2024), LLM judges of conver-
sational success on CANDOR correlate only moderately with
participants’ ratings (Züfle et al. 2026), and multi-question
LLM judgments combined by a small calibrated network
with judge-specific parameters agree better with individual
raters (Hashemi et al. 2024), motivating our appraisal head.

Personalization arms.
A 2×2 design (Fig. 1b): T00 is the
base twin; T10 adds one LoRA adapter (rank 16) shared by
all people, trained with the loss on each person’s own turns in
their excerpts; T01 adds a person-specific steering vector in
the residual stream, combining a per-person component with
a learned map of the person’s Big Five scores and trained on
the person’s own excerpts; T11 adds both.
Comparators.
B0 is a ridge regression on both people’s
questionnaires; B1 adds each person’s rating history from
their other calls (mean liking given, received and enjoyment),
information the twins never see. Both are fitted on 574 non-
held-out calls. C00 asks the base model to read both persona
cards and predict the liking directly, without a conversation.
Two rounds.
Round 1 is pre-specified: before any outcome
was read we froze the split, materials, arms, baselines, the
primary contrast ρ(T11) −ρ(T00) and the analysis (paired
bootstrap, 95% percentile intervals), and sealed predictions
before loading outcomes. Round 1 calls ran 40 turns and
each arm read its own ratings. Round 2 is exploratory and in-
corporates the diagnostics of §4.3: steering vectors retrained
so that the person-specific part carries most of their energy
(Fig. 4a), calls run to 1,300 words or 120 turns, and every
rating read by the plain base model. All 5,680 rehearsals
were regenerated. Round-2 analyses use outcomes already
seen and are not corrected for multiple comparisons.

Results
4.1
Twin rehearsals carry the signal
The base twin’s rehearsal predicts real liking at ρ = 0.277
(Table 1), above the questionnaire model B0 (0.242). The
same reader applied to the real held-out calls, cut by the
same rule, gives a ceiling of 0.321, and the rehearsal is
not significantly below it (+0.044 [−0.051, 0.142]). Beyond
the strongest baseline B1 (0.330), the rehearsal carries in-
dependent information: its partial correlation given B1 is
0.156 [0.047, 0.262], against 0.189 [0.081, 0.293] for the real
call, and adding either to B1 raises the out-of-fold corre-
lation from 0.315 to about 0.34 (+0.026 [−0.028, 0.077]
and +0.028 [−0.032, 0.083]), a smaller and non-significant
gain because it also pays for fitting the combination. Con-
versation adds most for newcomers without rating history
(Fig. 2d): over the questionnaire model (out-of-fold 0.216),
the real call adds +0.092 [0.021, 0.163] and the rehearsal
+0.062 [−0.014, 0.134]. Reading more of the real call helps
up to about 1,300 words (Fig. 2c), so the 1,300-word cut
keeps most of what the reader can use.
Figure 2a,b shows where the signal travels. Swapping in
another pair’s conversation erases it (0.090 real, −0.038 re-
hearsal), while removing the reader’s persona card, or giving
it someone else’s, leaves most of it. Reading the card alone
reaches 0.218 but adds nothing beyond B1 (partial 0.014).
What a twin contributes beyond questionnaires and history
therefore arrives through the conversation. Pretraining re-
call of CANDOR cannot explain it: rehearsals are newly
generated, held-out calls never enter twin construction, and
the swaps show that the signal needs this pair’s conversa-
tion. Rehearsals are generated from both partners’ cards, so
part of this is persona information expressed in dialogue;

4.4
A persona-prompted twin holds the
conversation, not yet its owner’s judgment
The conversation is not where the signal is lost: even on
real transcripts, the reader’s pair-level accuracy only matches
B1 (−0.009), so the limiting step is the post-call judgment.
A twin stands in for one person, so Figure 5 scores each
twin’s rating against its owner’s rating of that partner. Twin
ratings correlate with their owners’ at 0.23–0.24 and favor
the owner over the partner (+0.08 [0.01, 0.15]; Fig. 5b), so
they do carry person-specific information. But they pick who
liked the other more in only 53–54% of pairs (Fig. 5a), with
intervals that include chance, while the owner’s own past
ratings reach 56%. The person-specific part comes from the
card: without it the twin no longer favors its owner, and its
accuracy falls to chance level. Twins are also harsher than
people, rating partners4.3–4.5 wherepeoplesay 6.0 (Fig.5c).
Telling a twin its owner’s habits fixes the scale but not
the judgment. One sentence with the person’s mean past
rating moves the twin’s mean to 5.71 (Fig. 5c), nearly
halves its error and lifts the directed correlation to 0.284
(+0.04 [−0.05, 0.14] over no history). The twin’s own rat-
ing complements the history (partial correlation given his-
tory 0.197), but once told the history the twin anchors on it:
with another person’s history the correlation drops to 0.005
(own minus other’s: +0.279 [0.18, 0.37]), the partial corre-
lation falls to 0.137, and a plain statistical combination of
the history-free rating with the history does at least as well
(0.301). The same holds after rehearsals, where the twin told
its owner’s history reaches 0.286 and rates as it does after the
real call (directed difference −0.002 [−0.03, 0.02]). Told its
owner’s history, the rehearsal twin picks who liked whom
more in 57.8% of pairs, the only twin row whose interval ex-
cludes chance and about what the history alone gives (56.1%;
Fig. 5a); adding how past partners rated the owner changes
nothing further (directed −0.009 [−0.02, 0.00]).
Learning to judge.
Can the twin’s own post-call answers
become informative once it learns how people turn such im-
pressions into ratings? On 574 other real calls (none held
out), we fitted a small ridge head that combines the twin’s
answers to seven post-call questions (such as whether it
felt liked, enjoyed the call or would talk again) with the
owner’s questionnaire and rating history, and compared it
with the same head without the twin’s answers; we call
these heads H2 and H0 (Fig. 6a). Cross-fitted over the train-
ing calls, the twin’s answers raise the directed correlation
from 0.204 to 0.260 (Fig. 6b) (+0.056 [−0.001, 0.111], the
pre-specified primary contrast, whose interval just reaches
zero; none of 200 label permutations of the whole pipeline
reached this gain), while the same questions answered from
the persona card alone add nothing (−0.005), so the gain
comes from the conversation. Trained on real calls only,
the head reaches a pair-level correlation of 0.394 on the
held-out real calls and 0.378 on the rehearsals (Fig. 6c–e),
and improves on B1 out of fold (+0.070 [0.011, 0.130] and
+0.059 [0.001, 0.115], averaging four rehearsals); its ratings
sit on people’s scale (mean 5.89 against 6.0). The gain car-
ries over from real calls to rehearsals without measurable
loss (difference −0.001 [−0.052, 0.050]), while who liked
whom more still comes from the owner’s history (about 61%
with or without the twin’s answers). Part of the lift comes
from learning to weigh the history: H0 alone already gains

What a twin needs to judge like you.
Table 3 collects the
answer. The owner’s past judgments carry what the twin’s
judgment lacks, and stating them in the prompt makes the
twin anchor on them rather than integrate them; imitating the
owner’s voice did not help. Appraisal should therefore be a
component of the twin in its own right, learned from the per-
son’s past evaluations and validated person by person. Agent
architectures with explicit memory (Park et al. 2023), includ-
ing twins whose preferences evolve across encounters (Ye
et al. 2025), are natural places to integrate these evaluations,
and the protocol below measures the gain in the owner’s
own judgment. A first learned appraisal head already lifts
rehearsal-based prediction above the history baseline (§4.4);
learning each owner’s taste, rather than their leniency, will
need more than one to three past ratings per person.

An attribution protocol.
For any twin used to predict so-
cial outcomes we recommend reporting: (i) a baseline with
the same pre-interaction information, including rating his-
tory; (ii) the same reader applied to real interactions, as a
ceiling that locates any loss in the simulation or in the judg-
ment; (iii) swap controls for the persona and the interaction,
which separate signal from prompting artifacts and recall;
(iv) person-level, directed agreement and who-liked-more
accuracy rather than pair means only; (v) the own-versus-
other-person control for any personalization; and (vi) verba-
tim repetition against real dialogue.
When the rehearsal is the bottleneck.
The same protocol
identifies settings where the simulation, not the judgment,
loses the signal. In a pre-specified pilot on post-negotiation
liking (CaSiNo, Chawla et al. 2021; 1,030 pairs), the same
reader reached 0.523 on real negotiations but 0.159 on twin-
to-twin negotiations, below a ridge baseline (0.227). Com-
paring a rehearsal with its same-reader ceiling separates the
two cases (Fig. 7).
Scope.
CANDOR measures liking after one conversation
between strangers rather than dating outcomes, on a single
item near its ceiling (mean 6.0 of 7), and we read text only;
these bound what any text reader can reach. Each held-out
person meets one partner and has one to three past ratings,
which bounds person-level models. Our twins are persona-
prompted base models without dedicated cognitive modules,
so the judgment results describe this simplest design. We
use one open-weight model, as the data agreement excludes
external services. Our arms test two common ways to per-
sonalize style, not owner-likeness itself: the retrained vector
fits held-out speech no better than none (Fig. 4c).
6
Conclusion
AI twins can already hold the conversation: their rehearsals
predict real liking beyond questionnaires and rating history
and keep most of what the same reader finds in the real
call. They do not yet carry their owner’s judgment: sound-
ing like the owner lowered accuracy, and a twin told its
owner’s past ratings anchors on them. Learning from peo-
ple’s past evaluations helps, as a small appraisal head lifts
rehearsal-based prediction above the history baseline out of
fold. Twin-based social simulation should separate the sim-
ulation from the judgment, test each against a same-reader
ceiling and swap controls, and invest in learned, person-
specific appraisal rather than an imitated voice.
Broader Impact
Twin rehearsals could let people screen many potential con-
tacts, such as dates, cofounders, mentors or roommates, with-
out an hour-long first meeting each; this could especially help
people with little time, limited mobility or social anxiety to
widen their circles, and let anyone preview how a first con-
versation might go before they meet. For research, rehearsals

factual control, such as replaying one encounter with a single
input changed; researchers could pilot designs before recruit-
ing anyone, or study encounters that would be impractical or
unethical to stage. Our attribution protocol turns such studies
into auditable evidence: a same-reader ceiling, swap controls
and person-level metrics show where a twin’s signal comes
from, and our failure modes catch problems that training loss
and routine checks miss. The protocol needs only a model’s
ratings and reports aggregates, so other groups can apply it to
their own data without releasing anyone’s conversations. For
platforms, the same checks show before deployment whether
to improve the simulation or the judgment, and person-level
validation shows where a twin misrepresents its owner before
anyone relies on it. The same results mark the risks. Twins
do not yet carry their owner’s judgment, so their recommen-
dations should be hints that people confirm by meeting, not
decisions made for them. Twins need personal conversation
data, so consent, data minimization and the ability to in-
spect and delete one’s twin are essential; judgment learned
from past ratings can carry a person’s or a platform’s biases
forward and should be audited person by person and across
groups. Only its owner should control a twin, so that no one
else can use it to probe or manipulate them.
