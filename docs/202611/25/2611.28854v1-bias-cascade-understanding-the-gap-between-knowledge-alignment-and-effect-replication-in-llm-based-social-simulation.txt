Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to simulate
participants in social experiments. Existing research has primar-
ily focused on aligning social knowledge and isolated responses,
while overlooking whether simulations can reproduce the collective
effects of social interventions. This distinction is critical due to a fail-
ure mechanism we call a bias cascade: similar individual states can
lead to different actions, while agreement on isolated actions can
still produce divergent outcomes through dynamic interactions. As
a result, models may produce accurate social judgments yet fail to
reproduce the outcomes of classic human studies. To investigate this
problem empirically, we introduce REFLEX (Reproducing Effects of
Foundational Literature EXperiments), a benchmark of 30 counter-
factual social experiments grounded in classic human studies that
compares knowledge judgment with effect simulation. Knowledge
judgment directly predicts the direction of an intervention’s effect,
whereas effect simulation fixes the scenario and participants while
varying one prespecified social factor, with agents generating ac-
tions from local observations that are aggregated into macro-level
average treatment effects. Compared with human-study findings,
the best-performing model achieves net directional scores of 0.847
for knowledge judgment and 0.453 for effect simulation. Moreover,
longer causal chains are associated with poorer effect replication,
consistent with the bias-cascade hypothesis. These findings reveal
a substantial gap between knowledge alignment and effect replica-
tion, showing that current methods remain inadequate for reliably
simulating collective human behavior.

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to simulate
participants in social experiments, supporting institutional compar-
ison, intervention design, and experimental rehearsal [18, 20, 27].
These applications require simulations not only to capture how hu-
mans respond to changing social conditions, but also to reproduce
the collective effects observed in human experiments.
Existing approaches broadly follow two strategies to enhance
model capabilities: aligning social knowledge through demonstra-
tions, human preferences, simulated social interactions, and struc-
tured knowledge tasks [9, 23, 26, 29]; and simulating participants
based on roles, population profiles, survey responses, or personal
self-reports to approximate the judgments of target individuals or
populations [3, 20, 32].
However, these approaches leave important gaps. First, from a
theoretical perspective, existing methods emphasize isolated de-
cisions or individual attributes while underrepresenting dynamic
interaction mechanisms [19, 24]. Faithful simulation requires more
than plausible individual judgments. In the social sciences, empirical
associations between macro-level variables are not sufficient ex-
planations, because macro-level outcomes can emerge from micro-
level processes [15, 36]. To analyze effects faithfully, agents must

isolated responses. Optimizing only for response-level agreement
risks rewarding correlational shortcuts that fail under intervention.
We use Coleman’s boat from the social sciences to provide a
theoretical account: macro-level conditions shape individual states
through a situational mechanism, these states generate actions
through an action-formation mechanism, and interacting actions
produce macro-level outcomes through a transformation mech-
anism [10, 16]. This framework reveals two potential sources of
mismatch. At the action-formation stage, a model may reproduce
human states but apply a different decision rule, resulting in dif-
ferent actions. At the transformation stage, even agreement on
isolated actions may still lead to different feedback when agents
interact with one another. Errors across these stages can propagate
and accumulate, causing simulated effects to diverge from human
findings. We call this failure a bias cascade.
To test this hypothesis empirically, we introduce REFLEX (Re-
producing Effects of Foundational Literature EXperiments), a coun-
terfactual social experiment benchmark that tests whether model
simulations reproduce intervention effects documented in classic
social science studies. REFLEX compares model performance in
two settings. In knowledge judgment, models directly predict the
direction of an intervention’s effect in a given social scenario. In
effect simulation, each of the 30 tasks constructs intervention condi-
tions from a shared scenario, varying one prespecified social factor
while holding participant configurations and all other experimental
settings identical. Simulated participants act on locally available
observations, and their actions are aggregated into macro-level
outcomes. We estimate the treatment effect as the mean outcome
difference between conditions and compare its direction with the
corresponding human-study finding.
The best-performing model achieves net directional scores of
0.847 for knowledge judgment and 0.453 for effect simulation. The
lower simulation score reveals substantial limitations in reproduc-
ing human experimental effects, while the gap between evaluation
modes shows that accurate social judgments do not reliably trans-
late into faithful simulations. Longer causal chains are also associ-
ated with poorer effect replication, consistent with the proposed
bias-cascade account.
Our contributions are as follows:
(1) Theoretical analysis. We formalize the social simulation pro-
cess, showing that neither state matching nor isolated action
matching guarantees faithful macro-level effects.
(2) Benchmark construction. We introduce REFLEX, a bench-
mark of 30 counterfactual social experiments for evaluating
whether simulated treatment effects match the directions docu-
mented in human studies.
(3) Experimental validation. We quantified the gap between
knowledge alignment and effect replication, and found consis-
tent and significant differences among different LLMs.
(4) Empirical diagnosis. We identify an association between
longer causal chains and poorer effect replication, providing
evidence consistent with bias cascades.
2
Related Work
2.1
LLM-Based Social Simulation
Research on LLM-based social simulation has progressed along two
main directions: simulation frameworks and agent construction.
Frameworks model ongoing social interactions by defining agents’
backgrounds, memories, and goals, and incorporating environmen-
tal feedback. Representative systems include Generative Agents
[31], which supports memory and planning; Concordia [39], which
separates agent decision-making from environmental execution;
AgentSociety [33], which supports large-scale social experiments;
and OdysSim [43], which builds foundation models for human be-
havior simulation. Agent construction methods use demographic
attributes, interview data, or responses from human experiments to
create silicon samples or individual agents [3, 6, 13, 20, 32]. Together,
these approaches provide platforms for social simulation and ways
to ground agents in individual-level attributes. Rather than scaling
platforms or matching static responses, we examine whether inter-
acting agents can faithfully reproduce collective treatment effects
under controlled interventions.
2.2
Validity Challenges in Social Simulation
Prior work highlights that social simulation validity cannot be as-
sessed solely from final outcomes or average responses. Errors may
arise from the simulation process, the response distribution, or the
experimental setup. At the process level, a model may reach the cor-
rect outcome through an incorrect generative path or mechanism
[21, 30, 42]. Scheduling, information exposure, and environmental

update rules may also shape collective outcomes more strongly
than communication among agents [24]. At the distributional level,
synthetic responses may approximate the human mean while com-
pressing variance, distorting relationships between variables, or
underrepresenting minority opinions [5, 7, 22, 34]. Models may also
describe a target distribution yet fail to generate behavior consistent
with it across repeated calls [19]. At the experimental level, prompts,
role assignments, and interaction protocols can substantially affect
group outcomes [2, 28, 41]. Results may also depend on simulation
configuration, model scale, and action-selection methods [8, 11, 45].
More broadly, counterfactual estimates depend on the simulator,
and their real-world relevance hinges on its fidelity [14]. Although
these studies identify diverse validity risks, they do not explain
why models with human-aligned social knowledge still fail to re-
produce experimental effects at the macro level. We address this gap
by analyzing how mismatches in individual decision-making and
multi-agent interaction propagate and accumulate across stages.
2.3
Benchmarks for Social Simulation
Existing benchmarks assess group response distributions, individual
test-retest consistency, multi-turn social interaction, and simula-
tion processes and trajectories [18, 21, 35, 38, 40, 44]. They cover
response similarity, interaction capabilities, and process plausibil-
ity, but do not directly test whether intervention effects can be
reproduced through actual actions. Related work has extended eval-
uation beyond response similarity to effect reproduction. Studies
have tested whether LLMs can reproduce classic human experi-
ments [1] and estimated treatment effects from isolated simulated
responses [4]. Other work incorporates experimental materials, con-
ditions, and statistical procedures into end-to-end evaluations [27],
or separately measures behavioral distribution fit and contextual
adaptation [37]. Selecting models, prompts, or target populations
based on individual response errors has also not consistently re-
duced treatment effect errors [25]. Nevertheless, most evaluations
rely on static responses or treat distributional fit as a proxy for
causal fidelity. Few use counterfactual simulations to test whether
social knowledge translates into emergent group outcomes. Our
benchmark directly compares models’ directional judgments about
interventions with their ability to reproduce those treatment effects
through interactive simulation.

Theoretical Analysis and Benchmark Design
3.1
Bias Cascade Revealed by Coleman’s Boat
Our study addresses the following question: Why might existing
LLMs, despite possessing relevant knowledge and understanding
the consequences of human behavior under certain conditions, still
fail to reproduce outcomes observed in real human societies? We
use a bias cascade to explain this failure. The upper panel of Figure 1
illustrates this problem.
We use Coleman’s boat to analyze its underlying causes [10, 16],
drawing on a classic framework in social theory. As shown in
the bottom panel of Figure 1, the relationship between a macro-
level condition 𝑋and a macro-level outcome 𝑌can be decomposed
into a mechanism chain that moves from the macro level to the
micro level and back. The situational mechanism explains how in-
stitutions, resources, and information structures shape individuals’
circumstances. The action-formation mechanism explains how in-
dividuals choose actions in response to those circumstances. The
transformation mechanism explains how these actions, through
interdependence and environmental feedback, produce macro-level
outcomes.
With sufficient pretraining and post-training alignment, existing
LLMs are already close to humans in many value judgments, in their
understanding of social norms, and in their tendencies in routine
behavior [12]. We therefore introduce an assumption about how
models understand social situations as the starting point for our
formal analysis:
Assumption. LLMs understand social situations.
That is, given a specified type of social situation,
their ability to understand agents’ micro-level states,
namely beliefs and goals, is comparable to that of
humans.

Together, these results illustrate the difficulty of simulation. Ex-
isting models may be well aligned with relevant social knowledge
yet still struggle to reproduce the effects observed in real human
societies. The central challenge lies in these two sources of mis-
match in the simulation process. Discrepancies between models
and humans can cascade and amplify across stages, ultimately pro-
ducing substantial divergence between simulated and actual human
outcomes.

3.4
REFLEX Benchmark Design
Task design. REFLEX comprises 30 counterfactual social sim-
ulation tasks, grouped into six mutually exclusive social science
domains: behavioral and experimental economics; political econ-
omy and public governance; social psychology and group processes;
networks, communication, and collective intelligence; markets, rep-
utation, and labor; and culture, norms, and evolution. Interdisci-
plinary studies are assigned to a single category based on their
primary research question.
In effect simulation, paired runs of the same experimental unit
across conditions share agent profiles and environment instances,
differing only in the prespecified social factor. Agents persist across
multiple rounds, can access only locally visible information, and
do not see paper titles, expected effect directions, other agents’
private information, or global states. Agents can affect the envi-
ronment through structured actions, so their actions coevolve with
the overall environment. Scoring is strictly based on actual actions,
such as contributions, penalties, migration, link formation, offers,
and votes, rather than general descriptions of human behavior or
textualized trajectories. For comparison, knowledge judgment di-
rectly queries models to predict the treatment effect direction from
structured descriptions of the study’s scenario and intervention,
without multi-agent execution.
Participant profiles. The platform uses structured heterogeneous
profiles to initialize simulated participants. In survey-calibrated
mode, 2,185 complete adult records are selected from the SAPA
project data. Records are sampled at the individual level, preserving
the joint structure of Big Five personality traits, age range, and
education. The sample has a mean age of 30.3 years and an average
education level equivalent to college.

Experiment
4.1
Experimental Setup
Main experiment. The main experiment evaluates eight open-
and closed-source general-purpose commercial models from lead-
ing LLM providers: GLM-5.3, MiniMax-M2.7, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash,
Claude-Sonnet-5, Qwen3.8-27B, Kimi-K2.6, GPT-6-Luna, and Qwen3.6-
35B-A3B. For each model, we evaluate performance on both effect
simulation and knowledge judgment. For effect simulation, we run
all 30 REFLEX tasks for each model, sampling agent populations
with seeds 1 to 5. We use different sampling seeds to vary the
sampled population and repeat each experiment five times, while
keeping the environment seed fixed at 42. All results are reported
with bootstrap confidence intervals.
Ablation experiments. All ablation experiments use GLM-5.3,

4.2
RQ1: Is Knowledge Alignment Distinct from
Effect Replication?
Table 1 presents the full results of the main experiment. The direc-
tional alignment rate in effect simulation ranges from 0.493 to 0.660,
suggesting that the probability of a model making a directionally
correct choice is not negligible. However, errors have substantial
consequences for practical policy guidance: an incorrect effect es-
timate could lead to a misguided policy direction and potentially
cause social harm. The directional reversal rate in effect simula-
tion ranges from 0.207 to 0.353. To capture the combined impact of
correct and incorrect predictions, we use the net directional score
(NDS) as our primary metric. The results show that NDS for effect
simulation ranges from 0.140 to 0.453. These scores remain modest
and in several cases close to zero, only slightly outperforming ran-
dom guessing and indicating that models may produce nearly as
many incorrect conclusions as correct ones. The low effect simu-
lation score indicates that current methods remain inadequate for
simulating human behavior.
We also examine the gap between knowledge judgment and
effect simulation, which is illustrated in Figure 2. Consider GLM-
5.3, the best-performing model overall in both knowledge judgment
and effect simulation. Its NDS is 0.847 for knowledge judgment but
only 0.453 for effect simulation. The difference is substantial, at
0.393, with a confidence interval of [0.113, 0.593] that excludes zero,
indicating a significant difference between the two settings. We
further compare the NDS gap between knowledge judgment and
effect simulation across all models. The gap is positive for every
model, and each bootstrap confidence interval excludes zero. Thus,
the same model exhibits a large difference between the knowledge
judgment and effect simulation modes, indicating a fundamental
distinction between the two. This result is consistent with our
theoretical expectation: key differences in the simulation process
prevent this directional knowledge from being carried through into
simulated outcomes.
Taken together, these results confirm that knowledge alignment
and effect replication differ significantly, and that the knowledge-
effect gap is positive for every model.
4.3
RQ2: Is Effect Replication Associated with
Model, Information, Population Size, or
Wording?
Information ablation. We vary the information available to the
model at the start of each simulation, using three settings: providing
neutral background information about the experiment, providing
historical materials related to classic studies, and directly feeding
the model’s own knowledge judgment answer back into its context.
As shown in Table 2, NDS is 0.433 with neutral background infor-
mation and 0.413 with historical materials. Neither score improves
on the baseline. Feeding the model its own knowledge judgment
answer back into the context raises NDS to 0.520, a modest in-
crease of 0.067 over the baseline. However, the confidence interval,
[−0.027, 0.233], still includes zero. The confidence intervals for all

three experimental settings include zero, indicating that the differ-
ences are not significant. This suggests that even when the relevant
content is carefully isolated and background knowledge about the
expected effect is explicitly provided to the model, its simulation
results do not improve significantly.
Together with the results in Table 1, these findings suggest that
LLMs are not unaware of what constitutes plausible human be-
havior, yet simulation fails to reproduce outcomes they themselves
explicitly predict. This may seem counterintuitive, as none of the at-
tempted manipulations yields a significant improvement. However,
our theoretical analysis points to an explanation: the central reason
LLMs fail at social simulation is not insufficient input knowledge or
an inability to understand social situations. Rather, models do not
capture the actual patterns of human action implicit in that social
knowledge, and this error is further amplified through interaction
with the social environment.

6
Conclusion
A metaphor succinctly captures our findings:
Coleman’s boat cannot navigate the bias cascade.
Building on Coleman’s boat, we identify two gaps between micro-
level alignment and macro-level effect fidelity. First, aligned states
may lead to different action rules. Second, matched marginal actions
may generate different joint interactions and macro-level effects.
Our proposed REFLEX evaluates these gaps on 30 minimally inter-
vened counterfactual tasks by comparing model-estimated average
treatment effects with empirical findings and reporting a unified,
single-model net directional score. Even the best-performing model,
GLM-5.3, achieves scores of 0.847 on knowledge judgment and 0.453
on effect simulation, yielding a substantial gap of 0.393. Wording
ablations further reveal high sensitivity to prompt phrasing, while
regression analyses confirm that longer causal chains exacerbate
performance degradation.

A
Ethical Statement
This study did not recruit human participants and involved no
real payments, registrations, contact with real-world individuals,
or emergency-service calls. Helping, donating, and organizational
participation were simulated actions.
B
Limitations
First, each task uses only the direction of the effect reported in a sin-
gle classic study as its reference point. For computational tractabil-
ity, the platform resolves structured numerical outcomes using a
rule-based engine. Although this approach improves consistency
and reproducibility, it necessarily abstracts away from the com-
plexity of real-world social institutions and interactions and may
not capture the stability or generalizability of the underlying social
mechanism across broader contexts. Second, the population profiles
are sampled from a publicly available dataset and consist primarily
of adults within a specific age and education range. This design is
necessary to ensure experimental comparability. However, these
profiles may differ from the participants in the original studies and
therefore may not fully represent the heterogeneity of those study
populations. Third, because the original studies provide limited
data on participants’ underlying thoughts, we cannot directly eval-
uate the theory by isolating the independent contribution of each
step in Coleman’s boat and comparing the resulting predictions
with real-world observations. Instead, we assess this theoretical
question indirectly through the length of the causal chain and ex-
amine the underlying mechanism in greater detail through case
analyses. Fourth, in diagnosing the bias cascade, we manually code
causal-chain length on a scale from 1 to 4 to quantify its influence.
This coding procedure may introduce a degree of subjectivity.
C
Terminology
This paper involves four related terms that operate at different
levels. Knowledge judgment and effect simulation are two experi-
mental settings at the measurement level: the former asks a model
to predict, independently of the simulation environment and using
only the task background, the direction of the effect induced by a
given social factor; the latter places LLM agents in a constructed so-
cial environment, where they act on locally available observations,
and aggregates their individual actions into a macro-level outcome
from which the treatment effect is estimated. Knowledge alignment
and effect replication are the target properties corresponding to
these two settings, at the construct level: knowledge alignment
refers to the agreement between a model’s knowledge of social
situations and their consequences and human knowledge, whereas
effect replication refers to the agreement between the direction of
the treatment effect and the conclusions of human studies.
D
Core Motivation
D.1
The Importance of Causal Modeling
Recent years have seen substantial progress in LLM-based social
simulation and its evaluation in the social sciences. Our study differs
from this line of work by placing causal interventions at the center
of simulation design and using the resulting treatment effects as
the primary criterion for effect replication.


Bias Cascade: Understanding the Gap Between Knowledge Alignment and Effect Replication in LLM-based Social Simulation
Prior work has introduced end-to-end benchmarks for evaluating
simulation capabilities [18, 27], but these benchmarks do not pri-
marily assess effect replication. Instead, they generally characterize
model capabilities through direct comparisons between simulated
and observed responses or outcomes. This capability-oriented eval-
uation paradigm has two main limitations. Existing frameworks
often assess associational agreement without incorporating causal
designs. Although numerous evaluation metrics have been pro-
posed, these metrics may not capture the mechanisms central to
social simulation. Optimizing against them may reward spurious
correlations while obscuring the sources of simulation failure. A
causal approach to social simulation instead requires an explicit
social environment in which treatment effects can be examined
through counterfactual social experiments.
These considerations motivate a counterfactual social experi-
ment benchmark centered on cross-level causal processes in social
simulation. From a causal perspective, average treatment effects,
particularly their directions, provide a principled basis for assess-
ing whether simulations reproduce population-level intervention
responses. This is the core motivation behind REFLEX.
D.2
Coleman’s Boat as Explanatory Framework
Existing research on social simulation has also made limited use
of classical social science theory. Drawing on Coleman’s boat, our
study identifies a gap between knowledge alignment and effect
replication, and develops a theoretical account of this gap.
Coleman’s boat is a sociological framework that explains how
macro-level social conditions produce collective outcomes through
micro-level actions and interactions. Its central premise is that em-
pirical associations between macro-level variables are insufficient
for a sociological explanation. An explanation must also identify
how social conditions shape individual actions and how those ac-
tions, in turn, generate macro-level outcomes. The framework thus
connects macro-level social conditions, micro-level states, individ-
ual actions, and collective outcomes through situational, action-
formation, and transformation mechanisms. In our study, it provides
a unified analytical framework for considering both structural con-
straints and individual agency. This perspective calls for modeling
actors who adapt their behavior within dynamic environments,
rather than restricting evaluation to the static, one-shot choices
examined in much of the existing literature.
Some studies have considered the distinction between knowing
and acting [19]. However, most operationalize social simulation
through single-turn, questionnaire-style evaluations. Their primary
focus remains agreement with human responses rather than the
action capabilities required in dynamic settings. Such evaluations
mainly assess responses associated with individual characteristics,
while leaving interaction processes largely unexamined. These eval-
uations remain appropriate for psychological research questions
centered on individual responses. However, many social phenom-
ena cannot be understood solely by aggregating individually aligned
responses. They also depend on how knowledge judgments trans-
late into actions and how those actions interact over time.
This perspective informs the design of REFLEX, which evaluates
model capabilities through outcomes generated in an explicit simu-
lation environment. Coleman’s boat further guides our analysis of
the mechanisms that may account for the observed gap between
knowledge alignment and effect replication.
E
Methods and Assumptions
E.1
Model Assumptions
Within Coleman’s boat framework, macro-level knowledge of a
social context 𝑚is mapped through situational mechanisms to
a distribution of micro-level states 𝑄𝑞(𝑧| 𝑚), where 𝑧represents
individuals’ circumstances and states. Our theoretical analysis intro-
duces an idealized assumption about LLMs’ understanding of social
contexts: when a model’s knowledge of a macro-level social condi-
tion is aligned with human knowledge, the simulation system can,
under idealized conditions, map its representation of that condition
to the corresponding human distribution of micro-level states. The
rationale is that relevant social knowledge includes relationships
between social conditions and individual circumstances and states,
providing a basis for this macro-to-micro mapping. Our experi-
ments provide evidence of knowledge alignment for the knowledge
judgments we evaluate, although they do not directly establish
alignment at the micro-level state.
We treat micro-level state alignment as an assumption for two
reasons. First, we cannot fully reconstruct the social contexts and
individual circumstances of the original experiments, and there-
fore lack verifiable micro-level ground truth. Second, even if those
contexts could be reconstructed, the claim that AI systems and hu-
mans share identical subjective perceptions would remain difficult
to operationalize and test. We therefore focus empirical evalua-
tion on observable macro-level outcomes and adopt agreement
in treatment-effect direction as a falsifiable criterion. Specifically,
under corresponding counterfactual experimental manipulations,
we test whether the direction of the model-estimated macro-level
treatment effect agrees with that reported in prior empirical social
science research.
We also examine the alternative explanation that the observed
failures arise from limitations in environmental design. To this end,
we verify that each environment admits a configuration capable
of reproducing the empirical effect direction. This check assesses
whether the environments preclude effect replication by design; it
does not establish sufficiency in all respects. For aspects that cannot
be resolved experimentally, we rely on theoretical analysis.
E.2
Probing the Bias Cascade
The bias-cascade account within Coleman’s boat involves two core
transmission stages: the mapping from micro-level states 𝑄to
action-selection rules 𝜌, and the aggregation of marginal action
distributions into macro-level outcomes 𝑌through interaction tra-
jectories T. Our experiments cannot directly identify these stages.
First, Coleman’s boat is an explanatory framework rather than a
statistical measurement model. Second, the intermediate processes
are not identifiable from the available data. Separately identifying
the two stages would require complete individual-level trajectories
under counterfactual conditions, together with the joint response
distribution of the same individuals within an interaction network.
Such data are not available for the empirical studies considered
here. Direct identification of the intermediate processes underlying
a bias cascade is therefore beyond the scope of the current design.


Zheng et al.
Accordingly, we use the bias cascade as an explanatory account
of simulation failure, rather than claiming to directly identify its
causal contribution. Nevertheless, our analysis extends beyond the-
oretical interpretation. To investigate the gap between accurate
knowledge judgments and unsuccessful effect replication, we in-
troduce the variable CausalChain as an indirect empirical test of
the bias-cascade account. This variable characterizes the causal
and interaction structure linking individual actions to macro-level
outcomes, allowing us to examine observable patterns predicted by
the framework.
We find that effect replication rates decline as causal-chain length
increases and interactions become more complex. These macro-
level patterns are consistent with the framework’s predictions and
support the interpretation that simulation failures reflect the prop-
agation and amplification of biases during simulation rather than
deficiencies in static knowledge alone.
E.3
Population and Sampling
For all REFLEX tasks, agent populations are sampled from SAPA, a
shared, internally heterogeneous general-population pool. Varying
the agent seed resamples agents from this fixed pool rather than
reconstructing the demographic sampling frame of each original
human study. Differences between our sample pool and the pop-
ulations studied in the original experiments limit the validity and
generalizability of our findings. At the same time, using a common
population pool provides a consistent basis for cross-task compar-
isons and reduces variation attributable to task-specific sampling
choices. Our design therefore prioritizes comparability across tasks
over population matching to each original study.
We find no statistically significant effect of the agent seed under
the current design. This result indicates that resampling within the
SAPA pool does not produce detectable variation in the estimated
treatment effects. It does not establish an absence of meaningful
population heterogeneity: results may differ under sampling frames
matched to the original experiments, and the observed stability
may reflect limited variation in the sampling pool, limited model
sensitivity to population characteristics, or both.
In particular, reduced heterogeneity in model-generated actions
may itself contribute to the effect replication failures observed
in this study. This explanation is compatible with the proposed
theoretical framework, and our analysis does not rule it out. Because
population heterogeneity is not the primary focus of our research
question, we retain a common-pool design for cross-task evaluation
and leave population-matched sampling and the explicit analysis
of heterogeneity to future work.

G
Rule-Based Baseline
G.1
Evaluation Objective and Setup
One might object to the thesis of this paper: model performance
is not determined directly by deficiencies in LLM social simula-
tion capability; instead, the difference in differences is driven by
internal simulation noise, that is, the poor measured model perfor-
mance arises because the system itself admits no reasonable feasible
solution. We rule out this possibility using a rule-based baseline.
An alternative explanation for the performance decline observed
in the simulation experiments is not that the models lack the re-
quired capabilities, but that the task design makes high performance
fundamentally unattainable. This section evaluates and rules out
this possibility.
We examine whether the directional effects specified by REFLEX
are attainable in the current task environments. If an explicit rule-
based model using only local observations can produce population-
level effects that match the reference under the same environment,
action constraints, and settlement rules, then the tasks are direction-
ally satisfiable. To this end, we implement a purely rule-based agent
model in accordance with the large language model experimen-
tal setup. All configurations are generated once using the default
heuristic rules, without additional validation or hyperparameter
tuning.
We prompt GLM-5.3 to directly generate a rule that conforms to
real-scenario variation. Its information about how the environment
is executed is omniscient, but the rule is generated by the model
in a single pass, without repeated tuning to improve the score.
Notably, this model may not produce the best possible rule; the
values obtained on this basis should therefore represent a theoretical
lower bound on the highest achievable score for the problems in
the scenario.
G.2
Metric Results
The evaluation uses REFLEX’s 30 environments, action space, pri-
mary metric, and prescribed directions, with the environment seed
fixed at 42. Each task is evaluated once for each subject seed from
1 to 5. The rules receive only observations available to the subject
and do not access hidden global information. Actions continue to
be validated and settled according to the original REFLEX rules.
The resulting NDS is 0.853, with an average of 27.4 tasks per
seed exhibiting the prescribed direction, see Table 8. This result
substantially exceeds the best NDS of 0.453 obtained in the current
simulation experiments.
Moreover, after averaging across the five subject seeds for each
task, the estimated effect direction matches the human reference
for all 30 tasks. Thus, an explicit rule-based simulator constructed
for the current task set can reproduce the prescribed directions
across all 30 tasks under the default environment seed, although it
does not do so for every individual subject seed.
These results show that the reference directions in REFLEX are
attainable rather than inherently unsatisfiable. The LLM’s low NDS


Bias Cascade: Understanding the Gap Between Knowledge Alignment and Effect Replication in LLM-based Social Simulation
is therefore more plausibly explained by limitations in action for-
mation and interaction sequencing than by a benchmark design
that excludes the correct direction.
These experiments rule out the hypothesis that the poor mea-
sured model performance is because the system itself has no reason-
able feasible solution, and they support the hypothesis proposed in
this paper.

Simulation Platform Architecture
I.1
Layered Responsibilities
The layered architecture of our framework is shown in Figure 5.
The platform consists of layers for task specifications, the environ-
ment, populations and agents, logging, and result generation. The
responsibilities of these layers are strictly separated: the environ-
ment generates the information visible to agents and adjudicates
the outcomes of actions, the Python code executes the rules and
updates the state, and the logging layer stores the complete event
chain.
The platform follows a unified execution procedure:
(1) Generate observations. Based on the current global state, the
environment generates a locally visible observation for each
agent.
(2) Request decisions. The system provides the model with the
task constraints, the agent’s own information, and the observa-
tion for the current round, and requests a structured action.

I.2
Information Isolation and Recomputability
The ground truth required for evaluation is not exposed to agents.
The runtime environment projects each agent’s observable informa-
tion based on its role, location, and network relationships. Agents
cannot access other agents’ private information, hidden utilities, un-
delivered messages, or evaluation answers. Agents may submit only
valid actions and cannot directly modify resources, relationships,
matching outcomes, or market states.
All state changes are executed by Python code, including re-
source transfers, network relationship updates, agent matching,
market clearing, and penalties for violations. This design separates
model decisions from the actual consequences imposed by the en-
vironment and prevents models from bypassing the experimental
rules through their outputs.

Mechanistic Analysis of Examples
J.1
Positive: Punishment Raises the Cost of Low
Contribution
In the public-goods task, costly punishment is expected to increase
final-round contributions. Participants allocate fixed resources to
a public good, whose total contribution is multiplied and redis-
tributed. Punishment costs the sender and reduces the recipient’s
payoff. After observing contributions, agents decide whether to
punish, and the resulting payoffs affect the next round.
The decision rule is that punishment minus no punishment is
greater than zero. A positive effect indicates that the model under-
stands punishment as a way to deter free riding, uses it conditionally,
and causes recipients to increase later contributions.
For seed 1, contribution rates under no punishment are 0.4625,
0.2875, 0.1875, 0.0875, and 0, compared with 0.4875, 0.5625, 0.5625,
0.5625, and 0.5625 under punishment. The model uses punishment,
predicts the correct direction, and produces higher subsequent
contributions. This shows positive replication in knowledge, condi-
tional action, and interaction continuation.
After the initial punishment, the punishment group maintains
higher contributions without further punishment. Across repeated
runs, punishment is used in every run and the final-round contri-
bution difference is always positive.
Reflection. This is a fully correct simulation result, and it shows
that the model is capable of handling some situations. Overall, the
model’s behavior here is clear and correct, which may be related to
the fact that the causal chain length of this task is only 2.
J.2
Null: Universal Upvoting Erases the
Initial-Rating Difference
The initial-rating task tests whether changing a comment’s initial
score from 0 to +1 increases later upvotes. Readers sequentially
upvote, downvote, or abstain. Only the displayed initial score differs
across conditions, and each reader observes the accumulated score.
The decision rule is that initial-upvote minus neutral is greater
than zero. The comment concerns extending library hours during
exam periods. Each reader observes the current score and updates
it for the next reader. The task also includes an initial-downvote
condition.
The model knows that an initial upvote should increase later
upvotes, but all readers upvote under every condition. In Table 10,
subsequent upvote rates are 1.0000 under neutral, initial-upvote,
and initial-downvote conditions. Final scores are 12, 13, and 11,
respectively. Thus, the initial signal is represented in the input but
does not affect behavior. The model shows directional knowledge
but fails to reproduce human condition sensitivity.
Repeated runs produce the same result. The initial vote changes
the displayed score, but every subsequent reader chooses the same
action. The null effect therefore reflects identical action distribu-
tions rather than genuine condition sensitivity.

a situation in which everyone upvotes is almost nonexistent in
reality. This is a bias in the mapping from states to actions. The
reason may be that training overfits the model to user-pleasing

Tasks and Comparisons
Table 13 summarizes each task by its core comparison, expected
direction, original study, and task-level results. All 30 tasks are sup-
ported by stable bibliographic sources. Twenty-seven tasks have a
positive expected effect, while three have a negative expected effect.
Each manipulation varies only its registered social factor, such as
information visibility, an institutional switch, network structure,
or risk level.
Table 13 also reports task-level metrics, revealing substantial
variation in performance. Strong positive effects arise in tasks with
salient and locally actionable manipulations, including Public goods
and punishment with an NDS of 0.900, Repeated estimation and
social information with a value of 1.000, Intermittent contact and
collective problem solving with a value of 1.000, and Weak-link
coordination and group size with a value of 1.000. In contrast, per-
formance is poor when success depends on aggregating dispersed
information or sustaining coordinated exploration, as observed in
Voluntary entry to public projects with a value of −0.800, Network
coloring with local information with a value of −0.650, and Collab-
orative search and sharing with a value of −0.400. Tasks with weak
or ambiguous manipulations, such as Initial ratings and following
and Cultural-market popularity accumulation, cluster near zero at
0.000. Overall, benchmark difficulty is highly task-dependent. Fail-
ures are concentrated in tasks that require information aggregation,
coordinated exploration, or sustained diffusion, rather than sim-
ple local responses. This pattern is consistent with our categorical
analysis and assessment of causal chain length.
