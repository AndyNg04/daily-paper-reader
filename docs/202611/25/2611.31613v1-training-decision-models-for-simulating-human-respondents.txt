Abstract.
Researchers increasingly use large language models (LLMs) as synthetic respondents in market
research, conjoint analysis, and randomized experiments. However, synthetic choice data from
off-the-shelf generative LLMs bias downstream econometric inference. Neither proprietary models
nor higher reasoning effort reliably reduces this bias. We investigate decision models (System
One architectures) for silicon sampling. Using choice training, we update KEV model weights on
historical respondent-level choice microdata by conditional maximum likelihood, before inference and
without target-study data. Across three model scales, training on two discrete choice experiments
lowers treatment-effect error by 22% to 89% on four held-out studies from new domains (vaccines,
nutrition labels, consumer rights, and AI) and new response formats. A small choice-trained model
outperforms a much larger off-the-shelf model on three of the four studies. Although training on
single studies lowers cross-entropy, it can exacerbate treatment-effect error. Choice training thus
complements post-inference calibration by providing a stronger starting point for estimating human
preferences.
Keywords. Decision models, large language models, synthetic respondents, econometric inference,
marketing research

Introduction
Using foundation models as synthetic respondents has attracted growing interest in marketing and
behavioral research. In marketing, a primary goal is downstream econometric inference, such as
estimating attribute slopes in conjoint analysis or recovering causal treatment effects in randomized
experiments. Most studies on synthetic respondents generate synthetic choice data with off-the-shelf
generative large language models (LLMs), yet substituting these outputs for human responses
severely biases downstream analyses (Goli and Singh 2024, Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026).
A likely source of this bias is how LLMs are trained. During training, LLMs are rewarded for
producing a single correct answer on single-label objective benchmarks (e.g., mathematics, factual
questions) and for complying with safety rules. In these tasks, any probability placed on other
answers is considered error to be minimized. In contrast, simulating human choices requires a
probability distribution over all plausible responses, because respondents with the same observed
characteristics can differ in their preferences. Therefore, off-the-shelf LLMs tend to collapse their
response distribution onto a single answer and, because of their safety training, sometimes refuse to
answer. For instance, on a vaccine conjoint subsample (Kreps et al. 2020), off-the-shelf models incur
large ordinary least squares (OLS) treatment-effect error (MSEβ) relative to human respondents
(Wang et al. 2026). Contrary to common expectation, using proprietary models or increasing
reasoning effort can exacerbate this error (Figure 1).

Moreover, generative LLMs are slow and expensive because generating text tokens requires costly
Monte Carlo sampling to approximate choice probabilities. To avoid these costs, we study decision
models. Decision models replace a language model’s text-generation head with a pointer head
that scores candidate options directly (Deußer et al. 2026, Geuter et al. 2026, Palmer 2026).
Unlike LLMs that produce a probability distributions over all tokens in the vocabulary, decision
models produce a probability distributions over only the valid options in a task. These probabilities
eliminate Monte Carlo sampling cost and yield exact expected OLS treatment effects. Despite this
architectural advantage, off-the-shelf decision models still incur high treatment-effect error (Figure 1).
Because they are trained on similar single-label objective benchmarks, they treat disagreement
among respondents with the same persona as classification error (Gao et al. 2026).
In this research, we train decision models by updating their weights on historical human choice
microdata. We call this procedure choice training. Specifically, we minimize respondent-level
cross-entropy (CE), which is equivalent to conditional maximum likelihood estimation. Unlike
post-inference calibration methods that keep model weights fixed and require human data from the
target study (Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026), choice training occurs before inference and uses
no target-study human data. We therefore ask whether choice training on a small set of historical
datasets can reduce error on held-out test datasets that never enter training.

Our research makes the following contributions to synthetic respondents in marketing and behavioral
research. First, we show that choice training lowers treatment-effect error on unseen studies without
any target-study human data, complementing existing post-inference calibration methods (Hullman
et al. 2026). Second, we show that training decision models on just two studies with binary choices
transfers to new domains (vaccines, nutrition labels, consumer rights, AI, and offshoring) and new
response formats (three-option choices and five-point ratings). Unlike prior research, where training
on human choices failed to generalize to new domains (Brand et al. 2023) or even within the same
study (Wang et al. 2025, Wang et al. 2026), our training regime transfers to unseen studies. Third,
we show that training models to minimize cross-entropy loss does not guarantee improvements in
treatment effect recovery. Models trained on only one of our training studies did lower the CE
loss on every test dataset, yet sometimes led to worse treatment-effect error. On the other hand,
training on both studies mitigated these failures. This finding motivates novel training objectives
that target treatment effects directly.

Literature review
Early work on synthetic respondents prompted off-the-shelf generative LLMs with demographic
profiles to simulate human responses in political, economic, and marketing surveys (Argyle et al.
2023, Horton et al. 2023, Santurkar et al. 2023, Goli and Singh 2024). Although these models can
capture average preference directions, systematic discrepancies from human populations severely
bias downstream econometric estimates (Santurkar et al. 2023, Wadi and Fredette 2025, Leng et al.
2026, Wang et al. 2026). Common distortions include mode collapse (i.e., producing nearly the
same answer every time a question is asked) and prompt sensitivity. Recent marketing research
addresses these biases while keeping model weights fixed, relying on human data from the target
study or target individuals. These methods include post-inference econometric debiasing (Wang et
al. 2026), optimal post-inference reweighting across prompts and checkpoints (Leng et al. 2026),
and individual digital twins conditioned on hundreds of responses per person (Toubia et al. 2025).
Post-inference calibration uses a sample of human responses from the same study to correct model
outputs. Therefore, the final estimates remain statistically valid even when the model itself is
biased. However, how much these methods gain over human data alone depends on how closely the
underlying model approximates the target human population (Hullman et al. 2026).
A growing literature instead updates LLM weights directly on human microdata, either from the
target study itself, from a different study in the same product category, or from many unrelated
studies. Training LLMs on a small sample (e.g., 50 tasks) from the same study cannot remove the
bias in regression parameters (Wang et al. 2025, Ye et al. 2025, Wang et al. 2026) and still requires
human data for the same study. Moreover, research has shown that WTP estimates from a separate
study can transfer to a new study within the same product category, but fail to generalize to new
3


product categories (Brand et al. 2023). Outside marketing, large-scale behavioral training improves
out-of-study prediction across cognitive psychology (10 million choices across 160 experiments; Binz
et al. 2025), social-science experiments (2.9 million responses across 210 studies; Kolluri et al.
2025), and public-opinion surveys (Suh et al. 2025, Ku et al. 2026, Huang et al. 2026). Yet these
cross-study efforts rely on large multi-hundred-study corpora and autoregressive text generation.
Moreover, they evaluate predictive log-likelihood, marginal distributions, or cell-level distributional
distance rather than econometric treatment-effect recovery.
A third stream of research tackles the sampling variance of repeated text generation by using choice
probabilities in a single forward pass. Early studies extract next-token probabilities over option
symbols (e.g., A, B, and C) from LLMs, yet those probabilities remain sensitive to vocabulary and
token-frequency priors (Kadavath et al. 2022, Wadi et al. 2026). Dedicated decision models address
this sensitivity by scoring candidate options outside the vocabulary head (Palmer 2026, Wadi and
Ma 2026). However, because this architecture is new, it remains open whether pre-inference choice
training can adapt decision models for unseen-study econometric inference without using target-study
human data. In the following section, we formalize our training and evaluation methodology for
these models.

(qt,1, . . . , qt,Kt), with qt,k = P(yt,k = 1 | st, ct), spreads probability mass across options rather than
collapsing onto a single mode.

Equation 3 resolves an apparent tension in our objective function. Choice training minimizes
the same cross-entropy loss on the same one-hot label format as training regimes that have a
single correct answer. What separates the two is the distribution of qt. On objective tasks, qt
places all probability mass on the correct option (H(qt) = 0), so minimizing the KL term pushes
pt(θ) toward zero and one. On human choice data, if respondents with identical (st, ct) choose
differently, qt becomes interior (H(qt) > 0). Thus, the same loss function drives pt(θ) toward
interior probabilities rather than the most common option. Each one-hot choice is a single draw

from qt, hence training requires no aggregate choice shares. This property is essential in randomized
designs with sparse respondent covariates and task attributes, where each unique combination
(st, ct) is typically observed only once and, thus, empirical choice shares cannot be estimated cell by
cell. In such cases, the model recovers qt by sharing parameters across respondents and tasks with
similar covariates and attributes. Because this argument holds in expectation, a sufficiently flexible
model could still memorize individual choices. Therefore, choice training should still select the best
checkpoint on held-out validation data. Importantly, no target-study data enters Equation 2, so
downstream econometric evaluation measures genuine generalizability to unseen studies.
Econometric evaluation.

Experimental setup
4.1
Datasets
For choice training, we use seven datasets from distinct product and policy domains (Table 1).
Two datasets provide the respondent-level training microdata: a paired electricity-plan conjoint
(Electricity; Wu 2025) and a discrete-choice experiment on drone versus ground delivery across
four vendor contexts (Drones; Berke et al. 2023). Both training datasets vary price (monthly bill or
delivery fee) alongside non-price attributes. A separate airline conjoint (Flights; Goldfarb et al.
2026) dataset serves as our validation set, which we use to tune hyper-parameters and select the best
checkpoint (lowest MSEβ). Finally, we use four held-out test datasets to evaluate generalizability to
new studies, domains, and choice format.
Two test datasets include prices on products with price ranges absent from training (Consumer
rights and AI & offshoring), whereas the other two share no attributes with the training datasets
(Food labels and Vaccines). Response formats in the test sets are also different from the binary
choices used in training (Table 1). The test datasets have a three-option conjoint with an outside
option (Vaccines), a three-option identification task (Food labels), and a five-point ordinal rating
(AI & offshoring). Across all seven datasets, each choice task conditions on the respondent’s
observed covariates st in a standardized text format (Figure 2, panel a).

4.2
Choice-training regimes and protocol
At each model scale, we use a 2 × 2 design crossing the inclusion of Electricity (excluded
vs. included) with the inclusion of Drones (excluded vs. included) studies. This forms four choice-
training regimes: Off-the-shelf (neither dataset), Electricity (18,591 tasks), Drones (36,297
tasks), and Drones + Electricity (54,888 tasks).
Off-the-shelf evaluates the unmodified
decision model without choice training. This design tests whether single-dataset training transfers
to unseen datasets and whether pooling both choice datasets improves the model beyond each
individual dataset.
We evaluate the KEV-0.8B, KEV-4B, and KEV-9B open-weight decision models (Palmer 2026), each
loaded in bfloat16 precision with unit temperature. Choice training updates LoRA adapters (rank
r = 64, scaling αLoRA = 128, dropout 0.05) across all linear backbone projections jointly with the
trainable pointer head via Equation 2. Every trained run uses AdamW with a 5×10−6 peak learning
rate, a OneCycle schedule (10% warmup and cosine decay), weight decay 0.01, and gradient-norm
clipping at 1.0, for 3 epochs with mini-batch size |S| = 40 and random seed 42. Within each epoch,
tasks are shuffled by dataset (and by vendor context in Drones), partitioned into single-dataset
mini-batches, and interleaved randomly.

choice-training hyperparameters (Table W3), training curves (Figure W2), and attribute-level tables
and diagnostics (Figure W3; Table W4 through Table W9).

Results
5.1
Unseen-study transfer across domains and model scales
Relative to the off-the-shelf checkpoints, pooled choice training (Drones + Electricity) lowers
both MSEβ and CE in all 12 held-out test cells, reducing MSEβ by 22% to 89% (Table 2). Notably,
these gains extend both to datasets that share no attributes with the training studies (Food labels
and Vaccines) and to response formats absent from training (Figure 3).2 In particular, although
the training data contain only binary choices, pooled models still transfer to three-option choices
and five-point ordinal ratings.
Training on a single dataset, however, transfers to unseen studies less reliably.
Training on
Electricity alone and Drones alone each reduces MSEβ on three test datasets, yet raises MSEβ
above off-the-shelf levels in four of six cells in AI & offshoring (Figure 3). AI & offshoring is
the test dataset that has a five-point rating scale for its outcome variable. These failures arise even
though single-dataset training lowers CE in all 24 held-out test cells. A closer approximation of
individual choices (i.e., lower CE) therefore does not guarantee more accurate treatment effects.
Pooling both training datasets eliminates these failures, making unseen-study transfer more robust
than training on either dataset alone.
Choice training also reduces error more reliably than scaling model parameters. Similar to the pattern
seen for generative LLMs (Figure 1), scaling off-the-shelf decision models fails to improve treatment-
effect recovery consistently. Interestingly, choice-trained KEV-0.8B outperforms off-the-shelf KEV-9B
on three of the four test datasets, despite having an order of magnitude fewer parameters.

5.2
Attribute-level transfer across shared and unseen attributes
Because both training datasets include price, aggregate gains for the Consumer rights and AI &
offshoring datasets could indicate that the models have learned only price-related information.
9


However, attribute-level estimates rule out this explanation. On Consumer rights, pooled choice
training lowers MSEβ across the 34 non-price slopes by 23% to 41% at every model scale (Figure 4
and Table W6). Similarly, it reduces error across the 24 non-price job slopes on AI & offshoring,
by 33% to 34% at every scale (Table W7). Because Food labels and Vaccines share no attributes
with training, their gains likewise cannot stem from price information learned during training.
Despite these improvements, pooled choice training narrows but does not close the gap to human
estimates. Some of the largest human effects remain attenuated or over-estimated at every model
scale (Figure 4 and Figure 5). Full attribute-level estimates for the remaining datasets appear in
the Web Appendix (Table W5, Table W9, Figure W3, and Table W8).

Conclusion
Off-the-shelf foundation models, whether generative LLMs or decision models, struggle to recover
human treatment effects in unseen choice studies. Neither larger parameter counts nor higher
reasoning effort reliably mitigates regression estimates. In this research, we show that pre-inference
choice training of compact open-weight decision models provides consistent improvements. Training
on only two historical choice studies lowers both OLS treatment-effect error and cross-entropy across
every held-out test dataset and model scale. These gains extend to unseen attributes and new
response formats without any target-study human data calibration.
10


Figure 5. Vaccines OLS treatment effects by attribute for off-the-shelf and pooled choice-trained
models
Our results show how choice training fits alongside existing approaches for using AI models as
synthetic respondents. Unlike post-inference calibration methods that require human data from
the target study (Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026), choice training relies strictly on historical
datasets and can serve as a stronger starting point before any statistical calibration (Hullman et
al. 2026). It also generalizes beyond the domain of the training datasets and transfers across new
domains and response formats. Finally, whereas large-scale behavioral models depend on hundreds
of studies and autoregressive text generation (Binz et al. 2025, Kolluri et al. 2025), our approach
achieves unseen-study transfer from two datasets using a compact model architecture.
For marketing researchers, these findings carry the following practical implications. Before any
human data is collected, choice-trained decision models offer a stronger starting point that can
support exploratory pre-testing and experimental design while potentially reducing the residual
bias left for post-inference calibration. In addition, pooling various training datasets yields more
dependable transfer than single-dataset training. Also, choice training reduces error more consistently
than scaling model parameters. Because decision models also return exact choice probabilities
without Monte Carlo sampling cost, these gains require far less compute than generative LLMs.
Our results also expose a limitation of training on cross-entropy alone. Single-dataset training
lowers cross-entropy on every held-out test cell, yet, for some test datasets, it raises treatment-
effect error. Lower cross-entropy therefore does not guarantee more accurate experimental slope
estimation. One plausible reason is that cross-entropy weights every choice task equally without
directly penalizing bias in treatment-versus-control contrasts. Because decision models yield exact,
differentiable expected OLS slopes (Equation 4), future research can train jointly on respondent-level
cross-entropy and a direct treatment-effect loss.
Moreover, because pooled training combines two datasets and more choice tasks than either single-
11


dataset run, matched-volume ablations are needed to separate dataset diversity from data volume.
Similarly, choice-training generative LLMs and comparing with decision models would isolate
architectural advantages of decision models from training-data gains. On the estimation side,
choice-trained models still attenuate or overshoot some of the largest human effects at every scale.
Extending choice training to ordinal scales, non-US samples, and richer respondent covariates
therefore offers a natural next step toward generating more general synthetic respondents.

Off-the-shelf generative LLM baseline protocol
We compare off-the-shelf generative LLMs and decision models on a random subsample of 200
Vaccines respondents (seed 42; 1,000 three-option tasks), matching the AI-only sample size of
Wang et al. (2026). Each model’s choices enter the same OLS operator as the main analysis, and
errors are computed against the full-sample human slopes (N = 1, 971; 11 slopes).
Prompt. Generative LLMs receive the prompt of Wang et al. (2026, sec. 5.2.1). The system
prompt reads “You should act like a person with the following characteristics deciding whether or
not to receive a COVID-19 vaccination:”, followed by one line per respondent covariate (e.g., “Age:
18-29”). The user prompt lists options A and B by efficacy, protective duration, major and minor
15


side effects, authorization, origin, and endorsement, adds “C: You choose neither A nor B.”, and asks
“Which option would you choose as this person? Your response should be a single letter A, B, or C.”
Gemini prompts omit the respondent’s region, which the covariate mapping added after those runs.
Generation. We query Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite (minimal and high thinking) and Gemini 4 Argon
(low and high thinking) through the Gemini API with default sampling. We serve Qwen3.5-0.8B, 4B,
and 9B with vLLM in bfloat16, toggling reasoning through the chat template and using Qwen’s
official recommended sampling settings (reasoning off: temperature 0.7, top-p 0.8; reasoning on:
temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95; both top-k 20 and presence penalty 1.5) without an output-length limit.
Each generative LLM produces one response per task.
Parsing. A response counts as a choice only if, after removing surrounding whitespace, it is exactly
“A”, “B”, or “C”; all other responses are counted as refusals and excluded from the regression. Valid
responses enter the regression as one-hot choice vectors.
Decision models. Off-the-shelf KEV-0.8B, KEV-4B, and KEV-9B receive the same respondent
covariates and choice task in their native serialization and return a probability over the three options
in one forward pass, which enters the regression directly. Following the released model cards, these
baseline evaluations apply each checkpoint’s shipped calibration temperature (2.35, 2.41, and 2.19,
respectively).
