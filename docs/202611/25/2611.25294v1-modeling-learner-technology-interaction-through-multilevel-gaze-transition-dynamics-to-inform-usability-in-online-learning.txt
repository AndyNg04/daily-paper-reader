Modeling Learner–Technology Interaction Through Multilevel Gaze
Transition Dynamics to Inform Usability in Online Learning
Yueqi Weng, Jie "Jennifer" Lu, Fan Yang
Abstract—Gaze data provide fine-grained evidence of how
learners engage with instructional content and interface ele-
ments, complementing conventional usability evaluation methods.
However, eye-tracking analyses often examine within-slide visual
attention and across-slide navigation separately, limiting under-
standing of learner interaction throughout extended learning
experiences. This exploratory multi-method study integrated
area-of-interest transitions, gaze entropy, slide-level navigation,
and network-based representations to analyze seven eye-tracking
recordings of high school students interacting with an AI-
supported online biology lesson, complemented by three post-
intervention focus groups. Results showed individual differences
in within-slide gaze allocation and switching patterns. Learners
followed shared navigation pathways but differed in revisiting
and immediate return behaviors. The focus group findings
highlighted positive perceptions of interactive materials and
AI-generated feedback, alongside usability concerns related to
navigation, interface clarity, and assessment guidance. Together,
these outcomes show how integrating multilevel gaze analytics
with learner perspectives can provide complementary evidence
for identifying interaction trends relevant to usability evaluation.
Future research should test this approach with larger, more
diverse samples and examine relationships among gaze patterns,
usability, and learning outcomes.
Index Terms—Online learning, eye-tracking, gaze dynamics,
area of interest, usability
I. INTRODUCTION
O
NLINE learning environments can reflect continuous
learners’ behavioral trace data as they interact with
instructional content, yet these interactions are often rep-
resented through limited pathways, for example, observable
indicators such as clicks, page visits, and task duration [1].
Such indicators provide evidence of learner activity but capture
only part of how learners engage with a learning intervention
[2]. Learning engagement is multidimensional and context-
dependent [3], [4], so behavioral indicators need to be inter-
preted in relation to the learning activity and its surrounding
context [5]. Eye tracking provides a complementary source of
evidence by recording the spatial and temporal allocation of
visual attention [6]. By preserving information about where
attention is directed and how it changes over time, gaze data
provide a process-oriented representation that unfolds learners’
online interaction and complements action-based behavioral
trace measures [7], [8].
This process-oriented perspective makes gaze relevant to the
study of learning experience and usability in digital environ-
ments [9], [10]. Eye movements reflect the ongoing allocation
of visual attention during human–computer interaction and

have long been used to examine how users perceive and
interact with interface elements [11]. In educational contexts,
eye tracking has been used to investigate cognitive processing
and engagement with complex visual stimuli [6], moment-to-
moment attention during interaction with instructional content,
and cognitive load in multimedia learning [12]. Consequently,
gaze data can complement conventional behavioral traces
by providing evidence on visual processes through which
learners encounter, inspect, and navigate instructional infor-
mation [13]–[15]. For usability evaluation, these data can help
characterize how learners visually interact with instructional
materials and interface elements, providing behavioral evi-
dence that complements their reported experiences.
Realizing this potential becomes difficult when eye-tracking
analysis shifts from isolated tasks toward continuous and
dynamic learning sessions [16]–[19]. Common visual analysis
methods such as heatmaps and scanpaths are useful for char-
acterizing specific tasks and intervals, but these visualizations
are challenging to capture the multiple dimensions of eye-
movement behavior at once [20]. When applied across an
extended online intervention, analyses conducted separately
for individual activities, area of interest (AOIs), or time
intervals can fragment temporally structured gaze behavior,
making it difficult to trace how visual attention develops
across the interaction as a whole [21], [22]. Manual review
and annotation of large volumes of gaze data are also labor-
intensive [23], limiting the identification of recurring structures
across extended sessions without computational support [24].
Thus, the analytical challenge mainly lies in how to transform
continuous gaze traces into representations that retain mean-
ingful patterns of learner interaction across time.
Another limitation concerns the reliance on aggregate gaze
measures. Eye-tracking research commonly summarizes atten-
tion using fixation count, fixation duration, and dwell time
to predefined AOIs [6], [25], [26]. These measures connect
gaze behavior to certain design elements by quantifying where
attention is allocated and how long it remains there [15]. How-
ever, aggregation within AOIs provides limited information
about the temporal order in which attention moves between
elements [27], [28]. Temporal ordering is important because
transitions among AOIs characterize how visual attention
shifts, returns, and develops as learner interaction unfolds [28],
[29]. Learners can exhibit comparable aggregate attention to
the same interface elements while differing substantially in the
sequences through which those elements are viewed [29]. This
way, aggregate gaze measures alone may obscure differences
in the temporal organization and dynamics of learners’ visual
attention.

the temporal organization of gaze. Recent research [30] com-
bined conventional AOI measures with entropy- and velocity-
based metrics to characterize learner attention in an online en-
vironment. Gaze transition entropy quantifies the predictability
of gaze transitions among AOIs [27], [31]. Scanpath-based
approaches further represent gaze as an ordered sequence,
enabling analysis of transitions, recurrence, and similarity
across viewing patterns [29]. However, a remaining challenge
is linking gaze behavior across two nested levels: shifts of
visual attention among elements within a page of an online
program, and navigation across pages over the whole learning
session [32], [33]. Within pages, gaze moves among instruc-
tional and interface elements, while across pages, navigation
reflects how learners move through and revisit the broader
environment. Accordingly, AOI analyses capture local visual
behavior but not broader navigation trajectories, whereas page-
level traces capture navigation without representing within-
page gaze dynamics. Existing approaches to link these levels
of gaze analytics into a continuous representation of learner
online interaction remain limited, especially for examining
usability across an extended digital learning experience [34],
[35].
This study addresses this gap by modeling gaze as a
time-varying state within user interface design elements and
examining learner interaction through multilevel gaze dynam-
ics. The proposed framework integrates AOI, gaze transition,
recurrence, entropy-based metrics, and temporal measures to
connect within-page visual behavior with between-page nav-
igation. We apply this framework to an AI-supported online
lesson used by high school students in a research hospital
education program and triangulate gaze patterns with post-
intervention individual debrief data to interpret their usability
implications in relation to learners’ reported experiences.
II. LITERATURE REVIEW
A. Usability Evaluation of Online Learning Environments
Usability is commonly defined, following the international
standard ISO 9241-11, as “the extent to which a product can
be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with
effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context
of use” [36, p. 536]. In online learning environments, users
are also learners [37], so functional ease of use alone does
not show whether an environment supports learning. Usability
matters because the usability of a technology interface may
have a major influence on learning [38], and perceived us-
ability affects both learners’ experiences and their learning
outcomes [39]. When a system is difficult to use, learners
may spend more time learning the system than the content
[40]. This concern is especially prominent in online learning
environments, where technology serves as the primary medium
of instruction. A usability evaluation typically specifies what
is assessed and how. Regarding what is assessed, evaluations
commonly address effectiveness, efficiency, and learner satis-
faction [39], [41]. Educational researchers have extended these
technological criteria to pedagogical aspects, such as content,
instructional strategy, and learner support, and socio-cultural
aspects, such as communication and collaboration [42]. For e-
learning specifically, Ardito et al. [40] argued that evaluation
must jointly consider usability, accessibility, and didactic ef-
fectiveness. Zaharias and Poylymenakou [37] moved beyond
functional usability by combining web and instructional design
parameters, such as navigation, visual design, and learner
guidance and support, with intrinsic motivation to learn as
an affective usability measure. Regarding how usability is
assessed, evaluations draw on three main classes of methods
[42]. Inquiry methods, such as questionnaires and interviews,
ask users to report their experiences; inspection methods, such
as heuristic evaluation, have evaluators examine an interface
against established criteria; and testing methods, such as task-
based user testing and think-aloud protocols, involve observ-
ing users as they complete tasks. These evaluations span e-
learning, immersive, mobile, and game-based technologies,
with e-learning accounting for nearly two-thirds of studies
[42] and higher education being the main context in which
Internet-based learning platforms were evaluated [39].
Despite this growing body of work, current usability re-
search on learning technologies shows several methodologi-
cal drawbacks. First, it relies heavily on self-report. Inquiry
methods accounted for 64.8% of methods in the review by
Lu et al. [42], and questionnaires alone made up nearly half.
Studies of e-learning platforms in higher education likewise
relied mostly on simple research designs dependent on ques-
tionnaires, which were used in 50 of the 61 studies reviewed
[41]. However, questionnaires capture perceptions of use rather
than actual use [42], and self-reports can mislead. For example,
Davids et al. [38] found an e-learning resource unusable for
many participants, despite good satisfaction ratings reported in
an earlier evaluation. Second, methodological rigor is limited.
In the sample reviewed by Lu et al. [42], many questionnaires
and all task-based testing instruments were self-developed,
and “general usability” was the most frequently evaluated
technological outcome, typically without any explanation of
which usability features were measured. Moreover, 77.9% of
studies relied on a single method, even though no single
method captures all usability problems [42]. Third, outcome
measures remain narrow. Technological usability accounted for
over 85% of measures, compared with 13.4% for pedagogical
and 1.4% for socio-cultural usability [42]. Finally, evaluations
are often conducted summatively, late in development, as
confirmatory checks rather than as formative investigations of
learner behavior [42]. These drawbacks leave limited evidence
on how learners actually allocate attention and navigate con-
tent and interface elements during interaction. Process-oriented
measures, which capture learner behavior during interaction,
can address this gap. The E-TRACKING project, for example,
evaluated e-learning systems through learners’ exploratory eye
movements, offering an objective and quantitative complement
to mainly subjective and qualitative methods [43]. In another
study, Liu and Zhu [44] combined eye tracking with post-test
interviews to identify which elements of an e-learning course
homepage attracted or escaped attention, and found that the
combination yielded a richer interpretation than either method
alone. Building on such evidence, Lu et al. [42] recommended
eye-tracking metrics, such as fixation sequences and areas
of interest (AOIs), noting that they can reveal non-conscious
processes and design issues that traditional testing may miss.

B. Eye Tracking and Gaze Analytics for Usability Evaluation
Eye tracking has become increasingly common in educa-
tional technology research, particularly since 2014 [45]. For
usability evaluation, eye tracking records where users look and
the order in which their gaze moves across a display, without
relying on self-report, thus offering a more objective source
of evidence. Using such evidence to evaluate displays dates to
a 1950 study by Fitts et al. of military pilots’ eye movements
during instrument-landing approaches, which proposed that
fixation frequency on a display element reflects its importance,
fixation duration the difficulty of extracting information from
it, and transitions between elements the efficiency of their
arrangement [46]. These interpretations rest on what Just and
Carpenter [47] later formalized as the eye–mind assumption:
the eye remains fixated on an item as long as that item is being
processed, so gaze reveals what a user is attending to and pro-
cessing. Applying this assumption, usability researchers define
AOIs over interface elements and analyze the eye movements
within them to assess the visibility, meaningfulness, and place-
ment of each element [48]. For example, an unusually long
gaze on an icon before it is selected suggests that the icon lacks
meaningfulness and may need redesign [48]. Such measures
can distinguish design quality. Goldberg and Kotval [49] found
that a poorly organized interface produced longer scanpaths,
more fixations, and denser transition matrices between AOIs
than a well-organized one. They argued that eye-movement
analysis can enhance traditional usability evaluations, which
are often qualitative and of limited reliability. Reviewing five
decades of such work, Jacob and Karn [46] concluded that eye
tracking can contribute substantially to usability evaluation,
though not as a complete approach on its own.
Early eye-movement-based usability studies relied heavily
on cumulative fixation time within AOIs, and few examined
the sequential structure of scanpaths [49]. Aggregate repre-
sentations such as heatmaps likewise show which regions
were fixated but not the order in which they were visited
[31]. Yet order matters for usability. Two scanpaths with
identical spatial coverage can have very different transition
patterns: one direct and efficient, the other moving back and
forth between areas in a way that indicates uncertainty [48].
Transition-based measures capture this sequential information.
Goldberg and Kotval [49] used transition matrices to represent
eye movements between AOIs, treating a dense matrix as
a sign of extensive search and a sparse matrix as a sign
of more directed search. Krejtz et al. [31] extended this
approach by modeling each individual’s AOI sequence as
a first-order Markov chain and computing two normalized
entropy measures ranging from 0 to 1. Transition entropy
captures the predictability of switching between AOIs, and
stationary entropy captures how evenly attention is distributed
across AOIs. In multimedia learning, interactive and animated
visualizations produced lower transition entropy than static
illustrations, which the authors interpreted as more systematic,
less chaotic inspection of the learning material [50].
Interpreting these measures, however, requires caution. The
same metric can carry different meanings depending on the
task. For example, a high fixation frequency on an area during
browsing may indicate either interest in the content or diffi-
culty understanding it. During searching, the same frequent
fixations may instead reflect uncertainty about whether the
area is the target the user is looking for [48]. Entropy measures
are similarly context-dependent. In the art-viewing studies of
Krejtz et al. [31], low transition entropy combined with high
stationary entropy was tentatively linked to greater curiosity,
whereas high values of both accompanied recognition of
a familiar painting. Moreover, the difference in transition
entropy between viewers who did and did not recognize the
painting was significant for only one of six AOI grid sizes,
suggesting that whether entropy analyses detect an effect can
depend on how AOIs are defined. Interpretation becomes even
more difficult in dynamic interfaces, where scrolling, pop-
up messages, and user-initiated navigation make it harder to
determine what users were looking at [46]. In online learning
environments, where learners move across many pages or
slides, gaze patterns therefore need to be interpreted alongside
learners’ navigation.
III. METHOD
This exploratory multi-method research combined eye-
tracking analytics with post-lesson focus groups to examine
how learners interacted with an AI-supported digital lesson
and to identify usability issues. Eye-tracking captured gaze
allocation among interface elements and navigation across
lesson slides. Focus group interviews were held immediately
after learners explored the lesson, capturing their reported ex-
periences and perceptions of usability. The research questions
are stated below:
• RQ1: How do learner within-slide behaviors differ from
mul- tilevel gaze data?
• RQ2: What navigation trajectories do learners follow
through the digital learning intervention?
• RQ3: How can gaze patterns inform understanding of the
usability of the digital learning environment?
A. Learning Intervention Description
The learning environment is an online, case-based biology
lesson that follows the treatment journey of a young patient
with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). The lesson intro-
duced cell-cycle regulation through instructional content and
multiple interactive elements, including checkpoints, drag-and-
drop activities, a video, and multiple-choice assessments, to
help students connect and assess their understanding of cancer
and treatment. GPT-4o powered an evaluation agent for this
online intervention, providing personalized feedback during
three open-response activities, including case-based reflection
and lesson review. Guided by expert-authored reference an-
swers, the feedback provides accurate explanations and areas
for learners to improve their reflection. These activities provide
learners with opportunities to explain biological concepts,
apply their understanding to the patient case, and synthesize
content across the lesson.

B. Participants
We enrolled 18 high school students aged 16 to 18 in
an eight-week program at a research hospital. The program
offers hands-on mentored research to rising high school seniors
across biomedical disciplines. Participants had demonstrated
interest in biomedical research and related educational or
career pathways. We obtained informed consent from all
participants and, for those under 18, from a legal guardian.
After the eye-tracking sessions, we conducted three focus
group interviews with participating students (n=7) to ask about
their experience and provide feedback on the lesson.
C. Data Collection
1) Eye-Tracking Sessions: Each participant independently
explored the lesson for approximately 10–20 minutes on a
laptop equipped with a Tobii Pro Spark eye tracker. The
software exported individuals’ recordings with gaze points and
timestamps.
2) Focus Group:
The research team facilitated post-
intervention focus group interviews using a semi-structured
format, covering overall experience, interaction and naviga-
tion, perceptions of the AI features, and suggestions for
improvement. We recorded all focus group activities via Zoom
and exported and labeled the recordings by participant order.
D. Data Analysis
We implemented the eye-tracking analysis pipeline in
Python using OpenCV and NumPy for gaze detection, image
matching, coordinate processing, and geometric analysis. A
shared slide-element library was constructed once from a
reference recording of the course and then reused across
all participant recordings. We stored crops of visually stable
course screens as slide references and assigned each screen
a stable slide identifier, Sm. We labeled visually meaningful
instructional and interface components, such as instructions,
images, assessment regions, buttons, and navigation controls,
as AOIs shown in Figure 1. AOI boundaries were initially
proposed using EasyOCR and OpenCV and subsequently
reviewed and corrected by a researcher. Each AOI Emn was
represented as a two-dimensional polygon in screen-image

accepted only when the best matching distance was d ≤18;
otherwise, the previously assigned slide_id was retained.

keyframe, it was always assigned to its best-matching ref-

F. Qualitative Analysis and Synthesis
Focus group recordings were transcribed and analyzed using
content analysis. Transcripts were read in full and segmented
into meaning units about participants’ interaction with the


7
lesson. The learners’ self-reported pros and cons of the lesson
regarding the usability were categorized based on the STP
heuristics [52].
Quantitative gaze findings and qualitative findings were
integrated for analyzing the usability at interface features
and instructional activities levels. These were compared with
focus-group comments concerning the same features. Conver-

learner–technology interaction. Three major findings stand
out. Learners who completed the same lesson differed in
width, frequency, and sequence in their sampled slide elements
(RQ1). Learners generally walked through similar lesson con-
tent and followed some common slide-to-slide pathways, but
they differed considerably in slide visit times and backtracking
behaviors(RQ2). Finally, the gaze and navigation patterns
across slides and interface elements often aligned with learn-
ers’ description in the focus groups, helping identify specific
areas for design review(RQ3).
A. RQ1: How do learner within-slide behaviors differ from
multi-level gaze data?
Participants’ gaze reached about half of of the AOIs defined
on each slide (median coverage 0.500), and their visual
attention tended to be concentrated on some elements (median
H∗
S = 0.411). Participants learning behaviors differed broadly
by distributing their visual attention across the same interface,
with median H∗
S values ranging from 0.274 to 0.459 across
participants. These findings reveals added value of gaze ana-
lytics for understanding learner behavior. Although common-
use online behavior metric, such as the trace data can indicate
whether learners accessed a slide or performed an action [2],
such data do not fully reflect which instructional or interface
elements actually received visual attention [53], [54]. Gaze
analytics provides a more fine-grained view of how learners
allocate attention and process the information [55].
Switching rate and transition entropy captured different
aspects of learner behavior. P1 switched between AOIs most
frequently (3.48 transitions/s), but the low transition entropy
(H∗
T = 0.168) indicates that these switches tended to occur re-
peatedly among a limited set of AOI pairs. Across aggregated
participant behavior, median low transition entropy (0.258)
indicates that gaze switching concentrated within a relatively
restricted set of recurring pathways. These findings show
that gaze allocation and gaze-transition organization represent
complementary aspects of visual behavior [27], [29].
Sustained attention concentrated on assessment (36.1%) and
the navigation bar (26.3%), which together accounted for
roughly 62% of sustained dwell. The most consistent AOI-
level pattern was reciprocal switching between assessment and
navigation-bar elements on Slide 45(the assessment requiring
learner typing input), observed in all seven recordings (813 and
796 changes in the two directions). This suggests that partic-
ipants repeatedly moved between the task and the navigation
controls. Gaze analytics can’t explain alone the usability issues
or learning challenges.
B. RQ2: What navigation trajectories do learners follow
through the digital learning intervention?
Learners covered similar amounts of the lesson (37–46
unique slides) but moved through it differently where the
revisits accounted for 24.5% to 54.2% of visits, and immediate
A−B−A returns ranged from 6 to 22. Thus, only assessing


11
the unique-slide coverage would make learners’ lesson use
appear more similar than it actually was. This finding supports
the value of examining sequential navigation behavior to
characterize learning processes in digital environments [33].
Moreover, the A−B−A measure captured immediate return
behavior, identifying cases in which learners moved from one
slide to another and then directly returned to the previous
slide. This short back-and-forth navigation can unfold learners’
deep online learning engagement, as prior studies suggest that
such looping and revisitation patterns can reflect meaningful
learning processes, such as reviewing content after assessment
or returning to relevant material, and may differentiate higher-
and lower-performing learners [56], [57].
All seven participants followed the question–feedback se-
quence across Slides 18–24, and 47 directed transitions were
shared by at least four learners. Navigation diverged at
Slide 28, an interactive diagram with 12 distinct incoming and
11 outgoing connections and 42 revisits, branching to popup
slides (31, 33, 35, 36, 38) and an assessment slide (39). Some
of this hub-and-spoke movement is built into the design, since
popups return to the diagram. The other may be caused by the
assessment questions and results.
The highest navigation similarity was observed between P4
and P8 (Spq = 0.455). Each recording contained only about
65 slide transitions, while 127 distinct directed transitions
were seen across the full dataset, so individual transition
distributions were relatively sparse. P8, who had the highest
revisit proportion, also appeared in the two most similar
participant pairs, showing that navigation similarity reflects
the relative use of slide-to-slide connections rather than the
overall amount of revisiting. In addition, Spq is based on first-
order transition frequencies and therefore does not capture the
complete chronological sequence of navigation or the time
spent on each slide.
Finally, within-slide and across-slide behavior varied inde-
pendently in this sample. Comparing Tables II and IV, P6
had both the lowest switching rate and the lowest revisit
proportion, but P3 combined a low switching rate (1.94/s)
with a high revisit proportion (46.5%), and P1 had the highest
switching rate with a moderate revisit proportion (31.3%).
These are descriptive observations from seven recordings,
and we claim no association even though they illustrate why
within-slide and across-slide measures add information that
neither provides alone.
C. RQ3: How can gaze patterns inform understanding of the
usability of the digital learning environment?
Learners reported positive impressions of the interface,
interactive materials, and AI-generated feedback, but also
raised concerns about finding interactive elements, returning
to earlier responses, and understanding what the open-ended
assessments expected. Under the STP framework [52], these
map onto technological usability and pedagogical usability,
showing that the two are intertwined. Gaze and navigation
measures indicated where interaction concentrated and re-
curred, while focus-group accounts indicated how learners
experienced those features. Pairing behavioral traces with
learner talk make the findings more validated and builds on
eye-tracking evaluations of e-learning usability.
Assessment. Assessment elements received the most sus-
tained visual attention, and participants generally valued the
AI-generated feedback because it was personalized, explana-
tory, and encouraging. However, some participants reported
that they did not fully understand how much detail their
responses needed until they received the feedback. They
suggested providing exemplar responses and allowing students
to revise their answers after receiving feedback. This suggests
that students may need clearer expectations before submitting
their responses, not only feedback afterward. The usefulness
of AI-generated feedback may therefore depend partly on how
the assessment is structured, including the guidance provided
before submission and the opportunities given for revision.
Navigation. The navigation bar accounted for the second-
largest share of sustained dwell, indicating that learners de-
voted substantial visual attention to navigation controls. Re-
visits to previously viewed slides were also common, and all
seven participants showed switching between the assessment
and navigation-bar AOIs on Slide 45. According to the focus
group, participants generally understood how to move forward
and backward through the lesson, but some reported difficulty
returning to previously completed questions because the sys-
tem sent them back to the first question and required them to
resubmit later responses. It suggests that returning to earlier
content was an important part of learners’ interaction, but the
interface did not always support this behavior efficiently. De-
sign refinements could include preserving previous responses
and returning learners to the exact position from which they
navigated away.
Interactive materials. Participants generally appreciated the
clickable visuals and interactive diagrams, but some found
certain icons difficult to locate or match to the correct part
of the diagram. One participant also said the material moved
too quickly to fully engage with the content. The navigation
pattern around Slide 28 provides additional context for these
comments that learners frequently moved between the main
interactive diagram and its related popup screens, making
Slide 28 a recurring navigation point. Although this pattern
shows that learners interacted with the diagram, repeated
movement between the main slide and popup content does
not by itself indicate that learners fully processed the intended
instructional information. Popup content accounted for 10.0%
of sustained dwell, suggesting that these elements received
meaningful visual attention. In all, the behavioral and focus-
group findings suggest the value of clearer visual links be-
tween icons and their associated content, along with more
explicit guidance on what learners should examine in the
interactive diagram.
D. Implications
The study showa that gaze and navigation data can help
identify where usability problems may occur, while learners’
comments help explain why those problems occur. In the
aspect of instructional design, the findings point to four
possible refinements that could be tested in future versions


12
of the intervention: (1) allow learners to return to earlier
content without losing their responses or previous position, (2)
provide clearer expectations and exemplar responses before
learners submit their answers, (3) allow learners to revise
their responses after receiving feedback, and (4) make the
relationship between interactive icons and their associated
diagram content more explicit.
For researchers, the findings suggest several approaches
for analyzing learner interaction in the eye-tracking research.
First, integrate multiple aspects of eye-tracking dynamics to
examine learner behaviors for a deeper understanding and
more comprehensive capture of learner engagement. Second,
consider measures of revisiting and immediate A −B −A
returns alongside the number of unique slides visited. In this
study, participants visited similar numbers of unique slides but
differed considerably in how often and how quickly they re-
turned to previous slides. Third, frequently revisited slides and
frequently occurring AOI transitions can help identify specific
parts of an intervention for closer investigation through inter-
views or gaze-replay activities. The multilevel graph supports
this process by connecting participant-level, slide-level, and
AOI-level patterns in a single representation. Together, these
analyses reveal differences in learner interaction that aggregate
or single-level measures alone cannot capture.
E. Limitations and Future Work
This study used seven eye-tracking recordings from a single
AI-supported biology lesson, making the findings not general-
izable to other learners, subjects, or learning environments.
In addition, gaze coordinates were extracted from screen-
recorded gaze indicators, missing detections were filled using
a temporal hold procedure, and slide identity and AOI mem-
bership were estimated computationally. These processing
decisions may affect dwell and transition measures. Adjacent
AOI-label changes therefore should be interpreted as relative
indicators of switching behavior rather than exact fixations or
saccades. Additionally, the behavioral measures also cannot
explain learners’ intentions or learning outcomes. Although
focus-group data provided context for interpreting the gaze
patterns, we did not focus much on gaze analysis of individual-
level learning outcomes.
Future research should test the framework with larger and
more diverse samples and across different learning environ-
ments. It should also examine the robustness of the findings
under alternative gaze-processing choices and link multilevel
gaze and navigation patterns with learning outcomes, click-
stream data, and retrospective gaze-replay interviews. Finally,
the design refinements identified in this study should be
implemented and evaluated to determine whether they improve
usability and learning.
VI. CONCLUSION
This study examined learner–technology interaction in an
online learning intervention using a multilevel gaze analytics
approach that integrates within-slide visual attention with
across-slide navigation trajectories. The findings unveil in-
dividual differences in gaze allocation, switching patterns,
and navigation behaviors, along with recurring AOI transi-
tions and shared navigation pathways across participants. By
connecting AOI-level gaze dynamics, slide-level navigation,
and participant-level similarities, this approach provided a
temporally structured representation of how learners interacted
with instructional content and interface elements throughout
the intervention.
Integrating gaze-based findings with focus-group feedback
further shows the potential of using multilevel analytics to
inform technological and pedagogical usability evaluation.
This approach provides complementary information to con-
textualize reported experiences and identify interface and
instructional features that inform further investigation. Over-
all, this study offers an analytical approach to examining
learner–technology interaction beyond isolated gaze measures
and individual learning screens, with methodological impli-
cations for evaluating and refining extended digital learning
environments.
