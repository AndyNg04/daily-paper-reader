The Paradox of Agency-Preserving AI: Negotiating Learner Agency in Layered
Human–AI Interactions

OLOV ENGWALL, KTH Royal Institute of Technology, Sweden and Digital Futures, Sweden

Generative AI is increasingly used by learners in cognitively demanding tasks, but its capacity to provide immediate solutions raises
concerns about decreased learner agency. Meta-level AI systems that provide reflective and metacognitive scaffolding have emerged as
a promising approach to supporting learners’ self-regulation. However, these systems introduce a fundamental paradox: AI intended to
preserve learner agency can itself constrain that agency by regulating when, how, and why learners should reflect. To investigate this
challenge, we conducted a co-design workshop with 18 students engaged in programming and academic writing tasks using ChatGPT
extended with ChatWise, a meta-level AI system providing self-regulated learning-aligned feedback. Drawing on 185 prompt–feedback
entries, 174 written reflections, and participants’ design artifacts (17 storyboards and 5 group design proposals), we identified four
interconnected design tensions: assistance vs reflection, immediate intervention vs space for self-regulation, visibility vs attention
competition, and system adaptation vs user control. The study contributes a multidimensional account of learner agency that includes
task, cognitive, and interaction agency, along with empirically grounded interaction-design mechanisms to negotiate these tensions.

Generative AI (GenAI) offers learners immediate explanations, solutions, and feedback on cognitively demanding tasks
[19, 21, 22], which creates opportunities for personalized learning and timely assistance but may also encourage learners
to offload cognitive activities central to learning, including problem formulation, reasoning, problem-solving, and critical
evaluation [19, 20]. The fluent responses and frictionless interactions offered by commercial conversational GenAI
systems may nudge learners towards immediate task completion at the expense of sustained cognitive engagement
[21, 23]. These issues raise fundamental questions about learner agency: who decides which cognitive activities should

learner agency simply as the ability to interact with or control an AI system, recent human-computer interaction
(HCI) research conceptualizes it as relational, contextual, and dynamically configured through the distribution of
decision authority between humans and AI systems [2, 3, 49]. Meaningful learner agency extends beyond access to
choices or interface controls to learners’ ability to exercise consequential decision-making and influence how their
interactions with AI unfold [3, 28]. This authority may concern different aspects of the learning experience, including
task progression, cognitive participation, and the management of AI interactions [11, 36].
To address concerns about excessive cognitive delegation and preserve learner agency, researchers have explored AI-
based scaffolding designed to foster self-regulated learning (SRL), including self-monitoring and reflection [29]. Existing
approaches include modifying conversational AI behavior to encourage critical evaluation [23], introducing reflective
scaffolds grounded in pedagogical theories such as the Zone of Proximal Development [40] and Self-Determination

of agency, they can also impose additional cognitive demands, undermine motivation, or distrupt task progression
[8, 21, 37]. Moreover, overly prescriptive or poorly contextualised scaffolding risks shifting regulatory control from the
learner to the AI system [26]. This creates a paradox: AI systems designed to preserve learner agency may inadvertently
constrain it by determining when, what, and how learners should reflect [8, 32, 38].
This paradox becomes particularly salient in layered AI interactions, where learners engage with a task-oriented
primary AI alongside a secondary, meta-level AI intended to support reflection and regulate engagement with the
primary system. Whereas primary AI supports task accomplishment, meta-level AI scaffolds how learners approach,
evaluate, and regulate that assistance. Recent research demonstrates the potential of external metacognitive agents to
promote critical evaluation and more deliberate engagement with AI-generated outputs [16, 27]. However, introducing
this additional layer creates a distinct challenge for the distribution of decision authority: learners must regulate not
only which cognitive activities to delegate to the primary AI, but also whether, when, and how to engage with the
meta-level AI’s guidance. As these layers may introduce competing demands on learners’ attention, reasoning, and task
progression, the central design challenge extends beyond providing effective reflective scaffolding to ensuring learners
retain meaningful control over how such scaffolding shapes their learning processes.
Prior research suggests that the effectiveness of reflective scaffolding depends on the timing, presentation, and
adaptation of interventions to learners’ needs [9, 13, 37]. Feedback delivered after a complete AI-generated solution
may arrive too late to influence reasoning, whereas interventions introduced prematurely or too frequently may disrupt
task progression [21, 48]. Similarly, highly visible or information-dense feedback may compete for attention with the
primary task [10, 23], while adaptive interventions raise questions about who determines what support is appropriate
and how it should evolve [35]. From an HCI perspective, these challenges highlight the need to examine not only the
pedagogical content of reflective feedback but also how interaction mechanisms can enable learners to shape its timing,
visibility, intensity, and adaptation. In layered AI environments, such mechanisms become particularly important
because they influence the extent to which learners can actively regulate their engagement with meta-level AI support,
rather than merely respond to interventions initiated by the system.
Building on earlier work [15, 39], we investigate ChatWise, a lightweight browser extension that embeds SRL-aligned
metacognitive feedback [29] into learners’ interactions with commercial conversational AI systems without modifying
the primary AI’s responses. Previous evaluations provided initial evidence that ChatWise could encourage prompt
refinement, strategic awareness, and reflective engagement with AI-supported learning [39]. A subsequent participatory

authority across AI systems, rather than simply increasing the amount or intensity of reflective scaffolding.

Background
2.1
Cognitive offloading, reflection, and scaffolding with GenAI
Given the convenience of immediate and often complete solutions offered by commercial LLMs (e.g., Claude or ChatGPT),
learners favoring convenient shortcuts may (in)advertently delegate cognitive work embedded in learning tasks [21]. For
example, in their study, Jelson and colleagues showed that many students tend to delegate most of the writing process
to AI, ranging from topic exploration and planning to text generation and revision, with some students delegating
the entire essay-writing task [20]. However, even students who retained some involvement in the writing process
outsource some critical thinking processes such as ideation and opinion formation [20]. Similarly, scholars examined
how students used LLMs to delegate programming-related work, including code generation, debugging, and conceptual
explanation [19]. They found that behaviors that require greater effort from the learner, such as providing detailed
specifications for code generation, were positively associated with better learning performance, while lower-effort
behaviors, such as pasting raw error messages for the model to resolve, were negatively associated with learning
outcomes [19]. These patterns raise concerns about cognitive offloading [30] and ’metacognitive laziness’ [14]. When
learners can easily delegate cognitive effort to AI to improve task efficiency, they can miss opportunities to participate in
SRL—planning and selecting suitable learning strategies, monitoring their understanding, and evaluating and reflecting
on their learning process [18].
Existing research has explored different approaches that introduce deliberate friction in AI-mediated learning to
support reflective engagement. Li and colleagues for example, redesigned a chatbot to reconfigure the behavior of
the model, such as expressing uncertainty and reducing verbosity and found that reduced verbosity can improve the
detection of logically flawed responses by learners, while consistently expressing uncertainty can reduce the adoption
of incorrect chatbot responses [23]. Other work has drawn on learning theories to guide the design of scaffolds for
learner engagement. For instance, Sharma et al. developed a hybrid dialogue system combining a rule-based structure
based on SRL with LLM-generated, context-sensitive follow-up questions to support learner reflection [34]. Scholars

Previous work on reflective scaffolding has highlighted challenges related to disengagement, including increased

with learners’ use of task-oriented primary AI. We aim to explore design tensions and corresponding interaction
mechanisms for scaffolding that support learners in regulating cognitive delegation, enabling them to judge and decide
which cognitive activities to delegate, which to retain, and how to delegate in ways that are conducive to their learning.
2.2
Learner agency and delegation in AI-mediated learning
When GenAI systems are designed to take on increasingly proactive roles in interaction, they may initiate actions,
provide recommendations, and adapt their behavior in response to evolving contexts. Agency therefore is configured
through how human and AI roles are arranged (i.e., relational) and the opportunities each has to act, respond, redirect
interaction, and influence outcomes (i.e., contextual). The distribution of agency may vary across interaction contexts
and change as interaction unfolds (i.e., dynamic) )[45, 46]. In the AI-mediated learning contexts, scholars argue that
learner agency should be explained in terms of whether learners can make consequential choices and meaningfully
influence subsequent system behavior or learning pathways [28]. This means that learners have the option to reject a
system recommendation, and the system would adjust subsequent tasks or feedback based on the learners’ decisions.
Furthermore, scholars have emphasized the need to distinguish between the task initiative, which concerns leading
the development of a collaborative task, and the dialogue initiative, which concerns leading the current focus of the
discourse [11]. In their analysis, they have shown that a participant can take the conversational lead without taking
the lead in directing how the task should be accomplished [11]. Scholars also suggest that the learner initiative or
decision-making in mixed-initiative situations may have different purposes, such as reasoning and reflection, task
execution, or dialogue management [19, 34, 35]. They further argue that tracking the distribution of these two forms
of initiative can help systems adapt their responses and support coordination between users and the system [11].
Therefore, distinguishing between these initiatives and tracking the distribution is crucial, yet remain under-explored.
As GenAI closely engages with human reasoning and intentionality, Taylor and Krishna propose cognitive agency in
terms of people’s control, efficacy, and mastery of thinking and acting with AI. It emphasizes the value of metacognitive
monitoring of user behavior in their own thinking, exercise of judgment, and development of understanding through
collaboration with AI [36]. As learner agency is multidimensional and interactional, shaped through systems’ affordances,
decision policies, feedback structures, and orchestration behaviors [28], the distinction between task, cognitive, and
interaction dimensions is crucial for increasing our deeper understanding of students’ needs for agency-preserving AI
learner support.
However, examining learner agency in practice is particularly challenging as observable interaction alone may not
fully reveal how learners intend to allocate work and decision authority. Previous studies on large language models’(LLM)
delegation demonstrate that learners can face difficulties both in selecting appropriate delegation strategies and in
carrying out their intended boundaries [35]. The Agency Allocation Framework (AAF) [3] provides a structured
approach to operationalizing learner agency. It directs attention to specific decision points by specifying what decisions

2.3
Agency as an interaction-design problem: timing, attention, and control
Taking the HCI’s lens of agency, reflective scaffolding that preserves agency would need to consider how the interaction
and system design choices are configured to provide timely, controllable, and adaptive support. In this section, we focus
on three key aspects: (1) temporal organization, (2) attention and visibility, and (3) adaptation and user control.
Temporal organization. While GenAI is able to offer immediate metacognitive feedback, research has highlighted
the need to preserve reflective pauses that allow learners to engage in retrospective self-reflection and evaluation. For
example, Zhao and colleagues developed a self-regulated gamified VR system that provides immediate visual feedback
for quick error correction during English learning tasks. After each learning unit, learners were guided to assess
their expected performance, actual learning time, and learning strategies before receiving system-generated overall
diagnostic feedback [50]. Students who learned with this system showed better English performance and self-reported
self-regulation than those using gamified VR without the self-regulation support. In another study, SRLMentor, an
SRL-supportive GenAI system, similarly provided immediate diagnostic feedback to help learners identify knowledge
gaps, while also sending summarized scheduled feedback through periodic emails to provide learners with more space
for independent reflection [41]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. found that this type of immediate feedback paired with
delayed feedback timing shows the largest observed effect compared to either the immediate- or delayed-only feedback
in their meta-analysis [48]. They argue that such designs may support both reflection-in-action, where learners monitor
and adjust their approaches during the ongoing task, and reflection-on-action, where they revisit and evaluate their
learning processes after completing it [33].
Attention and visibility. In GenAI-mediated learning settings, reflective scaffolding faces an attentional trade-
off: guidance should be salient and detailed enough to support reflection [47], but overly detailed or prominent AI
explanations can increase cognitive load and lead to negative impact [1, 10, 12, 23]. Building on the principle of calm
technology, when information can move between the center and periphery of attention [43], prior HCI work has
explored ambient learning displays [5] and progressive disclosure [1, 24] approaches to regulate the attentional demands
of supportive information. In layered AI interaction, this becomes especially challenging as the noticeability of secondary
information also needs to be balanced against its potential to demand focal attention [10]. In the present study, therefore,
the important issue is that a meta-AI has to be sufficiently visible to influence behavior without competing with the
primary AI for focal attention.
Adaptation and user control. Learners differ in how they engage with AI support. Research on AI-mediated writing
indicates that learners’ cognitive delegation patterns vary with factors such as learning self-efficacy and the perceived
usefulness of AI [20]. Learners may also respond differently to interactive AI-supported environments, depending on
cognitive characteristics such as cognitive flexibility, i.e., the ability and willingness to consider alternatives and adapt
their responses to changing situations [44]. These differences suggest that effective learner agency-preserving GenAI
scaffolding needs to adapt and personalize support to different learner characteristics rather than provide uniform
guidance. However, adaptation also requires the system to make judgments about what kind of support is appropriate for
a learner. This can raise problems because the cognitive work learners intend to retain or delegate can differ depending

Together, these three dimensions highlight that preserving learner agency is not only a matter of what reflective
support is provided, but also when, how, and under whose control it is delivered. These design considerations become
particularly important when reflective scaffolding is introduced through a separate AI system that monitors and
intervenes in learners’ interactions with a primary AI. While such meta-level support may encourage reflection and
more deliberate engagement, it also introduces a potential tension: an AI system designed to preserve learner agency
may itself constrain that agency through its decisions about when and how to intervene.
2.4
Meta-Level AI for Regulating Human–AI Interaction
One emerging approach to providing such reflective scaffolding involves meta-level AI systems that operate alongside a
primary AI, supporting users in monitoring and regulating their interactions rather than directly assisting with task
completion. Recent work has explored the potential of these systems to manage cognitive offloading and support human
agency in cognitively demanding tasks. For example, Gmeiner et al. have introduced metacognitive support agents
that operate alongside the primary AI system, Autodesk Fusion 360’s Generative Design tool [16]. These agents aim
to help users regulate the cognitive processes involved in formulating design intent, exploring the design problem,
and evaluating AI-generated outcomes. Participants receiving such support generally produced more feasible designs,
with the interventions prompting them to consider previously overlooked design factors and revise inputs to the
generative design system. Park et al. instead have focused on users’ verification of AI outputs [27]. Their Thinking-box
presents sentence-level reflective checkpoints derived from patterns of distorted information. The intervention has
shown positive effects, such as encouraging more deliberate scrutiny of LLM responses and reducing missed instances
of distorted information relative to the baseline condition.
In line with this perspective, ChatWise focuses on regulating how learners engage with commercial conversational
AI rather than modifying the primary AI itself [39]. It combines learning analytics with SRL-aligned conversational
scaffolding to classify learners’ prompts and provide adaptive metacognitive feedback in real time. In a within-subject
field study, learners using ChatWise produced higher-quality prompts more than twice as often and showed greater
prompt refinement, strategic awareness, and reflective engagement with AI-supported learning. However, the impact
of introducing a meta-level AI into an ongoing learner-AI dialogue on learners’ conversational experience and sense
of agency remains under-explored. From the perspective of dialogic intelligence, such interventions may shape how
dialogue is opened, widened, deepened, sustained, and applied [42]. This motivates us to explore how learners negotiate
agency when engaging with a primary AI oriented toward task completion alongside meta-level feedback intended to
prompt metacognitive reflection.
2.5
Research Gap
Taken together, prior work demonstrates the potential of reflective and meta-level AI interventions to support learners
in regulating their engagement with generative AI. However, we still know relatively little about how introducing
a meta-level AI alongside a task-oriented primary AI reshapes learner agency. In particular, existing work has not
sufficiently examined how learners negotiate decision authority across these two AI layers, or how the interaction
design of meta-level guidance—including its timing, visibility, and adaptation—can support reflection without itself
becoming overly prescriptive, interruptive, or autonomous.

Method
We conducted a co-design workshop to investigate how learners negotiate decision authority when meta-level AI
guidance is layered onto their ongoing interaction with a task-oriented primary AI. The study was designed to: 1)
provide participants with situated experience of this layered interaction and 2) elicit reflection on where meta-level
intervention supported or constrained their learning, what challenges arose around the distribution of control, and how
they would redesign the interaction.
3.1
Participants and Study Context
The study participants were 18 graduate students (17 Master’s students and one PhD student; aged 23-46 years, M =
28.67, SD = 6.93) from a large European technical university who were recruited as knowledgeable co-designers in late
Spring 2026. The participants had prior academic backgrounds related to AI, HCI, and learning analytics, as well as
ethical considerations related to AI-mediated learning. This background enabled them to reflect both on their own
experiences as users of conversational AI systems in their own studies, and broader pedagogical and ethical implications
of AI-mediated learning. The study context was the students’ use of ChatGPT for course-related learning tasks, where
ChatWise was introduced to support learners in retaining decision-making authority over their cognitive engagement
and interactions with ChatGPT.
Participation was voluntary, and all workshop attendees received written information about the study aim and
procedure and completed a written informed consent form prior to data collection.
3.2
Procedure
During the co-design workshop, participants were guided through four activities (Fig. 1): (1) Individual Task, (2) Identify
Design Needs, (3) Ideation & Design, and (4) Peer Review.
To support reflection and co-design, we adopted the LeAF toolkit [15] as a facilitation tool to encourage participants
to articulate decision points that they considered important in the layered AI interaction and explore what meta-level
support is needed and how such support should be configured at those points. The toolkit consists of six card categories:
Learner describes learner profiles based on level of domain knowledge, SRL skills, or task confidence (e.g., proficient
in domain knowledge, novice in SRL); Behavior identifies interaction moments (e.g., formulating prompt, revisiting
chat history); Feedback provides pedagogical knowledge about feedback levels (e.g., task vs. process), learner needs
(e.g., affective vs. cognitive), and feedback stages (feed up vs. feed forward). Support Type represents how feedback may
be provided (e.g., reflective questions, checklists); Intervention Policy & Values suggests intervention conditions (e.g.,
immediate or delayed support, learner customization) and design values (e.g., privacy). Evaluation provides criteria for
evaluating interface designs. Each category also included blank cards to encourage participants to propose alternative
perspectives beyond the predefined options.
The toolkit enables participants to construct and evaluate meta-level AI intervention scenarios based on the following
structure: When [Learner] is doing [Behavior], the system provides [Feedback] via [Support Type], under [Intervention
Policy & Values].

3.2.1
Individual Task. The workshop began with an individual task to allow participants to experience ChatWise in
use. After providing informed consent, participants installed the ChatWise extension on their laptops. They received a
brief introduction to ChatWise as a feedback support tool for interacting with commercial conversational AI such as
ChatGPT. Participants then performed a learning task using ChatGPT under two conditions, first without ChatWise
support and then with the ChatWise extension enabled, allowing them to directly compare the two learning experiences.
They were required to select either a programming or a writing task, see our OSF repository 1. In the writing task,
participants selected an empirical research paper and wrote a short paper review, including specific strengths and
weaknesses supported by evidence from the paper. In the programming task, participants completed two LeetCode
Easy-level programming problems.
3.2.2
Identify Design Needs. After the individual task, the facilitator introduced the toolkit, explaining its categories
and illustrating them with examples. After that, the participants were divided into six groups to discuss their experience
with ChatWise in Miro 2 (i.e., an online collaborative whiteboard tool that helps teams work together visually). Using the
card toolkit as inspiration,they responded individually to three questions: (1) What gains did you experience when using
ChatWise to perform the task? (2) What challenges did you encounter when using ChatWise during the task? (3) What
could be improved in ChatWise to better support your AI-mediated learning? Following that, the participants engaged
in group discussions, where they collaboratively used affinity diagramming [25] to categorize their comments into
design themes. They then prioritized design needs based on: (1) ’must’ be implemented, (2) ’should’ be implemented,
and (3) ’could’ be implemented.
3.2.3
Ideation & Design. The next phase includes storyboarding and wireframe sketching based on their identified
design needs. Participants used the toolkit to elaborate their storyboard individually that shows the improved design.
Participants then evaluated each storyboard through individual dot voting within groups. The voting used three criteria:
usefulness, learning impact, and feasibility. Usefulness is explained in terms of whether the storyboard met learners’
needs and would improve the interaction experience with conversational AI. Learning impact – whether the proposed
requirement could meaningfully influence learners’ thinking and the learning process. Feasibility refers to whether
the idea could be integrated into conversational AI without major engineering barriers. After selecting storyboard
directions, the groups sketched wireframes (simple visual blueprints of the revised ChatWise extension) of their revised
ChatWise concepts.
3.2.4
Peer Review. The workshop concluded with oral group presentations and peer critique. Each group shared its
design proposal with the larger group. Other participants wrote comments on the proposals. Their reflective comments

3.3
Data Analysis
Across the 18 participants who provided their student-LLM chatlogs, we obtained 227 logged interaction entries. Each
entry contains a learner prompt submitted to ChatGPT and the corresponding meta-level feedback generated by
ChatWise, which we refer to as a learner prompt-ChatWise feedback pair, together with the participant ID, ChatWise’s
SRL classification label, and a timestamp. For example, one learner prompt-ChatWise feedback pair consists of the
learner prompt ’can you please first give some possible options?’, and the corresponding meta-level feedback asking the
learner to clarify the task, describe what they had already considered, and specify what they hoped to achieve. Each
participant contributed one or more logged interactions. We excluded 42 entries that were not related to the learning
tasks, leaving a final total of 185 entries. Of these, 59 were associated with the programming task and 126 with the
writing task.
In addition, we collected 174 individual written reflection comments, 17 storyboards, and 5 group design proposals.
One group proposal was excluded because a group member did not consent to the use of their data for this research.
The storyboards and group design proposals are available in our OSF repository 3.
We adopted a mixed qualitative analysis approach. First, we used deductive coding in chat logs to examine how
learners responded to the meta-level AI support (ChatWise) when engaging with the primary AI, namely ChatGPT. We
then conducted a reflexive thematic analysis of the coded interaction patterns, participants’ reflections, and design
artefacts to gain a deeper understanding of learners’ experiences with layered AI interactions.
3.3.1
Deductive Coding of Chat Logs. Learner prompts and corresponding ChatWise feedback were first coded separately
according to their interactional purpose. We then compared each ChatWise intervention with the learner’s subsequent
prompt to examine whether the subsequent prompt shows observable traces consistent with the meta-level suggestion.
We combined the comparison results with the interactional purpose code assigned to the preceding learner prompt
to determine under which interactional purposes observable consistency with, or bypass of, the meta-level feedback
occurred. In the writing logs, for instance, after ChatWise asked one learner to clarify more specifically what they had
already considered, the learner’s subsequent prompt provided several aspects of the paper that they viewed as strengths
or weaknesses. We treated this as observable evidence consistent with the prior meta-level suggestion.
When the learner’s subsequent prompt did not show observable traces consistent with the ChatWise suggestion,
we compared the purpose of the ChatWise intervention and the content of the preceding learner prompt to examine
whether there were observable factors that could account for the bypass. During this process, we did not attribute
learner prompt changes to the offered meta-level AI support, because such changes could also result from the primary
AI’s response, learners’ evolving reasoning, or other aspects of the conversational context.
3.3.2

analysis [6, 7] was performed on the coded chat-log patterns, the self-reported reflections of the participants, the peer
critiques and their design artifacts. This approach was chosen because it is suitable for examining both shared and

gains, concerns, and their desired system features. During the initial analysis, we identified recurring challenges in
learners’ interactions with the primary AI and the meta-level AI, alongside interaction mechanisms that were frequently
mentioned in relation to these challenges. Although these challenges relate to the design considerations discussed in
Section 2.3, our analysis revealed how they manifest as competing needs and expectations of meta-level AI support.
Therefore, we interpreted them as potential ‘design tensions’ and performed a second round of coding to refine their
scope and boundaries. Our analysis resulted in the four design tensions reported in the Findings (see Section 4).
4
Findings
Our deductive analysis of the learner-LLM logs suggests that learners’ subsequent prompts did not always consistently
align with the meta-level AI suggestions they received. We identified four observable response patterns: (1) observable
responses consistent with its suggestions, (2) continued task progression while bypassing prompted reflection, (3) explicit
contestation of the meta-level intervention, and (4) an indeterminate category in which prompt quality observably
improved, but it remained unclear whether the improvement stemmed from meta-level AI feedback, primary AI input,
or learners’ own reasoning. The first two response patterns occurred in certain interaction contexts. The observable
alignment occurred when learners clarified task requirements and context, expressed prior understanding or judgments,
outlined solution approaches, or sought further guidance on suggested reasoning activities. Bypass was observed when
learners wanted to request complete code or drafts or sought surface-level revisions such as wording or format.
To understand the possible reasons behind learners’ different responses to meta-level AI feedback, we conducted a
reflexive thematic analysis across the coded chat-log patterns, participants’ reflections and peer critiques, and design
artifacts. Four themes were developed. We refer to them as design tensions in learners’ interactions with primary-
and meta-level AI: (1) assistance versus reflection, (2) immediate intervention versus space for self-regulation, (3)
visibility versus attention competition, and (4) system adaptation versus user control. These tensions highlight learners’
challenges related to different consequential decisions. Table 1 summarizes the subthemes within each tension and
indicates the data sources contributing to each subtheme. In the following sections, we use these tensions to organize
our findings on how learners experienced and negotiated agency when interacting with a task-oriented primary AI
alongside meta-level AI guidance (RQ1). Within each tension, we also present participants’ proposed design responses
to these challenges (Group 2-6, see Table 2), which inform our discussion of RQ2.

4.3
Tension 3: Visibility vs Attention Competition
Participants’ reflections and design proposals highlight the need for meta-level feedback to remain noticeable enough
to inform learners’ ongoing reflection and interaction. Yet making meta-level guidance more prominent could also
increase its attentional demand, creating competition with the primary AI and disrupting the learner’s ongoing task
flow.
P3 noted that the ChatWise mediated feedback “disappeared too quickly” while they were still focused on solving the
task, and P1 proposed to “keep feedback on screen but allow conversations [with ChatGPT] to continue as well.”. At the
same time, several participants described various forms of attentional interference. ChatWise could “cover up the prompt
window” (P1), obscure the ChatGPT response (W8, P4), “suddenly appear” in a floating window that “interrupted my

(W1, W3, P2, P9), suggesting that attentional competition arose not only through spatial obstruction but also through
information load.

to decide whether to receive further guidance on improving their prompt. Group 5’s proposals complemented this with
dedicated interface regions or retained feedback histories that kept guidance accessible without requiring continuous
focal attention. These design ideas sought to coordinate the attentional roles of the two AI layers. These design ideas

5
Discussion
This study examined how learners experience and negotiate agency when reflective meta-level AI is introduced alongside
a task-oriented generative AI system. Our findings identified four interconnected design tensions: (1) assistance versus
reflection, (2) immediate intervention versus space for self-regulation, (3) visibility versus attention competition, and (4)
system adaptation versus user control. They also revealed how learners attempted to address these tensions through
different interaction-design proposals.
Rather than suggesting that agency can be preserved simply by increasing reflection or providing additional interface
controls, our findings indicate that agency depends on which decisions remain consequential for learners and how
authority over these decisions is distributed between the learner, primary AI, and meta-level AI. We discuss the
implications for conceptualizing learner agency, designing reflective scaffolding, and developing agency-preserving AI
interactions.
5.1
Learner Agency as Negotiated Decision Authority across Layered AI Interaction
Our findings extend understanding of learner agency in AI-mediated learning by distinguishing three interrelated
dimensions of decision authority: task agency, cognitive agency, and interaction agency.
Existing HCI research increasingly conceptualizes agency as relational and dynamically configured through human-AI
interaction rather than as an individual attribute or a fixed distribution of control [2, 49]. Similarly, the Agency Allocation
Framework proposed by Borchers, Viberg and Kizilcec [4] emphasizes the importance of examining specific decision
points, the actors involved, and their respective authority. Our findings build on these perspectives by illustrating how
decision authority becomes distributed not only between a learner and a task-oriented AI, but also across a secondary
AI system intended to regulate the learning interaction.
Task agency concerns learners’ authority over the direction, sequencing, and progression of their learning activities.
Our findings suggest that this dimension becomes particularly consequential when meta-level interventions influence
when learners proceed with a task, revise their prompts, or receive assistance from the primary AI. Participants’
preference for guidance before prompt submission illustrates how the temporal organization of AI support may
influence these decisions. However, participants also emphasized the importance of iterative interaction, suggesting that
preserving task agency requires maintaining opportunities to revise and reconsider task strategies rather than enforcing
a predetermined sequence of reflective steps. This distinction builds on Chu-Carroll and Brown’s differentiation between
task initiative and dialogue initiative [11]. While conversational systems may provide opportunities for users to direct

the immediate dialogue, this does not necessarily imply that they retain authority over the broader task trajectory. Our
findings extend this distinction to ’layered AI interactions’, where a meta-level system may influence task progression
without directly performing the learning task itself.
Cognitive agency concerns learners’ authority over which reasoning, planning, evaluation, and problem-solving
activities they undertake themselves and which they delegate to AI. Prior research has highlighted the risk that
generative AI can facilitate cognitive offloading by making complete solutions readily available [19, 20]. Taylor and
Krishna’s [36] conceptualization of cognitive agency similarly foregrounds users’ capacity to exercise judgment, monitor
their thinking, and develop understanding through AI collaboration.
Our findings add an important qualification: preserving cognitive agency does not necessarily require minimizing
cognitive delegation. Participants considered some forms of AI assistance appropriate, particularly when they had
already completed the cognitively demanding parts of a task or sought help with relatively superficial revisions.
Conversely, meta-level interventions that failed to recognize these circumstances were experienced as unnecessary or
inappropriate. This suggests that cognitive agency depends not simply on the amount of reasoning delegated, but on
learners’ opportunities to make informed and contextually meaningful decisions about that delegation.
Interaction agency concerns learners’ authority over how AI systems participate in their learning, including the
timing, visibility, intensity, and personalization of support. Participants sought opportunities to dismiss, expand, revisit,
configure, and contest meta-level feedback. These preferences align with research emphasizing that meaningful agency
requires more than nominal access to choices; learners must be able to influence subsequent system behavior [28]. The
distinction between these three dimensions contributes to existing conceptualizations of learner agency by making
different objects of decision authority analytically visible. Importantly, the dimensions are interdependent. For example,
an intervention designed to strengthen cognitive agency by encouraging independent reasoning may simultaneously
restrict task agency by delaying progression and interaction agency by limiting the learner’s ability to dismiss the
intervention.
We therefore propose that agency in layered AI environments should be understood as a negotiated configuration of
decision authority across multiple dimensions, rather than as a single property that can be uniformly increased through
additional scaffolding or user controls.
5.2
Designing Meta-Level AI That Learners Can Regulate: Criteria and Interaction Mechanisms
This section concerns the interaction-design requirements for reflective meta-level AI. Prior work has demonstrated
the potential of metacognitive scaffolding to support learners’ reflection, monitoring, and evaluation of AI-generated
information [16, 27, 41]. However, our findings suggest that the educational value of such interventions depends not
only on their reflective content but also on how they are integrated into learners’ ongoing interaction with the primary
AI. If agency is a configuration of decision authority rather than a property to be increased (Section 5.1), design cannot
simply add reflective prompts or interface controls. It must specify which decisions learners retain authority over and
how.

DC1. Context-sensitive and contestable guidance. Prior research suggests that learners’ decisions about cognitive
delegation vary according to their goals, expertise, and task context [35]. Our findings illustrate the implications of this
variability for reflective support in layered AI interaction. Participants questioned feedback that interpreted legitimate
assistance requests as inappropriate delegation or failed to recognize earlier reflective efforts. These experiences indicate
that reflective interventions should consider conversational history and the learner’s immediate interactional purpose
with the primary AI, as well as the meta-level AI’s own earlier guidance. Importantly, contextual adaptation should
be accompanied by opportunities to correct or contest the system’s interpretation. Otherwise, the meta-level AI risks
becoming the sole authority for determining what constitutes appropriate learning behavior. This criterion recognizes
that judgments about appropriate cognitive delegation should not be determined exclusively by the meta-level AI.
DC2. Learner-controlled timing of reflection. Existing research suggests that combining immediate and delayed
feedback may support both reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action [48]. Our findings further indicate that, in
layed AI interaction, the timing of reflection is consequential because primary AI responses can rapidly change learners’
motivation to engage with subsequent guidance. The preference for pre-submission feedback in many participant
storyboards suggests that reflection may be more meaningful when learners can consider guidance before receiving
complete assistance. However, the findings also suggest that reflection can develop iteratively rather than through a
single intervention. The value of pre-submission support should not be equated with mandatory intervention. Instead,
systems should create opportunities for reflection at consequential decision points while allowing learners to proceed,
defer, or revisit the guidance. Learners should thus retain control over the timing of reflective engagement.
DC3. Persistent and revisitable feedback. Existing approaches to SRL scaffolding often distinguish immediate diagnostic
support from later opportunities for reflection [41]. Our findings suggest that feedback persistence may provide another
way to support this temporal flexibility. Participants described reflective suggestions that became relevant only after
subsequent task interactions, but were no longer accessible. Maintaining feedback histories and revision traces may
allow learners to reconnect earlier guidance with their evolving understanding. This shifts the design emphasis
from delivering feedback at an assumed optimal moment toward preserving opportunities for learner-controlled
re-engagement. Learners can then determine when previously delivered feedback becomes relevant to their ongoing
reasoning and task decisions.
DC4. Peripheral-to-focal presentation. The attentional demands of reflective support have been recognized in prior
research on information overload and the presentation of AI-generated explanations [10, 23]. Our findings connect
these concerns to learners’ decision authority. Participants did not simply request less feedback; rather, they proposed
ways to control when feedback should occupy focal attention. Their proposals for expandable cards, dedicated interface
regions, and concise indicators suggested a common design direction: guidance should remain accessible without
continuously demanding attention. These proposals resonate with the ’calm technology’ principle, suggesting that
information should move between the center and periphery of attention [43]. In layered AI environments, progressive
disclosure and spatial separation can therefore serve not only usability goals but also agency-related purposes by
allowing learners to regulate the attentional relationship between primary and meta-level AI. This criterion emphasizes
the learners’ ability to regulate attentional prominence.

5.3
The Paradox of Meta-Level AI: Supporting Regulation without Taking Over
A broader theoretical implication of this study’s findings concerns the paradox of introducing additional AI support
to preserve learner agency. Research on AI-mediated learning has increasingly explored approaches that introduce
deliberate friction, reflective prompts, and pedagogical constraints to counteract excessive cognitive offloading. For
example, Klein et al. [21] argue that frictionless AI assistance can undermine sustained cognitive engagement and
propose stronger structures for preserving educationally meaningful reasoning. Similarly, Sharma et al. [34] and Li et al.
[23] investigated ways of shaping AI interactions to promote more reflective and critical engagement (for more, see
Background).
Our findings suggest that such interventions introduce a second-order problem of regulation: when an AI system is
introduced to regulate learners’ engagement with another AI system, who regulates the regulator?
The meta-level AI does not merely provide additional information. Through decisions about when to intervene, what
behavior to encourage, which forms of delegation to discourage, and how to personalize feedback, it can influence the
conditions under which learners exercise their own judgment. Consequently, an intervention intended to strengthen
cognitive agency may unintentionally restrict task or interaction agency. This tension was evident in participants’
responses to ChatWise. Some appreciated being encouraged to reconsider their prompts and engage more deliberately
with learning tasks. Others questioned the system’s judgments, particularly when it discouraged assistance that they
considered appropriate. Similarly, participants valued adaptive guidance but wanted opportunities to correct or influence
how the system interpreted their expertise and preferences. These findings challenge the assumption that more reflective
scaffolding necessarily leads to greater learner agency. They also suggest that agency-preserving design cannot be
reduced to a simple choice between stronger AI regulation and unrestricted learner autonomy. Stronger constraints
may protect opportunities for meaningful reasoning, but they may also become inappropriate when applied without
sufficient sensitivity to learners’ goals, prior efforts, and task contexts.
Building on the Agency Allocation Framework [4], we argue that reflective AI support should be examined in terms
of the specific decisions it influences and the authority learners retain over those decisions. This includes the ability to
accept, reject, defer, revise, or contest interventions, as well as the ability to influence how future support is configured.
From this perspective, agency-preserving meta-level AI should not aim to maximize reflection or minimize delegation,
but to support learners’ capacity to regulate their own engagement with AI assistance. This distinction is particularly
relevant to layered AI systems, where the introduction of additional agents or feedback mechanisms can increase the

complexity of decision-making. The design challenge is therefore not only to coordinate the functions of multiple AI
systems, but also to ensure that their coordination does not make learners’ own decisions less consequential.
5.4
Limitations and Future Research
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the study involved 18 graduate students
with backgrounds related to AI, HCI, and learning analytics. Their familiarity with AI-supported learning and design
concepts may have enabled detailed reflection on interaction mechanisms, but their perspectives may not represent
those of learners with less technical experience or different educational backgrounds. Second, participants engaged
in relatively short programming and academic writing tasks within a co-design workshop. Agency-related tensions
may unfold differently during prolonged learning activities, where learners develop more stable interaction habits
and where the consequences of cognitive delegation become more apparent over time. Third, although the chat-log
analysis identified observable patterns of alignment, bypass, and contestation, these patterns cannot establish whether
meta-level feedback caused changes in learners’ behavior. Learners’ subsequent prompts may also have been influenced
by primary AI responses, prior knowledge, or evolving task understanding. Finally, the proposed design criteria emerged
from participants’ experiences and co-design artifacts rather than from an evaluation of implemented alternatives.
Their effectiveness in preserving learner agency, improving self-regulation, or supporting learning outcomes remains
to be established.
Future research should therefore investigate how the proposed criteria can be operationalized in functioning meta-
level AI systems and evaluated across different tasks, learner populations, and durations of use. Particularly important
questions concern how learners exercise control over intervention timing, how they contest inappropriate guidance,
and whether opportunities for learner-steerable adaptation translate into meaningful influence over subsequent
system behavior. Longitudinal and experimental studies could further examine the relationships between perceived
agency, observable decision-making, cognitive engagement, and learning outcomes. Such work would help establish
whether agency-preserving interaction mechanisms can support educationally valuable reflection without introducing
unnecessary constraints on learners’ autonomy and task progression.
6
Conclusion
As generative AI becomes increasingly integrated into learning, preserving learner agency requires more than en-
couraging reflection or limiting cognitive delegation. This study examined how learners experience and negotiate
decision authority when task-oriented generative AI operates alongside reflective meta-level AI. Through an analysis
of learner–AI interactions, participant reflections, and co-design artifacts, we identified four interconnected design
tensions concerning assistance and reflection, intervention timing, attention, and adaptation. These tensions reveal a
fundamental paradox: AI systems introduced to support learners’ self-regulation may themselves constrain agency by
determining when, how, and under what conditions learners should reflect.
Our findings contribute to HCI research by demonstrating that learner agency in layered AI interactions is mul-
tidimensional and dynamically negotiated across task, cognitive, and interaction agency. Importantly, supporting
one dimension of agency may constrain another, highlighting the need to move beyond treating learner control or
reflective engagement as uniformly beneficial. Building on these insights, we synthesized five empirically grounded
design criteria, supported by concrete interaction mechanisms, for developing context-sensitive, temporally flexible,
revisitable, attention-aware, and learner-steerable meta-level AI. These criteria provide directions for designing reflective

Ultimately, we argue that the central challenge of agency-preserving AI is not simply deciding how much assistance
or reflection AI should provide, but who has the authority to make those decisions. Rather than regulating learners toward
predefined forms of desirable engagement, meta-level AI should support learners in exercising informed judgment over
their own cognitive participation, task progression, and AI interactions. The future of agency-preserving AI therefore
depends not only on designing systems that help learners reflect, but on ensuring that learners retain meaningful
authority over how AI shapes their thinking and learning.
