ABSTRACT
High-quality annotated datasets are critical for advancing computational social sci-
ence (CSS) research, yet collecting, curating, and analyzing large-scale data from
dynamic sources such as online social networks (OSNs) remains a persistent bottle-
neck. Researchers must also translate substantive questions into queries, relevance
criteria, and defensible interpretations. Large language models (LLMs) can assist,
but they can also obscure the judgments, potentially reducing researcher oversight
and critical evaluation. We present AI4OSN, a mixed-initiative platform that keeps
researchers in control of data collection, relevance labeling, and sensemaking while
making AI assistance available as an inspectable advisory resource. In a systematic
user study (N=18) using X/Twitter data, participants reported above-benchmark
perceived usability (mean SUS score of 79.7), increased confidence in LLM-assisted
annotation and analysis tasks, and low cognitive workload during AI-supported
sensemaking. Notably, novice participants showed descriptively greater alignment
and higher reported agreement with AI output than experienced participants, un-
derscoring the need to support differentiated verification practices across expertise
levels. Overall, this work contributes both a human-centered system for social data
analysis and empirical insights into how AI-assisted interfaces shape researcher be-
havior, trust, and decision-making. Our findings highlight design opportunities and
tensions in building accessible, transparent, and agentic AI systems for social media
and web research.

2014; Benkaouz and Erradi, 2015; Blanco et al., 2016), transportation (Rashidi et al.,
2017), education (Hamid et al., 2015), and crisis (Zhou et al., 2023). Despite their
importance, accessing and analyzing OSN data remains difficult for many researchers
(Stieglitz et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2025). Existing workflows often depend on APIs,

(CSS) research and disproportionately affect researchers with limited programming
backgrounds. At the same time, prior work on data cascades shows how early data

such data is therefore not only a technical problem but also an interaction design
challenge. Researchers must make sense of noisy and rapidly evolving content while
maintaining transparency and methodological rigor (Sapountzi and Psannis, 2016).
However, many existing tools emphasize full automation over supporting user under-
standing and control, leaving a critical gap for human-centered approaches (Amershi

LLMs create opportunities to support annotation, summarization, and sensemaking
at scale, but they can also introduce bias, ambiguity, and inappropriate reliance (Ash-

decision-making, people need opportunities to interrogate, correct, and contextualize
AI output rather than merely receive it (Amershi et al., 2019; Bu¸cinca et al., 2021).
Research in data analytics similarly shows that trust is shaped by both perceived
system behavior and users’ opportunities to verify its recommendations (Bach et al.,
2022; Tutul et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2019). These concerns are especially consequen-
tial in social media research, where a seemingly simple relevance judgment may embed
substantive, contextual, and ethical choices.
With these concerns in mind, we develop AI4OSN (AI-assisted platform for on-
line social networks) as a mixed-initiative research platform designed around three
requirements: (R1) preserve researchers’ ownership of queries and relevance criteria;
(R2) make AI-mediated transformations inspectable and revisable; and (R3) support
calibration by exposing AI suggestions as advisory inputs. The system instantiates
these requirements through project-based workspaces, manual-first labeling, optional
prompt-configured AI suggestions, and source-linked analysis outputs (Figure 1).

We present an AI4OSN system that integrates LLM assistance into social media
research workflows while preserving researcher agency over queries, labeling, and inter-
pretation. To understand how researchers perceive and use such AI-assisted workflows,
we conducted a mixed-methods study with 18 participants. Rather than comparing
AI and human accuracy, the study characterizes perceived usability, revision behavior,
and accounts of trust and verification. Our contributions are:
• We introduce AI4OSN, demonstrating a design approach to human-centered,
researcher-controlled AI assistance for social media research
• We establish a set of design requirements for researcher-controlled AI assistance
in social media research, grounded in human-computer interaction (HCI) prin-
ciples regarding human control, verification, and reliance.
• empirical findings showing how participants treated AI suggestions as contestable
evidence, which motivate design implications for prompt support, provenance,
uncertainty communication, and human revision.
2. Related Work
This section reviews prior work on social media and web-based research, particularly
studies that focus on large-scale data collection, filtering, and analysis, as well as the
challenges of data quality and bias. We also review research on human–AI collaboration
that examines AI support for human decision making, including trust, transparency,
and oversight.
2.1. Social Media and Web Research
OSNs have become a central data source for computational social science (CSS), en-
abling large-scale analysis of public opinion, behavior, and social dynamics. Prior work
has leveraged platforms such as Twitter, Reddit, and Facebook to study misinforma-
tion (Guo et al., 2020; Nandakumar and Rajivan, 2024; Saxena et al., 2023), bias
(Carrascosa et al., 2015; Kulshrestha et al., 2017), social trends (Allcott et al., 2018;
Noor et al., 2024), and a wide range of related domains. An extensive review of more
than 2,300 studies summarizes common pipelines and application domains. It identifies
open challenges related to temporal dynamics, causal inference, and the gap between
academic methods and commercial practice (Rodr´ıguez-Ib´anez et al., 2023). This re-
view further highlights core challenges, including noisy data quality, over-reliance on
traditional techniques (lexicons, tokens, Bayesian methods, bag-of-words), trade-offs
between scalability and interpretability, and limited support for end-to-end research
workflows.

2.1.1. OSN Collection
Beyond existing curated OSN datasets, several platforms and frameworks have been
developed to support OSN collection by automating parts of the data collection and la-
beling process. Toolkits such as Twarc1 facilitate large-scale data collection from X.com
(previously Twitter) APIs. In contrast, Pushshift2 has been widely used to archive
and retrieve Reddit data for research purposes. In addition, annotation pipelines and
workflow-based systems support downstream labeling and preprocessing of social me-
dia data (Bernstein et al., 2011). Although these tools exist, they often require tech-
nical expertise and have limited support for integrated analysis and human-centered
workflows.
2.1.2. OSN Analysis & Platform
In addition to the technical details of data collection, researchers commonly employ a
range of analytic techniques, including topic modeling, time series analysis, sentiment
analysis, network analysis, and clustering, to examine patterns in public opinion, be-
havior, and social interaction (Ghosh et al., 2025). Among these methods, sentiment
analysis remains one of the most widely used techniques. Surveys of aspect-based sen-
timent analysis highlight persistent difficulties in extracting meaningful aspects and
aligning them with their correct polarity in complex, noisy language (Nazir et al.,
2020). Studies on contextual sentiment analysis show that lexicon-based approaches
struggle when sentiment depends on genre and situational context (Muhammad et al.,
2016). Several platforms are currently available, such as the PennMAP3, GDELT4,
and MediaClound5, to investigate media transparency and accountability. However,
these platforms primarily support static data exploration and lack interactive capa-
bilities for human-in-the-loop analysis and real-time data collection. In contrast, our
approach enables interactive workflows and supports real-time data collection, allowing
for dynamic and scalable human–AI collaboration.
2.2. Human-AI Collaboration
Human–AI collaboration has become a central concern in Human-Computer Interac-
tion (HCI) research, particularly as AI systems become more prevalent in analytical
and creative domains. Prior work emphasizes explainability, interactivity, and com-
plementarity, in which people and AI systems have distinct roles in jointly producing
outcomes (Amershi et al., 2019; Bansal et al., 2020; Shneiderman, 2020). Building on
foundational work on mixed-initiative systems (Horvitz, 1999), recent research empha-
sizes designs in which both human and AI agents contribute actively to goal achieve-
ment as a promising approach for supporting human judgment while leveraging AI
capabilities (Amershi et al., 2019). Core design principles include supporting user con-
trol, enabling appropriate reliance on AI suggestions, promoting transparency and
accountability, and facilitating human-AI alignment on task-critical values.
Recent work has further examined how interaction design can improve trust and
understanding in AI-assisted decision-making. In data exploration and visualization,

2.2.1. Researcher Control and Trust Calibration
For research workflows, the central design question is not whether an AI system can
produce a label or summary, but how its output becomes accountable to the people in-
terpreting it. Human-AI interaction guidance emphasizes maintaining human control,
supporting efficient correction, and helping users understand when to rely on system
output (Amershi et al., 2019). Research on onboarding for human-AI collaboration
further emphasizes the need to communicate a system’s capabilities, limits, and the
user’s role in the workflow (Cai et al., 2019). Work on cognitive forcing functions shows
that interfaces can reduce over-reliance by requiring users to engage critically with AI
recommendations (Bu¸cinca et al., 2021). In data analytics tasks, agreement behavior
and self-reported trust can diverge across cases; consequently, designers should treat
trust as an evolving calibration problem rather than as a proxy for system quality
(Tutul et al., 2024).
We derive three requirements for AI-assisted social media research: researchers must
be able to articulate and revise their own relevance criteria; they must be able to in-
spect the relationship between AI output and the underlying posts; and they must be
able to treat AI suggestions as revisable inputs rather than final decisions. These re-
quirements guide the AI4OSN workflow and our evaluation. Although the platform can
link users to associated media, the present study evaluates a text-centered X/Twitter
workflow.
2.2.2. Perceptions of AI-Assisted Tools
Recent research increasingly examines how AI-generated outputs shape user trust,
confidence, and analytical behavior, motivating evaluations that extend beyond AI
model accuracy (Kalving et al., 2024). Users’ trust in AI systems is closely tied to
how outputs are presented, explained, and contextualized within workflows (Amershi
et al., 2019; Hoffman et al., 2018). Reliance on LLM-generated annotations intro-
duces new risks that can distort downstream analysis. Recent work demonstrates that
seemingly reasonable design choices, such as prompt formulation and model selection,
can substantially alter statistical conclusions, a phenomenon characterized as “LLM
hacking” (Baumann et al., 2025). These findings underscore the need for validation,
transparency, and human oversight in AI-assisted research workflows.
User perceptions and attitudes toward AI-assisted tools are shaped by factors such
as the perceived source of suggestions, semantic clarity, and explainability. For in-
stance, data science practitioners evaluate and trust AI-generated features differently
depending on whether outputs are framed as human-produced or AI-produced, with
transparency playing a key role in adoption and perceived usefulness (Zhu et al., 2024).
Research on explainable AI further shows that poorly aligned explanations can lead

to over-reliance or skepticism rather than calibrated trust (Kizilcec, 2016).
Recent qualitative evidence similarly shows that people interact with generative AI
differently across phases of a task, valuing it as a benchmark, booster, executor, or
amplifier while remaining concerned about superficial engagement (Sandhaus et al.,
2025). In social media research, these concerns are compounded by practical barri-
ers such as workflow fragmentation, data access complexity, and trust in automated
outputs (Wang et al., 2025). Rather than treating AI assistance as an independent
analytic authority, our study examines how a mixed-initiative workflow can support
researchers’ own criteria, verification practices, and responsibility for interpretation.
3. AI4OSN Platform Development
Design rationale. AI4OSN was designed to support researcher-controlled transi-
tions between three stages of social media inquiry: converting a research interest into
a query, mapping that query onto relevance judgments, and distilling a labeled collec-
tion into preliminary interpretations. The interface operationalizes the requirements
identified above by retaining user-authored queries and labels as the primary record
(R1), keeping source posts and configurable AI inputs available for inspection (R2),
and presenting AI labels and summaries as suggestions that users may review, revise,
or reject (R3). The following description distinguishes implemented capabilities from
proposed future extensions.

4. Usability Study
Following established practices for usability evaluation in HCI, we conducted a task-
based, mixed-method evaluation in which participants completed representative sys-
8


tem workflows while we collected complementary behavioral, subjective, and qualita-
tive measures (Bastien, 2010; Hornbæk, 2006; Landauer, 1988). This approach enables
usability to be examined through both participants’ interactions with the system and
their reported experiences, rather than relying on a single post-use measure (Horn-
bæk, 2006). Similar task-based and mixed-method protocols have also been employed
in recent evaluations of AI-assisted and sensemaking systems (Fan et al., 2022; Kar-
wankar et al., 2026; Rachatasumrit et al., 2021). The goal here was to characterize how
participants experienced the workflow, applied their own relevance criteria, and de-
scribed trust and verification when AI suggestions were introduced. The study received
approval from the Institutional Review Board of the affiliated university (Protocol
#2037012-3).
The overall study design comprised three modules (duration of approximately
45 minutes): (1) a pre-survey, (2) an interface interaction phase, and (3) a post-
survey followed by a semi-structured debrief interview. During the interface interaction
phase, participants completed a sequence of four tasks reflecting the core workflow of
AI4OSN: (1) project creation, (2) query configuration, (3) data review and relevance
labeling, and (4) AI-assisted analysis and interpretation. Each task was instrumented
with system logging, post-task questionnaires, and observational notes to capture both
performance and subjective experience. The study concluded with a standardized us-
ability assessment and a semi-structured interview to elicit reflective feedback on us-
ability, trust, and sensemaking.

4.1. Participants
We recruited 18 participants and compensated them $15 per hour. Consistent with HCI
usability research standards (Nielsen and Loranger, 2006), this sample size enables rich
qualitative analysis and identification of critical usability issues, which is appropriate
for mix-initiative system design and iterative improvement. This approach provides
essential foundational evidence prior to large-scale validation studies. The HCI litera-
ture demonstrates that N=18–20 is sufficient for identifying 85% of usability issues in
interactive systems (Turner et al., 2006; Virzi, 1992).
Demographic information is reported in Table 1. Participants were recruited through
multiple social media groups and were aged 18 or older, with no restriction on recruit-
ment to our university affiliates. The sample included a broad age range, with 72.2%
under 30. Gender representation was balanced (55.6% female, 44.4% male), and par-
ticipants reflected diverse racial and political backgrounds, with 44.4% opting not to
disclose political affiliation.
Educational attainment was high, with 44.4% holding a Master’s degree and 11.2%
holding doctoral or professional degrees. Expertise was concentrated in computing and
data-related fields (57.9%), with additional representation from social and behavioral
sciences (26.3%) and health and life sciences (15.8%). This sample offers a range
of valuable perspectives for a constructive evaluation, spanning both social media
researchers and individuals with varying levels of AI experience.
Digital informed consent for participation in the study has been obtained from
all participants prior to the study. After that, participants completed a pre-study
Qualtrics questionnaire7 about their research background, OSN familiarity, and prior
AI use. For descriptive analysis, we grouped participants by self-reported OSN-research
experience: the OSN-novice group (n = 9) had not previously conducted social media
research, whereas the OSN-experienced group (n = 9) had used OSN data in aca-
demic research. This grouping does not denote AI expertise; most participants (n=17)
reported frequent prior use of AI tools.
4.2. Tasks via AI4OSN Platform
After the pre-survey, participants explored AI4OSN in a mixed-methods, task-based
session. We assessed perceived usability, workload, and trust-related experiences, as
well as descriptive interaction patterns. Participants completed the following tasks in
sequence:
Task 1: Project Creation. Participants logged in to the platform and created a
new research project (as shown in Figures 1-2 in the Supplementary Materials). This
task evaluated the clarity of onboarding, navigability, and system feedback during
initial setup.
Task 2: Query Configuration. Participants configured a data collection query by
selecting multiple keywords and specifying a retrieval limit (as shown in Figures 2 and
3). This task assessed participants’ understanding of search parameters, constraints,
and interface affordances.
Task 3: Relevance Labeling. Participants reviewed retrieved social media posts
and manually labeled at least 25 posts as Relevant or Non-Relevant based on their
judgment, as shown in Figure 4. They were asked to articulate their labeling criteria
to support interpretive analysis. Subsequently, participants were asked to use the AI

Labeler function (Figure 5) to examine their initial choices. They were free to accept or
revise AI suggestions, with their decisions reflected in labeling behavior and agreement
metrics.
Task 4: Insight Generation. Participants engaged with AI-generated summaries
and analytic outputs (similar to Figure 6) and verbally described insights derived from
the results. This task elicited accounts of sensemaking support, perceived quality of
the outputs, and trust in AI assistance.
For analysis, we first analyzed task-level performance using descriptive statistics.
Task completion times were summarized using means and standard deviations, and
task success was assessed qualitatively based on successful completion of required
steps.
To characterize interaction after participants saw AI suggestions, we calculated the
percentage of initially AI-disagreed manual labels that participants changed (Shift)
and the percentage of final manual labels that still differed from the AI suggestion
(Gap). These measures capture calibration and revision behavior rather than establish-
ing expert-validated ground truth, consistent with the study’s focus on characterizing
participant engagement with AI-generated suggestions. Perceived cognitive workload
was measured after each task using the Raw NASA Task Load Index (Raw TLX)
(Hart, 2006). Participants rated six workload dimensions: mental demand, physical
demand, temporal demand, performance, effort, and frustration on a 7-point Likert
scale following each task. Performance ratings were reverse-coded prior to analysis.
We report task-level descriptive statistics to characterize workload patterns across
the workflow and examine how perceived workload changed between manual and AI-
assisted interaction stages.
4.3. Post-study Questionnaires
Following Task 4, participants completed an AI trust and sensemaking questionnaire
assessing their perceived correspondence between AI output and their own interpre-
tation, together with their comfort using the output in research contexts. Agreement
with AI-generated summaries was assessed using a three-item agreement questionnaire
rated on a 5-point Likert scale. We report item-level descriptive statistics and a com-
posite agreement score computed as the mean across items. Internal consistency was
evaluated using Cronbach’s α, and a one-sample t test compared the composite score
with the neutral midpoint. These measures represent participant perceptions rather
than independently verified output quality.
At the end of the study, participants completed the standard 10-item SUS (Bangor
et al., 2008) to evaluate the overall system, which was scored according to standard
procedures and summarized as means and standard deviations. Scores were interpreted
relative to established usability benchmarks to contextualize perceived usability. We
additionally examined participants’ responses to the SUS item measuring anticipated
future use to assess perceived adoption and acceptance of the system.
We also collected 5-point self-reported confidence ratings for data collection, label-
ing, and analysis before and after the session and report paired t-tests with Cohen’s dz.
These immediate self-reports should be interpreted as perceived confidence following
exposure to the workflow, not as evidence of durable learning or independently verified
skill improvement.

4.4. Debrief Interview
After the surveys and platform exploration, we conducted a semi-structured interview
of approximately 10–15 minutes. The guide asked participants about their relevance
criteria, their responses to AI labels and summaries, trust and verification, workflow
challenges, and desired future supports. Interviews were digitally recorded, transcribed
from Zoom audio8, manually checked for accuracy, and de-identified before analysis.

5. Results
We first discuss pre-survey results on participants’ perceptions of OSN and AI use. We
then examine participant interactions with the AI4OSN platform and assess cognitive
workload across tasks. SUS results for the overall system and participant perceptions
of AI-generated content are presented next. Finally, we analyze changes in confidence
for conducting OSN research using post-survey data and discuss additional interview
feedback through thematic analysis.
5.1. OSN Preference
As shown in Table 3, participants reported frequent engagement with a diverse set of
OSNs, with YouTube (72.2%), LinkedIn (61.1%), and Instagram (55.6%) being the
most regularly used platforms, reflecting everyday patterns of content consumption
and social interaction. In contrast, platforms commonly emphasized in OSN research,
such as X (Twitter) and Reddit, were used by smaller subsets of participants (27.8%
and 22.2%, respectively), suggesting that many participants interact with these plat-
forms primarily as casual users rather than as expert or power users.

with OSNs: 33.3% spent 30 minutes to 1 hour per day, 33.3% spent 1 to 2 hours per day,

engagement and indicate that participants were well-experienced with OSN platforms

5.2. AI Preference
In addition to OSN usage, the pre-study survey examined participants’ prior expe-
rience with AI-based tools, including usage frequency, types of tools used, and per-
ceptions of AI effectiveness and trust, as summarized in Table 4. Overall, participants
reported widespread and frequent use of AI tools, indicating that AI is already embed-
ded in their everyday work practices. A substantial majority of participants (72.2%)
reported using AI-based tools daily or near daily, while an additional 22.2% reported
weekly use. Only one participant (5.6%) reported rare use, and none reported occa-
sional or no prior use of AI tools. This high level of adoption suggests that participants
14


are generally experienced AI users rather than novices. As a result, this group is well-
suited to evaluating AI-assisted workflows, such as AI4OSN, in OSN research contexts.
Participants’ AI experience focused on chat-based text-generation tools. Nearly all
participants (94.4%) reported using systems such as ChatGPT, Claude, or Copilot for
drafting, summarizing, and answering questions. Fewer participants reported experi-
ence with more specialized tools, including data analysis or model-building systems
(44.4%), recommendation or decision-support tools (33.3%), and data labeling or tag-
ging tools (22.2%). Experience with image or video analysis tools was limited to 16.7%,
and only 5.6% reported using other AI tools. This gap highlights an opportunity for
AI4OSN, which focuses on structured data annotation and analysis beyond conver-
sational assistance.
Despite differences in tool familiarity, participants consistently perceived AI as ben-
eficial to their work. Half of the participants reported improvements in workflow ef-
ficiency, while 16.7% described the impact as transformative. The remaining partici-
pants reported moderate or slight improvements, and none reported no benefit. These
responses indicate that participants view AI as an integral component of their work
rather than a supplementary aid. Most participants reported always or often trusting
AI-generated outputs (77.8%), while the remaining participants reported sometimes
trusting them (22.2%). Consistent with this cautious stance, most participants (83.3%)
reported frequently verifying or revising AI outputs. One participant reported never
verifying AI-generated results. This pattern reflects active human oversight rather than
passive reliance.
Additionally, participants expressed strong expectations for transparency and ex-
plainability. Nearly all participants (94.4%) agreed that AI-based analysis tools should
explain how conclusions are produced. This finding reinforces the need for human-
centered designs that emphasize transparency, accountability, and user control.

5.4. Cognitive Workload (NASA-TLX)
We assessed perceived cognitive workload after each task using the Raw NASA Task
Load Index (Raw TLX). Participants rated six workload dimensions, mental demand,
physical demand, temporal demand, performance, effort, and frustration, on a 7-point
Likert scale following each task. Performance ratings were reverse-coded prior to anal-
ysis. Figure 8 summarizes task-wise workload distributions, and we report mean values
to highlight trends across tasks.
Task 1: Project Creation. The first task was associated with a low perceived
workload across all dimensions. Mean mental demand was low (M = 2.06), with sim-
ilarly low physical demand (M = 1.44) and temporal demand (M = 2.00). Effort
(M = 1.50) and frustration (M = 1.61) remained minimal, indicating that partici-
pants found project setup straightforward and non-taxing. Reverse-coded performance
scores were high (M = 5.56), suggesting that participants felt successful in completing
this task. Overall, Task 1 imposed minimal cognitive burden and served as a gentle
onboarding step.
Task 2: Query Configuration. Mean mental demand rose to M = 3.06, reflecting
the cognitive effort required to translate research goals into query parameters. Effort
also increased (M = 2.56), while temporal demand remained moderate (M = 2.22),
indicating that participants did not feel rushed. Frustration remained relatively low
(M = 2.06). Performance ratings remained high (M = 5.78), suggesting that, despite
increased cognitive effort, participants remained confident in their task outcomes.
18


Task 3: Relevance Labeling. The third task had the highest workload across
dimensions, consistent with prior work showing that manual annotation is often the
most challenging stage of the OSN research workflow (Wang et al., 2025). Mean mental
demand peaked at M = 3.83, and effort increased substantially (M = 3.39), reflecting
the sustained attention and judgment required for manual labeling. Physical demand
also increased relative to earlier tasks (M = 2.44), while temporal demand remained
moderate (M = 2.28). Frustration increased modestly (M = 2.17), suggesting that
some participants experienced difficulty during prolonged review. Despite this, per-
formance ratings remained favorable (M = 5.06), suggesting that participants felt
capable but cognitively taxed during this stage.
Task 4: Insight Generation.
Task 4 had lower reported mental demand ($M=2.00$) and effort ($M=1.94$) com-
pared to the preceding manual-labeling task; temporal demand was $M=1.39$, frus-
tration was $M=1.61$, and performance was $M=6.17$. Our sequential, integrated
task design (Hornbæk, 2006) reflects real-world research workflows where data col-
lection, annotation, and analysis occur naturally in succession. While task order and
goal differences prevent the isolation of AI’s individual contribution, this design en-
ables assessment of the complete workflow experience, which is our primary research
goal. Participants reported substantial decreases in mental effort and frustration when
transitioning from manual annotation to AI-assisted insight generation, suggesting
that AI assistance effectively reduced cognitive burden during interpretation while
maintaining workflow coherence.

5.7. Pre-post Confidence Gains
We examined changes in participants’ self-reported confidence before and after using
AI4OSN across three skills: collecting, labeling and annotating, and analyzing data.
Confidence was measured on a 5-point Likert scale. Paired t-tests were used to as-
sess within-participant changes, with Cohen’s dz reported as effect size. Normality of
difference scores (Shapiro–Wilk) was satisfied for most skills, with minor deviations
expected given small sample sizes. Across all participants (N = 18), confidence in-
creased significantly for all skills (Figure 11c). Confidence in data collection increased
from M = 2.72 to M = 4.06 (+1.33; t = 4.97, p < .001, dz = 1.17), labeling from
M = 2.50 to M = 3.67 (+1.17; t = 3.21, p = .005, dz = 0.76), and analysis from
M = 2.89 to M = 3.83 (+0.94; t = 2.72, p = .015, dz = 0.64), indicating substantial
gains across the workflow. We further examined differences in each group as shown
in Figure 11a and 11b. Both groups showed consistent pre- and post-improvements
across all skills. For experienced participants, gains were significant for data collection
(p = 0.016, dz = 1.02) and moderate for analysis (dz = 0.58), but minimal for labeling
(p = 0.594, dz = 0.18). In contrast, novice participants exhibited larger and more con-
sistent improvements, with significant gains in data collection (p = 0.005, dz = 1.28)
and labeling (p = 0.001, dz = 1.66), and moderate gains in analysis (p = 0.069,
dz = 0.70). This suggests that the system is especially effective in supporting users
with limited prior experience.

Through systematic thematic analysis, we discuss each theme in detail, as shown in

5.8.1. Labeling Criteria
Participants described relevance labeling as a multi-stage judgment process rather
than a simple keyword matching task. Novice participants (P1, P7, P14, P15) and ex-
perienced participants (P2, P3, P8, P12) all reported that they first checked whether
posts explicitly contained the target term or a closely related phrase. As P7 explained,
“keyword detection is definitely the first thing.” This suggests that the lexical match-
ing function served as an entry point for topic assessment across users, regardless of
experience.
However, participants consistently emphasized that keyword presence alone was in-
sufficient for final decisions. Among experienced participants, this deeper assessment
was often described as a more structured, domain-oriented process. P2 looked for “a bit
of context related to the keyword” while P6, P11, P12, and P13 described using broader
thematic alignment, additional conceptual markers, discourse authenticity, and indi-
rect topical references to judge relevance. These accounts suggest that experienced
participants tended to treat labeling as an interpretive task that required connecting
individual posts to wider semantic or thematic patterns. Similarly, novice participants
also relied on contextual reasoning, though their criteria were more often framed in
terms of intuitive, personal, or example-based judgments. P5, P7, P9, P15, and P16
described assessing whether posts reflected the intended topic meaning, excluding jokes
or overly opinionated content, distinguishing between close topical variants, and con-
sidering whether the content would meaningfully contribute to the discussion. This
indicates that they were more likely to ground their decisions in personal familiar-
ity or immediate impressions of relevance rather than explicitly articulated analytical
rules.
Interestingly, P5 and P7 incorporated supplementary cues when textual evidence
was limited. They manually check URLs, images, or linked content from social media
posts for additional context, while P4 relied on prior familiarity with a niche product
domain and stated they were “very confident in assessing whether the tweets were
related or not.” These responses suggest that participants sometimes extended the
unit of analysis beyond the post text itself when determining relevance was difficult.
Despite these strategies, ambiguity persisted as a key part of the task. P3 noted
uncertainty when posts were only loosely connected to their selected topic, and P4
reported low confidence in AI-generated labels for a niche, emerging topic. Overall,
the findings show that participants across both groups combined keyword screening
with contextual interpretation and occasional use of external cues, but differed in
how they operationalized these strategies. Experienced participants generally applied
more structured and domain-driven criteria, whereas novice participants relied more
on intuition, personal relevance, and surface-level signals.
5.8.2. Platform Strengths
Participants described the strengths of AI4OSN as effective AI support, a clear and
navigable workflow, and low barriers to use. The AI-generated summary emerged as
the most consistently valued feature. 77.8% (n = 7) of experienced participants char-
acterized the summaries as clear, comprehensive, and well aligned with their own
interpretation of the data. For example, P3 described the summary as “super com-
plete. . . and well structured,” while P6 noted that it was “very easy to understand.”
P2 further suggested that the summary offered a more holistic account than man-
ual summarization. Novice participants expressed similar views, with P1, P5, P9, and
P15 emphasizing that the summaries helped them quickly grasp narrative and senti-
22


ment patterns. These responses suggest that the summary function supported both
interpretability and sensemaking across different levels of expertise.
Participants also viewed AI-assisted labeling as a major strength, though its per-
ceived value differed somewhat by experience level. Experienced participants P6, P8,
and P10 emphasized efficiency and alignment, describing the feature as helpful for
accelerating decisions and reducing effort. P6, for instance, noted that the AI labeling
was “literally very easy and fast,” and that the AI-labeled posts closely matched their
own judgments. Novice participants, such as P11 and P15, valued this feature more
as a reflective aid, particularly on the revision page, where they could compare their
own labels with AI outputs side by side. This pattern suggests that experienced users
tended to see AI labeling as a time-saving analytical support. In contrast, novice users
also used it to calibrate or reconsider their decisions in uncertain cases.
A third recurring strength was the platform’s interface and workflow, which many
participants linked to the system’s overall usability. Most participants (61%) provided
explicit positive feedback on the interface, highlighting the clarity of the layout, visible
navigation, and step-by-step task progression. P1 described the workflow as “very
straightforward” and “sequential in nature,” while P9 appreciated features such as
sorting posts by recency or engagement. These responses suggest that participants did
not view usability as separate from AI support, but rather as a condition that made
the platform’s AI features easier to interpret and apply.
Finally, accessibility and ease of use were especially salient for participants with less
technical confidence. P1 described the platform as “quite intuitive and very simple to
follow,” P16 emphasized that it was accessible regardless of technical background,
and P8 noted that the instructions were very clear. Overall, the findings indicate
that participants perceived AI4OSN as strong not only because it provided useful
AI assistance, but also because that assistance was embedded in a transparent, ac-
cessible, and easy-to-navigate workflow. While both groups converged on the value of
summaries and interface clarity, experienced participants more often emphasized AI la-
beling alignment and efficiency. In contrast, novice participants more often highlighted
accessibility and support for sensemaking.
5.8.3. Platform Limitations
AI label reliability was a major discussion across both groups. Experienced participants
P8, P10, and P12 emphasized instability, non-determinism, and incorrect relevance
judgments, with P10 noting that LLM outputs can vary even at low temperature
settings. Novice participants P9, P13, and P17 similarly reported disagreements with
AI labels, with P13 stating that the output “seemed way off what I was expecting.” and
indicated they would rely entirely on their own judgment. These responses indicate
that confidence in AI assistance depended not only on perceived accuracy but also on
consistency and predictability.
Participants also pointed to gaps in effort and guidance across the broader workflow.
P6 and P9 described manual labeling as burdensome when reviewing posts one by one.
This feedback highlights the potential value of AI-assisted data curation. Prompt writ-
ing was especially difficult for P5 and P9, while P2 requested more example prompts,
and both P2 and P7 wanted clearer explanations of differences across GPT models.
Overall, these findings collectively point to opportunities for improvement in interface
clarity, AI label reliability, and prompt-writing guidance.
23


5.8.4. AI Trust Level
Participants reported varied levels of trust in AI-generated outputs. Overall, one-third
of participants (P2, P3, P6, P8, P14, P15) expressed high trust, describing the AI
analysis or summaries as accurate and helpful for completing tasks; for example, one
participant stated, “I’m comfortable using AI labeling because it will help us complete
the labeling task, no need to do it manually.” (P8). Seven participants (38.9%) re-
ported moderate trust, indicating they were willing to use AI outputs as references
but preferred to verify results before fully relying on them, as P11 explained: they
were “very willing to take the result as a reference” but would not use it directly.
In contrast, a smaller portion of participants (16.7%, n = 3) expressed low trust, of-
ten raising concerns about mislabeled data or uncertainty about the training data,
with one participant noting that they were “not believing them” and would still label
posts manually (P7). In contrast, one participant (P4) reported no trust in the AI,
while another (P2) expressed uncertainty about using AI outputs in professional or re-
search contexts. Overall, participants tended to calibrate their trust, using AI support
cautiously alongside human judgment.
5.8.5. Factors Influencing Trust in AI
Participants identified several factors that shaped their trust in AI-generated outputs,
emphasizing that trust develops through transparency, accuracy, experience, and ex-
ternal validation rather than being assumed by default. All experienced participants
explicitly discussed accuracy, reliability, and familiarity with the workflow. They noted
that consistent, correct outputs increased confidence over time (P4, P6), and trust was
reinforced when AI tools aligned with prior experience or established professional prac-
tices (P6, P12). Several experienced users also highlighted the value of quantitative
indicators, references, or reasoning in summaries for better understanding of AI out-
puts (P3, P10, P11, P18). 88.9% (n=8) of novice participants similarly emphasized the
importance of transparency and explanations. They expressed a need for AI labels and
summaries to provide reasoning, supporting resources, or context to build confidence
in the outputs (P1, P7, P15, P17). Some highlighted additional factors, such as inclu-
sive and representative data (P16) and verification by experts or trusted individuals
(P4, P9). Social influence also played a role, with trust increasing when recommen-
dations came from trusted individuals (P4). Overall, participants described trust as
developing through transparency, accuracy, experience, and external validation rather
than being assumed by default.
5.8.6. OSN Related Concerns
Participants reported several concerns about AI-assisted social media analysis tools,
with privacy and confidentiality emerging as the most common issues. Two-thirds
of participants (66.7%, n=12) expressed concern that social media data, even when
publicly available, could still expose personal beliefs, identities, or other sensitive at-
tributes. As P1 noted, this “could have harm on people if they’re identified.” Similar
concerns were raised by P2 and P14, who questioned how AI systems access, learn
from, and potentially reuse online data without clear visibility into downstream use.
While both novice and experienced participants raised privacy concerns, experienced
participants were more likely to situate them alongside broader issues such as data
representativeness and system security.
Consistent with prior work (Anzum et al., 2022; Wang and Singh, 2023), concerns
24


about output quality were also common, with 44.4% of participants (n=8) raising
them. Participants worried that biased training data, skewed post weighting, and the
overrepresentation of bot-generated or repetitive content could distort analytical out-
comes (P9, P10, P13). Some also questioned whether AI-generated summaries and
labels could present inaccurate or misleading interpretations without enough trans-
parency or explanation (P7, P15). Novice participants often framed these concerns in
terms of fairness and misrepresentation, whereas experienced participants more often
pointed to underlying technical sources of error and instability.
A smaller group of participants focused more specifically on system dependabil-
ity in practice. P12 and P18 emphasized that inconsistent outputs could undermine
trust in real analytic settings. At the same time, a few experienced participants (P3,
P6) reported no major concerns. Overall, these findings suggest that privacy concerns
were broadly shared, while experienced users tended to articulate more specific tech-
nical risks and novice users more often expressed broader apprehension about fairness,
misuse, and loss of control.
5.8.7. General Feedback
Participants expressed broadly positive perceptions of the system and interest in its
future use. Most participants (77.8%, n=14) described the tool as helpful, accessible,
and effective for tasks such as accessing, filtering, and labeling social media data (P3,
P5, P10, P13, P18). A recurring strength was the integration of multiple research steps
into a single workflow, which participants described as intuitive, efficient, and in some
cases “easy to trust,” especially when transparency features such as source links were
available (P6, P18).
Positive views appeared across experience levels, though with different emphasis.
Experienced participants highlighted reliability, efficiency, and professional utility, of-
ten framing the system as a way to reduce technical barriers and support broader anal-
ysis. Novice participants also found the system approachable, but more often suggested
the need for clearer onboarding, step-by-step guidance, and downloadable reports for
offline review (P2, P7, P17).
Interest in future development was also clear. One third of participants (n=6) ex-
plicitly suggested extensions such as collaborative interpretation support, broader data
sources beyond X/Twitter, and applications in domains like job searching or profes-
sional networking (P6, P7). At the same time, some experienced AI users expressed
a balanced view, recognizing both the system’s usefulness and the persistent limits
of AI, as reflected in P7’s comment that “the concern will never be as low as 0%.”
Overall, the findings suggest strong acceptance of the system, alongside expectations
for greater guidance, transparency, and extensibility
6. Discussion
This mixed-method study examines how participants used a researcher-controlled AI-
assisted workflow across query construction, data collection, relevance labeling, and
early-stage sensemaking. We synthesize the findings as evidence about workflow expe-
rience, calibration of AI suggestions, and the support participants considered necessary
for responsible use.
25


6.1. Experiencing a researcher-controlled workflow
As reflected in the SUS score (Section 5.5) and NASA-TLX rating (Section 5.4),
AI4OSN demonstrates strong overall usability, and aligns with participants’ reported
likelihood of future use (M = 5.55), indicating both ease of learning and perceived
value for recurring research tasks. A notable outcome of the study is the magnitude
and consistency of pre-post confidence gains across the OSN workflow. Participants’
confidence increased significantly for data collection (the largest gain), labeling, and
analysis, particularly among the novice group, which had little prior exposure to OSN
research, as shown in Section 5.7. These improvements suggest that AI4OSN may
function not only as an automation tool but also as a skill-building scaffold, helping
users learn how to operationalize research goals into queries, structure annotation crite-
ria, and interpret results. This matters for non-technical, non-experienced researchers,
who may be capable of substantive interpretation but face barriers to data access and
workflow orchestration. These findings further validate the insights from our initial
study Wang et al. (2025). Additionally, our results suggest that supporting confidence
should not mean increasing automation to the point of disengagement. Participants
changed only a small portion of labels after AI suggestions, indicating that they re-
mained actively involved and retained interpretive control. In OSN research, where
meaning depends on context, norms, and domain expertise, a human-in-the-loop ap-
proach such as our AI4OSN may be more appropriate than optimizing solely for max-
imum agreement with AI.
6.2. Calibrating AI suggestions against researcher judgment
Our results highlight a nuanced form of human-AI collaboration in which AI serves
as an advisory partner rather than an authoritative annotator. Quantitatively, par-
ticipants changed relatively few of their initial labeling decisions after seeing AI sug-
gestions (mean shift = 16.15%; see Table 5, 6), yet the final mismatch rate between
manual and AI labels remained high (mean gap = 51.63%). This combination is re-
vealing: participants engaged with AI suggestions but did not substantially defer to
them. Rather than interpreting this gap as a failure of AI labeling, our qualitative
data suggest it reflects deliberate calibration: participants most often reported moder-
ate trust, using AI outputs as a reference point while maintaining ownership of labels
and verifying discrepancies. This aligns with the pre-study findings that participants
commonly verify AI outputs and strongly prefer explainable AI tools. Participants also
reported that AI assistance was beneficial and suggested that AI annotations would
be more useful if they included additional context, such as explanations of how the
labels were generated, consistent with work showing that appropriate reliance requires
support for verification rather than generic transparency (Bu¸cinca et al., 2021; Tutul
et al., 2024). Preliminary interview feedback indicates that many participants found
the AI-generated summary helpful for quickly understanding data trends and senti-
ment without any programming or specialized software skills. The post-task agreement
scale further supports this finding. Participants’ composite agreement with the AI-
generated analysis was significantly above neutral and showed high internal reliability.
Therefore, even though AI labeling did not closely match human judgments, partic-
ipants still perceived AI outputs as valuable for sensemaking and triage. We found
OSN relevance labeling is inherently interpretive and context-dependent. Participants
described moving from keyword matching to deeper semantic interpretation, filtering
jokes or opinionated content, and sometimes consulting linked resources. These prac-
26


tices likely lead to divergence from an LLM’s default interpretation, especially when
prompts are brief or underspecified. Indeed, most participants relied on a simple tem-
plate prompt and did not iterate extensively, which may have limited the model’s abil-
ity to learn nuanced criteria. Several participants explicitly requested clearer prompt
guidance, and many expressed concerns about inconsistent AI labeling.
Participants’ prompting behaviors further reveal how researchers engage with AI
assistance in structured research tasks. Most participants used the provided template
prompt with minimal changes, while fewer introduced system messages or iterated on
their outputs multiple times to refine them. When participants adjusted settings (e.g.,
choosing a stronger model or setting the temperature near zero), they did so with the
explicit goal of improving consistency. These behaviors suggest that researchers are
developing an emergent prompting literacy that prioritizes stability and reproducibil-
ity, values aligned with research practice. However, participants reported difficulty
writing effective prompts. They requested more guidance, highlighting the need for
interface-level support for prompt development rather than assuming users already
possess prompt engineering skills, particularly in a research context.
6.3. Supports for responsible reliance
Participants’ responses suggest that they view LLM-based assistance as promising for
reducing overhead and supporting interpretation, but only when paired with trans-
parency and safeguards. Participants actively downloaded and reviewed the dataset
to spot-check AI annotations against their own decisions, reinforcing careful human-in-
the-loop validation. They expressed moderate-to-positive agreement with AI-generated
analysis and praised the summaries as well-structured and useful for understanding
trends. Participants treated AI outputs as reference signals, emphasized verification,
and requested explanations, references, and indicators of how the AI arrived at con-
clusions. AI4OSN enables users to engage more deeply with OSN data and think
critically by revising search queries, adjusting prompts, and refining their analyses.
This demonstrates that the platform not only supports efficient data exploration but
also actively fosters reflective, human-in-the-loop research practices. This points to
an important tension in AI-assisted OSN research: LLMs can accelerate analysis, but
they can also amplify risks that are already salient in OSN contexts, including privacy
concerns, representational harms, and biased or noisy data sources. Indeed, privacy
concerns were prevalent in interviews, as were concerns about bias, hallucination, and
overconfident system behavior. For OSN research tools, these concerns are not periph-
eral; they influence whether researchers feel comfortable using AI-generated outputs
in real studies. Experienced and novice participants differed in how they calibrated
trust in LLM-based systems. Experienced users were more likely to rely on prior ex-
perience. They demonstrated greater accuracy in integrating AI outputs into their
workflow, whereas novice users relied more on transparency, explanations, and ex-
ternal validation to build confidence. This suggests that effective OSN research tools
should support both groups by combining reliable performance for experienced users
with interpretable and explainable outputs for novices.
6.4. Design Recommendations for AI-Assisted OSN Research Tools
Collectively, our results suggest that AI4OSN demonstrates strong usability and adop-
tion potential for end-to-end OSN research (RQ1). The system supports downstream
27


analysis by accelerating sensemaking and offering advisory signals rather than replac-
ing human judgment (RQ2). Our findings also indicate that researchers are more likely
to embrace LLM-based assistance when systems foreground transparency, reliability,
and responsible handling of OSN data (RQ3). These insights motivate mixed initia-
tive designs that reduce cognitive burden during interpretation while preserving user
agency.
6.4.1. Research-oriented prompt support, not just prompt templates.
Instead of treating prompts as an open-ended text box, systems can embed prompting
supports that align with research goals: reusable prompt templates linked to projects,
versioning to track changes, short “prompt diagnostics” (e.g., detecting underspec-
ification), and small calibration sets (e.g., showing a few representative posts and
asking the user to confirm criteria). Such features could improve AI label alignment
while maintaining transparency and reproducibility. From the user’s perspective, sys-
tems should provide built-in quality feedback for prompt formulation and reduce the
expectation that researchers become prompt engineers. This design direction aligns
with recent literature showing that generative AI tools shift work from production to
evaluation (Simkute et al., 2024), making task allocation, continuous feedback, and
interpretability central design concerns.
6.4.2. Transparent and responsible AI assistance.
To support responsible adoption, AI4OSN-like systems should incorporate trans-
parency mechanisms tailored to research workflows: (1) clear provenance, including
links to underlying posts and the subset of data informing each summary; (2) uncer-
tainty communication, such as confidence cues and “insufficient evidence” flags; (3)
bias-aware summaries, including indicators of duplicated content, bot-like patterns, or
skewed sources; (4) privacy-aware defaults, such as redaction options and reminders
for ethical handling of identifiers; and (5) access to raw content, such as original data
and AI-generated annotation labels. These features directly address the factors par-
ticipants identified as critical for trust, including the need for explanations, consistent
reliability, and opportunities for external validation.
6.5. Limitations and Future Work
Our usability study was conducted at a scale appropriate for early-stage evaluation,
though future work could benefit from engaging a broader and more diverse partic-
ipant population. The AI components build on existing LLMs whose behavior may
evolve, suggesting opportunities to examine how different model families influence
system performance and evaluation outcomes. Our current evaluation focuses on task
performance on the X platform and user perceptions, which provides a useful foun-
dation for understanding usability and workflow integration. Building on this, future
work can extend evaluation to longer-term deployments to better understand how
trust, reliance, and expertise develop over time. Additional directions include exploring
adaptive agents that respond to user expertise and task context, as well as advancing
governance and transparency mechanisms, such as auditing tools and explainability
features, to support more informed and accountable AI-assisted research practices.

7. Conclusion
We present AI4OSN, a human-centered, multi-agent platform that enables accessible
social media and web research. The system integrates LLMs into data collection, anno-
tation, and analysis workflows while preserving human judgment. An iterative usabil-
ity study demonstrates that AI4OSN improves workflow clarity and reduces cognitive
overhead for researchers working with complex and noisy social media data. Results
indicate that users learn from the dataset through direct interaction with the plat-
form, engaging in iterative refinement of queries, prompts, and analytical strategies.
AI assistance supports this process by enabling rapid sensemaking and preliminary
analysis without requiring programming or specialized tools. This pattern reflects the
value of mixed-initiative design, in which AI augments exploration and understanding
rather than replacing human interpretation. Participants reported significant increases
in confidence across all tasks and benefited from AI-assisted analysis that remained
transparent and inspectable. The observed interaction patterns point to design op-
portunities for agentic AI systems that support user agency, critical reasoning, and
responsible research practices. AI4OSN serves as a practical entry point for curating
high-quality datasets and generating early insights for downstream analysis. We plan
to publicly release the platform framework to support future research.
