Structure-Aware Transformers for Hierarchical
Authorization in Personal AI Agents
Dongyu Cui∗
Jie Ding†
Xuan Bi‡
Abstract
Personal AI agents can carry out complex, multi-step tasks on users’ behalf, but doing so often
involves numerous permission requests. Manually reviewing each request is time-consuming,
while indiscriminate approval poses privacy and security risks. Predicting users’ authoriza-
tion preferences offers a way to reduce this burden while preserving user control. However,
authorization prediction is challenging because permission requests are often hierarchical and
observed user decisions are sparse and potentially correlated. To address these challenges, We
propose the Parent-gated Authorization Transformer for Hierarchies (PATH) to predict users’
authorization preferences for personal AI agents. PATH is a novel neural network architecture
that combines hierarchy-aware attention masks with a parent-gated probability formulation to
ensure hierarchy-consistent predictions. Hierarchy-level embeddings further support learning
from sparse observations and patterns shared within each authorization level. We establish
finite-sample risk bounds and provide sufficient conditions for consistent authorization proba-
bility estimation and algorithmic convergence. Experiments on simulated data, AI-generated
user profiles, and real user authorization data demonstrate strong predictive performance with
zero hierarchy violations.

Introduction
The rise of agentic AI systems enables personal AI agents to manage users’ everyday digital tasks
in an increasingly autonomous manner. AI products are often able to accomplish a wide range of
workflows on behalf of users through self-directed behavior.
The high degree of delegation and growing reliance on AI agents in human activities also
introduce substantial privacy, security, and safety concerns. For example, there have been reports
of AI agents deleting important user emails or making unauthorized purchases without appropriately
informing the user (Martindale 2026, McHugh 2026). In another example, AI coding agents with
terminal or repository access have executed unintended commands (Udinmwen 2025, Liu et al.
2025, Li 2026), modified critical files (Liu 2026), or exposed sensitive credentials due to overly
broad permissions or prompt injection attacks (Mehta 2026). These incidents highlight the risks
associated with granting AI agents extensive autonomy.
On the other hand, overly restrictive
permissions can require users to approve most AI actions manually, which could increase user
burden and reduce operational efficiency. Therefore, it is crucial to strike a balance where AI agents
are granted the appropriate level of authorization to accomplish desired tasks, while minimizing
the potential risks for each user.
Meanwhile, possible AI actions are often structurally dependent, making it hard for statistical
formulation and inefficient to grant authorizations independently. Here the dependencies among AI
actions may constitute a hierarchy, or a directed acyclic graph (DAG) in general, where authorizing
an action may require parent authorizations. Figure 1 provides an illustrative example, where a
node denotes a possible AI action, and an edge denotes a structural dependency. For instance,
authorizing an AI agent to “Place Order” may require authorizations for both “Add to Cart” and
“Access Credit Card Info.” In other words, granting permission for a child action infers permission
for their “parent” actions.
Several existing works have attempted to facilitate user authorization. For example, Progent
(Shi et al. 2025) provides a tool to restrict the behavior of agents when they want to perform some
potentially risky operations. Similarly, AgentSpec (Wang et al. 2026) enables users to specify their
2


safety requirements and constrains agent behavior within these user-defined boundaries. However,
most of these solutions are rule-based, where the same authorization criteria are applied to all
users.
In fact, users may differ substantially in the actions they are willing to delegate to AI
agents. For example, one user may allow an agent to complete purchases within a predefined
budget, while another may require explicit approval for every transaction. Such differences make
personalization essential for predicting authorization decisions and determining appropriate levels
of agent autonomy.
In this paper, instead of setting user-agnostic rules, we propose to learn user authorization
preferences through data and achieve personalized user authorization prediction for agentic AI
systems. Specifically, we intend to develop a novel structured prediction model that uses extracted
user features to predict authorization preferences. We envision that, based on such a model, each
user can be presented with a personalized batch of actions predicted to align with their authoriza-
tion preferences, which enables users to approve multiple actions collectively and streamline the
authorization process.

Two lines of research are related to our work. One is hierarchical multi-label classification,
where labels are organized according to a pre-specified tree or DAG structure (Rousu et al. 2006,
Wang et al. 2009, Bi & Kwok 2015). For example, Chow & Liu (1968) proposed a method to
approximate the probability of each node in a tree, Lafferty et al. (2001) proposed conditional
random fields, and Taskar et al. (2003) proposed Markov networks. These methods aim to model
the dependencies among labels using a structured probabilistic framework. There are also methods
that train independent classifiers for one or several nodes, and then predict the tree structure by
combining the classifiers (Barbedo & Lopes 2006, Fagni & Sebastiani 2007, Qiu et al. 2009, Silla Jr
& Freitas 2011, Read et al. 2021). Nevertheless, these methods usually require post-processing
steps to enforce hierarchical consistency. In the meantime, many of the traditional hierarchical
multi-label classification methods do not jointly model all nodes, which may not be able to capture
dependencies and result in suboptimal performance.
The second line of research focuses on the development of deep learning architectures for
structured prediction tasks, which has drawn a growing interest in recent years (Peng et al. 2021).
For example, Tai et al. (2015) proposed tree-structured long-short-term memory networks and
Socher et al. (2013) proposed recursive neural networks for sentiment treebanks. These methods
could capture the structural dependencies among nodes in a tree or graph. Furthermore, Wehrmann
et al. (2018) proposed neural architectures HMCN-F and HMCN-R that combine both local and
global information to predict hierarchical labels, and a new penalty function is designed to enforce
the hierarchical consistency. Giunchiglia & Lukasiewicz (2020) proposed C-HMCNN, which adds
a constraint layer on top of the neural network to ensure that the predictions satisfy the hierarchical
constraints.
Despite these progress, none of the existing prediction models is designed for personalized
user authorization in agentic AI systems. Therefore, several issues remain unresolved. First, in an
agentic AI system, users usually do not have the bandwidth to make decisions for every AI action.
For example, it is unrealistic for a user to specify access permissions for every file on a device.
As a result, the authorization decisions collected in practice are usually sparse, indicating only the
4


subset of actions that users have previously encountered and responded to. This poses a challenge
for learning and inference, as a prediction model must infer authorization decisions based on sparse
observations while still respecting the underlying hierarchical structure (Liu et al. 2022). To the
best of our knowledge, most of the existing methods typically assume that the complete hierarchical
outcomes are fully observed during training, which is often not the case in agentic AI systems.
Second, some methods estimate node-level marginal probabilities independently, which can
produce structural violations and require post-processing to enforce hierarchical consistency. A
structural violation occurs when a child node is predicted to be authorized while at least one of
its parents is predicted to be unauthorized. Resolving violations after prediction also introduces
ambiguity: it may be unclear whether to authorize the child’s unauthorized ancestors or revoke
authorization for the child. Third, existing methods may struggle to capture complex dependencies,
including correlated nodes within the same hierarchy.
To address these issues, we propose a novel neural network architecture called PATH (Parent-
gated Authorization Transformer for Hierarchies). Specifically, instead of estimating marginal
probabilities and enforcing consistency afterward, PATH adopts a parent-gated probability formu-
lation and directly models conditional probabilities to take into account the chain-rule nature of
the hierarchical structure. To estimate these conditional probabilities, PATH further incorporates
the hierarchical structure into a masking mechanism of self-attention. As a result, the proposed
design ensures hierarchical consistency, and hence avoids the need for post-processing or additional
penalty terms. We further introduce depth-level embeddings. By sharing information across these
nodes, this design simultaneously addresses the sparsity caused by partially observed authorization
decisions and captures shared patterns among nodes at the same hierarchical depth. We also derive
the theoretical results and conduct experiments, both of which confirm the effectiveness of our
method.
The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the problem formulation
and our proposed method. Section 3 establishes its theoretical guarantees. Section 4 evaluates its
performance against competing methods using simulated data, AI-generated user profiles, and real
5


user authorization data. Finally, Section 6 concludes the article.

all of its parent permissions.
Based on the above setup, the problem of predicting authorization can be formulated as learning
a function g : X →Y that maps observed features to a valid DAG-structured output while satisfying
the hierarchical consistency constraint. Under this setting, we also seek to optimize function g that
achieves high prediction performance while being able to handle incomplete observations effectively
during training. In this paper, we only focus on the setting where the DAG structure G is known
and fixed, as it represents the authorization rules of the underlying agentic AI system. However,
we allow each Yi to be partially observed.

Theoretical Analysis
In this section, we develop a theoretical analysis of the proposed PATH for learning hierarchical
authorization probabilities from partially observed labels. We first establish finite-sample bounds
for BCE risk and probability estimation error. The bounds separate model approximation, statistical
estimation, optimization error, and selection bias, which allows us to construct explicit conditions
for probability and risk consistency. The proof is provided in Appendix.

Simulation Studies
We conduct three simulation studies to evaluate the performance of our method and compare it with
other competing methods in different scenario. The first study considers conditionally independent
node-level mechanisms, providing a baseline setting for evaluation. The second study introduces
additional correlations among authorization decisions. The third study uses AI-generated synthetic
user profiles and their responses, providing a more realistic setting.
Each dataset consists of user features and corresponding hierarchical authorization labels fol-
lowing a fixed authorization structure, with incomplete observations of structured outputs. Across
all three studies, we evaluate both predictive accuracy and adherence to hierarchical constraints.
We compare PATH with four baselines. Notice that, all baselines were designed only for complete
structured outputs, while details of model parameters and data structure are provided in Section C
of Supplementary Material:
• Independent Classifier (IC; Cox 1958, Boutell et al. 2004): An independent classifier is
conducted at each node that ignores the hierarchical structure, treating each node as an
independent binary classification problem. We use logistic regression as the independent
classifier.
18


• Chain Classifier (CC; Zhang & Zhang 2010, Sucar et al. 2014): A classifier for each node
using only the samples for which all parent nodes are authorized. That is, the model estimates
the conditional authorization probability of node j given that all of its parent permissions are
authorized. For prediction, it follows a top-down strategy, where the root node is predicted
first, followed by its child nodes using the conditional probability recursively. We use logistic
regression as the classifier.
• HMCN-F (Wehrmann et al. 2018): A neural network architecture designed for hierarchical
multi-label classification. It jointly learns global representations for the entire label hierarchy
and local representations for individual hierarchy levels, and combines their predictions to
improve classification performance while encouraging hierarchical dependency by applying
a penalty term.
• C-HMCNN (Giunchiglia & Lukasiewicz 2020): A state-of-the-art hierarchical multi-label
classification neural network that incorporates a constraint layer to ensure predictions satisfy
the hierarchical constraints without post-processing.

Discussion
In this paper, we proposed a novel method, namely Parent-gated Authorization Transformer for
Hierarchies (PATH), for predicting hierarchical authorization structures in agentic AI systems.
PATH leverages a Transformer architecture with a hierarchy-structured attention mask and a parent-
gated probability formulation to ensure hierarchical consistency in the predictions. This design
also ensures that the model actually learns the probability chain rule that forms the hierarchical
structure.
In addition, we also adopt a depth-level embedding that accommodate incomplete
authorization observations and capture the correlation among nodes at the same hierarchical depth.
Theoretically, we establish a prediction error bound that characterizes the effects of approximation
error, estimation error, optimization error, and missingness-induced bias. We further establish
statistical consistency and derive convergence guarantees for the proposed optimization algorithm.
We evaluated our method on simulated, AI-generated user data and real-world data analysis. The
results demonstrate strong performance in prediction while maintaining zero hierarchy violations.
For future work, we will extend the proposed framework to incorporate more complex inputs, such
as multimodal data and users’ interaction histories with AI agents. Furthermore, we will also
incorporate industry-scale datasets and integrate the proposed approach into real-world products
27


for deployment and valuation.

B.3
Scope and feasibility of the assumptions
The theorem is conditional on (O1)–(O4); those properties do not follow merely from using a
Transformer or BCE. The oracle must target the exact empirical objective in (S.6). For example,
independent uniform sampling of users with per-user loss m−1 P
j Oijℓ(pθ,j(Xi), Yij) gives an
unbiased gradient when differentiation is valid. A random denominator equal to the number of
observed labels in a batch generally changes this expectation. Dropout, weight decay, and other
penalties require separate objective agreement or a transfer bound.
The update (S.10) specifies a decreasing learning rate, a second-moment coefficient 1 −c/t,
no bias correction, and an inside-square-root stabilizer. A fixed-learning-rate implementation of
standard bias-corrected Adam is not covered by this proof. Likewise, random reshuffling without
10


replacement does not by itself establish conditional unbiasedness at each update. The condition
k ≥1 is for each deterministic stopping iteration; (S.1) is not a simultaneous confidence bound
over all iterations or an arbitrary data-dependent stopping rule.
The local formulation of smoothness is deliberate. A nonconstant globally bounded objective
on RD cannot have both a globally L-Lipschitz gradient and a global PL inequality with positive
constant. Indeed, for x+ = x + ∇J(x)/L, the lower smoothness bound would give
J(x+) −J⋆≥J(x) −J⋆+ ∥∇J(x)∥2
2L
≥(1 + µ/L)(J(x) −J⋆).
Repeated ascent from a positive gap contradicts the global upper bound. This matters for globally
clipped losses. Our assumptions instead require a suitable neighborhood and invariant trajectory,
whose existence must still be justified. ReLU or hard-clipping kinks can prevent the assumed
smoothness if a trajectory crosses them, and PL can fail at nonglobal stationary points. Equality
Jn = Fn on a restricted set alone does not guarantee equality of their gradients; (O3) explicitly
specifies the required gradient. Equality on an open neighborhood of the trajectory is a sufficient
way to align gradients.

C.1
Models Parameters
PATH
. For a p-dimensional input, the user encoder is p →128 →128, with ReLU after each
affine map and dropout 0.1 between the two maps. Its output is added to learned node and depth
embeddings. Two pre-normalized Transformer encoder layers use four attention heads, hidden
11


dimension 128, feed-forward dimension 512, GELU activation, and dropout 0.1. A node attends
only to itself and its ancestors. A shared linear head followed by a sigmoid gives a base score qij.
The root score is fixed at one.
For optimization, all Transformer runs use Adam, with learning rate 10−3, weight decay 10−4,
batch size 64, model initialization seed 42, positive-class BCE weight 1, and hierarchy-penalty
coefficient 0. For label prediction, the threshold is 0.5 throughout all experiments.
HMCN-F
. There is one local branch per graph depth. Every global hidden layer has width 128,
each local hidden layer has width 64, the activation is ReLU, and dropout is 0.6. Local and global
probabilities are combined with weight β = 0.5. Training sums masked global BCE, the masked
local BCE terms, and a hierarchy penalty with coefficient 0.1; the penalty is the mean squared
positive child-minus-parent difference of the fused probabilities, averaged over users and direct
edges without an observation mask. For optimization, Adam uses learning rate 10−3 and weight
decay 10−5, with batch size 64. The original HMCN-F runs disable their own post-processing
option.
C-HMCNN
. The network has three affine layers, p →128 →128 →m, where m is the number
of graph nodes. The hidden activation is ReLU and dropout is 0.7. The model uses its missing-
label-aware constraint loss and a max-constraint layer at inference. Adam uses learning rate 10−4
and weight decay 10−5, with batch size 4. HMCN-F and C-HMCNN fit feature preprocessing
on training data and select checkpoints by observed-node micro-average precision (AP), using
threshold 0.5 for binary predictions.
