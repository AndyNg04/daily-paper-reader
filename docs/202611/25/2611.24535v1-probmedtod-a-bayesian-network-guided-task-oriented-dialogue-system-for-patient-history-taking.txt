Abstract
Patient history taking is a strategic, multi-turn process that refines diagnostic
hypotheses through iterative questioning, mirroring clinical reasoning where each
inquiry updates beliefs over candidate diagnoses. Deploying AI in this setting is
constrained by privacy, cost, and latency, making Small Language Models (SLMs)
a more desirable backbone compared to larger LLMs. However, SLMs capture
only the fast, intuitive “System 1” side of this process and lack explicit mechanisms
for the deliberative “System 2” reasoning needed to maintain and update diagnostic
uncertainty over time. Bayesian Networks (BayesNets) offer a natural complement,
providing an interpretable framework for auditable probabilistic belief tracking.
Yet, clinical BayesNets are difficult to construct due to their reliance on expert-
curated structure, dependencies, and parameters.
We introduce ProbMedTOD, which automatically synthesizes a clinical BayesNet
from a small number of clinical notes in three stages: ontology building, structure
prediction, and parameter estimation. ProbMedTOD then integrates the resulting
BayesNet with SLMs, enabling probabilistic reasoning in multi-turn dialogues.
A BayesNet Agent maintains real-time posterior distributions over diagnoses,
while a Policy Agent picks diagnostically informative questions. Experiments on
DDXPlus and MIMIC-IV Notes show that ProbMedTOD outperforms LLM-only
and retrieval-based baselines, with especially strong gains for smaller models.

Introduction
Patient history taking is key to diagnosis [Engel and Morgan, 1973, Hampton et al., 1975, Boyd and
Heritage, 2006]. It is a goal-driven, multi-turn dialogue to aimed identify the principal diagnosis,
the condition mainly responsible for the visit [CMS, 2024]. Symptoms are often vague, partial, or
overlapping, forcing clinicians to reason under uncertainty. Clinicians therefore use deliberative
“System 2” reasoning, updating hypotheses and selecting queries by clinical value [Garibaldi and
Olson, 2018]. Table 1 illustrates this: the doctor picks the query (Fatigue) to sharpen belief about TB.
Recently, large language models (LLMs) have shown strong results on medical QA and dialogue
benchmarks [Li et al., 2023, Dou et al., 2023, Tu et al., 2024, Savage et al., 2024, Pal and Sankara-
subbu, 2024, Singhal et al., 2025]. However, privacy and resource constraints in real clinical settings
make SLMs an attractive practical choice. SLMs excel at the fast, fluent “System 1” side of dialogue,
but lack an explicit mechanism to maintain and update a diagnostic belief or to select questions that
maximize diagnostic value [Guan and Baral, 2021, Xu et al., 2024, He et al., 2024].
Bayesian Networks (BayesNets) have long been used for diagnosis under uncertainty [Shwe et al.,
1991, Jaakkola and Jordan, 1999, Polotskaya et al., 2024], offering explicit belief updates and

conversational strengths of SLMs with the reliability of Bayesian diagnosis? To answer this question,
we propose our framework ProbMedTOD, in which at each dialogue turn, a BayesNet Agent extracts
symptom and risk-factor states and performs real-time Bayesian inference to update diagnostic
posteriors. A Policy Agent uses these posteriors to select the most informative next question. Finally,
a Diagnosis Agent predicts the top-5 principal diagnoses from accumulated evidence.
Unfortunately, clinical BayesNets are costly to build, requiring expert-curated ontologies, manually
specified structures, and carefully estimated parameters. ProbMedTOD addresses this with an
automated pipeline that constructs a BayesNet from a limited number of clinical notes in three
stages: ontology construction identifies relevant diseases, symptoms, and risk factors; structure
learning infers their dependencies; and parameter estimation assigns conditional probabilities directly
predicted by an LLM—no expert annotation required.
In summary, our main contributions is ProbMedTOD, which to the best of our knowledge is the
first system to combine BayesNets with SLMs for medical history taking, featuring an automated
BayesNet construction pipeline and probabilistic multi-turn dialogue. On high-fidelity simulations
on DDXPlus and MIMIC-IV Notes, ProbMedTOD significantly improves upon LLM-only and
retrieval-based baselines, with especially strong gains for smaller models. Code and data are available
at https://github.com/dair-iitd/ProbMedTOD.

Related Work
Our work broadly belongs to the line of research in which symbolic AI and/or task-specialized
modules are combined with general-purpose language models for strong task performance in areas
such as complex reasoning [Mittal et al., 2025], knowledge-base question answering [Patidar et al.,
2024, Sawhney et al., 2025], relation extraction [Rathore et al., 2026], and dialog [Saley et al.,
2024b]. Within the medical domain, neuro-symbolic approaches [Yang et al., 2025], such as those
of Zhang et al. [2023] and Hong et al. [2024], extract logical rules from medical text to support
symbolic reasoning. Related work by Zhang et al. [2025], Babakov et al. [2025] studies BayesNet
structure discovery from ontology metadata. While similar in spirit, they typically do not perform
probabilistic belief estimation or maintain a dialogue-conditioned belief distribution, capabilities
central to ProbMedTOD.
With the rise of LLMs, their application in medical tasks has expanded rapidly, showing strong
performance in medical QA and diagnosis benchmarks [Chen et al., 2023, Singhal et al., 2023,
Savage et al., 2024, Pal and Sankarasubbu, 2024, Tu et al., 2024, Singhal et al., 2025, Sellergren
et al., 2025]. Recent work has focused on adapting LLMs to medical dialogue settings. Li et al.
[2023], Feng et al. [2026] fine-tuned LLaMA on doctor-patient conversations to build a QA system.
2


Other approaches, such as Xu et al. [2024] and He et al. [2024], use chain-of-thought prompting
[Wei et al., 2022] to capture clinical reasoning, distilling this into smaller models. Several systems
[Sree et al., 2024, Varshney et al., 2025, Rose et al., 2025] also integrate external medical knowledge
through retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [Lewis et al., 2020]; for example, Xu et al. [2024]
uses RAG to infer likely diagnoses from dialogue context. However, none of these systems explicitly
model diagnostic uncertainty. They rely on medical relationships learned during training, without
probabilistic reasoning or belief updates. In contrast, ProbMedTOD maintains a diagnostic hypothesis
using a BayesNet, enabling principled updates and structured decision-making under uncertainty.
BayesNets have a long history in medical diagnosis, offering a principled way to model uncertainty
and reason under incomplete information. Seminal efforts include the probabilistic reformulation of
the INTERNIST-1/QMR system [Shwe et al., 1991] and the use of variational inference for scalable
reasoning in the QMR-DT network [Jaakkola and Jordan, 1999]. More recent work highlights the
continued relevance of BayesNets in healthcare [Polotskaya et al., 2024].
Two closely related works are those by Guan and Baral [2021] and Pavez and Allende [2024].
Guan and Baral [2021] use a Bayesian framework to select the next symptom to inquire based on
expected information gain, aiming to reduce diagnostic uncertainty. However, their system operates
on structured inputs and lacks conversational capabilities. Pavez and Allende [2024] combine a
BERT-based classifier with a Bayesian Network for mental health diagnosis, using BERT to generate
a prior over diseases that is refined via probabilistic inference. While both approaches share our
focus on uncertainty-aware reasoning, they do not leverage natural language dialogue. In contrast,
ProbMedTOD integrates Bayesian inference with the fluent interaction abilities of LLMs, enabling
data-driven, interpretable, and conversational medical history-taking.

Problem Definition
Let D be the set of m candidate diseases. A TOD system for medical history taking interacts
with a patient over multiple turns. At each turn t, the system observes the dialogue history Ht =
{(q1, a1), . . . , (qt−1, at−1)} of prior doctor and patient utterances denoted by qi and ai. It then
selects the next doctor inquiry qt. At the end of the dialogue, the system outputs the top-5 diagnoses.
A natural baseline is to prompt an LLM with Ht and D, asking it to decide the most informative qt.
This approach relies on implicit model reasoning and lacks an explicit mechanism to treat diagnostic
uncertainty. In contrast, A BayesNet over risk factors R, symptoms S, and diseases D (Figure 3)
provides a complementary, structured alternative. Given an evidence state consisting of confirmed
and denied symptoms and risk factors ES = (S+, S−, R+, R−), it computes a diagnostic believe as
the posterior DS = P(D | S+, S−, R+, R−), enabling explicit, interpretable belief updates.
In this work, we propose ProbMedTOD, the first TOD system that combines conversation capabilities
of LLMs with probabilistic framework of BayesNets for medical history taking. A key challenge
is enabling effective interaction between the LLM and the BayesNet during dialogue to extract
evidence and update beliefs. Further, clinical BayesNets are difficult to construct, requiring expert-
defined ontologies, graph structures, and parameters. We address this by automatically constructing a
clinically valid BayesNet from a small corpus P of patient profiles.
4
ProbMedTOD System
We first describe the overall architecture of ProbMedTOD for medical history taking assuming that
the BayesNet is given. Figure 1 provides a high-level overview of the system. ProbMedTOD consists
of three main components: BayesNet Agent for evidence extraction and belief update, Policy Agent
for dialogue policy, and Diagnosis Agent for final diagnosis. All three agents are prompt-based and
use chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting.
BayesNet Agent:
At turn t, BayesNet Agent processes the latest doctor-patient exchange (at, ut) to
update the evidence state ESt and compute the diagnostic belief DSt. A key challenge is that SLMs
struggle to directly compare free-form patient utterances against large clinical ontologies. Ontologies
often contain hundreds of expert terms, while patients describe symptoms in lay language (e.g., "can’t
catch my breath" →dyspnea). To bridge this gap, BayesNet Agent employs a retrieve–rerank pipeline
that narrows the search space while leveraging the LLM’s reasoning capabilities.
3


First, an LLM classifies whether ut contains evidence about symptoms, risk factors, or neither. This
lightweight routing step avoids unnecessary retrieval when the patient response is non-clinical (e.g.,
clarifications or pleasantries). Second, based on the classification, candidates are retrieved separately
from the symptom set S and the risk-factor set R using the SapBERT [Liu et al., 2021] clinical
retriever. The top-10 symptom nodes and top-10 risk-factor nodes are selected, skipping irrelevant
retrievals as needed.
Finally, another LLM prompt resolves each candidate as confirmed, denied, or unmentioned, yielding
per-turn evidences. These findings are aggregated into the cumulative evidence state ESt. Finally,
BayesNet Agent uses the BayesNet to compute the diagnostic belief state DSt = P(D | ESt), a
posterior distribution over diseases.

The BayesNet Construction
Clinical BayesNets are not always available, and constructing one from scratch is difficult. Defining
the node set requires domain expertise because relevant symptoms and risk factors may be missing
or inconsistently named across standard ontologies. Specifying the graph structure demands that
clinicians reason explicitly about causal relationships among diseases, risk factors, and symptoms.
Estimating the parameters is harder still: the strength of each relation must be quantified, but the
patient-level data needed for statistical estimation are often costly to obtain. A BayesNet should also
capture patterns in the target dataset, such as occupational or travel-related risk factors that may not
appear in a standard ontology. To address these challenges, ProbMedTOD constructs a BayesNet
from a small corpus P of patient notes for a given disease set D. Construction proceeds in three
stages: ontology building, structure prediction, and parameter estimation. The notes identify the
symptoms and risk factors that should be represented, medical references provide evidence for the
graph structure, and an LLM maps the text in these sources to nodes, edges, and parameters.

Ontology Building.
The first stage defines the node set from the clinical variables discussed in the
patient notes P. For each note, we prompt an LLM to extract symptoms and five types of risk factors:
past medical history, family history, exposures, habits, and travel history. Pooling these mentions
produces synonyms and vague terms. In Figure 2, for example, wheezing and wheeze refer to the
same symptom, while feeling unwell is too vague to form a useful node.
Passing an entire category to the LLM would create long prompts and require it to compare many
unrelated terms. We instead embed the terms using SapBERT [Liu et al., 2021] and use agglomerative
clustering to form small groups of likely matches. We recursively divide clusters larger than 30 terms.
The LLM then merges equivalent terms, assigns canonical names, and removes terms that are not
useful for diagnosis. Thus, clustering narrows the candidate set, while the LLM makes the final
decisions. We repeat this process for k = 3 rounds. The final symptom category defines S, and the
other categories form the risk-factor set R.
Structure Prediction.
Once the node set is fixed, we determine which nodes are connected. Co-
occurrence in the small note corpus is not reliable evidence of a clinical relation, so we select a
5


medical reference for each disease. For every Dj ∈D, the LLM generates search queries and
fetches2 candidate pages from authorative sources such as CDC, NHS and MedlinePlus. Once parsed,
the LLM ranks these pages, and we select the highest-ranked page as the source document for Dj.
We predict edges separately for each disease-category pair, dividing each category into batches of at
most 50 nodes. The LLM retains relations supported by the selected reference and orients them as
Rk →Dj or Dj →Si. In Figure 2, recent travel remains in R because it occurs in the notes, but it
has no edge to asthma because the reference does not support that relation. The union of the retained
edges forms the final graph structure.
Parameter Estimation.
With the network structure in place, we estimate the parameters of the
Noisy-OR model. Estimating them from P would be unreliable because the corpus is small and is not
intended to provide population-level frequencies. We therefore use the LLM to provide numerical
estimates of the parameters. It estimates a baseline probability pj for every disease, a strength rkj for
every retained Risk→Disease edge, and a strength qji for every retained Disease→Symptom edge.
We do not estimate strengths for absent edges, and we fix every symptom leak to λi = 0.001. This
produces a parameterized BayesNet that can be used by BayesNet Agent for the inference.
The pipeline can also use expert input when it is available. For example, an expert ontology,
graph structure, or parameter set can replace the corresponding stage while the other stages remain
unchanged. We study these replacements in our component ablation. We provide the prompts used
for BayesNet construction in Appendix F.

Experimental Setup
We evaluate ProbMedTOD on principal diagnosis prediction using simulated multi-turn patient
interviews. The system conducts a history-taking interview and returns a ranked list of candidate
diagnoses. A system performs well when the true primary diagnosis appears near the top of this list.
Patient Simulator and Evaluation.
Evaluating medical TOD systems is challenging because
several valid questioning paths can lead to the same diagnosis. Existing turn-level protocols, such
as action F1 and BLEU [Budzianowski et al., 2018, Hosseini-Asl et al., 2020, Saley et al., 2024a],
compare each system turn with a fixed reference dialogue. This is too restrictive for clinical settings.
For example, asking about fever before chills can be just as reasonable as asking in the opposite order.
These metrics can therefore penalize clinically appropriate behavior and fail to capture the flexibility
needed for diagnostic reasoning.
We instead use a dialogue-level evaluation framework. A patient simulator [Luo et al., 2024, Liu
et al., 2025, Feng et al., 2026, Kyung et al., 2025] interacts with ProbMedTOD over multiple turns.
The simulator is powered by Qwen3.8-27B [Qwen Team, 2026b] and generates patient responses
conditioned on the underlying clinical note. This setup tests whether BayesNet-guided question
selection helps ProbMedTOD elicit more diagnostically useful information than unstructured LLM
querying. The full simulator prompt is provided in section F.8. Our standard simulator answers
negatively when a queried fact is absent from the clinical note. Because real patients may instead
guess, be unsure, or respond ambiguously, we also consider a noisy simulator that randomly gives a
plausible diagnosis-consistent answer, reports the fact as unavailable, or expresses uncertainty.
At the end of each simulated interview, ProbMedTOD outputs a ranked list of candidate diagnoses.
We compare this list with the true primary diagnosis, encoded using the first three characters of
the ICD code. We report Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) that gives partial credit when the correct
diagnosis is ranked highly and Hit@k (k = 1, 3, 5) that measures the proportion of cases where the
correct diagnosis appears in the top k predictions. Together, these metrics evaluate diagnostic utility
at the dialogue level, without penalizing valid alternative questioning strategies.
Datasets.
We conduct experiments on DDXPlus and two specialities from MIMIC-IV Notes.
DDXPlus [Fansi Tchango et al., 2022] is a dataset of synthetic patient profiles generated from a
proprietary medical knowledge base. It covers 49 conditions across multiple specialties. Each profile
contains a structured set of symptoms and antecedents, together with a differential diagnosis list.

We use 144 cases to construct our BayesNet and 882 cases for evaluation. In addition, we construct
BayesNet (Oracle) from the one million patient cases available with DDXPlus. This data-driven
network serves as an upper bound on the BayesNet constructed from the small note corpus.
MIMIC-IV Notes[Johnson et al., 2023] contains de-identified discharge notes from real hospital
encounters. As a representative case, we focus on pulmonary diseases, a specialty with clear ICD-
coded primary diagnoses, including pneumonia, tuberculosis, and heart failure. Following Kyung et al.
[2025], we use the notes from patients’ first visits and map ICD-9-CM codes to ICD-10-CM, retaining
only unambiguous one-to-one mappings. We then remove diagnoses with nonspecific qualifiers (e.g.,
“unspecified,” “unidentified,” or “defined elsewhere”). Finally, we use Gemini-3.8-Flash to only retain
cases where the diagnosis ICD-10-CM code strongly agrees with the discharge diagnosis from the
notes. The resulting pulmonary subset contains 17 diseases at the three-character ICD-10-CM level,
with 66 notes for BayesNet construction and 197 cases for evaluation.
Finally, we include a second MIMIC-IV Notes specialty, abdominal. We use the dataset released by
Hager et al. [2024] consisting of four common diseases. We use 100 cases for BayesNet construction
and 599 cases for evaluation. Dataset statistics are summarized in table 12.
Baselines.
Our goal is to test whether ProbMedTOD improves the diagnostic performance of
SLMs (≤8B parameters). We evaluate three models: Nemotron-3-Nano-4B (Nano-4B) [NVIDIA
Corporation, 2026], Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B. We compare four configurations for each SLM
model. Base performs history taking and diagnosis only using model’s capabilities. Base + RAG
augments the model with retrieved medical evidence. Base + BayesNet uses the automatically
constructed network for belief updates and question selection. Base + RAG + BayesNet combines
both forms of augmentation. On DDXPlus, we additionally evaluate Base + BayesNet (Oracle) to
assess the scope of improvement when a large patient corpus available for BayesNet construction.
For RAG baselines, we use the set of document fetched during BayesNet construction. We break
down the documents into chunks and retrieve the top-10 most similar chunks based on language
model generated queries. These chunks are added to the model context for generating the next inquiry
or the final diagnosis, without blowing up the context size. We provide further details in section C.
We evaluate large models, Qwen3-32B [Qwen Team, 2025], Qwen3.5-122B [Qwen Team, 2026a]
and Gemini-3.8-Flash [Google DeepMind, 2026] under Base and Base + BayesNet configurations.

Results
We organize the evaluation around two research questions: a) how much does BayesNet guidance
improve diagnostic performance for ProbMedTOD, and where do the gains come from? and b) how
effective and reliable is the automatically constructed BayesNet itself?

Posterior Calibration.
For these diagnostic rankings to support probabilistic decision-making, the
confidence assigned to them also matters. To evaluate this confidence, we conduct calibration study
using Brier@k, comparing the total probability assigned to the top-k diagnoses with whether the
correct diagnosis appears in that set. The raw posteriors tend to be overconfident, with Brier@1 scores
of 0.39 on DDXPlus and 0.44 on MIMIC-Pulm (table 5). Importantly, fitting a single temperature
parameter on each dataset’s training split reduces test-set Brier@1 to 0.22 and 0.18, respectively,
with improvements also in Brier@3 and Brier@5. The network’s diagnostic signal thus comes with
confidence estimates that can be improved through a simple calibration step.
Oracle comparisons support the disease–symptom estimates.
These predictive results estab-
lish the network’s utility, but leave open how closely its underlying probabilities reflect empirical
diagnostic relationships. We compare its parameters with those of the oracle BayesNet constructed
from one million DDXPlus cases (section 6). For each parameter, we compute the KL divergence
between the oracle and estimated probabilities, then average within each parameter category; lower
values indicate closer agreement. Two sampling baselines draw probabilities uniformly at random
or from a fitted Gaussian distribution, with results reported over 100 seeded samples. For disease–
symptom probabilities, the constructed BayesNet achieves an average KL of 0.38, compared with
0.50 for uniform sampling and 0.41 for Gaussian sampling. This agreement supports the quality of
the disease–symptom estimates. However, disease priors and risk factor–disease probabilities are
not directly comparable across the two networks: the constructed network estimates them for the
general population, including healthy individuals, whereas the oracle estimates them for a population
consisting only of patients with a disease. Their KL values, and consequently the overall average,
cannot be interpreted directly as estimation accuracy.

Conclusion
We propose ProbMedTOD, the first TOD system for medical history taking that integrates SLM’s
conversational capabilities with interpretable probabilistic reasoning of BayesNets. ProbMedTOD
leverages a dedicated BayesNet Agent that maintains running diagnostic beliefs over candidate
diagnoses and a Policy Agent selects clinically informative questions, thus combining System 1 and
System 2 reasoning in a unified framework. To avoid costly curation of BayesNets, ProbMedTOD
proposes an automated pipeline that sythesizes BayesNet ontology, structure, and parameters based
on given set of diseases and small corpus of patient profiles. The pipeline is flexible, allowing expert
intervention at any stage, for further improvements.
We validate ProbMedTOD on two datasets: DDXPlus and MIMIC-IV covering different clinical
specialties. Results show that ProbMedTOD consistently improves performance of the paired base
models across the metrics, with larger gains for smaller models. Ablation studies confirm importance
of each pipeline component, with graph structure learning being the most impactful. BayesNet
guidance also leads to more clinically relevant questions and more accurate diagnoses in qualitative
analysis. Together, these results highlight the benefits of combining structured Bayesian inference
with conversational LLMs, and point toward a more transparent, reliable, and clinically grounded
paradigm for medical dialogue systems.

Limitations
While ProbMedTOD demonstrates strong performance across our evaluations, it has several limita-
tions that warrant discussion. ProbMedTOD relies on a predefined set of diseases for constructing the
Bayesian network. In practice, clinical settings serve highly diverse patient populations where the
relevant disease space may shift over time or vary by region. Supporting such diversity would require
automatic or on-the-fly extension of the Bayesian network. Although this direction is both interesting
and technically feasible, we leave it to future work.
ProbMedTOD adopts a tripartite Bayesian network structure linking symptoms, diseases, and tests.
While this structure is well-motivated for medical dialogue, it is only one of many possible graph
topologies. Alternative structures, such as those incorporating risk factors, patient history, or treatment
outcomes, may better capture the complexity of real clinical reasoning and are worth exploring in
future work.
The Bayesian network in ProbMedTOD is constructed using Gemini 3.8 Flash, a relatively lightweight
model chosen for efficiency. Using larger, more capable LLMs for this construction step may yield
higher-quality conditional probability estimates and richer graph structures, potentially improving
downstream dialogue performance.
Our experiments are conducted exclusively in English. Medical dialogue systems must ultimately
serve multilingual populations. Wether the probabilistic reasoning encoded in the Bayesian network
transfers effectively to other languages or clinical terminologies is an interesting question.
Impact Statement
ProbMedTOD advances development of AI systems for medical diagnosis, with potential to improve
patient outcomes and reduce healthcare costs. By integrating probabilistic reasoning through Bayesian
networks, our approach enhances the accuracy and reliability of diagnostic suggestions in simulated
clinical dialogues. This could lead to more informed decision-making by clinicians and better patient
care.
Further, our method allows clinical practitioners to specify their own diagnostic knowledge as
BayesNet components in structured form, which can be used to guide the dialogue and diagnosis.
This is an interesting human-AI collaboration setting in medical settings, where clinicians can inject
their expertise into the system to improve performance and trustworthiness.
Finally, ProbMedTOD works well with small language models, which helps democratize access to
advanced diagnostic tools in resource-constrained settings, where large models may be impractical.
By enabling effective medical dialogue systems with smaller models, our work has the potential to
broaden the reach of AI-driven healthcare solutions and reduce disparities in access to quality care.

B.4
Alternative Question-Selection Strategies
We compare ProbMedTOD with two alternative strategies. The Naive Bayes variant reuses the
disease–symptom edges and parameter estimates constructed by ProbMedTOD, but adopts a simpler
probabilistic structure. Information Gain instead greedily selects the question with the largest expected
one-step reduction in diagnostic uncertainty.
The learned disease–symptom model is useful even in a simpler structure.
As shown in table 10,
Naive Bayes improves MRR over the Base model in all six SLM–dataset comparisons, by 0.02–0.07.
On DDXPlus, its gains for Qwen3 4B and Qwen3 8B are significant across every metric. Because
this variant uses the disease–symptom relations and estimates produced by our construction pipeline,
its strong performance provides additional evidence that these learned components capture useful
clinical dependencies. The complete BayesNet nevertheless achieves higher MRR than Naive Bayes
in all six SLM settings, indicating that the full structure provides further value.
Greedy information gain is not aligned with final diagnostic accuracy.
Information Gain trails
the complete BayesNet in six of eight model–dataset comparisons and ties it in one, with Nano 4B on
DDXPlus as the sole exception. For Qwen3 4B and Qwen3 8B, it yields little or no improvement
over Base and is consistently below the complete BayesNet. This result is expected from its myopic
objective: a question that maximally reduces uncertainty at the current turn need not produce the
most useful sequence of evidence for the final diagnosis. In contrast, ProbMedTOD allows the policy
model to interpret the BayesNet belief jointly with the dialogue history when selecting the next
question.

B.5
Diminishing Gains for Larger Models
We examine why Bayesian Network (BN) guidance benefits smaller models such as Qwen3 4B
more than the stronger Gemini 3.8 Flash. To understand this difference, we compare baselines and
corresponding ProbMedTOD predictions on sets of cases stratified by whether the gold diagnosis
appears in the final BayesNet differential. Table 11 summarizes the results across DDXPlus cases
and MIMIC-IV (Pulmonary) cases.
The results suggest that stronger baseline performance leaves less room for useful BayesNet guidance.
When the BayesNet includes the correct diagnosis, Qwen3 4B gains 0.21 MRR on DDXPlus and 0.14
on MIMIC-IV (Pulmonary), whereas Gemini gains only 0.05 and 0.01, respectively. This pattern is
consistent with the BayesNet supplying diagnostic candidates that the smaller model would otherwise
miss or rank poorly, while providing information that the stronger model can already support on their
own.
The reduced benefit does not coincide with lower BayesNet coverage. Gemini has higher gold
coverage on both datasets. Nevertheless, excluding the correct diagnosis remains associated with
losses, reaching 0.23 MRR for Gemini on each dataset. Thus, as baseline capability increases, the
additional value of helpful guidance shrinks while the cost associated with misleading guidance
persists. This balance explains the substantial net gains for Qwen3 4B and the absence of comparable
gains for Gemini.

with temperature set to 0.0, and our prompts explicitly forbid verbatim repetition. If a model violates
the format or repeats itself, we resample up to five times with increasing temperature values: 0.1,

For RAG baselines, we use the same set of document fetched during BayesNet construction. We
divide these documents into overlapping chunks of 512 tokens. At each dialogue turn, the language
model generates retrieval queries from the conversation so far. We embed the queries and chunks
with the SapBERT (cambridgeltl/SapBERT-from-PubMedBERT-fulltext) [Liu et al., 2021]
encoder and retrieve the 10 most similar chunks based on cosine similarity. These chunks are added
to the model context for generating the next inquiry or the final diagnosis.
All our simulations take about 3 hours to run per model per dataset. BayesNet synthesis is a one
time operation that takes about 4 hour per dataset. The majority of this time is spent on parameter
estimation, which requires multiple calls to the LLM for each disease-symptom and risk-factor
disease pair.
