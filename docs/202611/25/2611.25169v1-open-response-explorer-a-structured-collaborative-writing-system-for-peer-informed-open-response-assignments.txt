Open Response Explorer: a Structured Collaborative Writing System for
Peer-Informed Open-Response Assignments
ZIWEI GU, Harvard University, USA
RUIXUAN CHEN, Santa Monica College, USA
ELENA GLASSMAN, Harvard University, USA
Peer-informed open-response assignments—where students view, connect, and learn from peer contributions—hold the potential to
foster collaborative knowledge building, yet the unstructured nature of free-form prose prevents students from effectively comparing
and making sense of peers’ work. We present Open Response Explorer, an interface that recenters open responses on structured idea
units through purpose-based writing and peer response exploration, augmented by common-role text segment highlighting for easy
comparison. A within-subjects lab study (N=12) validated the usefulness and usability of the system, demonstrating how it shaped the
open-response drafting and peer exploration practices. We discuss implications for purpose-centered authoring and exploration that
transform free-form prose into structured, comparable, and collectively navigable knowledge artifacts.

Introduction
Open-response assignments—such as paper reviews, case briefs, design justifications, and short-answer explanations—are
central to learning across K–12, undergraduate, and graduate education. They create space for explanation, argumenta-
tion, and reflection, and are traditionally performed privately to assess individual mastery. However, evolving classroom
designs [11] have also explored the use of public open responses for collaborative knowledge building in communi-
ties of learners [66, 72]. For example, discussion forum posts invite learners to contribute ideas in their own words,
encounter diverse perspectives, and refine their ideas through social exchange [56], a practice shown to improve
course performance [13]. Decades of research in social learning and studio pedagogy1 suggest that this visibility allows
students to view, compare, and model their work after the diverse strategies of their peers, rather than working in
isolation [5, 67], thereby transforming a classroom of individuals into a community of inquiry [55]. Drawing on this
theoretical grounding, we envision a hybrid model we term peer-informed open-response assignments—tasks where
students respond to a shared prompt but are encouraged to view peer responses at some stage of the assignment
lifecycle (before, during, or after drafting) to leverage the public corpus for comparative reflection.
However, realizing the social and pedagogical potential of this hybrid model is challenging due to the unstructured
messiness of raw prose. Existing peer learning platforms (e.g., SWoRD [17], Calibrated Peer Review [63], PeerStudio [49],

Automated essay evaluation can assign scores or surface features of writing at scale [68], but scoring does not, by itself,

Argument mining methods can detect claims and evidence [69], and Large Language Models (LLMs) can extract themes

at scale, contextualize their own responses, and compose with comparison in mind. Consequently, when students
(and instructors) attempt to navigate peer contributions, hundreds of heterogeneous responses can quickly become
cognitively overwhelming, making it difficult to identify similar or contrasting ideas, trace strong and weak arguments,
or build on promising directions in others’ work. As a result, peer engagement often collapses into ad hoc commenting,
while grading and feedback remain extremely difficult and inconsistent [3, 56].
To address these challenges, we present Open Response Explorer (ORE), a novel interface designed specifically for
peer-informed open-response assignments, where students benefit from seeing, comparing, and building on classmates’
ideas. ORE reduces the intrinsic cognitive demands of reading and reasoning about the emerging set of peer contributions
by making idea units first-class, comparable objects. ORE provides (1) Structured Authoring to a shared prompt,
where students compose text in linear, purpose-labeled fields rather than a single text blob, yielding responses that are
structured by design; (2) Structured Exploration with Common-Role Text Segment Highlighting, an exploratory
view that organizes the class corpus by purposes and highlights analogous segments in distinct colors to facilitate
side-by-side comparison; and, either directly from that exploratory view or via one-click retrieval of the pre-existing
comment that is most similar to a student’s on-going draft, (3) Peer-Linked Authoring with Common-Role Text
Segment Highlighting, which allows students to write commentary on a particular peer’s response that contrasts
with or extends it with the assistance of that role highlighting. Together, these interactions reframe open-response work
from solitary text production to purpose-centered collaborative knowledge construction and exploration.
To evaluate ORE, we conducted a within-subjects lab study with 12 participants. The results showed that participants
found the system to make it easier to write, explore, and learn from a peer-informed open response assignment.
Participants particularly appreciated how structured authoring, coupled with common-role text segment highlighting,
facilitated writing, comparing, and sensemaking of peer responses at scale.
In summary, we contribute:
(1) Novel purpose-centered structured authoring and exploration with role-based highlighting and peer-linked
authoring to support peer-informed open-response assignments;
(2) ORE, which instantiates these techniques for writing and exploring open-ended responses;
(3) A within-subjects lab study with 12 participants that validated the usability and usefulness of ORE for a peer-
informed open-response assignment.

Related Work
2.1
Peer-Informed Open-Response Assignments: Practices, Challenges, and Existing Tools
Peer-Informed open-response assignments such as discussion forum posts, mock program committee (PC) reviews,
collaborative case studies, and in-class formative writing are increasingly common across K–12 and higher education
because they elicit personal articulation and, unlike solitary assessments, invite students to view and engage with

one another’s work, creating opportunities for social learning [5]. The pedagogical logic behind these “compose–
contextualize–compare–revise” assignment workflows draws directly from studio pedagogy—a model grounded in
social learning theory where work is public, and learning occurs through the visible construction of artifacts and peer
engagement [5, 67]. In physical studios (e.g., architecture, art), seeing peer work is not considered cheating, but rather
an essential scaffold for students to acquire professional norms and diverse strategies [48].
However, when operationalizing the benefits of social learning within written assignments, effectively translating this
“visible work” culture to asynchronous text-based environments like discussion boards remains a significant challenge.
Unlike visual artifacts that support rapid scanning and comparison, raw prose is linear and opaque; without structural
support, the “studio” experience often becomes cognitively overwhelming rather than scaffolding. In particular, as the
number of responses grows, the free-form nature of prose exacerbates this cognitive bottleneck: peers find it hard to
locate comparable ideas in one another’s work [63], and instructors struggle with grading consistently and providing
feedback [23], often relying on peer or automated grading [7, 23] and feedback [10, 49].
Mainstream course platforms provide basic workflows for peer learning, e.g., peer review, discussions, and inline
annotations, but generally leave responses as unstructured text streams. For example, Canvas supports peer-review
assignments and graded discussions [43, 44], Blackboard offers threaded discussions [8, 9], and Moodle includes
discussion forums and PDF annotation for assignment feedback [50, 54]. Social annotation platforms such as Perusall
provide helpful structure to student discussions by requiring every comment to be anchored to some point in the
assigned reading, or be a response to another comment that is itself anchored to the text. The spatially distinct anchoring
of different conversations in the margins and the specificity of the text they are anchored on help students find and
append commentary that is relevant [1, 2, 19, 59]. Recently, LLM-powered systems have also been proposed. For
example, GLITTER [58] supports material-grounded, asynchronous discussion via concept clustering and reflective
summaries, but it does not support students in structuring their responses or provide features for sensemaking across
similarly-themed responses. Thus, if the responses are complex, nuanced, long, and/or quite numerous, the challenge
still remains.
Collectively, these tools offer improvements in participation and feedback pipelines, but fall short of addressing the
core challenge of open responses: the inherent complexity and messiness of unstructured text, which is difficult to
digest, compare, or synthesize at scale without support.
2.2
Finding or Creating Structure in Text for Sensemaking at Scale
A parallel thread in HCI develops interfaces that surface structure across many texts to support comparison, triage, and
sensemaking. A number of such systems either (1) identify and reify pre-defined shared structure or (2) attempt to
help users see structure emerge that no one necessarily explicitly knew in advance. Informed by theories of human
concept learning like Variation Theory [52], Mesotext [31] introduces text clustering and rendering techniques such
as Positional Diction Clustering with ‘grid’ and ‘interleaved’ views of the results to reveal commonalities and variations
across many LLM responses. Informed by Structure-Mapping Theory [30], AbstractExplorer [36] enables comparative
close reading across many similarly structured scientific abstracts by combining role highlighting with structure-aligned
ordering and alignment. CorpusStudio [20] leverages the same theories to guide the design of system features that
surface emergent writing patterns across many papers in a corpus to help authors outline and write with community
norms in mind. Similar strategies were used in education contexts at scale as well, primarily intended for teachers to
gain better awareness of—and give more, better feedback on—the design choices made by their students at much larger
scales, e.g., thousands of student solutions in a Massive Open Online Course like in OverCode [34] and Foobaz [32].

Analogous ideas appear in code domains as well: ParaLib [75] uses the same theories to justify concept annotations
and color-consistent highlighting to help users mentally align semantically analogous code segments across libraries,
and Examplore [35] visualizes API usage examples in Java at scale by displaying their (canonicalized) variation within
one shared structure—a fixed code skeleton based on the authors’ knowledge of the possible code structures around
API calls in Java. Structured code editors like Javardise [65] also uses structure to help beginners learn programming
by constraining editing to syntactically valid program code.
Designers of co-adaptive machine teaching have engaged with these techniques as well. In qualitative analysis, which
is conceptually closer to the assessment side of open response assignments, PaTAT [27] synthesizes human-interpretable
textual patterns to support human-AI collaborative coding (i.e., assigning descriptive labels to individual textual items
in a collection) and codebook evolution. Codebook evolution is a form of human-AI co-adaptive machine teaching, and
there too the strategy of generating structured text has been found to be helpful to those reviewing and assessing them,
i.e., in MOCHA [26]—where the strategy was designed to clarify the human’s mental model of the code’s definition. In
our work, rather than an AI generating structured text, the students doing the collective sensemaking are. And while
prior learnersourcing work like Glassman et al. [33] enforced a structure to help students provide effective hints to
each other, our work opens this strategy up to collaborative discussion together for open response environments, not
programming problems that are highly constrained by needing to pass particular test cases.
In summary, across these systems, the structuring process typically happens downstream—transforming or annotating
already-written text or code to expose latent structure. In contrast, our work expands what we know about the upstream
production of text: by introducing structured authoring where students write in typed fields (or “idea units”), ORE
makes structure explicit at input time so that output is structured by design, which can then be leveraged by later
exploration, comparison, and feedback processes with less heavy post-processing.
2.3
Role- and Semantic-based Highlighting
Semantic role labeling is a well-studied task in natural language processing that assigns roles (e.g., Agent, Patient,
Instrument) to spans in text, underpinning many downstream applications [12, 60]. Though approaches have evolved
from feature-rich classifiers [60] to neural span-based and transformer models [22, 71], classical pipelines that combined
parse-based features with machine learning that established early baselines remain relevant [39].
HCI research has also focused on text highlighting as a visualization technique, which has been shown to direct focus
and attention productively according to eye-tracking studies [14]. Another recent study on constrained highlighting
also demonstrates that some highlights can improve reading comprehension compared to no highlight [45], suggesting
that role-aware highlighting may productively steer attention. In the context of data annotation interfaces, Context [4]
compares highlight- versus reasoning-based designs and finds that newcomers benefit from contextual aids layered
atop highlights, suggesting that exposing semantic role labels to novice users may be useful.
Numerous prior HCI systems have utilized text highlighting as a means to facilitate sensemaking. Early work on
skimming support introduced machine-learned or human-annotated highlights to emphasize salient parts of sentences.
For example, VarifocalReader [47] highlights key sentence segments in different colors to aid rapid reading. Building
on this idea for scholarly reading, Scim [25] helps readers skim academic papers by using colored highlights to guide
attention to predefined content types such as “Objective” and “Method.” ClioQuery [37] assists historians with exploring
queries in context through linked views coupled with text highlighting. Within the broader Semantic Reader project [51],
Relatedly [57] highlights unexplored, dissimilar information in related work sections of unread papers to steer attention
to previously unseen content. Finally, AbstractExplorer [36] segments sentences into grammar-preserving chunks

Our ORE builds on this trajectory and applies role-based highlighting to structured student-authored open responses,

3.1
Design Goals and Rationales
To tackle the challenges of making sense of large text corpora that undermine the learning potential of peer-informed
open-response assignments, we synthesized findings from prior research on learning science, sensemaking systems, and
related human cognitive theories, supplemented by our classroom observations, to derive four design goals for ORE.
G1. Breakdown monolithic single responses into a sequence of separate purpose-specific fields Unstructured
open responses are difficult to process for both students and instructors, especially as their number grows. They quickly
accumulate into impenetrable piles, since each response must be consciously interpreted on its own. As a result, people
typically read them sequentially, relying on memory and any mental schema built from prior responses to interpret
each new one [61]. This makes it especially challenging to identify relationships—such as common themes or outliers
across students’ responses—or to situate a particular response within the context of a larger class.
Prior research in the learning sciences has proposed idea-based learning [38] and idea-centered knowledge build-
ing [40, 41]. In parallel, Weintrop and Wilensky [73] demonstrated that block-based representations can reduce
representational complexity for novice programmers, compared with text-based representations. However, this ap-
proach has yet to be specifically applied to written assignments, and the possible analogical transfer of this strategy to
natural language assignments would likely only be an incomplete mapping [30] since natural language has far more
semantic ambiguity than a programming language.
Research on argument mapping and concept mapping also shows that externalizing various components of a piece
of writing, such as claims, evidence, and relations, scaffolds higher-order reasoning and improves clarity [21]. Building
on this line of research, we propose a shift from writing free-form prose to a sequence of separate units of prose for
statements of different, specific purposes, making purpose fields the primary entity of composition and comparison in
open-response assignments.
G2. Co-define a minimal shared vocabulary of purposes. Collaboratively generated classification systems, or
folksonomies, demonstrate that shared vocabularies can emerge from community tagging rather than top-down tax-
onomies [74]. From a distributed cognition perspective, such shared external representations function as collective
cognitive artifacts that support group understanding [42]. Crowdsourcing research further shows that complex tax-
onomies can be constructed through small, distributed contributions [16]. We thus intend to enable students to co-define
a minimal shared vocabulary of purposes, allowing the schema to evolve with classroom practices and reflect the
diversity of perspectives present, instead of having the purposes pre-defined by the instructor.
G3. Support scalable response comparison. Grouping sentences by shared purpose (G1.) offers an opportunity to
further identify and reify common roles of text segments within each sentence to facilitate cross-sentence comparison.
According to Structural Mapping Theory, a well-established framework for human concept learning in cognitive science,
understanding and sensemaking arise from mapping relationships between alignable structures, particularly in complex
tasks [28, 29]. (Comparison as a means for sensemaking is also instantiated in recent systems that improves close
reading at scale [31, 36].) We can provide further support to help readers compare large sets of responses by revealing

analogous and conceptually comparable roles that are embedded in prose, especially when the text being directly
compared has the same purpose (G1).
G4. Embed peer context into drafting when appropriate. In certain types of assignments, peer activity can
increase engagement and improve revision quality when integrated during the drafting process [17, 49]. Knowledge-
building perspectives emphasize that learning advances when individuals notice, build on, and differentiate peers’
contributions [66]. We thus intend to bring timely, relevant peer responses into the writing context to support building-on
and perspective-taking in discussion-based assignments without displacing authorial agency.
G5. Stage peer exposure to balance independence and collaboration. We designed the system to support config-
urable visibility to peer work to serve different pedagogical goals. For assessment-oriented tasks, the system supports
independent drafting where students write privately. In this mode, the benefits of structured authoring primarily accrue
to the instructor, who can more easily navigate and grade the structured corpus. For collaborative learning tasks, the
system supports open or staged access to peer context, where students view peer work either immediately or after
independently drafting their own work. This flexibility aligns with frameworks for both productive failure and social
constructivism [15, 46, 53, 64]. While ORE enables both workflows, the rest of the paper focuses on the open exploration
mode to illustrate and evaluate the more general collaborative sensemaking and knowledge building workflow.
3.2
Key Features
ORE comprises four complementary features that, together, address the challenges of making sense of free-form text in
open-response assignments and realize our design goals (§3.1):
(1) Structured Authoring (purpose-based writing), which recenters composition on text units with distinct
purposes (G1–G2);
(2) Structured Exploration with Common-Role Text Segment Highlighting, which organizes peer work for
like-with-like comparison at scale (G1–G3);
(3) Peer-Linked Authoring, which brings peer context into view when drafting one’s own response—when
appropriate to support building-on and perspective-taking—while preserving authorship (G4).
(4) Instructor-configurable Modes, which allow phasing or disabling peer exposure (e.g., turning off Peer-Linked
Authoring or releasing Explore only after submission or only to instructors) to balance independence and
collaboration (G5).
3.2.1
Structured Authoring (purpose-based writing). To recenter authoring on a sequence of purposes (G1) and enable
class-wide collaborative generation of those purposes (G2), ORE replaces a single text box with a sequence of purpose
fields. Each field is a distinct unit of thought labeled with a purpose (also called its type). The class co-defines a shared
palette of field purposes for the assignment, so that purposes are explicit and comparable across students.

management (Fig. 1D) is integrated into the same flow: View Types reveals the class-defined palette of purpose options,

3.2.4
Instructor-configurable modes. To accommodate assignments with different expectations for independence, ORE
provides per-assignment controls. Peer-Linked Authoring (§3.2.3) can be disabled to avoid in-draft peer influence.
Access to Structured Exploration with Common-Role Text Segment Highlighting (§3.2.2) on the Explore page can be
scheduled: instructors may enable it during drafting for collective knowledge-building tasks, restrict visibility until
after submission for assessment-oriented tasks, or disable it entirely for private assignments. These controls modulate
whether and when peer context appears without altering the underlying authoring model.
Together, the Write and Explore views support a compose–contextualize–compare–revise workflow common in
peer-informed assignments that aligns with our design goals outlined in §3.1. On Write, students externalize thoughts

3.3
Example Scenario
In a graduate human–computer interaction course, Maya is using ORE to complete a Mock PC assignment. The goal is to
write a review of a research paper and, after submitting, read classmates’ reviews to collectively reach an accept/reject
decision. Because the instructor requires independent reviewing first, only the Write tab is visible at the beginning. Maya
sees that reviews are composed as fields, which prompts her to structure her thoughts. From the field-type dropdown,
she finds types already defined by earlier reviewers: “One-sentence Summary,” “Significance of the Problem,” “Related
Work,” “Approach,” “Evaluation,” “Discussion,” and “Suggested Next Steps.” She begins by adding a “One-sentence
Summary” and a “Significance of the Problem” field, then provides comments on “Related Work” and “Approach.” When
she wishes to comment on writing quality but finds no suitable type, she creates a new type, “Writing and Style,” and
places her remarks there. This new type then becomes available to the whole class. After writing, she drags fields to
refine flow, deletes a redundant field, taps Show Preview to read the stitched review, and submits her review.
Upon submission, the Explore tab appears. Twenty-five peers have posted, so there is substantial material to navigate.
Curious about what her peers think about the evaluation part of the paper, Maya toggles the “Evaluation” type to view
all comments about the evaluation study. Automatic role labels for this aspect, including participant, method, measure,
strengths, and weaknesses, highlight analogous segments across reviews, allowing her to quickly scan positive and
negative points for “Evaluation.” She briefly hides a label to reduce visual load, focusing on weaknesses and quickly
finds a common pattern. Many reviewers, like Maya, note limited participant diversity and recommend broader age
coverage. Spotting a methodological limitation she had not considered, she clicks Respond to register agreement and
add a short note. She repeats this process for “Related Work,” “Approach,” and “Discussion.” By reordering reviews by
similarity to her own, she is able to quickly gauge whether her views are common or outlying. After exploring the
class corpus type-by-type, she feels confident about the distribution of opinions and makes an informed accept/reject
decision for the Mock PC.
3.4
Implementation
ORE is a web application2 implemented with Next.js (App Router), React, and TypeScript, styled with Tailwind CSS. We
use Zustand, a lightweight client storage, for local editor states. Drafts autosave locally via a debounced POST to the
API for reload during the session. Usernames, field types, and submitted responses persist in a MongoDB database for
continued access.
In the Explore page, common-role text segment highlights are derived using a light-weight, human-in-the-loop
pipeline. First, a phrase splitter segments each sentence into clauses and base phrases including noun/verb chunks,
producing candidate spans. Next, a Large Language Model (OpenAI GPT-5) assigns a provisional role to each span

4
User Study
To evaluate the usability, usefulness, and effectiveness of ORE, we conducted a lab user study3 with 12 participants. We
are mainly interested in answering the following three research questions:
• RQ1: How does ORE influence how students write, explore, and learn from open-response assignments?
• RQ2: What is the perceived usability and utility of ORE’s features?
• RQ3: How effective is ORE at supporting students’ learning and feedback giving in open-response assignments?
4.1
Participants
We recruited 12 participants (5 male, 7 female) from an R1 university through departmental mailing lists. Seven of the 12
participants were between the ages of 25 and 34, while the remaining five were younger (18–24). The majority (six) were
PhD students, five were Master’s students, and one was a Post-Doc researcher. Participants reported using a variety of
tools to complete open-response assignments, including (from most frequent to least frequent) Word processors (e.g.,
Microsoft Word, Google Docs), collaborative writing platforms (e.g., Notion, Overleaf), AI writing/feedback tools (e.g.,
ChatGPT, Claude), learning management systems (e.g., Canvas, Blackboard, Moodle), grading/annotation tools (e.g.,
Gradescope, Turnitin), online survey or form tools (e.g., Google Forms, Qualtrics), handwritten responses (paper, tablet
stylus), and collaborative annotation tools (e.g., Perusall).
4.2
Study Setup
4.2.1
Task. Participants completed two short open-ended writing tasks, one on each interface condition. They were
each given a task description script (see Appendix A). For each task, participants were told to imagine a course
assignment in which they write a design argument4 [62] in response to a prior HCI research paper, which is a typical
peer-informed open-response assignment scenario that surfaces core affordances of ORE. To simulate a peer-informed
learning environment (e.g., a visible discussion board), several classmates’ responses were made available as references
that may or may not fully model good practice. A brief primer on design arguments was provided to each participant as
well.
For each task, participants were instructed to: (1) read the Abstract (and optionally the Introduction section) of the
research paper, (2) review their classmates’ responses, and (3) write the best short design argument (2-5 sentences) they
can. Since participants were not expected to have prior familiarity with design arguments, this workflow was chosen to
mimic a scaffolding scenario where learners leverage peer models to overcome the cold start problem and internalize
the genre norms of the assignment before attempting their own contribution.

Each task lasted 15 minutes total, including both exploration of classmates’ responses and writing. Participants
were allowed to draw language from the assigned paper when helpful (copy–paste phrases). The task instructions
also emphasized that (1) there are no right or wrong answers; (2) the study evaluates the interfaces’ effectiveness, not
participants’ intelligence; and (3) participants may agree or disagree with the paper and/or classmates’ responses.
4.2.2
Data. To simulate a populated learning environment and overcome the cold start problem, the peer response
lists were initially seeded with 10 synthetic design arguments generated by an LLM (OpenAI GPT-5). These seeds
were generated with prompts designed to mimic the variable quality and structure of typical student writing. Prior
work has similarly utilized synthetic peer content in controlled studies to balance ecological validity with experimental
control [58].
To increase the ecological validity of the corpus as the study progressed, we employed a bootstrapping approach. At
the end of each session, participants were asked: “As mentioned in the informed consent, would you like to anonymously
donate your written responses to the study, so that future participants can view them along with the existing ones, attributed
to a pseudonym? This won’t affect your compensation.” All participants consented. These responses were appended to
the corpus, meaning later participants encountered a larger and more realistic mix of synthetic and human-authored
content.
Importantly, we observed that there were no substantial differences in length or quality between the initial synthetic
set (𝑀= 63.5 words, 𝑀= 3.25 quality score) and the participant-generated set (𝑀= 65.75 words, 𝑀= 3.29 quality
score). Furthermore, because the study used a within-subjects design, any increase in task difficulty due to corpus
growth affected both the control and experimental conditions equally for a given participant, preserving the validity of
the comparative results.
4.2.3
Conditions. Participants used the following two interfaces in counterbalanced order:
• Interface A (ORE). The full ORE system with Structured Authoring, Structured Exploration with Common-Role
Text Segment Highlighting, Peer-Linked Authoring, similarity reordering and search features. All features are
enabled throughout the task.
• Interface B (baseline). A simplified version of ORE providing a minimal authoring interface and access to
classmates’ responses as a flat list, with only the search feature and the ability to “Respond” to a peer’s response,
similar to a discussion-board platform.
For each interface, participants wrote a design argument in response to one of two prior CHI papers (Paper 1,
Paper 2). Paper assignment to condition and condition order were both counterbalanced across participants to control
for order-related effects such as learning or fatigue and to account for differences in paper difficulty.
4.3
Procedure
All studies were conducted remotely via Zoom and facilitated by the first author. Each study took approximately 60
minutes, and each participant received a $20 Amazon gift card as compensation.
After providing verbal informed consent, participants completed a pre-study survey. They then read the task
instructions and received a short primer on design arguments. After that, they accessed the interfaces via a web link (no
installation required) and completed two counterbalanced task blocks on the two interfaces. Each task block consisted
of a quick walkthrough of the interface, a timed task that lasted 15 minutes with a 5-minute reminder, followed by a

6
Discussion
Overall, ORE significantly reduced mental demand and was rated significantly higher than the Baseline on all five
usability/utility items—making it easier to explore classmates’ writing, identify patterns and themes, distinguish strong
from weak responses, make writing clearer/easier, and deepen conceptual understanding. In the post–study survey,
participants also judged ORE significantly more helpful and more desirable for future use. Qualitative findings echoed
these results: participants described how structured authoring helped them plan and articulate ideas and how common
role text segment highlighting accelerated scanning and comparison. Taken together, the results validate the usability
and the usefulness of ORE’s core scaffolds for helping students explore, write, and learn from this open-response
assignment.
6.1
On structured writing and exploration
Our results suggest students welcome structure, but “structure” is task- and data-dependent. For tightly scoped tasks
such as paper reviews and design arguments, purpose-oriented fields align rhetorical function across writers and enable
like-with-like exploration. For divergent tasks such as brainstorming and debates on controversial topics, however,
idea-oriented fields can foreground distinct framings and alternatives while still allowing comparison by role when
appropriate. This dual stance is consistent with process models of writing [24, 70] and with writing-to-learn findings
that organization supports understanding [6].
Participants favored structured authoring and exploration, yet outcome quality did not improve significantly in a
single, short session. One interpretation may be that structure delivered process gains (lower mental demand, faster
sensemaking, clearer plans) without immediate product gains under tight time and limited iteration [6].
However, this structural scaffolding introduces a tension: the risk of over-structuring. While borrowing structure
helps novices internalize genre norms—a key benefit of social learning [5]—it may unintentionally steer students
toward surface-level mimicry or homogenization of responses. This suggests that the degree of enforced structure
should be inversely related to learner expertise: strict, purpose-labeled units scaffold novices by reducing the “blank
page” problem, while advanced students may require flexible, user-defined structures to encourage divergence. Future
designs should therefore make structures task-tunable and instructor-configurable, allowing educators to fade structural
constraints as students gain proficiency.
6.2
Beyond common-role text segment highlighting
Participants valued highlights that align analogous roles across responses. At the same time, some found dense labeling
overwhelming, consistent with cognitive theories that warn against excessive signaling [70] as well as more recent
HCI work that shows constrained highlighting is preferable to unlimited highlighting [45]. A practical pattern could
be selective defaulting: enabling a small, high-utility subset of roles by default, with progressive disclosure to add
more roles based on user needs. Additionally, participants also requested write-time highlights, which could bring
timely, formative feedback on rhetorical coverage at the risk of premature interference or convergence. Staging may
reconcile this risk by revealing write-time highlights only after a minimal draft or on explicit request, aligning with
productive-failure/engagement principles [15, 46].

6.3
Implications for Instruction and Adoption
While our study focused on the student experience, ORE also introduces new affordances and challenges for instructors.
Adopting ORE requires a pedagogical shift from solitary assessment to visible, community-based inquiry, which may
encounter friction in contexts where privacy is the default norm. However, this visibility offers distinct advantages for
scaling feedback. By reifying the rhetorical structure of student responses (e.g., separating need thesis from approach
thesis), ORE potentially transforms grading from a linear reading task into a structural verification task. Instructors
can visually scan the corpus to identify students who are missing specific rhetorical moves (e.g., an empty or missing
“Evidence” unit) without reading every word of prose. Furthermore, the ability to view all “Counter-arguments” side-by-
side enables instructors to rapidly identify common misconceptions or distinct clusters and outliers of student thought,
allowing for more targeted class-wide interventions rather than repetitive individual comments.
6.4
Limitations and future work
Despite ORE’s promising contributions, there are several limitations regarding our study design, participant population,
and current technical implementation. First, our participants were graduate students who likely possess higher familiarity
with peer-informed writing. While this population was appropriate for evaluating the usability of the system’s advanced
features, future work should investigate how novices, who may struggle more with the cognitive friction of structuring
their thoughts, adopt the tool. Relatedly, although ORE was designed for course use, our evaluation was a lab study
intended to validate usability and perceived utility. We plan to conduct a deployment of ORE in an authentic college-level
course to complement lab study findings with in–situ observations of how students engage and learn using the system.
Second, our role–aligned highlighting still relies on a human–in–the–loop. Future work could refine the pipeline
toward greater automation and robustness, while maintaining labeling reliability and accessibility. Finally, regarding
scalability, as the number of purpose and role types scales, simple linear rendering of fields and color-coding may
become ambiguous or overwhelming, necessitating hierarchical menus or filtering mechanisms. Similarly, our current
approach assumes a degree of structural convergence, but open–response work is diverse and structures are task- and
data-dependent. For tasks where student responses are highly divergent or require entirely unique structures, the utility
of common-role alignment may diminish. Future iterations could explore the use of adaptive schemas to handle this
heterogeneity across disciplines.
7
Conclusion
While peer-informed open-response assignments hold the potential to transform classrooms into communities of inquiry,
the messiness of unstructured prose often hinders deep social exchange and peer learning at scale. We presented ORE,
a system that addresses this barrier through structured authoring to make rhetorical purposes explicit and comparable
by design, and structured peer response exploration with role-based highlighting to facilitate sensemaking at scale.
Our evaluation confirms that these scaffolds significantly reduce the mental demand of writing and sensemaking,
helping students better identify patterns and themes in each other’s writing. Ultimately, this work opens the door for
new classes of structured, purpose-centered writing and sensemaking interfaces that can unlock the full pedagogical
potential of collaborative knowledge building and social learning.

A
User Study Task
Task Instructions:
In this study, you will complete two short open-ended writing tasks on two different interfaces.
Imagine you’re completing a course assignment where you’re asked to write a design argument in response to a
research paper. A few of your “classmates” have already submitted their responses, and you can view them as examples.
Your classmates are learning too—they may or may not fully understand design arguments yet either.
Your task is to 1) read the Abstract (and optionally the Introduction section) of the research paper, 2) review your
classmates’ responses, and 3) then write the best short design argument (2-5 sentences) you can.
What is a Design Argument?
A design argument is a short essay that makes the case for a proposed tool or approach by articulating the need for
the tool and how the proposed approach uniquely addresses the need in context. A more detailed description can be
found here.
You will have 15 minutes to complete each task, including both exploring others’ responses and writing your own
response.
Remember:
- There is no right or wrong answer.
- The tasks are a test of the system’s effectiveness, not your intelligence.
- It’s okay to use the language of the authors (i.e. copy-and-paste from the original paper).
- You can disagree with the author and/or your classmates!
