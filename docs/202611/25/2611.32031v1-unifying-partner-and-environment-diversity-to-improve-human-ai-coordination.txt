Abstract
Real-world applications of modern AI systems require generalization to both
unseen environments and novel human users. The field of zero-shot coordination
(ZSC) has focused primarily on deploying AI to cooperate with novel partners in
otherwise familiar environments. Popular population-based methods achieve this
by partnering the agent with a diversity of simulated human users during training
in a fixed environment, but does not consider the more realistic scenario where
both the users and the environment are unseen during training. Recently, training
across environments has been explored in training a single self-play agent, which
shows better generalization on both novel human users and unseen environments.
In this work, we ask whether jointly modeling partner diversity and environment
diversity yields stronger coordination with novel partners in novel environments.
We introduce Cross Environment Co-Play (CECP), a two-stage framework that
first trains a population of self-play agents across a distribution of procedurally
generated environments, and then trains a cooperator policy against this partner
population. To further couple social and environmental reasoning, we add a next-
state prediction module that encourages representations to encode both partner
behavior and task structure. We evaluate CECP on both a neuroscience-inspired
cooperative foraging task, and the popular ZSC benchmark Overcooked, under
held-out partner and held-out environment conditions. CECP achieves the strongest
performance among the compared baselines and attains state-of-the-art results
under our evaluation protocol for human-AI collaboration. We analyze the model’s
latent representation encoded by the next-state prediction module, showing it can
not only improve the performance of the cooperative AI, but also provide more
interpretable latents, demonstrated by the inference of partner skill levels. 1

Introduction
AI continues to make incredibly rapid progress, especially in digital domains. However, current AI
progress looks very different from the idealized future many of us imagined. We may have solved
the Navier-stokes problem, but a true household robot that helps with chores and alleviates manual
labor remains the stuff of science fiction; we are still left doing our own dishes! To realize this
vision of physically assistive AI, we need an AI agent that can assist many different people, in many
different homes, across a wide range of tasks; a capability humans exercise effortlessly, but one that
AI agents still struggle with. We define the joint adaptation to both environments and novel humans
as socio-situational shift.

Zero-shot coordination (ZSC) [3] provides a common framework for studying AI generalization
across diverse human users. In typical ZSC benchmarks such as the cooperative cooking game Over-
cooked, agents are paired with unseen human partners [24, 30, 13, 4, 27] on the same environment
that is used for training. Currently, over 700 papers have been written on ZSC using Overcooked,
and almost all of them focus purely on covering the space of possible partners, while neglecting
the dimension of being able to cooperate on-the-fly with a new partner on a new environment with
potentially new tasks or objectives. Only a handful of papers [11, 20], have focused on the idea of
training agents across multiple different environments in a ZSC context. Intriguingly, Jha et al. [11]
found that merely training on diverse environments can lead to effective generalization to human
partners, even when using self-play. However, as yet, no one has successfully explored combining
both partner and environment diversity. We hypothesize that algorithms that can handle this high
degree of both task and strategic complexity will be required for some of the most beneficial uses of
AI, such as embodied cooperative assistants.
In this work, we study cooperative generalization under simultaneous socio-situational shift. We
introduce Cross-Environment Co-Play (CECP), a meta-reinforcement learning approach designed to
unify these two axes. The training of CECP proceeds in two stages (Figure 1a). Training environment
and partner setup: A population of agents is trained via self-play over a procedurally generated
distribution of environments, producing a diverse set of policies (the partner pool) grounded in varied
dynamics. Training-stage for meta-RL: A cooperator policy is trained against a frozen population
sampled from the partner pool, exposing it to heterogeneous partner behaviors while retaining broad
environmental variation. Evaluation: The cooperator will be evaluated against novel partners (for
example, human players) in unseen environments.
Training along both partner behavior diversity and environment variation simultaneously makes
robust representation learning especially challenging due to high non-stationarity. To address this,
we incorporate a next-state prediction auxiliary objective for the cooperator agent (Figure 1b, blue).
The intuition is that in cooperative settings, predicting state transitions requires modeling both
environment dynamics and partner actions, encouraging the agent to build representations that capture
both. Enabling the cooperator agent to implicitly predict the partners’ actions may also lead to faster
inference and better adaptation to the partner’s coordination behaviors. With the ablation study, we
show that the auxiliary training objective consistently improves generalization performance across
the joint diversity training regime.

We evaluate CECP in a cooperative foraging task and Overcooked domains under protocols that
include both held-out partners and held-out environments. Across these settings, CECP consistently
outperforms strong baselines, demonstrating improved generalization under combined social and
environmental shifts. Furthermore, we conduct human experiments and show that under real-time
evaluation against novel human players, CECP reached the highest coordination performance with
human participants, and participants rated CECP agents as most likely to adapt to their actions. In
our interpretability analysis, we find that including the next state predictive objective and partner
diversity encourages the model to form representations of partner actions and skill level (Figure 1c).
Our contributions are:
• We propose CECP, a two-stage training paradigm that combines partner diversity and environment
diversity in a unified setting.
• We empirically demonstrate improved performance on both the cooperative foraging tasks and
Overcooked, a popular ZSC benchmark, under simultaneous socio-situational shift.
• We find that CECP agents form representations that predict partner actions and infer partner skill
level, providing a useful tool for insightful and interpretable analysis.

Related Work
Zero-shot Coordination (ZSC). Self-play (SP) describes the training method in which an agent
learns from repeated games with itself. Despite the great success in competitive tasks like Starcraft
and Go [25, 23], in collaborative settings, however, SP agents are known to struggle in cooperative
tasks, in that they often fail to coordinate with novel partners [24]. SP agents tend to learn specific
coordination patterns that are work only when playing with themselves. Extensive works [30, 13,
14, 22, 4] use Population-based training (PBT) methods [10, 3] to build a diverse population
of training partners, aiming to cover the test-time behaviors of novel human players. While the
above methods are typically trained and evaluated on the same single environments, our work further
explores injecting environment diversity in both the stage of creating the partner population and the
stage of training the cooperator against the population.
Meta-reinforcement learning (Meta-RL) aims to learn policies that adapt rapidly to novel tasks by
training on a distribution of tasks or environments, rather than a single fixed setting [2, 28, 6, 26, 31].
Similar methods have also been extended to multi-agent settings [32, 19, 18, 1, 29]. Recently in
ZSC, Jha et al. [11] demonstrated that leveraging meta-RL to train a single self-play agent across a
distribution of procedurally generated environments improves the agent’s ability to also coordinate
with unseen partners. However, when Jha et al. [11] attempted to combine partner and environment
diversity, they were not able to achieve good performance with standard models, motivating our
investigation into what techniques would be needed to handle this highly complex training distribution
Predictive Auxiliary Objective Recent work suggests that predictive modules can improve partner
adaptation in multi-agent coordination. In particular, explicit partner-action modeling has proven
useful for inferring partner intent [18] and conditioning ego behavior on predicted teammate actions
[32, 27, 17]. Beyond direct partner prediction, auxiliary predictive objectives have also improved
representation learning in cooperative multi-agent settings [7, 9]. In meta-RL and under partial
observability, predictive objectives have similarly been shown to improve Bayes-adaptive belief
representations [31, 12]. Our work is inspired by Ndousse et al. [16], which demonstrated that
a next-state predictive auxiliary objective enabled social learning in a multi-agent context, with
the reasoning that accurate next-state prediction must implicitly capture both partner action and
environment transitions.

We motivate our experiments with three questions related to our
method:
Q1: Can CECP leverage environment and partner diversity to gen-
eralize to novel environment/partner combinations?
Q2: Does our method obtain better performance than competitive
baselines when coordinating against novel human users?
Q3: How do next state prediction and partner diversity shape rep-
resentation learning and ZSC performance?
5.1
Environments
Cooperative Foraging: In this task, inspired by cooperation tasks
used in neuroscience [5], 2 agents are in a grid-world with 2 goal locations. To jointly receive
reward, both agents must reach and stay on the same goal location. To generate a distribution of
environments, we uniformly sample locations to place the two goals, and hold-out a set of goal
locations for evaluation (Fig 2).
Overcooked:
In the popular ZSC benchmark Overcooked [3], two players need to cooperate
with each other to divide cooking responsibilities, and players jointly receive a reward by delivering
completed dishes (Figure 3). 5 original layouts (Figure 14, Appendix) are traditionally used to
evaluate different coordination challenges. We evaluate our agents on the 5 original layouts, which
are held out during training for CEC and CECP methods, and on an additional 100 procedurally
generated layouts that are also held-out during training.
5.2
Baselines
We evaluate our method, CECP, against existing baselines in the literature, particularly self-play
(SP), fictitious co-play (FCP, [24]), and cross-environment cooperation (CEC, [11]). For Overcooked
evaluations, we also include: Maximum Entropy Population (MEP, [30]), a population-based method
which enforces population diversity through a population entropy bonus; Cross-play optimized,
Mixed-play enforced Diversity (CoMeDi, [22]), another population-based method which enforces
population diversity through mixed play; and Peer Adaptation method with Context-aware Exploration
(PACE, [15]), a method which seeks to improve a cooperator by adding a peer-identification reward.

Results
6.1
CECP generalizes to novel environment and partner settings in cooperative foraging
To investigate Q1, we evaluate CECP along with existing baselines in the Cooperative Foraging
task. To test generalization to both novel environments and novel partners, we hold out a test
set of goal locations, and a test set of partner agents for evaluation. Figure 4 shows these results.
While all methods perform well in the context of familiar partners and familiar environments, SP
(purple) and FCP (blue) both fail to generalize when goal locations are different from their training
environment. In comparison, CEC (green) generalizes to held-out goal locations well, but suffers a
drop in performance when paired with a novel partner. Under the simultaneous socio-situational shift
(Figure 4, bottom right), CEC gets about half the performance of CECP (red). Our method is the only
one that can simultaneously generalize to cooperate with new partners in new environments.
The drop in CEC performance from familiar to novel partners suggests that in this setting, environment
diversity alone does not prevent self-play agents from forming conventions, and motivates leveraging
the diversity of conventions formed across a population of CEC agents to train a cooperator agent
(CECP), to generalize to unseen partners.

familiar/novel environment and partner settings. All previous methods, except CEC, which also
adopts cross-environment training, fail on held-out environments. CECP shows additional robustness
on socio-situational shift.

To directly address Q2, we next tested each method’s ability to coordinate with humans on Coordina-
tion Ring and Counter Circuit layouts. N = 73 participants were tasked with playing a 200 timestep

(Figure 7), users rated CECP above baselines in adaptability, and lower than baselines in frustration
and interference.

How is CECP able to generalize under socio-situational
shift? To probe this question (Q3), we sought to under-
stand which components drive CECP’s improved Human
ZSC performance. Empirically, removing the next-state
prediction module leads to a clear drop in cross-play per-

importance of prediction.
To better understand why, we draw on the insight of
Ndousse et al. [16], who reason that in a cooperative
task, successful next-state prediction depends not only
on understanding how the environment evolves, but also
on anticipating what the partner will do (see Appendix
section 8.7, Figure 17).
More broadly, across many partners and environments, a
well-trained CECP agent predicts an AI partner’s move-
ment (up, down, left, right, or stay) at rates well above
chance throughout an episode (Figure9a, Best AI Part-
ners), suggesting that next-state prediction encourages the
agent to learn an implicit model of partner behavior. This
movement prediction generalizes to humans as well, where
human subject movements are also predicted by CECP at
levels above chance (Figure 9a, Human Subjects). Further-
more, we find that CECP’s ability to accurately predict its human partner’s movements is correlated
with human’s performance, suggesting that movements stemming from a more-skilled partner are
easier to predict.

Conclusion
We introduce CECP, a two-stage training framework that unifies partner and environment diversity for
zero-shot coordination. By training a partner population via self-play across procedurally generated
environments and then training a cooperator against that population, CECP produces agents that
generalize across simultaneous partner and environment shift. We further showed that a next-state
prediction objective and partner diversity improves performance and encourages the cooperator to
form representations that implicitly track partner behavior and skill level.
Limitations and future work: CECP performance is limited by the amount of diversity present in
the both the partner population and environment distribution it is trained on. Future work improving
on partner population diversity or broadening the range of environments may further improve
generalization performance of CECP.
We find that next-state prediction improves coordination, but its mechanism is not fully understood:
the latent representation captures broad skill level rather than fine-grained, partner-specific action
intent. Disentangling partner-specific and environment-specific information in the latent z (for
instance via a structured bottleneck) may make representations more interpretable and sample-
efficient.
Finally, while the Overcooked task has been the standard for testing Human-AI coordination, more re-
cent benchmarks [8] featuring additional complexity and partial observability may further demonstrate
the efficacy of CECP.

CECP Training
Predictor module details. (1) An RNN encoder qϕ(z | τ0:t), with observations fed in via a CNN
feature extractor, mapping observation histories to a latent representation z. (Figure 1b, green). (2) A
prediction head pθ(st+1 | z, ac
t) that decodes the next state from the latent and the cooperator’s own
action (Figure 1b, blue). The latent z and one-hot action encoding are concatenated, projected to a
flat vector, reshaped into a grid, then passed through de-convolution layers to reconstruct the next
observation. (3) A policy head πψ(ac
t | z), conditioned on the latent z and trained via PPO (Figure
1b, red).
Procedural generation details. To generate a distribution of environments, we leverage the procedural
environment generation method proposed in [11], varying onion, pot, and delivery port placement as
well as layout orientation, which is an extension of the JaxMARL project [21].

ments. To do so, we follow the procedural environment generation and training proposed in CEC
[11].

training checkpoints are used to form a partner population, with 2 partner agents held-out for testing.

Overall, we observe that the CECP partner population’s behavioral diversity is similar to that of
FCP’s partner population, though across all layouts, CECP’s min JSDpair is slightly higher than other
methods. While diversity-aware methods (MEP, CoMeDi) increase JSDpop, they also produce lower

conventions.
To further confirm that agents in CECP’s partner population are indeed forming distinct conventions,
we next examined each partner’s average action distributions under specific scenarios in the Coordi-
nation Ring and Counter Circuit layouts. In Coordination Ring, when an agent is holding an onion,
facing upward, positioned in the bottom left corner, the population is split between policies preferring
to move up and initiate a clockwise movement, and policies preferring to moving right to initiate a
counterclockwise movement (Figure 13a). Similarly, in Counter Circuit, when an agent is holding an
onion, facing leftward, positioned right below the right pot, the population is split between policies
preferring to moving left, and policies preferring to move up/interact with the pot (Figure 13b).

8.5
Human-AI Coordination Evaluation
Participants: We recruited N = 73 participants via Prolific under a protocol approved by our
university’s IRB. Participants were aged 19–72 (median = 28.5), of whom 43.1% identified as male,
55.6% as female, and 1.4% did not to say. Prior to the experiment, participants reported their
familiarity with the Overcooked video game on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = no experience, 5 = very
experienced). The median experiment time was 34 minutes, and each participant was paid $11 USD
(averaging $19.46/hr) for their time and effort.
Procedure: Following a tutorial round, each participant was randomly assigned to one of two layouts:
Counter Circuit or Coordination Ring. Participants then played one episode with each of the tested AI
methods, for a total of 6 episodes per participant. For each method, a random agent was drawn from
a pool of 8 trained agents (each initialized with a different random seed) to control for seed-level
variance. Each episode lasted 200 timesteps.

8.7
Partner movement prediction
To assess whether agents implicitly model their partner’s behavior, we evaluate partner action
prediction accuracy as a proxy for learned partner representations. At each timestep, the CECP
cooperator agent is queried for its predicted next state conditioned on its own sampled action. The
partner’s predicted action is then inferred from this predicted state by examining the difference in
partner positions between the predicted next state and the current state.
Figure 17: Example next-state prediction from a CECP agent (red), coordinating with a partner (blue).
At state st (left), in order to fully predict the true next state st+1 (middle), CECP’s prediction (right)
must capture both the blue partner’s action to put the onion in the pot, and the environment transition
of onions in the pot increasing by 1.
8.8
Latent Representation Analysis
Partner Checkpoint decoding: We evaluate a trained CECP agent that is paired with partners
of early/middle/late checkpoint stages. For each partner, we run 20 episodes, and collect the per-
timestep activations in the CECP agent’s latent z layer . At each timestep, a multi-class linear
discriminant analysis LDA classifier is fit across partners and episodes on the full latent vector to
classify checkpoint stage. We report the average test classifier accuracy using 5-fold cross-validation.
PCA analysis: The matrix collected from a CECP agent’s latent z activity is reshaped to
(n_units, n_timesteps × n_episodes), mean-centered per unit, and decomposed via full SVD. Episode
trajectories are projected into the space of the top two principle components, and plotted as time-
indexed curves colored by partner label (early vs. late), with average trajectories plotted as well.
