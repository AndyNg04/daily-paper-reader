Abstract—Teleoperation of mobile robots represents a critical
capability
for
operations
in
hazardous
environments,
yet
maintaining adequate situational awareness remains challenging
without effective visualization and control interfaces. This work
presents a multi-modal extended reality (XR) system for robot
teleoperation and scene exploration that integrates immersive
visual and haptic feedback modalities. To address motion sickness
commonly induced by 360-degree camera feeds in head-mounted
displays (HMDs), the system employs a fixed dual-camera
configuration: a forward-facing camera for primary navigation
and a rear-facing camera for enhanced situational awareness
without operator discomfort. For navigation in confined or
low-visibility conditions, a 16-channel Velodyne LiDAR generates
real-time 2D occupancy grid maps, which are converted into
vibrational haptic feedback on handheld controllers to provide
intuitive, non-visual obstacle detection. User evaluation with 20
participants demonstrates that this haptic modality significantly
reduces the cognitive load associated with obstacle avoidance
during teleoperation.

I. INTRODUCTION
Mobile robot teleoperation remains a fundamental approach
for scenarios where full autonomy is unreliable or human
judgment is essential, such as in disaster response, industrial
inspection,
and
exploration
of
hazardous
environments
[1],
[2].
XR
teleoperation
systems
leverage
real-time
sensor data, including visual and spatial information, to
construct immersive operator interfaces. By rendering the
robot’s sensory stream in real time, these systems support
timely and safe operator decision-making, a principle also
adopted in recent sensor-driven digital twin frameworks
for safety-critical monitoring [3]. While advancements in
sensing and autonomy have expanded robotic capabilities,
the efficacy of teleoperation relies heavily on the quality
of
the
human-robot
interface
(HRI).
Specifically,
the
operator’s ability to maintain situational awareness of the
robot’s immediate surroundings is critical for navigation
performance and safety [4]. Traditional teleoperation interfaces
predominantly rely on visual feedback provided by onboard
cameras, visualized on 2D monitors. Although widely adopted,
these systems often suffer from a limited field of view
(FoV) and insufficient depth perception, making it difficult
for operators to accurately assess distances to obstacles or
comprehend the global spatial layout [18].

mitigate

significantly

within a 3D reconstruction of the remote environment,

to
conventional

[5],
[6].
Studies
comparing desktop monitors to VR interfaces consistently
demonstrate that the spatially coherent nature of VR leads to
superior situational awareness and manipulation performance
[16]. Existing literature has explored visual enhancement
strategies in VR. Approaches utilizing RGB-D cameras and
Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) techniques
allow for the real-time reconstruction of 3D environments
[3], [7]. However, high-fidelity visualizations often introduce
significant
bandwidth

and
visual

Furthermore, improper camera handling in VR can lead to
severe motion sickness (cyber-sickness). To address these
issues, recent dual-mode architectures have been proposed
that decouple navigation views from task-specific views, such
as manipulation, allowing each viewpoint to be optimized
for its respective perceptual requirements [11]. Specifically,
the use of fixed stereoscopic dual-camera feeds has been
shown to provide necessary depth perception without the
disorientation often caused by 360-degree video streams [12],
[13]. However, dependence on visual feedback alone presents
a significant constraint. In conditions of low visibility, narrow
passages, or high visual complexity, operators may not
identify nearby obstacles through visual feedback alone.
To address this, alternative sensory modalities have been
investigated. Haptic feedback has been successfully employed
in robotic manipulation to convey contact forces [9] to the
teleoperator and also to facilitate more intuitive navigation by
employing HMD devices [10].
While haptic feedback has been widely explored in mobile
robot
teleoperation,
ranging
from
vibrotactile
formation
control [25] to the use of haptic tablets in 2D interfaces
[26], these conventional systems largely rely on non-immersive
interfaces that still demand high visual cognitive load.
Prior studies have demonstrated that multi-modal haptic
systems
significantly
improve
operator
immersion
and
efficiency compared to visual-only feedback [15]. More
recent advancements have introduced haptics into immersive
environments but often rely on cumbersome force-feedback
mechanisms
applied
to
the
operator’s
body
[27]
or
focus exclusively on fine manipulation and grasping [28],
[29].
In
contrast,
the
novelty
of
our
system
lies
in
integrating lightweight, directional haptic feedback within
a fully immersive XR environment to achieve sensory
substitution. By mapping a real-time LiDAR occupancy
grid directly to left and right handheld VR controllers, our
architecture provides a continuous, non-visual “sixth sense”
for spatial awareness.Additionally, theoretical frameworks
using occupancy grid maps continue to serve as the primary
approach for robust environmental representation in these
autonomous and semi-autonomous systems [14].
To incorporate robust mapping and intuitive user feedback,
we propose a multimodal XR-based teleoperation system.
While recent advancements in teleoperation have relied on
SLAM approaches to enhance situational awareness through
photorealistic
3D
reconstructions,
these
systems
require
immense computational hardware (e.g., high-end GPUs) and
place the entirety of the spatial processing burden on the
operator’s visual channel. In contrast, our system proposes
a fundamentally different architectural solution: rather than
maximizing visual fidelity, we maximize cognitive efficiency.
By
integrating
a
dual-camera
stream,
lightweight
2D
occupancy grids with low latency, and LiDAR-driven haptic
feedback, our system provides immediate spatial awareness
that does not require heavy rendering times and remains robust
in low-visibility conditions where RGB-D-based SLAM would
fail.
A direct haptic feedback loop maps real-time LiDAR scans
to vibrational cues on handheld VR controllers. This enables
operators to perceive the proximity of obstacles, reducing
visual dependence and mitigating sensory mismatches that
contribute to simulator sickness [13]. The main contributions
of this work are:
• A
human-centred
immersive
teleoperation
interface
designed to enhance situational awareness for efficient
navigation;
• A multimodal perception framework that integrates
dual camera streams with real-time 2D occupancy grid
mapping, enabling operators to access both local visual
feedback and global spatial context simultaneously;
• A LiDAR-based haptic feedback mechanism that delivers
distance-dependent, directionally intuitive vibration cues
through handheld VR controllers to enhance obstacle
awareness without increasing visual cognitive load.

B. Visual Feedback and Mapping
The following describes the visualization and mapping
components of the system:
• Camera-Based Visual Feedback: The representation of
the camera feeds and the environmental map within
the head-mounted display (HMD) must be carefully
designed to support effective navigation. The visual
interface subscribes to the dual RGB streams transmitted
via ROS 2 topics. Within the Unity environment [22],
these feeds are rendered on virtual display surfaces
anchored relative to the operator’s viewpoint. Rather
than performing computationally expensive stereo depth
reconstruction, the system leverages the fixed baseline to
provide complementary perspectives. This dual camera
setup reduces computing load and eliminates blind spots
inherent to single-camera configurations [12]. Crucially,
the rendering of camera feeds is decoupled from the
operator’s head pose. Instead of tracking the user’s
head movements directly (which can induce sensory
conflict), the feeds act as stabilized visual references.
This decoupling is a strategic measure to mitigate motion
sickness (cybersickness), allowing the operator to scan
the environment visually without being constrained to the
robot’s instantaneous orientation [13].
• 2D Occupancy Grid Mapping: To establish global spatial
context beyond the immediate camera field of view
(FoV), the system generates a real-time 2D occupancy
grid map derived from LiDAR data. Mapping is handled
by the SLAM Toolbox package within the ROS 2
ecosystem. The SLAM algorithm publishes a standard
nav_msgs/Occupancy Grid message, encoding the
probability of free, occupied, and unknown space based
on accumulated LiDAR scans and odometry. This map
is streamed to Unity and projected as a planar overlay
within the virtual environment. The robot’s estimated
pose is synchronized with this map in real time, allowing
the operator to correlate local visual cues with the
global topology. This hybrid visualization addresses
the limitations of purely vision-based teleoperation by
revealing obstacles that lie outside the active camera
frustum [14], [16].
C. ROS–Unity Integration
Data exchange between the robot and the XR interface
is managed via a TCP-based ROS–Unity communication
bridge. Sensor data (images, LiDAR scans, and odometry)
is serialized into standard ROS messages and transmitted
to the Unity client, where it is deserialized into native
XR assets. In the reverse direction, control inputs from the
HTC Vive Pro handheld controllers [19] are captured by
the Unity input system, translated into linear and angular
velocity commands, and published to the robot’s velocity topic.
This bidirectional architecture ensures modularity; perception,
mapping, and control subsystems can be developed and
validated independently [9].
III. MULTIMODAL PERCEPTION INTERFACE
The teleoperation interface is designed to mitigate the tunnel
vision often associated with camera-only teleoperation. By
integrating visual streams with spatial mapping, the system
balances immediate maneuverability with global situational
awareness through a unified egocentric operating paradigm. In
Fig. 3, the layout shows the front and rear camera views and a
2D occupancy grid map. A menu interface allows operators to

A. Visual Layout and Interaction
The XR environment presents two distinct layers of
information. The primary layer consists of live feeds from
the dual RealSense cameras, rendered as stabilized virtual
panels fixed relative to the robot’s forward vector. This fixation
ensures that the operator’s visual reference remains consistent
with the robot’s heading, reducing the sensory conflict that
contributes to simulator sickness [13].
B. Global Context Awareness
To compensate for the limited FoV of the physical cameras,
the system visualizes the generated 2D occupancy grid
map as a secondary planar element. We place the map
below the operator’s main view, positioned similarly to an
automotive instrument panel, rather than overlaying it on
the video. This prevents visual clutter, allowing the operator
to control/navigate the robot using the camera feed while
periodically checking the map for hidden obstacles [14]. In
this way, the operator maintains continuous awareness of the
robot’s global position.
C. LiDAR-Based Haptic Feedback
While the visual interface addresses spatial orientation,
visual load can still become overwhelming in cluttered
environments. To offload perceptual demand, the system
implements a vibrotactile feedback loop driven by raw LiDAR
data. This subsystem functions as a background collision
warning mechanism, providing intuitive cues without requiring
direct visual attention. The system computes the minimum
Euclidean distance to the nearest obstacle, denoted as dmin.
To provide graded feedback, a linear decay function maps
this distance to a vibration intensity I ∈[0, 1], which is sent
to the corresponding HTC Vive Pro handheld controller. The
vibration intensity is defined as:

While recent studies in whole-body force-feedback have
proposed non-linear mappings, such as sigmoid functions,
to prevent the physical discomfort of constant pushing
during navigation, our system employs localized vibrotactile
feedback. For such vibrotactile cues, linear scaling effectively
functions as an intuitive background collision warning without
introducing the complexity of non-linear tuning. To resolve
this haptic feedback in a way that matches the operator’s
egocentric reference frame, the processed LiDAR point cloud
is segmented along the robot’s longitudinal axis, effectively
dividing the scan into left and right sectors relative to the
robot’s base link. Consequently, the system establishes a direct
lateral correspondence: obstacles detected in the left sector
modulate the vibration of the left handheld controller, while
those in the right sector drive the right controller. Using this
direct mapping, the operator can instinctively perceive the
direction of a potential collision, such as a wall near the robot’s
side, using only vibrational feedback, even if the obstacle is
outside the camera view.
D. Teleoperation control
Control authority is maintained through the HTC Vive
Pro handheld controllers using a direct mapping scheme to
ensure low-latency response. The system is deployed on a
Clearpath Husky mobile robot, a differential-drive platform
governed by linear (v) and angular (ω) velocity commands.
As illustrated in Fig. 4, the operator and the mobile robot can
be located in different areas. The operator interacts with the
robot exclusively through an immersive VR interface, without
direct line-of-sight to the physical platform. This separation
emphasizes the fully remote nature of the teleoperation
setup and eliminates reliance on visual contact with the

setup.
robot or its environment. By directly translating inputs from
the handheld controller into robot velocity commands, the
system ensures that control remains intuitive, even though
the operator is working remotely. This arrangement enables
safe experimentation and deployment in environments where
human presence is impractical or hazardous, while maintaining
a strong sense of agency and control for the operator.
User input is captured from the vertical and horizontal
axes of the trackpad of the controller held in the operator’s
dominant hand. These analog values are normalized and
mapped to the standard ROS geometry_msgs/Twist
message structure. To prevent abrupt acceleration that could
induce motion sickness or wheel slip, the raw inputs are passed
through a software-defined velocity smoothing filter before
being published to the /cmd_vel topic.
The control loop and the haptic feedback loop operate
asynchronously. Nevertheless, the operator effectively closes
the overall control loop by responding to the vibration cues. As
the vibration intensity I (see Equation 1) increases, indicating
closer proximity to obstacles, the operator is naturally
prompted to reduce the linear velocity v or adjust the angular
velocity ω. This results in safer navigation behavior without
imposing autonomous braking or shared-control overrides by
the system [16].
IV. EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION
To evaluate the efficacy of the proposed multimodal
interface, we conducted a comparative user study focusing on
navigation performance, safety, and operator workload. The
study aimed to quantify the benefits of integrating occupancy
grid mapping and haptic feedback into the teleoperation loop.
A. Experimental Setup
The
experiments
were
conducted
in
a
static
indoor
environment with a fixed obstacle configuration along the
navigation path (see Fig. 1). Obstacles of varying height and
material were deliberately placed along the route, including
low-profile objects and partially occluded panels, to challenge
the robot’s perception under limited field-of-view conditions.
The layout required precise maneuvering and continuous
path adjustment, emphasizing the robot’s ability to detect
obstacles, maintain clearance in confined spaces, and safely
traverse cluttered indoor environments. During the experiment,
the users were equipped with a VR headset and two VR
controllers to interact with the robot A total of 20 volunteers
participated in the study. The demographic survey indicated
that 55% of participants had zero prior experience with robot
teleoperation, and 55% had never used VR headsets before.
This novice-heavy distribution highlights the system’s intuitive
design, as no extensive training was provided.
Participants were required to teleoperate the Clearpath
Husky robot from a designated start point to a finish point as
quickly as possible while minimizing collisions. To evaluate
the impact of each sensory channel, each participant performed
the navigation task under three distinct modalities:
• Mode 1 - baseline: Visual feedback only (front and rear
RGB cameras).
• Mode 2 - visual augmentation: Dual cameras with
real-time 2D occupancy grid map.
• Mode 3 - proposed system: Dual cameras with a 2D
occupancy grid map and LiDAR-based haptic feedback.

C. Qualitative Analysis (User Study)
Following
the
trials,
participants
completed
a
comprehensive
usability
questionnaire.
The
user
study
aimed to identify the best modality for teleoperating a mobile
robot in virtual reality. The three navigation modalities
were evaluated in terms of their usability, intuitiveness, and
efficiency. The results strongly validate the system’s design
choices regarding comfort and feedback utility:
• Physical comfort and motion sickness: Simulator sickness
is a critical challenge in VR teleoperation. Our decoupled
camera rendering approach proved highly effective, as
95% of participants reported no symptoms of nausea,
dizziness, or fatigue after the experiment, and only
one participant reported minor eye strain. These results
validate the suitability of the proposed system for
extended use.
• Efficacy of haptic feedback: Survey responses (see
Fig. 6) showed overwhelming support for the haptic
subsystem. Participants strongly agreed that the vibration
feedback helped them avoid collisions and that it was
immediately clear why the controllers were vibrating.
Participants disagreed with the statement that vibrations
were distracting, confirming that the feedback loop
provided intuitive cues without increasing cognitive load.
• Sensory reliance: When asked about their navigation
strategy in Mode 3, a large number of users agreed with
the statement: “I relied more on the vibration than the
video to know when I was too close”. This confirms our
hypothesis that haptic feedback successfully offloads the
visual channel, allowing operators to perceive obstacles
in their blind spots without constantly scanning the video
feed.
V. PERFORMANCE ANALYSIS & OPERATIONAL INSIGHTS
The experimental results validate the core hypothesis
that multimodal sensory feedback significantly enhances
teleoperation
performance,
particularly
in
restricted
environments. Beyond the raw metrics of time and collisions,
several key insights emerge regarding operator behaviour and
system applicability.
A. Mitigation of Cognitive Load
A primary challenge in remote teleoperation is the high
cognitive demand placed on the operator, who must mentally
reconstruct a 3D environment from 2D video feeds. The
qualitative feedback from our study suggests that the proposed
haptic system successfully offloads this demand. By providing
directional haptic cues, the system effectively externalizes
the collision-check process. Operators no longer need to
constantly scan the periphery of the video feed for obstacles;
instead, they can focus their visual attention on the global
path planning (via the map) and forward trajectory, relying on
the haptic interface to monitor the robot’s immediate vicinity.
This sensory substitution is likely the driving factor behind the
23% improvement in navigation speed, as operators felt safer
maintaining higher velocities in narrow corridors.
B. Bridging the Skill Gap
One of the most significant findings of this study is the
system’s ability to democratize expert-level performance. In
the baseline modality (Mode 1), the performance variance
was high, with novice users struggling significantly with depth
perception and spatial awareness. The introduction of haptic
feedback (Mode 3) drastically reduced this variance (as evident
in Fig. 5), allowing users with no prior robotics experience to
perform comparably to those with moderate experience. This
suggests that the multimodal interface acts as a skill equalizer,
making it highly suitable for rapid-deployment scenarios, such

C. Implications for Low-Visibility Scenarios
While our experiments were conducted in a well-lit
indoor environment, the reliance on occupancy grids and
LiDAR-based
haptics
provides
significant
advantages
in
low-visibility conditions. In real-world scenarios involving
smoke, fog, or darkness, standard RGB cameras (Mode 1)
exhibit limited functionality. However, the proposed system’s
reliance on LiDAR for both mapping (Mode 2) and haptic
feedback (Mode 3) implies that navigation capabilities are
preserved even if the visual input is compromised. This
redundancy makes the architecture particularly robust for
subterranean or hazardous environments.
D. Limitations
Despite the demonstrated success, the current system
relies on a 2D occupancy grid, which assumes a structured
environment
with
vertical
walls.
This
approach
poses
limitations when encountering obstacles that do not appear
at the LiDAR’s scan height, such as tables with overhanging
tops or uneven terrain. This limitation, observed in the
“stop-and-check”
behaviour
in
Mode
2,
highlights
the
necessity for 3D volumetric mapping. Although separating
the camera view helped prevent motion sickness, it caused
a temporary sense of disorientation. New users required a
short time to adapt to the difference between where they were
looking and where the robot was moving.
VI. CONCLUSION & FUTURE WORK
This paper presented a robust, multimodal XR-based
teleoperation framework designed to address the inherent
limitations
of
conventional
visual-only
interfaces.
By
integrating a stabilized dual-camera stream, a real-time 2D
occupancy grid map, and a directional LiDAR-based haptic
feedback, the proposed system significantly enhances operator
situational awareness while mitigating simulator sickness.
Our
experimental
validation
with
20
participants
demonstrated that the addition of haptic feedback serves as an
intuitive sixth sense, reducing the cognitive load required for
obstacle avoidance. The quantitative results show that the full
multimodal system (Mode 3) improved navigation efficiency
by 23% and reduced collision frequency by 60% compared
to the baseline. Notably, the system proved particularly
beneficial for novice operators, effectively narrowing the
performance gap between inexperienced users and experts.
Furthermore, the decoupling of the camera view from the
operator’s head pose successfully eliminated motion sickness
symptoms in 95% of trials, validating the ergonomic viability
of the system for extended operations.
While the current system establishes a strong foundation
for immersive teleoperation, several key enhancements can be
made to further expand its capabilities:
• 3D map construction and local costmaps: We are
currently extending the mapping module to support 3D
volumetric mapping [17]. This will enable the operator to
perceive vertical obstacles, such as overhangs or uneven
terrain, that a 2D occupancy grid might miss, and will
facilitate the generation of local costmaps to support more
intelligent path planning.
• Dynamic obstacle filtering: To improve map clarity in
populated environments, we are developing dynamic
filtering algorithms. This will differentiate between static
infrastructure, e.g., walls, and moving agents, e.g.,
humans and robots, preventing transient objects from
leaving permanent ghost obstacles on the navigation map.
• Multi-sensor fusion: To address the minimum range
limitations of LiDAR (near-field blind spots), we are
studying the addition of ultrasonic or infrared proximity
sensors. Fusing this data will provide redundant safety


checks for objects in immediate proximity to the robot.
Beyond these enhancements, the proposed framework could
also be integrated with Vision-Language Models. By utilizing
the headphones and microphone integrated into the VIVE
headset, the addition of VLM-based tools can allow operators
to request semantic descriptions of the scene from the
robot [24], or to interact with the surroundings through
natural language. Such an integration can further benefit
from robot-agnostic control capabilities, enabling commands
to be directed to specific robots through a centralized and
robot-agnostic architecture [23].
