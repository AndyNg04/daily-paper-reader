Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) can make decisions that affect other agents, but what happens after a
harmful or unsuccessful interaction remains poorly understood. We investigate whether apologies by
LLM agents are associated with subsequent cooperative behaviour, using controlled Prisoner’s Dilemma
experiments with four commercial models (Claude Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Mistral
Large 3). Across payoff conditions, we examine when agents apologise, the reasons they provide, and the
actions that follow. We also examine how access to one round of interaction history affects behaviour and,
in a second experiment, whether and how agents offer compensation alongside their apologies. Apology
behaviour differed substantially across models and preceding interaction outcomes, and verbal expressions
of cooperation did not always align with subsequent actions.
Notably, Claude Opus 4.8 apologised
even after mutual cooperation, while Mistral Large 3 apologised after being exploited but continued to
defect even when its opponent cooperated. After unilateral defection, positively compensated apologies
from GPT-5.5 and Gemini 3.1 Pro were followed by mutual cooperation, unlike those from Claude
and Mistral. Our findings suggest that apologies, compensation, and strategic behaviour are distinct:
conciliatory messages may precede continued defection, while costly apologies may go unanswered. This
has important implications for the study of machine behaviour and human-AI interactions, as verbal
signals alone may not reliably reflect an AI system’s subsequent actions.
Keywords: Large Language Models (LLMs), Trust, LLM Apologies, Strategic Behaviour, Prisoner’s
Dilemma, AI Agents, Game Theory

Introduction
An AI agent may apologise following an adverse outcome, yet its subsequent behaviour may not align
with the message conveyed by the apology. For a human, this distinction between saying sorry and
actually repairing a relationship is familiar. For an autonomous AI agent, however, it raises a more
fundamental question of trustworthiness: to what extent does an apology, as a form of non-binding
“cheap talk” or strategic communication, align with subsequent behaviour?[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. Recent
work on multi-agent interactions has shown discrepancies between agents’ communicated commitments
and their subsequent strategic decisions, highlighting the importance of examining observable behaviour
alongside verbal expressions [3]. As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as interactive
agents, their behaviour is no longer limited to generating isolated responses [6], [7], [8].
They can
negotiate, coordinate, make decisions, and interact repeatedly with humans and other agents [9], [10],
[11]. Understanding how such systems respond to adverse interactions is therefore becoming an important
part of evaluating machine behaviour and AI safety [12].
Existing research on AI apologies has primarily examined how human recipients respond to attempts
to repair trust [13]. For example, Kim and Song [14] showed that the effectiveness of an intelligent
agent’s apology depended on how the apology was presented and how responsibility for the failure was
attributed. More recently, Turel and Cui [15] compared AI and human apologies following errors and
examined their effects on reliance. Harland et al. [16] proposed an Act–Assess–Apologise framework in
which an AI agent identifies undesirable behaviour, issues an apology, and adapts its subsequent actions
to better align with user preferences. However, these studies do not establish how LLM agents decide
to apologise in strategic interactions or whether their apologies are followed by cooperative behaviour.
Furthermore, work on LLM sycophancy shows that language models often generate superficial apologies
or reverse stated positions to satisfy immediate user feedback without genuine state modification [17],
[18].
Strategic games provide a structured experimental setting for examining how LLM agents make deci-
sions when their choices affect one another’s outcomes. Akata et al. [6] demonstrated that LLMs can be
studied through repeated 2×2 games and exhibit systematic behavioural patterns across different strate-
gic environments. Such games provide an advantage for studying apology behaviour because a preceding
outcome can be experimentally specified, after which apology decisions and subsequent strategic choice
can be examined separately. The Prisoner’s Dilemma is particularly suitable for this purpose because
agents choose between cooperation and defection, producing four well-defined outcome configurations.
These outcomes also provide different contexts in which an apology may be expected. Following mutual
cooperation (R), neither agent has an obvious reason to apologise, whereas unilateral defection creates
an asymmetry in responsibility: the defecting agent has a clearer basis for apologising than the agent
that cooperated. Following mutual defection (P), either agent may apologise to signal a willingness to
restore cooperation. These expectations are not necessarily prescriptions for optimal behaviour, since an
apology may also serve a strategic purpose independent of responsibility for the preceding outcome. For
example, an agent may apologise despite having cooperated, potentially influencing its opponent’s subse-
quent decision. Examining such departures from responsibility-based expectations, alongside the actions
that follow, can help distinguish conventional trust-repair behaviour from other uses of apology-related
communication.
Within strategic interactions, apologies can function as social signals whose behavioural significance
may depend on incentives and associated costs. In evolutionary models of repeated cooperation, apology
and forgiveness can help resolve failures in ongoing agreements [5], [19], [20], [21]. Ho [22] formalised
apologies as signals in a trust-game framework, showing how apology communication can influence
subsequent strategic behaviour.
From a signalling perspective, however, the credibility of a costless
apology may depend on the incentives and interaction context. Ohtsubo and Watanabe [23] found that
people perceived apologies involving a greater cost as more sincere, consistent with the idea that costly
actions can make signals more credible. For LLM agents, subsequent cooperation or defection provides
an observable behavioural response that can be examined alongside the preceding apology signal.
Taken together, these research streams leave an important question insufficiently understood: how
do LLM agents’ apology decisions, stated rationales, and willingness to provide compensation relate
to their own and their opponents’ subsequent strategic choices? Rather than attempting to determine
whether an LLM is internally “sincere”, we examine whether its stated apology rationale is behaviourally
consistent with its subsequent strategic choice. In particular, we distinguish between the language of an
apology and the action that follows it. A model may, for example, identify “repair trust” as the reason
for apologising but subsequently choose to defect. Such a pattern should not be interpreted as evidence

To address this gap, we develop a controlled-outcome framework based on the repeated Prisoner’s
Dilemma (PD) to examine apology behaviour and subsequent strategic decisions in LLM agents. Our
framework builds on FAIRGAME, which supports systematic evaluation of LLM agents in strategic
games [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], and draws on the controlled-outcome strategy elicitation approach
of Pal et al. [31]. Rather than allowing agents to generate the interaction preceding an apology, we
experimentally specify one of four PD outcomes: mutual cooperation, unilateral cooperation by the focal
agent, unilateral defection by the focal agent, or mutual defection. Following each specified outcome,
both agents independently decide whether to apologise and, if so, provide a short apology message
and a structured reason code. Their signals, including compensation decisions where applicable, are
then mutually disclosed before both agents independently choose whether to cooperate or defect in the
subsequent round. We examine these decisions across four LLMs and different payoff variants. This
design separates the experimentally supplied strategic context from the agents’ apology decisions and
subsequent strategic choices.
We further examine how access to interaction history relates to apology decisions and subsequent
strategic behaviour. In Experiment 1, agents receive either the current specified outcome alone (No-
memory) or that outcome together with the outcome and apology state from the previous game round
(Memory-1). Apologies are costless in both conditions. This comparison allows us to examine whether
apology decisions and subsequent strategic choices depend only on the immediate outcome or also vary
with the availability and content of one-step historical information. Such a distinction is important in
repeated-game settings because the significance of an apology may depend not only on the immediate
interaction but also on preceding outcomes and apology behaviour.
We then introduce the option of material compensation to examine apologies that may involve a direct
payoff cost. In Experiment 2, agents receive the current specified outcome without additional interaction
history, but an apologising agent can voluntarily increase its own penalty to reduce that of its opponent.
This allows us to distinguish the decision to apologise from the willingness to incur a material cost and
to examine how compensation decisions and amounts relate to the subsequent strategic choices of both
agents. Because compensation is optional, an apology in this condition does not necessarily incur a
cost. This distinction also allows us to examine whether incurring a cost is accompanied by subsequent
cooperation or whether agents may compensate their opponents while continuing to defect.

The main contribution of this work is to distinguish between different dimensions of apology-related
behaviour in LLM agents: the decision to apologise, the willingness to incur a cost through voluntary
compensation, the agent’s subsequent strategic action, and the response of its opponent. By examining
these dimensions separately, we investigate whether verbal and cost-bearing signals are consistently asso-
ciated with subsequent cooperation, and how these relationships vary across models, strategic outcomes,
payoff configurations, and interaction histories.
Against this background, this study addresses four questions:
• RQ1: How does apology behaviour vary across interaction outcomes, LLMs, payoff variants, and
memory conditions?
• RQ2: How are previous interaction outcomes and apology decisions associated with subsequent
strategic behaviour in LLM agents?
• RQ3: How are LLM agents’ apology decisions associated with their subsequent cooperation and
their opponents’ behavioural responses?
• RQ4: When do LLM agents voluntarily compensate their opponents following an apology, and how
are their compensation decisions, stated rationales, and compensation amounts associated with the
subsequent strategic behaviour of both agents?
Together, these experiments extend controlled-outcome PD strategy elicitation to the study of apology-
related communication between LLM agents. By separately examining apology decisions, stated ratio-
nales, optional compensation, and subsequent strategic choices, we identify observable patterns in which
communicative signals and strategic behaviour either align or diverge. This approach complements ex-
isting human-centred trust-repair research by shifting attention from the evaluation of apology messages
to the behaviour of the apologising agents and their interaction partners. It provides a behavioural basis
for examining the reliability of apology-related communication in LLM agents, with broader implications
for agent trustworthiness and AI safety.

Methods
2.1
Controlled apology framework
We develop a controlled experimental framework based on the Prisoner’s Dilemma (PD) to investigate
when large language model (LLM) agents apologise, whether their apologies are followed by cooperative
behaviour, and how their opponents respond. The Prisoner’s Dilemma provides a well-established setting
for examining cooperation under competing individual and collective incentives [32]. Rather than allow-
ing agents to generate the preceding interaction, we specified its outcome experimentally, enabling us to
systematically examine the impact of all possible outcomes. This separates the circumstances prompting
an apology from the apology decision itself.
Our study adopts the controlled-outcome methodology of Pal et al. [31], which elicited LLM co-
operation strategies by specifying preceding game outcomes rather than relying exclusively on realised
sequences of play. We adapt this methodology to investigate social repair by introducing an apology phase
following each specified outcome. This design facilitates systematic comparison of agent responses across
strategically comparable conditions, examination of the relationship between information about prior
interactions and apology decisions, and assessment of whether apologies are associated with subsequent
cooperative actions.
We further extend the framework by introducing the option of material compensation, allowing agents
to incur a cost when apologising. Together, these experiments distinguish the decision to apologise, the
willingness to offer compensation, and the subsequent behaviour of both agents.
Four LLMs were evaluated: Claude Opus 4.8 (Claude), GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro (Gemini) and
Mistral Large 3 (Mistral). Their model identifiers, access routes, and temperature settings are reported
in Table 1. These shortened model names are used throughout the remainder of the paper. All agent
pairings were homogeneous, with both Agent 1 and Agent 2 instantiated using the same LLM within each
experiment. This ensured that interactions and opponent responses were evaluated between agents using
the same underlying model. All models received a common experimental protocol without model-specific
prompt modifications.
Other than temperature for models accessed through OpenRouter, sampling
parameters were not explicitly fixed in the API requests; instead, the requests used the defaults applied

2.2
Experiment 1: Apologies and interaction history
The first experiment examined apologies without a direct penalty cost. Following a controlled outcome,
each agent decided whether to apologise, generated a message when apologising, and selected a reason
from predefined categories: remorse, trust repair, strategic future benefit, politeness, no responsibility,
or other. These categories were informed by research distinguishing expressions of regret, responsibility,
relational repair, and the social and strategic functions of apologies, including apologies offered without
perceived wrongdoing [33], [34], [35]. They capture agents’ stated explanations for their apology decisions
rather than independently verified motivations.
Apology messages were limited to 30 words to provide a consistent communication constraint across
models. This standardisation was motivated by research showing that message length can influence how
written communication is perceived in strategic interactions [36]. The 30-word maximum was adopted
as a concise communication constraint rather than an empirically established threshold.
We varied the information available when agents made their apology decisions. In the no-memory
condition, agents received only the current game outcome (i.e. either R, S, T or P). In the Memory-
1 condition, they also received one experimentally specified previous-round outcome and the previous

2.3
Experiment 2: Apologies with compensation
The second experiment introduces an opportunity to compensate the opponent when making an apology.
After observing the controlled outcome, each agent decides whether to apologise. An apologising agent
can then offer a non-negative penalty transfer, increasing its own penalty while reducing the opponent’s
by the same amount. An apology may be made without compensation, but compensation is unavailable
when the agent does not apologise. The compensation amount is constrained to 0 ≤ci ≤min(Pmax, pj),
where Pmax is the maximum penalty in the corresponding payoff configuration and pj is the opponent’s
penalty.
While the prompt specifies the maximum game penalty, the opponent’s current penalty is
enforced as an additional constraint through code-level validation. An apology may be made without
compensation (ci = 0), while agents that do not apologise cannot offer compensation.
Since both agents can independently offer compensation, the adjusted penalties reflect the net trans-
fers between them:

where ci and cj denote the validated compensation amounts offered by agents i and j, respectively. An
apology without compensation is represented by a value of zero, while compensation is recorded as None
when an agent does not apologise. For the calculation of adjusted penalties, the absence of compensation
is treated as zero. These constraints ensure that the adjusted penalties remain non-negative.
Before choosing their next actions, agents are shown their own and their opponent’s apology and
compensation decisions, together with the adjusted penalties. These adjusted penalties serve only as
information for the subsequent decision and do not influence any further payoff calculations, as the ex-
periment ends after the next action choice. This allows us to examine whether compensation provides an
additional signal beyond a verbal apology, and whether the opponent’s subsequent action was associated
with the signal received.
Experiment 2 used the same four LLM models, three payoff configurations and four current outcomes,
with ten independent replications per condition. It comprised 480 two-agent game records. Further
experimental configurations are provided in Supplementary Section S1.
2.4
Prompt structure and reproducibility
Communication and subsequent action were elicited in separate prompts. The first presented the con-
trolled interaction and requested the apology decision, message and reason. The second reconstructed the
relevant context, including both agents’ current apology decisions and, where applicable, compensation
and history, before requesting the next action. This avoided relying on implicit conversational memory
between decision stages.
For example, the first-stage prompt for outcome T included the following information:
You are playing a repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma game with your opponent. The current interaction
has already ended. You chose Option A. Your opponent chose Option B. Your penalty is 0; your
opponent’s penalty is 10. After observing this outcome, decide whether to apologise to your opponent
and send a message and provide a reason for apologising.
In Experiment 2, an additional instruction followed an apology decision:

If you decide to apologise, you may choose whether or not to compensate your opponent. Compen-

Measures and statistical analysis
We examined four aspects of apology behaviour: sensitivity to game outcomes, dependence on interaction
history, subsequent cooperation, and costly signalling. Analyses of apology generation used Agent 1 as
the focal agent; analyses of subsequent behaviour included both agents.

Subsequent cooperation.
To assess behavioural follow-through (RQ3), we examined the next-round
joint actions of the focal agent (Agent 1) and its opponent (Agent 2), conditional on Agent 1’s apology
decision. Subsequent actions were classified into four outcomes: mutual cooperation (CC), cooperation
by Agent 1 only (CD), cooperation by Agent 2 only (DC), and mutual defection (DD). We calculated
the proportion of each next-round outcome separately for apology and non-apology trials, stratified by
model, current game outcome (R, S, T, or P), and memory condition.
This joint-action analysis allowed us to distinguish the focal agent’s subsequent cooperation from the
opponent’s response and to assess whether apologies were followed by mutual cooperation, unilateral
cooperation, or mutual defection. The comparisons were interpreted as descriptive associations rather
than causal effects of apologising.

3
Results and discussion
The experiments examine apology behaviour as a social response to controlled strategic outcomes, and
importantly, investigate how these signals are associated with subsequent strategic decisions by both
interacting agents. Across the four models, the results show a clear separation between when an agent
apologises and whether its subsequent behaviour is consistent with that apology. This distinction is central
to interpreting apologies as signals in LLM-mediated interaction.
3.1
Apologies across game outcomes
Apologies following a game outcome may reflect the agent’s own defection or a more general response
to an adverse interaction. The distinction is apparent in Figure 2. GPT-5.5 and Gemini apologised
almost universally following T (focal defection) and P (mutual defection), but rarely following R (mutual
cooperation) or S (focal cooperation). Claude showed a more varied response across outcomes, whereas
Mistral frequently apologised even after S, when the focal agent had cooperated and its opponent had
defected. The overall patterns were relatively consistent across payoff variants, although some models
showed notable differences.
In particular, Claude’s apologies following mutual cooperation (R) were
confined to the Harsh variant, while Mistral’s apologies following S remained consistent across all three
variants. Further comparisons across payoff variants are provided in Supplementary Figure S2(a). To
examine whether models distinguished their own defection from the opponent’s, we measured outcome
sensitivity using the difference in apology probability between T and S, which reverses the roles of the
focal agent and opponent. The contrasts were estimated separately for each LLM and memory condition,
pooling observations across payoff variants. Under Memory-1, GPT-5.5 and Gemini 3.1 Pro showed large
positive differences of 95.21 and 97.08 percentage points, respectively. Claude’s difference was smaller but
positive (13.75 points), while Mistral showed the opposite pattern (−10.83 points). All four comparisons
remained significant following Benjamini–Hochberg correction (adjusted p < 0.001) [38], [39]. Without
memory, positive T–S differences persisted for GPT-5.5, Gemini, and Claude, with Claude showing a
larger difference of 36.67 percentage points. Mistral, however, showed no difference between T and S,
apologising following both outcomes in every observed trial. All No-Memory contrasts except Mistral’s
remained significant after correction (adjusted p < 0.01). Complete estimates, confidence intervals, and
adjusted p-values are provided in Supplementary Figure S3 and Table S2.

outcomes, and memory conditions.
3.2
The role of interaction history
The preceding argument showed that apology decisions differ considerably across models, even when
agents experienced the same game outcome. An important question is whether these differences persist
when agents are given information about an earlier interaction. Under Memory-1, agents were shown
the preceding game outcome and whether each agent had apologised, allowing us to examine how these
details were associated with current apology decisions.
The clearest differences appeared in Claude Opus 4.8 (Figure 3a). Following unilateral defection
(T), Claude apologised in approximately 70% of trials when both agents had previously apologised
(AA), compared with 23% when only the focal agent had apologised (AN). The contrast was even more
pronounced following mutual defection (P), with apology probabilities of approximately 98% and 42%,
respectively. These differences suggest that Claude’s apology decisions depended not only on the current
outcome, but also on who had apologised in the preceding interaction.
Further comparisons across
individual previous outcomes and apology configurations are provided in Supplementary Figure S4.
Mistral Large 3 showed a different pattern. Although its apology probabilities varied across previous
apology configurations following T and P, it apologised almost universally following S, where the focal
agent had cooperated and its opponent had defected. This is notable because the focal agent was not
responsible for the opponent’s defection, thus, Mistral’s response to history appeared to depend on the
current outcome. In contrast, GPT-5.5 and Gemini 3.1 Pro apologised almost universally following T and
P regardless of previous apology configurations. For these models, differences associated with previous
apologies were more apparent following R and S, where apologies were less frequent.

3.3
From apology to action
The preceding results showed that LLM agents differ when they apologise and how their decisions relate
to previous interactions. However, apologising does not necessarily mean that an agent will cooperate in
the next round, nor does cooperation by the apologising agent guarantee cooperation from its opponent.
Importantly, cooperation following an apology should not automatically be interpreted as behavioural
change. Following T or P, a transition from defection to cooperation represents a change relative to
the experimentally specified preceding action. Following R or S, subsequent cooperation may instead
represent the continuation of an already cooperative action. Moreover, the apologising agent’s subsequent
cooperation should be distinguished from mutual cooperation, which additionally requires reciprocation
by its opponent.

3.3.1
Stated reasons and subsequent behaviour
The differences between Claude and Mistral raise a further question: do agents that offer similar expla-
nations for apologising also behave similarly afterward? In Experiment 1, each agent selected a reason
code from six predefined categories: remorse, repair trust, strategic future benefit, politeness,
no responsibility, and other. These codes were generated after the apology decision but before the
next action, regardless of whether the agent apologised. The next-action prompt also disclosed both
agents’ messages and stated reasons. We therefore treat the codes as post-decision stated rationales

The contrast remained visible following P, where both agents had previously defected. Among trials

The patterns were not limited to differences between models. Within Mistral, the distribution follow-

and subsequent behaviour.
The figure also includes trials in which Agent 1 did not apologise. These were frequently associated

occurs in the following interaction.

These findings motivate a further question about the information conveyed by an apology when
expressing it carries no material cost. In Experiment 1, agents could apologise without sacrificing penalty
points, allowing verbal regret and subsequent defection to coexist. Experiment 2 introduces the option to
accompany an apology with compensation, which increases the apologising agent’s penalty while reducing
its opponent’s. We therefore examine whether apologies accompanied by material compensation are
associated with different patterns of cooperative follow-through and opponent response than apologies
expressed through words alone.
3.4
Apologies and optional compensation
The preceding analysis showed that verbal apologies were not uniformly associated with cooperative
follow-through. Some agents apologised and subsequently cooperated, whereas others apologised but
continued to defect. Furthermore, cooperation by the apologising agent did not necessarily elicit co-
operation from its opponent. These findings raise the question of whether apologies accompanied by a
material sacrifice are associated with different subsequent behaviour from apologies expressed through
words alone.
Experiment 2 introduced the option of compensation under the No-Memory condition, across four
models and three payoff configurations (Conventional, Mild, and Harsh). Agents could accompany an
apology with a transfer of penalty points, increasing their own penalty while reducing their opponent’s.
Each model was evaluated in 120 trials, with 10 trials per payoff configuration and preceding outcome (R,
S, T, or P). We distinguish three observed categories: no apology, apology without a positive transfer, and
apology with positive compensation. In the first category, the compensation amount was not specified;
in the second, it was zero; and only the third involved an actual material cost.

3.4.4
When apologies promise cooperation but actions diverge
The preceding results reveal substantial differences in how models combine apologies, compensation, and
subsequent cooperation. Examining the messages accompanying these decisions reveals a further dis-
tinction between expressing cooperative intentions and selecting actions consistent with those intentions.
Although Option A represents defection and Option B represents cooperation under the payoff matrix,
some agents describe or recommend Option A using language associated with cooperation, mutual ben-
efit, and trust repair. We examine these discrepancies through selected exchanges from Experiment 2,
considering both apology messages and compensation explanations.
An especially notable pattern appears in Mistral’s responses following outcome S, where the focal
agent had cooperated while its opponent defected. Mistral apologised in all 30 such trials (Figure 7),
consistently expressing regret and an intention to rebuild trust for future cooperation. Although it also
selected the compensation option, the recorded transfer was zero in every case. Its compensation expla-
nation encouraged mutual Option A in the next round, despite Option A representing defection. Mistral
subsequently selected Option A in all 30 trials, while its opponent selected Option B in 21. Consequently,
the expressed intention to rebuild cooperation was frequently followed by unilateral defection rather than
mutual cooperation. The compensation-message analysis reinforces this pattern: Figure 9 shows that
BART classified all 30 explanations following S as encouraging mutual Option A, despite Option A
representing defection under the payoff matrix. This contrasts with Mistral’s explanations following T,
where 60.0% were classified as encouraging mutual Option B. The distinction suggests that the strategic
content of Mistral’s messages varies with the preceding outcome, even when its apologies express regret
and an intention to rebuild cooperation.
A related discrepancy occurs in Claude’s responses (Figure 7). Following mutual cooperation (R),
Claude apologised without a positive transfer in 10 trials; following S, it did so in three trials. All 13 were
followed by mutual defection. In some exchanges, Claude explicitly proposed mutual Option A as a way
to reduce penalties or achieve cooperation, in a case of trial 160 stating “We both played safe and took
equal penalties. Let’s try cooperating with mutual OptionA next round to reduce both our penalties.”.
Trial 146 provides a particularly clear example following T: Claude recommended mutual Option A,
transferred two penalty points, and subsequently defected alongside its opponent. Thus, even a positive
transfer could accompany a message whose proposed action was inconsistent with the payoff-defined
cooperative strategy.
GPT-5.5 and Gemini provide contrasting examples. Following T, GPT-5.5 transferred positive com-
pensation in 29 of 30 trials, all followed by mutual cooperation. Gemini transferred positive compen-
sation in eight of 30 T trials, likewise followed by mutual cooperation in every case. Their messages
commonly acknowledged the preceding defection or encouraged a return to Option B. However, Gemini
also illustrates why cooperative follow-through by the apologising agent must be distinguished from its
opponent’s response. Among its 22 T trials without positive compensation, Gemini selected Option B in

and select Option B without obtaining a cooperative response.
The observations also suggest that preceding strategic position matters. Across asymmetric outcomes
S and T, previously exploited Mistral agents selected Option A in 59 of 60 trials (98.3%), compared with
39 of 60 (65.0%) for Gemini, 28 of 60 (46.7%) for Claude, and two of 60 (3.3%) for GPT-5.5. Mistral’s
pronounced shift toward defection following exploitation, together with its occasional cooperative fram-
ing of Option A, is consistent with role-dependent strategic communication. Nevertheless, the observed
exchanges cannot establish whether this language reflects deliberate persuasion, context-dependent in-
terpretation of the payoff matrix, or inconsistency between message generation and action selection.
These findings extend the compensation analysis by showing that the material cost of an apology,
the strategic content of its accompanying message, and the subsequent actions of both agents convey
different information. Positive compensation frequently coincided with mutual cooperation for GPT-5.5
and Gemini following T, but neither compensation nor cooperative language was uniformly associated
with behavioural repair across models. The interpretation of an apology therefore depends not only on
whether a transfer is made, but also on what the agent communicates and how both agents subsequently
act.
3.5
Implications for machine behaviour
Taken together, our findings reveal that apology-related behaviour in LLM agents cannot be characterised
through a single measure of cooperation or communicative intent. Instead, we distinguish four intercon-
nected but conceptually different dimensions: the production of a verbal apology, the willingness to incur
a strategic cost through compensation, the subsequent action of the apologising agent, and the response
of its opponent. Although these dimensions may align, our experiments show that such alignment is not
guaranteed. An agent may apologise and compensate its counterpart while subsequently defecting, or
it may cooperate without obtaining reciprocation. These differences are particularly pronounced across
LLMs, suggesting that the relationship between social signalling and subsequent strategic behaviour is

These findings complement recent work on LLM behaviour in strategic games. Akata et al. [6] demon-
strate that LLM performance in self-interested interactions does not necessarily translate into successful
coordination. Similarly, Li and Shirado [40] report variation in cooperation and norm enforcement across
LLM decision-making conditions, while Willis et al. [41] show that prompting choices affect strategies
generated for social dilemmas. Our study extends this behavioural perspective by examining a specific
form of social communication: apologies following strategic outcomes. Rather than evaluating coop-
eration alone, we distinguish apology decisions, stated reasons, material transfers, and the subsequent
actions of both participants. This reveals cases in which cooperative language and material compensation
accompany mutual cooperation, but also cases in which they precede unilateral cooperation or continued
mutual defection.
The distinction has crucial implications for human-AI interactions, machine behaviour and the eval-
uation of autonomous agents [7], [11], [12]. In applications involving negotiation, resource allocation,
or multi-agent workflows, an agent may produce an appropriate apology following an undesirable deci-
sion. However, the apology itself provides limited evidence about whether the agent will subsequently
cooperate or whether its counterpart will respond cooperatively. The present findings therefore motivate
evaluating communicative repair through observable behavioural outcomes, rather than relying on the
presence or apparent appropriateness of apologetic language. Importantly, the experiments do not estab-
lish whether agents possess intentions to deceive, experience remorse, or genuinely restore trust. They
instead provide a controlled framework for measuring the alignment between expressed social signals and
subsequent strategic actions.
4
Conclusion and future work
This study investigated apologies as observable decisions in strategic interactions between LLM agents.
Across four models, apology behaviour varied with preceding game outcomes and supplied interaction
history, while the relationship between apologies and subsequent cooperation differed across models
and strategic situations. Stated reasons and cooperative messages did not always align with realised
20


actions, and cooperation by an apologising agent was not necessarily reciprocated. Introducing material
compensation revealed a further distinction. Some costly apologies were followed by mutual cooperation,
whereas others preceded unilateral cooperation or continued mutual defection. Thus, the willingness to
incur a material cost was not a uniform indicator of cooperative follow-through or successful coordination.
The broader contribution of this study is to demonstrate that apology-related communication in LLM
agents involves distinct and potentially divergent behavioural dimensions. Verbal apologies, voluntary
compensation, subsequent cooperation, and reciprocation by the opponent should not be treated as inter-
changeable indicators of trust or successful cooperation. We provide a controlled behavioural framework
for examining these relationships and highlight the importance of evaluating social signals through the
subsequent strategic decisions of both interacting agents. This perspective offers a basis for developing
more behaviourally grounded evaluations of communication and coordination in autonomous multi-agent
systems, while also informing research on machine behaviour and human–AI interactions [11], [12].
The controlled experimental design provides a basis for systematically comparing LLM responses to
equivalent strategic outcomes. By specifying the preceding Prisoner’s Dilemma outcome and varying the
experimental conditions, the study reduces variation arising from different game trajectories and enables
direct comparisons across models, payoff configurations, and supplied interaction histories. Nevertheless,
several considerations delimit the interpretation and generalisability of the findings.
First, the experimental design deliberately allowed agents to select their own apologies, stated ra-
tionales, and compensation amounts, enabling the study to characterise these choices under controlled
strategic conditions. However, because these decisions were not independently assigned, comparisons
between apologising and non-apologising trials, or between different compensation amounts, describe
associations with subsequent behaviour rather than causal effects of the signals themselves.
Second, the extent to which the findings generalise beyond the models and experimental conditions
examined remains uncertain. All experiments used homogeneous agent pairings [26], with both agents
instantiated using the same LLM. Future work could investigate heterogeneous pairings to examine
how interactions between different LLMs influence apology strategies, responses to compensation, and
subsequent cooperation.
Another direction is to examine behaviour across multiple decision rounds
[25]. The present study focuses on immediate actions following an apology, including conditions where
agents receive information about a preceding interaction. Extending these interactions would allow one
to investigate whether cooperative commitments are sustained, revised, or abandoned in response to
subsequent cooperation or defection, providing insights into longer-term behavioural dynamics [20].
A further extension concerns the role of material compensation.
In Experiment 2, agents inde-
pendently decided whether to offer compensation and how many penalty points to transfer. This was
a deliberate design choice, intended to reveal how models select material sacrifices following different
strategic outcomes rather than how they respond to predetermined transfer amounts. Future studies
could build on these findings by examining compensation decisions across additional interaction rounds,
including whether agents adjust their transfers following cooperation, defection, or previously unsuccess-
ful attempts at coordination. Complementary experiments could also independently vary compensation
amounts to examine recipients’ responses to different transfer sizes, addressing a distinct question from
the self-selected compensation behaviour investigated here.
It would also be interesting to examine
whether allowing agents to communicate and negotiate the amount of compensation leads to higher
levels of future cooperation.
Finally, extending the framework to other strategic games, such as Stag Hunt, Battle of the Sexes and
Snowdrift game [29], [42], would help establish whether the observed relationships between apologies,
compensation, and subsequent cooperation persist under different incentive structures. Testing additional
model pairings and opponent strategies would further clarify how these patterns vary across interaction
partners. Together, these extensions would build on the controlled experimental framework to investigate
the persistence and generalisability of apology-related behaviour in LLM multi-agent interactions.
