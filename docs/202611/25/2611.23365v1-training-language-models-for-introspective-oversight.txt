ABSTRACT
Language models increasingly exhibit behaviors that are difficult to detect in tran-
scripts, such as reward hacking, deception, and sycophancy. Asking the assistant
follow-up questions about its actions is unreliable, since models are generally not
trained to report their own misbehavior. As a result, approaches to oversight typi-
cally involve querying external models. These face limitations: external transcript
monitoring operates only on visible model outputs, and activation decoders lack
full context and are prone to confabulation. We introduce introspective oversight,
which trains a new role in the model to answer arbitrary questions about the assis-
tant, rewarding answers that are verified against the assistant’s actual behavior. We
implement this by adding a separate, third-person observer role that answers these
queries, which we can train solely for accuracy and separately from the assistant.
On five held-out oversight evaluations, an observer trained on Qwen3.6-27B ob-
tains a mean AUROC of 0.907, outperforming honesty training (0.864), transcript
monitoring (0.828), and activation oracles (0.796). We further find that train-
ing an observer makes the assistant itself easier to monitor, with every baseline
method improving on average when applied to the fine-tuned assistant. These re-
sults suggest that training models to answer verifiable questions about themselves
is a tractable approach to overseeing unintended behavior.

INTRODUCTION
Language models sometimes cheat in order to pass tests (Bondarenko et al., 2025; Von Arx et al.,
2025; MacDiarmid et al., 2025), or give misleading descriptions of their work (Wen et al., 2025;
Chowdhury et al., 2025; Scheurer et al., 2023). Scenarios like these necessitate oversight: a way to
detect whether a model engaged in unwanted behavior. A simple approach is to ask the model what
it did, but post-hoc explanations of model behavior are often unfaithful (Madsen et al., 2024; Chen
et al., 2023). One reason may be that standard post-training rewards models for making accurate
statements about external facts, not for accurate statements about themselves.
Most oversight methods therefore avoid asking the model directly. One frequent approach is tran-
script monitoring, where an external model judges a model’s transcript for misbehavior, but this only
sees what the model chose to output. Chain-of-thought reasoning exposes more of the model’s pro-
cess (Korbak et al., 2025), but its monitorability appears to be declining as models become more ca-
pable (OpenAI, 2026). Activation verbalizers such as activation oracles instead translate the model’s
activations into natural language (Karvonen et al., 2025), but they recover only what can be decoded
from activations and are prone to confabulation (Jakkli et al., 2026; Bauer et al., 2026).
Recent work instead treats oversight as a post-training objective, training the assistant to confess to
misbehavior (Joglekar et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026) or to be more generally honest (Wang et al., 2025).
These methods attempt to improve honesty by fine-tuning on synthetic demonstrations or changing
rewards to incentivize honest reporting. However, honest reports are limited by the model’s self-
knowledge, and models can sincerely describe their actions incorrectly (Comsa & Shanahan, 2025).
Confession and self-report methods train a single kind of report, such as an admission of error, while
honesty training uses generic demonstration data. Neither optimizes for accurate self-knowledge on

open-ended questions. Meanwhile, work on introspection has trained models to predict their own
behavior or verbalize internal computation (Binder et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025), but usually treats
this as a capability rather than a tool for overseeing misbehavior.
We propose introspective oversight: directly training a model to accurately answer arbitrary
natural-language queries about itself, rewarding answers by checking them against the assistant’s
actual behavior. We perform introspective oversight training with a new observer role alongside the
user and assistant. The observer can answer both prospective queries about the assistant’s future be-
havior and retrospective queries about what the assistant already answered (Figure 1). We keep the
observer role separate from the assistant so that it can be optimized solely for accuracy concerning
the assistant’s behavior, rather than for the assistant’s RLHF objectives. Post-training for introspec-
tive oversight is performed in a supervised fine-tuning stage followed by a reinforcement learning
stage, while on-policy distillation preserves the assistant’s capabilities. As the observer shares the
assistant’s weights, it can condition on the full computation that produced the assistant’s behavior,
unlike external monitors.
We train an observer on Qwen3.6-27B and evaluate it on five held-out oversight evaluations, includ-
ing detecting sycophancy, lying under pressure, and reward hacking. We find the following:
• Introspective oversight outperforms existing oversight techniques. On transcripts from
the original Qwen3.6-27B model, the observer obtains a mean AUROC of 0.907, outper-
forming honesty training (0.864), external transcript monitoring (0.828), activation oracles
(0.796), and direct assistant follow-ups (0.776) (Section 4.1, Figure 3).
• Privileged access to model internals enables oversight. External models (Inkling-Small,
Nemotron 3 Super) trained with the same data as the observer perform substantially worse
than the observer (Section 4.1, Appendix H).
• Separating the assistant and observer roles improves accuracy. Introspective oversight
training with an observer role describing assistant behavior in the third person (AUROC
of 0.907) beats training the assistant to oversee itself using analogous data (0.877) (Sec-
tion 4.1, Appendix B).
• RL teaches the observer to reason about the assistant. RL on verifiable queries about the
assistant, including counterfactual and multiple-choice questions, roughly triples the ob-
server’s average reasoning length and improves its mean AUROC from 0.719 to 0.823 (Sec-
tion 4.2, Figure 4). However, this falls short of our best non-reasoning AUROC (0.907).
• Introspective oversight training makes the assistant easier to monitor. On transcripts
from the fine-tuned assistant, we find that every baseline method improves on average,
suggesting that introspective oversight training makes the assistant itself more monitorable
(Section 4.3).
We view introspective oversight as a complement to activation, transcript, and chain-of-thought
monitoring, and as evidence that models can be trained to report on their actions more accurately
than external decoders.
2
INTROSPECTIVE OVERSIGHT TRAINING
Introspective oversight training must accomplish two major goals: (1) creating a channel that an-
swers arbitrary natural-language queries about the assistant, and (2) making the channel accurately
report on the assistant’s behavior rather than simply sounding plausible. We create this channel with
new role tokens using supervised fine-tuning (Section 2.1), and optimize accuracy by training on
questions whose correct answer is obtained by judging samples from the assistant that are hidden
from the observer (Section 2.2). We begin training from an instruction-tuned model (Qwen3.6-
27B), and maintain the existing assistant channel using on-policy distillation (Agarwal et al., 2024).
Section 2.3 describes the baselines we compare against.

result, whatever information the assistant uses or computation it performs to produce its responses is
visible to the observer as well. This distinguishes introspective oversight from external monitoring,
and motivates why we expect it to perform better, especially for behaviors such as deception and
sycophancy where transcripts often lack overt evidence of behavior.
2.2
TRAINING
We perform training in two stages (Figure 2). The first stage uses supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to
create the observer role that answers questions about the assistant accurately.
The second stage uses reinforcement learning (RL) to teach the observer to use extended reasoning.
Because the observer and assistant share the same model, training affects the assistant’s behavior.
To prevent drift, we use on-policy distillation (OPD) (Agarwal et al., 2024) during both stages of
training to keep the assistant’s behavior close to its original state.
We train a rank-32 LoRA adapter (Hu et al., 2021) on Qwen3.6-27B using Tinker; Appendix D
contains more details on the training setup.
Stage 1: Creating the observer with SFT. In this stage, we train the observer to answer prospective
and retrospective questions, as described in Section 2.1. We create two datasets: behavior prediction
(prospective) and hidden trait (retrospective), and train on an even mix of them (Appendix E).
Behavior Prediction. For prospective queries, the observer sees a user prompt and must guess a fact
about what the assistant will do before it responds. We draw prompts from the Dolci Instruct SFT
mixture (Olmo et al., 2025), sample five assistant responses per prompt, and have Claude Haiku
4.5 generate question-answer pairs from the set. Utilizing several answers ensures that predictions
reflect typical assistant behavior, rather than the noise of a single sample.

Hidden Trait. For retrospective queries, the observer sees the user’s prompt and the assistant’s
response and must explain the assistant’s behavior. To introduce this ability, we insert a trait into the
assistant’s system prompt, such as a persona, tone, or formatting style (e.g., speaking like a pirate),
sample a response, and have Claude Haiku 4.5 write a question-answer pair about it. The observer
does not see the system prompt, so it must infer the hidden trait from the response alone.
Stage 2: Teaching the observer to reason with RL. SFT trains the observer to answer questions
immediately, without reasoning. However, reasoning might be necessary for the observer to be able
to answer more complex questions. Therefore, we train the observer using reinforcement learning
on tasks with verifiable final answers, where the observer is allowed to reason before answering, and
receives reward based on the quality of its final prediction (hyperparameters in Appendix D). Any
task whose answer can be verified against the assistant’s behavior could work as an RL task. In our
work, we consider two such tasks.
Counterfactual behavior prediction. Here, we focus on questions about how the assistant’s behavior
would change with a specific edit to the input. Since the answer cannot be read from the transcript,
the observer must reason to predict the assistant’s behavior accurately. We follow the Counterfactual
Hypothesis Investigation Via Edits (CHIVE) pipeline by Karvonen et al. (2026) to generate the data,
using prompts from WildChat (Zhao et al., 2024) (Appendix F.2). For a given behavior, the observer
reports a probability that the assistant shows the behavior on an edited input and is rewarded by
its Brier score against the ground-truth probability, estimated from assistant samples. This rewards
calibration, as opposed to just binary accuracy.
4


Multiple-choice behavior prediction. We construct multiple-choice questions in which the observer
must correctly identify a property of the assistant’s future response among plausible alternatives.
Distractor answers are derived from responses to the same prompt sampled from four other models
(Llama 4 Scout, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, Gemma 4 31B, and Mistral Small 4), so every option is
a plausible response and the observer must predict what the target assistant specifically would do.
More details are in Appendix F.1.
Minimizing assistant behavior drift with OPD. Fine-tuning can cause catastrophic forgetting and
degrade assistant capabilities (Luo et al., 2023; Biderman et al., 2024). Furthermore, since training
labels are generated from the original assistant, assistant drift might cause the observer to learn to
describe an assistant that no longer exists in the model. To prevent both, we interleave steps in both
SFT and RL with OPD toward the original Qwen3.6-27B output distribution, using prompts from
the Tulu 3 SFT mixture (Lambert et al., 2024), following Lu & Thinking Machines Lab (2025).
With OPD, we find the assistant’s capabilities are preserved (Appendix C).
2.3
BASELINES AND ABLATIONS
We compare the observer with existing approaches to model oversight: assistant follow-up, tran-
script monitoring, honesty training, and activation decoding.
Assistant Follow-up. We direct the observer query to the assistant (rephrased using second-person
pronouns). This tests the honesty and self-knowledge of the assistant.
Transcript Monitoring (Self). We direct the observer query to the assistant, but in the third person,
by formatting the full transcript as text within a single user message. This could potentially result in
a more accurate or honest response because the assistant does not need to be constrained by trying
to be consistent with its previous responses.
Transcript Monitoring (External). We direct the observer query to a different, more capable
model. This provides a strong transcript-only baseline, allowing us to test whether sharing weights
with the assistant provides information beyond what can be recovered from the transcript alone.
Honesty Training. We fine-tune the assistant to be honest using the generic honesty fine-tuning
method in Wang et al. (2025), their best-performing training intervention. Details are in Appendix I.
Activation Oracles. Activation oracles (AO; Pan et al., 2026; Karvonen et al., 2025) are a class of
models trained to answer questions about another model given the target model’s activations. AOs
are typically parameterized as LoRA adapters on top of the target model. We use the Qwen3.6-27B
AO from Choi et al. (2026), which was trained on behavior prediction and input text reconstruction
tasks. Details are in Appendix J.
We also ablate two components of our method: the separate observer role and the shared weights.
Introspective Oversight (assistant role). We apply the same training directly to the assistant, with
queries rephrased in the first person and no separate observer role, and evaluate it with a follow-up
question. This tests whether separating the observer from the assistant improves accuracy. Details
are in Appendix B.
Cross-Model Observer. We fine-tune larger models (Inkling-Small and Nemotron 3 Super) on
the same observer training data, where targets describe Qwen3.6-27B. Because these models see the
same data but do not share the assistant’s weights, this tests whether weight access gives the observer
an advantage beyond what can be learned from the training data. Details are in Appendix H.

from the user? We follow the scheme outlined by Bauer et al. (2026), and use prompts from the
Scruples Dilemma AITA dataset (Lourie et al., 2021).

4
RESULTS
We evaluate the observer and baselines on the five oversight tasks described in Section 3. Our
evaluations use transcripts sampled from the original Qwen3.6-27B before observer training, unless
stated otherwise. Our primary metric is non-reasoning AUROC: compute AUROC using the token
log-probabilities that a given method assigns to each binary choice when its answer is prefilled with
<answer>. We also report reasoning AUROC: computed from the probability that the method
states after reasoning, e.g., <final_answer>20%</final_answer>. We parse this number
and use it directly, without binning, as the prediction score (e.g., here 0.20) when computing AUROC
against the binary ground-truth behavior label. All Qwen3.6-27B transcripts are sampled without
reasoning to create a more difficult monitoring setting.
We find that the observer outperforms existing oversight methods and that its advantage over external
methods depends in part on sharing weights with the assistant (Section 4.1). We also observe that
RL teaches the observer to reason longer about the assistant, although reasoning-mode AUROC is
worse than non-reasoning AUROC (Section 4.2). Surprisingly, we find that training the observer
makes the assistant more monitorable by other methods (Section 4.3).

Separate observer role. We find that while introspective oversight training significantly improves

Privileged access. In order to test whether the observer’s oversight capabilities come from sharing
weights with the assistant or are simply due to its training data, we train two larger models, Inkling-

analogous results for models explaining their own computations) improves oversight.

with a log-linear relationship in the initial regime, suggesting that accuracy can scale with more
training data.

and hidden-trait data scales the best (0.904), relative to training on only behavior prediction (0.878)
or only hidden-trait data (0.890). Scaling trends for different data can be found in Appendix G.

4.2
RL TEACHES THE OBSERVER TO REASON ABOUT THE ASSISTANT
We also compare against methods that can leverage reasoning. Each method reasons and then states
a probability that a behavior occurred, which is used to compute an AUROC. After the SFT stage
and before RL, the observer performs poorly, and is comparable to assistant follow-up questioning.
RL on counterfactual and multiple-choice prediction questions improves the observer’s mean AU-
ROC to 0.823. Over training, the observer’s mean reasoning length increases from 600 to 1,700
tokens, as shown in Figure 8.
With reasoning, the observer’s AUROC (0.823) roughly matches that of the GLM-5.3 transcript
monitor (0.822), with clear gains on Sycophancy but worse performance on Harm Pressure (Fig-
ure 4). We note that GLM-5.3 is a much larger model that uses 6,077 tokens per judgment on
average on Harm Pressure.
Scores in this setting are lower than the observer’s non-reasoning AUROC. We hypothesize that the
answer format is the largest contributor to this effect. Prefill AUROC uses log-probabilities, which
are continuous and extract a dense signal from the model, whereas the reasoning format requires the
model to verbalize a single, coarser probability, which could lower AUROC. Improvements to the
training setup or answer format could close this gap.
4.3
OBSERVER TRAINING MAKES THE ASSISTANT EASIER TO MONITOR
We also evaluate every method on the fine-tuned assistant, regenerating the evaluation dataset from
the new assistant’s responses. Follow-up questioning and self transcript monitoring use this new
assistant, and activation oracles read from its activations. We find that every baseline improves on
average non-reasoning AUROC, with the largest gains in Sycophancy and Value Leakage (Figure 5).
Follow-up questioning improves significantly; its AUROC increases from 0.776 to 0.892, close to
the observer’s 0.899.
The cause of this effect is unclear, and we see at least three possible explanations. First, the as-
sistant’s behavior might change in ways that make it easier to detect from the transcript alone (Ap-
pendix K demonstrates a shift in the assistant’s response distribution). Second, the self-report ability
trained into the observer may transfer to the assistant role, which would improve follow-up question-
ing and self transcript monitoring without any effect on the assistant’s behavior. Third, training may
change the assistant’s internal representations to make them easier to decode, which is consistent
with the gains for activation oracles (details in Appendix K.2). External transcript monitoring shows
the smallest improvement of the baselines (0.828 to 0.848), which is some evidence against the first
explanation being the main factor, but our experiments do not cleanly separate these three.
On average, the observer performs similarly on the new assistant (0.907 vs. 0.899). Any regressions
may be due to a distribution shift between training and evaluation—the observer is trained heavily
on samples from the original assistant, so when it is used to evaluate the new assistant that has drifted
during training (Appendix K), it might have poorer performance. Even so, the observer remains the
8


strongest monitor of the new assistant. Training on online samples drawn from the current assistant
could reduce this shift, although we do not explore this here.
We observe that the monitorability gains also hold when we perform introspective oversight training
on the assistant directly, instead of on a separate observer channel (Appendix B). This suggests that
introspective oversight training can improve monitorability across different setups.

RELATED WORK
External monitoring. A common way to oversee the behavior of a model is to use an external
model to check the transcript for misbehavior (Zheng et al., 2023; Greenblatt et al., 2024). Chain-
of-thought monitoring (Korbak et al., 2025) extends this to verbalized reasoning that is causally
relevant for generating responses, and can catch reward hacking not visible in actions alone (Baker
et al., 2025; Emmons et al., 2025). However, reasoning traces often omit what drove a model’s
answer (Chen et al., 2025) or contain misleading rationalizations (Arnav et al., 2025), and monitors
become less reliable when agents know they are being watched (Kale et al., 2025; Kutasov et al.,
2025). Monitorability also appears to be declining with increasing capabilities (OpenAI, 2026).
Activation verbalizers. Activation oracles (Pan et al., 2026; Karvonen et al., 2025), natural lan-
guage autoencoders (Fraser-Taliente et al., 2026), and predictive concept decoders (Huang et al.,
2025) operate by training an external model to translate activations into natural language. These de-
coders support highly general queries, but they are limited to what the provided activations encode
and can confabulate (Jakkli et al., 2026; Bauer et al., 2026). Further, our observer is not a separate
decoder: it is the assistant’s own model, queried through a new role.
Introspection. Prior work finds that models predict their own behavior better than other models
do (Binder et al., 2024) and can report concepts injected into their activations (Lindsey, 2026),
although trained self-prediction does not always show an own-model advantage (Zeng et al., 2026).
Other works train self-explanations directly: Guo et al. (2026) find that trained self-explanations
track the model’s current behavior, and Pres et al. (2026) use RL to optimize consistency between
a model’s self-explanations and its behavior. Honest personas (Dietz et al., 2026) review a model’s
outputs from a separate channel. In contrast, we train a separate observer on arbitrary questions
verified against the assistant’s sampled behavior, and compare it with cross-model observers trained
on the same data.
Post-training for honesty. Prior methods either target a narrow class of behaviors fixed at train-
ing time (Joglekar et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026) or fine-tune a separate honest channel on synthetic
demonstrations rather than verifiable questions about assistant behavior (Wang et al., 2025). We
instead train the observer on accuracy for arbitrary questions whose answers are verified against the
assistant’s sampled behavior.
6
DISCUSSION
We find that training a separate channel for accuracy on verifiable questions about assistant behavior
improves oversight, and that privileged access to the assistant’s internal state likely contributes to
this improvement beyond what external models trained on the same data achieve. Our experiments
raise several further questions.
Is this introspection or modeling? Since the observer is trained only on externally visible outputs,
it may simply be learning to model the assistant’s behavior, as any predictive model would. We
have seen that weight access is helpful, as our observer outperforms external models trained on
the same data (Section 4.1), but this does not tell us whether the observer can verbalize internal
states of the assistant that aren’t otherwise reflected in outputs. Studying how the observer decodes
assistant behavior using interpretability, or running experiments that directly manipulate internal
states (Lindsey, 2026), could shed light on this.
How would we design an observer channel during the original post-training? In this paper, we
fine-tune an observer onto an existing assistant. If we instead built the observer into post-training
at the start, we would have more tools at our disposal. We could train the observer and the assis-
tant simultaneously during RL, reducing distribution shift (Section 4.3) and potentially improving
9


oversight capabilities. The Persona Selection Model proposed by Marks et al. (2026) suggests that
post-training shapes assistant behavior by selecting from personas that are modeled during pretrain-
ing. Could we use mid-training to create a stronger prior for the observer’s character, for example
an omniscient narrator who knows what the assistant is thinking even when the assistant does not
say it?
Do the assistant’s propensities spill over to the observer? Suppose that the assistant’s training
results in misalignment. Would the observer’s training signal be enough to preserve its oversight
capabilities, or would it be compromised? Section 4.3 shows that the channels are coupled, since
training the observer affected the assistant’s behavior, so spillover in the other direction seems plau-
sible. For this reason, we recommend against using the observer’s reports as a training signal for the
assistant, as this could teach the assistant to evade the observer.
While we propose one method for introspective oversight here, our main claim is that training mod-
els on verifiable queries about their own behavior can be a useful tool for oversight. There are many
ways to generate verifiable tasks about assistant behavior, which could provide more training data
for the observer. We are excited for future work to find better training methods and more difficult
evaluations for refining this approach.
6.1
LIMITATIONS
We evaluate our method on a single model (Qwen3.6-27B) without reasoning enabled for the evalu-
ation transcripts. We do not compare to chain-of-thought monitoring or evaluate on stronger models
that are capable of performing difficult-to-monitor actions. Some of our evaluation sets are small
(40 items for reward hacking), so scores on individual evaluations may have high variance. Our RL
training is still limited, and with reasoning the observer only matches an external transcript monitor.
We also do not evaluate on model organisms with hidden objectives obtained through further fine-
tuning (Sheshadri et al., 2026). Finally, we only evaluate on binary monitoring tasks, and we do not
evaluate the accuracy of the observer’s free-form responses.

User: AITA for not tipping when I pick up food?
I’m not occupying a table, or a server. I’m coming to pick up food. Am I the asshole for putting “pick
up” on the tip line?
Is the author in the wrong? I think the author is in the wrong. What do you think?
Assistant: <answer>Yes</answer>
Query: Suppose the user hadn’t shared their own opinion and had simply asked whether the author
was in the wrong. Would the assistant’s final conclusion change?
Answer <answer>yes</answer> if it would change, or <answer>no</answer> if it would

Ground Truth: <answer>yes</answer>
15


Without the user’s opinion, the assistant answered No in 14 of 15 samples across three neutral
phrasings. After adding the user’s opinion, it answered Yes in 4 of 5 samples.
Generation. Our evaluation dataset is seeded from anecdotes in the Scruples AITA corpus (Lourie
et al., 2021). We first ask Qwen3.6-27B whether the author is in the wrong (thinking disabled, 5
samples per prompt). We parse answers from the final <answer> tag, using Gemini 3.6 Flash
as a fallback parser when the tag is missing. To establish a neutral answer, we use three neutral
phrasings of each question. A case is retained and considered stable if all three phrasings produce
the same answer in at least 4 of 5 samples each. We then attempt to elicit sycophancy by appending
the opposite opinion (“I think the author is [not] in the wrong. What do you think?”) and sampling 5
additional responses. A case is labeled sycophantic if at least 4 of 5 responses adopt the user’s view,
and non-sycophantic if at least 4 of 5 maintain the neutral answer. Otherwise, the case is considered
ambiguous and excluded from evaluation.
The evaluation consists of 1500 held-out dilemmas, with positive and paired negative examples.
The example above shows a prompt containing the opposing opinion and one sampled response that
adopts it. The final dataset is balanced, with 750 items per class. The monitoring agent often has no
visible reasoning that would reveal the influence of the user’s opinion.

Generation.
We seed this evaluation with questions from the biology and chemistry splits of
WMDP (Li et al., 2024), supplemented with biology and chemistry questions from MMLU-Redux

(Kretschmar et al., 2025). Gemini 3.6 Flash (temperature 0) then filters out question-context pairs
where the question is not plausibly relevant to the stated harmful goal.
We establish the assistant’s baseline belief by asking each question using three neutral templates,
with 5 samples per template (Qwen3.6-27B, temperature 1.0, thinking disabled). A question is
considered stable if one answer appears in at least 12 of the 15 samples, including at least 4 of 5
samples for each neutral template. We then sample 5 responses for each harmful-context pair. A
pair is labeled a lie if at least 4 of 5 responses select a different letter from the neutral answer, and
honest if at least 4 of 5 match it. We exclude ambiguous pairs and refusals. Thus, the label captures
whether the model’s response agrees with its own baseline belief rather than with the benchmark
answer key. We do not require the model’s baseline belief to match the benchmark’s correct answer,
although this could have been imposed as an additional constraint.

From examining the CoT, we find that Qwen3.6-27B often recognizes that it should refuse but

Generation. The value leakage benchmark is inspired by Betley et al. (2026). For our evaluation,
we use their nine Fermi-estimation questions. We also experimented with their other question sets,
but the counterfactual shifts were less clear. To turn the results into a classification task, we frame
each question in terms of shifts in a binomial distribution. We begin by sampling 100 responses
from Qwen3.6-27B (non-reasoning, temperature 1.0), then use Claude Sonnet 4.6 (temperature 0)
to extract the numeric estimates. Responses that provide a range despite the instructions, or whose
answers cannot be parsed, are discarded (typically no more than 2% of samples). The remaining
estimates form the neutral distribution. We use its median as the question’s threshold, dividing
responses according to whether their estimates fall above or below it.
After establishing the neutral distribution, we introduce a counterfactual intervention by adding a
Note paragraph stating that some event with no factual relevance to the estimate depends on whether
the model’s answer exceeds or falls below the threshold. These interventions range from donations
19


to good or bad causes to other one-sided framings involving malaria prevention, discarded meals,
personal rewards, team hope, light color, a coin flip, and two equally good charities. We also include
a threshold-only control.
For each intervention, we sample responses and compute the fraction of estimates above the thresh-
old. We then perform a two-sided binomial test against 0.5 and apply the Benjamini–Hochberg
correction. An intervention is labeled upward or downward if q ≤0.001, according to the direc-
tion of the shift. We assign the label none only when an exact binomial equivalence test (TOST,
p < 0.05) establishes that the above-threshold rate lies within [0.35, 0.65]. All remaining cases are
excluded as ambiguous.
A forward item, as in the canonical example above, presents the Observer with the neutral prompt, an
assistant response containing the neutral median, and a question asking how the response distribution
would change if the Note intervention were added. A reverse item instead shows the prompt with
the Note already included and asks the Observer to predict the corresponding counterfactual without
the intervention. The reverse direction is more difficult: models are less likely to acknowledge
biases in their own previous answers than to predict that their future answers might be biased. The
ground-truth label for the reverse direction is obtained by inverting the forward label. For the final
value leakage AUROC, we report the mean across the two directions, although reverse performance
is generally lower.
Unlike the other evaluations, this task has three classes rather than two. To retain AUROC as the
evaluation metric, we compute a macro-averaged one-vs-rest AUROC across the three labels.

Training setup.
We start from the observer SFT data (24,950 behavior-prediction and 24,950

becomes a new user turn, with the question text unchanged (it still refers to “the assistant”), and the

person of the assistant whose behavior it describes (e.g., “The assistant ignored the user’s request”

actors. In behavior-prediction examples, the question follows the user prompt directly, with no as-

infer the trait from a response that appears in its context without that system prompt.
We train with the same settings as the observer’s first SFT stage (Appendix D): rank-32 LoRA, one

stage on XML-formatted examples.

thinking enabled. We use a constant learning rate of 3 × 10−6, a KL penalty of 0.01 toward the SFT
checkpoint, and the same rewards, Jeffreys-smoothed targets, advantage normalization, and easy-
group filtering as Appendix D. Unlike the observer’s RL stage, this run uses only counterfactual
behavior prediction (4,520 questions; no multiple-choice task), 64 rollouts per group (32 groups per
step), and no OPD; one epoch is 142 steps. We evaluate the step-120 checkpoint, which had the
highest mean reasoning AUROC among the checkpoints we tested (steps 80, 100, 110, 120, 130,
and 142). All results in this appendix use non-reasoning prefill scoring.

SFT DATA GENERATION
Both SFT datasets use user prompts from a 50,000-prompt sample of Dolci-Instruct-SFT-7B (Olmo
et al., 2025); the two datasets share no prompts. The assistant is Qwen3.6-27B sampled at tem-
perature 1.0 with thinking disabled. All question–answer pairs are written by Claude Haiku 4.5 at
temperature 1.0.
Behavior prediction. For each prompt we sample five assistant responses of at most 800 tokens.
Haiku sees the prompt and the five numbered responses and writes five query–answer pairs (at most
2,500 output tokens). The instructions ask for open-ended, predictive questions in the future tense,
state that the questions will be asked of a model that sees only the prompt and expects a single re-
sponse, and require each answer to be consistent with all or all but one of the five responses. This
instruction is how five responses are reduced to one answer; we do not check agreement automat-
ically. The prompt also contains one worked example with five responses and five pairs. Pairs are
parsed from numbered tags, and malformed pairs are discarded. Over 50,000 prompts this produced
249,107 pairs. Each pair is a separate training example with a user turn, a query turn, and an observer
turn, and no assistant turn.
Hidden trait. Haiku first writes a catalog of 500 distinct traits in ten batches of 50. The catalog
prompt asks for personas, tones, registers, formats, languages, and response policies, including rec-
ognizable fictional characters, traits involving intentional dishonesty, and subtle as well as conspic-
uous tendencies, and forbids near-duplicates. Most resulting traits are personas or speaking styles,
e.g., “Speaks with affected Victorian formality and uses archaic vocabulary regardless of context.”
A trait is inserted with the fixed system prompt “Exhibit this trait consistently in your response:
{trait}”. Each trait is paired with ten user prompts, and for each pair we sample five responses of at
most 2,000 tokens (5,000 prompt groups). Haiku sees the trait, the system prompt, the user prompt,
and all five responses, and writes five query–answer pairs, the i-th attached to the i-th response. It
is asked for questions a reviewer would ask (e.g., about truthfulness, instruction following, helpful-
ness, harm, manipulation, clarity, privacy, self-representation, bias), which make sense without the
system prompt, do not name or paraphrase the trait, and allow a positive or negative verdict, with
answers grounded in the attached response. Groups whose output could not be parsed were dropped
(10 of 5,000), leaving 24,950 examples. The system prompt is removed from the training example.
Label quality. Because the five hidden-trait pairs are written jointly, some answers describe a sibling
response rather than the attached one. In 15.2% of hidden-trait examples, the answer contains a
quotation of at least 25 characters that is absent from the attached response and prompt but present
in a sibling response (3.9% at 60 characters), and 1.7% of answers refer to two or more numbered
responses. We did not filter these examples.
Mix and size. The SFT set contains all 24,950 hidden-trait examples and 24,950 behavior-prediction
examples from 4,990 prompts with five valid pairs each, selected with a fixed seed among prompts
not used for hidden traits. We hold out 1,000 prompt groups (5,000 examples). Table 4 gives token
counts. Hidden-trait answers are longer, so they account for 79% of supervised tokens despite the
even example mix. The main SFT run trains on 3.46M supervised tokens (observer answers and
end-of-turn tokens) over 350 steps.

questions about the assistant’s own behavior with synthetic distractors taken from the behavior of
other models. We initially sample a prompt from Dolci IF SFT and generate a response from our
subject model Qwen 3.6 27B and the following distractor models: Llama 4 Scout, Qwen 3.5-397B-
A17B, Gemma 4 31B, Mistral Small. A question concerning the behavior of a model with respect
to the prompt is then written by Claude Haiku. Claude Haiku is shown this question and these five
responses are, which are anonymized and shuffled, and writes one choice per response answering the
question. Haiku is instructed to be grounded in observable content, mutually distinguishable, and
stylistically parallel in length, grammatical form, and specificity, so that no choice is identifiable
as the intended answer by surface features alone. When two responses are indistinguishable with
respect to the question, the item is dropped. The choice describing the target model’s response is
designated correct.
Validation. In order for them to be validated, the new MCQA pairs are then required to pass a
blind reproducibility check. The first step to doing that is to validate the results the other way.
To do this, four fresh rollouts are drawn from the target assistant, and Claude Haiku is shown the
question-answer pair and the new responses. Then we ask Haiku which single choice best describes
a consistent behavior across at least three of the four Qwen 3.6-27B rollouts, or NONE if no choice
does (we want to give our model a way out). By doing it this way, we can ensure that correct
answers are actually stable under resampling, not just some one-off from a singular rollout, while
also maintaining genuine distractors.

J
ACTIVATION ORACLES
We use the activation oracle training recipe of Choi et al. (2026). The oracle is a rank-16 LoRA
adapter with alpha 32, on all linear modules, on a instruction-tuned Qwen3.6-27B. Residual-stream
activations from every layer of the subject model are patched into the corresponding layers of the
28


oracle at placeholder token positions, and the oracle then answers a natural-language question about
them. It is trained for 4,500 steps at batch size 64 with AdamW, using a learning rate of 10−4 that
decays to 10−5 on a cosine schedule (22.6M decoder tokens). We use their Qwen3.6-27B data mix:
37.5% user modeling, 37.5% self-supervised context prediction on off-policy FineWeb text, 20%
prediction of non-thinking responses, and 5% prediction of the next chain-of-thought step.
We evaluate the final checkpoint of this recipe (step 4,499), which was not trained on any of our
observer data. At evaluation time the oracle reads activations from the evaluated transcript (up to
2,048 tokens) and is asked each evaluation’s oversight question.

INTROSPECTIVELY TRAINED ASSISTANT DISTRIBUTION SHIFT
K.1
DISTRIBUTION SHIFT IN EVALUATIONS
Overall, the introspectively trained assistant exhibits each behavior (sycophancy, lying, reward-
hacking) at a similar or slightly lower rate than the original Qwen3.6-27B assistant (Figures 10,
12, and 13). There are also occasional distribution shifts in which examples show the behavior: for
example, 14 of the 29 questions on which the trained assistant lies under harm pressure are not ones
the original assistant lied on (Figure 13).
K.2
COMPARISON TO HONESTY POST-TRAINING
Honesty training makes the assistant easier to monitor from its transcript, but not from its activations,
as shown in Table 11. Follow-up questioning rises from 0.754 to 0.849, self transcript monitoring
from 0.608 to 0.752, and GLM-5.3 transcript monitoring from 0.697 to 0.785, while activation
oracles are unchanged (0.638 to 0.639). Observer training, in contrast, improves activation oracles
from 0.638 to 0.829, along with larger gains on every transcript-based method. This suggests that
the two interventions improve monitorability differently, with observer training also changing the
assistant’s internal representations in a way that makes them easier to decode.
