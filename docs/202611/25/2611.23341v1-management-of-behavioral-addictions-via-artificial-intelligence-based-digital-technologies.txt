1. Introduction
Addiction is widely recognized as a chronic and relapsing disorder involving persistent engagement in re-
warding behaviors despite harmful consequences. It is typically associated with impaired behavioral control
and dysregulation of neural reward and control systems [1, 2]. Behavioral addictions, also known as pro-
cess addictions, involve persistent engagement in rewarding behaviors despite negative consequences and
diminished behavioral control [3, 4].
Epidemiological evidence indicates rising prevalence rates for gambling disorder, internet gaming dis-
order, problematic smartphone use, and online behavioral addictions, particularly among adolescents and
young adults [5, 6]. These disorders impose substantial societal burdens through academic decline, produc-
tivity loss, psychiatric comorbidity, and healthcare utilization. Traditional treatment approaches, including
cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), motivational interviewing, and relapse-prevention protocols, demon-
strate efﬁcacy but remain constrained by limited accessibility and engagement challenges [7].
Behavioral addictions are inherently context-dependent and temporally dynamic, with craving intensity,
cue exposure, and self-regulation capacity ﬂuctuating across everyday environments [8]. Consequently,
episodic clinic-based interventions are often insufﬁcient for sustained relapse prevention [9, 10]. In paral-
lel, the same digital environments that facilitate addictive behaviors also generate ﬁne-grained behavioral
traces, enabling continuous monitoring and real-time assessment. Leveraging these data streams, recent
advances in digital phenotyping, mobile sensing, and artiﬁcial intelligence have enabled a shift toward pre-
cision mental health and adaptive, context-aware intervention strategies [11, 12, 13, 14, 15].
These developments support AI-driven DTx and just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAIs), which en-
able personalized, real-time intervention delivery. However, despite rapid technological advances, existing
literature remains fragmented across modality-speciﬁc reviews, AI-centric prediction studies, and disorder-
speciﬁc surveys, highlighting the need for a uniﬁed and comprehensive framework for digital management
of behavioral addictions.
1.1. Deﬁnition and taxonomy of addictions
Building on the general addiction model, behavioral addictions are deﬁned as non-substance-related be-
haviors characterized by persistent engagement despite adverse consequences and diminished behavioral
control [3, 4]. These conditions involve repetitive, reinforcement-driven behaviors that provide short-term re-
ward or relief but lead to long-term functional impairment. They share core features with substance use dis-
orders, including compulsive engagement, escalation of behavior, withdrawal-like symptoms, cue-reactivity,
and elevated relapse risk [3, 4].
Despite the absence of exogenous substances, similar neurocognitive and behavioral mechanisms are
implicated, particularly those related to RL and impaired inhibitory control [1, 2]. Theoretical frameworks
such as the Interaction of Person–Affect–Cognition–Execution (I-PACE) model conceptualize behavioral
addictions as dynamic and interactional processes [4]. These processes emerge from the interplay between
individual predispositions, affective states, cognitive biases, and executive control mechanisms [4]. Within
this perspective, addictive behaviors are understood as failures of self-regulation maintained through con-
ditioned cue-response associations and maladaptive learning processes [4]. Importantly, behavioral addic-
tions are increasingly conceptualized as dimensional phenomena characterized by ﬂuctuating vulnerability
states rather than ﬁxed diagnostic categories.
From a taxonomical perspective, behavioral addictions can be categorized along multiple dimensions
[4], including (i) the primary behavioral domain (e.g., gambling, gaming, compulsive buying), (ii) modality
(digital versus non-digital), and (iii) underlying reinforcement mechanisms (e.g., reward-seeking, emotion
regulation, social reinforcement). Gambling disorder is formally recognized as a behavioral addiction in
DSM-5-TR and ICD-11, while gaming disorder is included in ICD-11 and listed as a condition for further study


in DSM-5-TR [16, 17, 18]. In addition, problematic internet use (PIU), excessive social media engagement, and
smartphone overuse are widely studied forms of digital behavioral addictions [6, 19, 20, 21]. Other forms, in-
cluding compulsive online pornography consumption and binge-eating behaviors, are also discussed within
the broader behavioral addiction spectrum [4, 3]. A schematic overview of this multidimensional taxonomy
is presented in Fig. 1, providing a structured representation of domains, modality, and underlying mecha-
nisms, along with shared core processes.

Digital behavioral addictions constitute a rapidly expanding subgroup, driven by ubiquitous connectivity,
algorithmically optimized engagement systems, and continuous access to personalized content [19]. These
digital environments amplify reinforcement cycles through mechanisms such as variable reward schedules,
social feedback loops, and real-time content personalization [19, 4]. This multidimensional taxonomy pro-
vides a conceptual foundation for understanding behavioral addictions and supports the development of
computational models, digital phenotyping approaches, and adaptive intervention systems discussed in

1.2. Prevalence and societal impact
Behavioral addictions have shown a marked increase in prevalence over the past decade, particularly in the
context of rapid digitalization and widespread access to internet-enabled devices [6, 19, 22, 23]. Epidemi-
ological studies suggest that PIU affects approximately 6–7% of the global population, with higher preva-
lence observed among adolescents and young adults [6, 20, 24, 25]. Internet gaming disorder (IGD) has
been reported with prevalence rates ranging from 1% to 10% depending on population characteristics and
diagnostic criteria [26, 22, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. Excessive social media use and smartphone overuse
have also emerged as signiﬁcant concerns, with elevated prevalence reported particularly in younger pop-
ulations [34, 35, 36, 37, 19, 21].
Beyond prevalence, behavioral addictions impose substantial individual and societal burdens. At the

individual level, these conditions are associated with impaired academic and occupational functioning,
reduced quality of life, and increased risk of comorbid psychiatric disorders, including depression, anxiety,
and attention-related conditions [38, 39, 40, 27]. Their chronic and relapsing nature further contributes to
long-term functional impairment and diminished psychosocial well-being [41].
At the societal level, behavioral addictions contribute to productivity loss, increased healthcare utiliza-
tion, and broader economic costs. Emerging evidence also suggests that excessive digital engagement
may negatively impact social relationships through mechanisms such as social withdrawal, reduced face-
to-face interaction, and altered communication patterns [42, 43, 19]. Moreover, the algorithmic design of
digital platforms—optimized for engagement and retention—can amplify addictive usage patterns at scale,
raising concerns regarding ethical design, user autonomy, and public health implications [44, 45, 46].
Notably, vulnerability to behavioral addictions is not uniformly distributed. It is shaped by demographic
factors (e.g., age, gender), psychological traits (e.g., impulsivity, sensation-seeking), and environmental
conditions (e.g., accessibility, social context) [47, 48, 49, 50]. Adolescents and young adults are consis-
tently identiﬁed as high-risk groups due to developmental sensitivity and intensive exposure to digital envi-
ronments [6, 51, 19, 52].
Taken together, the increasing prevalence and multifaceted societal impact of behavioral addictions
highlight the need for scalable, adaptive, and technology-driven approaches to prevention, monitoring, and
intervention [13, 53, 9, 23, 11]. These trends further motivate the integration of digital technologies and
artiﬁcial intelligence into addiction management frameworks, as discussed in subsequent sections.
1.3. Motivation for digital approaches in prevention, treatment, and recovery
The increasing prevalence and dynamic nature of behavioral addictions necessitate innovative approaches
that extend beyond traditional, clinic-based treatment models. Conventional interventions, including CBT,
motivational interviewing, and relapse-prevention strategies, have demonstrated effectiveness; however,
they remain constrained by limited accessibility, high cost, social stigma, and the inability to provide con-
tinuous monitoring and real-time support [7, 54, 55].
Behavioral addictions exhibit substantial variability across real-world contexts, with ﬂuctuations in crav-


ing, cue exposure, emotional states, and self-regulation capacity. This complexity reduces the effective-
ness of episodic, clinic-based interventions and underscores the need for continuous, context-aware, and
adaptive support for relapse prevention [4, 8]. Digital technologies offer a transformative opportunity to
address these limitations by enabling continuous, context-aware, and scalable intervention strategies. The
widespread adoption of smartphones, wearable devices, and internet-connected platforms has facilitated
the emergence of digital phenotyping approaches, which enable passive and real-time monitoring of behav-
ioral, physiological, and contextual data [12, 19, 56]. These data streams provide unprecedented insights
into user behavior and risk states, forming the basis for personalized and adaptive intervention systems.
In parallel, DTx have evolved from static content delivery systems into intelligent, data-driven platforms
capable of tailoring interventions to individual needs. Artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) and machine learning tech-
niques enable predictive modeling, user proﬁling, and dynamic decision-making, thereby supporting preci-
sion mental health frameworks [14, 15, 57, 58]. These approaches facilitate the development of adaptive
treatment pathways that respond to changes in user behavior and context over time.
A particularly promising paradigm in this domain is the concept of JITAIs, which aim to deliver the
right type of support at the right time and in the right context. JITAIs leverage real-time data and sequen-
tial decision-making models to optimize intervention delivery, improving both engagement and treatment
effectiveness [59, 8, 60, 61, 62].
Furthermore, the integration of multimodal data sources—including behavioral logs, sensor data, and
contextual signals—has enhanced the ability of AI systems to model complex addiction dynamics. Multi-
modal learning approaches enable more accurate risk prediction, early detection of relapse, and personal-
ized intervention strategies [63, 64, 65, 66].
Recent studies have also demonstrated the feasibility of AI-based prediction models for identifying
addiction risk and behavioral escalation using digital behavioral traces [67, 68]. At the same time, digi-
tal mental health ecosystems—including mobile health applications, conversational agents, and telehealth
platforms—continue to expand access to care while introducing new challenges related to engagement,
regulation, and ethical considerations [69, 70, 71].
These developments highlight the critical role of digital technologies and AI-driven systems in advanc-
ing the prevention, treatment, and long-term management of behavioral addictions.
1.4. Previous works and our contributions
The growing interest in behavioral addictions and digital health technologies has led to a substantial body
of review literature spanning multiple disciplines, including psychology, psychiatry, computer science, and
digital health. Existing works can be broadly categorized into several thematic groups.
First, theoretical and clinical reviews have focused on conceptual frameworks and the underlying mech-
anisms of behavioral addictions. Notably, the I-PACE model and its extensions provide a comprehensive
theoretical basis for understanding the interaction between individual vulnerabilities, affective processes,
cognitive biases, and executive control mechanisms [4]. More recent work has further advanced this per-
spective by integrating insights from neuroscience and clinical practice. Despite these efforts, existing
studies remain fragmented, and no work provides a uniﬁed framework integrating these dimensions.
Second, intervention-oriented reviews have examined the effectiveness of traditional and digital treat-
ment approaches. Systematic reviews of web-based interventions highlight the potential of online platforms
for delivering scalable treatments, particularly in the context of behavioral addictions such as problematic
internet use and gaming disorder [72]. Meta-analyses on relapse prevention strategies further emphasize
the importance of continuous support and adaptive intervention mechanisms in improving long-term out-
comes [73]. Early digital platforms further illustrate the feasibility of technology-assisted addiction man-
agement systems [74].


Third, computational and artiﬁcial intelligence–driven studies have explored the role of data-driven mod-
eling in addiction research. Reviews in this domain highlight the growing use of machine learning and com-
putational approaches to model addictive behaviors, predict risk, and support decision-making [65, 58].
These studies demonstrate the potential of AI in advancing personalized and predictive frameworks for
addiction management.
Fourth, digital mental health and e-health reviews have investigated the role of emerging technologies
in transforming mental health care delivery. Studies in digital psychiatry and mobile health emphasize
the increasing adoption of digital platforms, the potential for scalable interventions, and the challenges
associated with engagement, regulation, and ethical considerations [75, 56, 69, 76].
Fifth, recent work has also examined the broader research ecosystem, including data sharing practices
and ethical considerations in addiction research. These studies highlight critical issues related to data
governance, privacy, transparency, and fairness in AI-driven systems [77, 70, 71].
Existing studies provide valuable insights into speciﬁc aspects of behavioral addiction research. The-
oretical models establish a strong conceptual foundation, while intervention-focused reviews and meta-
analyses demonstrate the effectiveness of treatment strategies. Digital platforms and AI-driven studies
further highlight the feasibility and potential of technology-assisted addiction management systems. How-
ever, these contributions remain largely isolated and do not fully support an integrative perspective.

typically address isolated components, such as theoretical models, speciﬁc intervention modalities, or AI-

2. The Clinical and Psychological Foundations of Addiction
2.1. Neurobiological mechanisms and reward systems
For a long time, addiction was viewed solely as a disease resulting from the direct stimulation of the brain’s
reward systems by psychoactive substances such as alcohol, cocaine, or heroin [79]. In this classical ap-
proach, it was accepted that substances activate the mesolimbic dopaminergic pathways, leading to in-
creased dopamine in the nucleus accumbens (NA), and that this forms the basis of addiction [80]. How-
ever, it is now clear that the process is more complex than this. While dopamine elevation is signiﬁcant, it
is believed that the primary mechanisms sustaining addiction are neuroplastic changes that develop over
time and the reorganization of neural networks [81].
The functioning of the reward system is crucial at this point. The mesocorticolimbic system operates as
a circuit connecting the ventral tegmental area (VTA), the NA, and the pre-frontal cortex. Dopamine released
from the VTA enhances reward anticipation in the NA, while the prefrontal cortex evaluates this information
to determine whether to continue the behavior. It should be particularly emphasized here that dopamine is
not merely a molecule associated with pleasure. Rather, it serves as a signal that guides learning through
“reward prediction errors.” Better-than-expected outcomes ensure that the behaviors leading to these out-
comes are reinforced via dopaminergic mechanisms [82, 83]. From a clinical perspective, this mechanism
helps explain why behavior can become so persistent, particularly under variable and unpredictable reward
conditions such as in gambling or online gaming.
Beyond dopamine release, changes at the receptor level are equally important. The D1 and D2 receptors
located in the striatum have distinct functions; while D1 receptors primarily facilitate the reinforcement of
reward-related behaviors, D2 receptors are associated with inhibitory control [2]. It has been demonstrated
that chronic substance use leads to a decrease (down regulation) in D2 receptors in particular, and that this
is associated with impaired impulse control [84, 85]. This phenomenon can be considered the neurobiologi-
cal basis for the clinically common state of “being unable to stop despite knowing one should not.” The fact
that similar ﬁndings have been demonstrated in behavioral addictions suggests that these disorders share
a common biological basis [3, 86]. Table 2 shows key neurobiological mechanisms in behavioral addictions
and their clinical implications.

This process is also linked to the development of tolerance. Stimuli that initially elicit a strong dopamin-
ergic response begin to have a weaker effect over time, and the individual repeats the behavior more fre-
quently or intensely to achieve the same effect [87, 88].
At the same time, the “reward sensitivity” model provides an important framework for understanding
addiction. According to this model, over time, cues associated with the reward come to the forefront rather
than the reward itself, and these cues create a powerful motivational pull. In this process, craving behavior
becomes pronounced, and the individual often continues the behavior not to seek pleasure but to reduce
this intense craving [89]. This distinction is clinically signiﬁcant as it shows that the processes of craving
and pleasure can be dissociated from one another.
As addiction progresses, the role of dopamine diminishes relatively, while the glutamatergic system
becomes more prominent. In particular, changes in glutamatergic projections extending from the pre-frontal
cortex to the NA play a signiﬁcant role in behavior shifting from goal-directed to habit-based and compulsive
[80]. Therefore, addiction can be viewed not merely as a process driven by reward-seeking, but also as a
habit that becomes established over time and a disorder of behavioral regulation [90].
Although dopamine plays a central role, the contribution of other neurotransmitter systems cannot be
overlooked. The serotonin system inﬂuences impulsivity and behavioral inhibition, and it is thought that
disruptions in this system may increase the tendency toward short-term rewards [91]. While glutamate
plays a decisive role in learning processes and the maintenance of behavior, the endogenous opioid system
modulates the reward experience [88, 92]. The endocannabinoid system, meanwhile, functions as a high-
level modulator that regulates the overall sensitivity of this network [93].
In recent years, evidence that behavioral addictions also develop through similar neurobiological mech-
anisms has broadened the scope of the concept of addiction. It is known that behavioral addictions such
as gambling, online gaming, and compulsive shopping also activate the reward circuit and are maintained
in a similar manner [3]. This has also been reﬂected in the classiﬁcation changes made in the DSM-5 and
ICD-11 [94]. Current data indicate that behavioral addictions overlap signiﬁcantly with substance addictions
from both neurobiological and clinical perspectives [86].
The role of the prefrontal cortex is particularly important in terms of behavioral control. While the dor-
solateral prefrontal cortex is involved in impulse control and working memory, the orbitofrontal cortex plays
a role in the evaluation of reward value, and the anterior cingulate cortex plays a role in error monitoring pro-
cesses [95]. Functional changes in these regions contribute to the maintenance of compulsive patterns by
reducing an individual’s capacity to regulate behavior. Neuroimaging studies have also reported signiﬁcant
changes in frontostriatal circuits associated with addiction [81].
It has been shown that similar neural circuits are functionally affected in behavioral addictions as well.
In neuroimaging studies, particularly in individuals with gambling disorder and internet gaming addiction,
increased activity in striatal regions associated with reward processing and decreased activation in pre-
frontal areas associated with executive control have been reported [82]. Additionally, it has been shown
that an increased neural response to addiction-related cues (“cue reactivity”) is prominent in these indi-
viduals and is associated with craving. These ﬁndings support the notion that common neurobiological
mechanisms—involving heightened reward anticipation and weakened inhibitory control that contribute to
the maintenance of behavior are also relevant in behavioral addictions.
In conclusion, addiction is a condition that arises from the interaction of a broad neural network in-
volving reward processing, learning, and executive control processes. While the dopaminergic system is a
key component of this process, the contribution of glutamatergic and other neurotransmitter systems re-
sults in a more complex structure [87, 88]. Changes in dopamine receptor levels and functional differences
in frontostriatal circuits provide an important framework for understanding the clinical characteristics of
addiction.


2.2. Psychological models
In understanding behavioral addictions, the psychosocial model offers a more holistic framework that goes
beyond explanations limited to biological mechanisms, instead addressing individual, interpersonal, and
sociocultural levels together [96, 97]. In this approach, addiction is viewed not as a condition reducible
to a single cause, but rather as a dynamic process resulting from the individual’s developmental history,
psychological characteristics, learning processes, and interaction with their environment [98]. Behavioral
addictions are characterized by repetitive behavioral patterns that are maintained despite short-term re-
wards and long-term negative consequences. It is known that these behaviors share classic addiction
components such as salience, tolerance, withdrawal, conﬂict, and relapse. All of this suggests that treating
addiction solely as a neurobiological process is insufﬁcient; it must be evaluated within a multidimensional
network of interactions.
In this context, the I-PACE model developed by Brand et al. [98] provides an important theoretical frame-
work for explaining the development and maintenance of behavioral addictions. According to the model,
speciﬁc internet use disorders such as gaming, gambling, pornography, shopping, and social media use
arise from the interaction of the individual’s neurobiological and psychological predispositions (genetic
traits, early life experiences, depression, social anxiety, and impulsivity), coping strategies, cognitive biases
regarding internet use, and the interaction of affective-cognitive responses to situational triggers. Within
this framework, impulsivity, reward sensitivity, and personality traits form the individual foundation; mean-
while, emotional responses and cognitive processes (particularly craving and cognitive distortions) emerge
as the primary mechanisms that trigger and sustain behavior. Executive functions play a role in regulating
behavior, particularly through impulse control and decision-making processes; environmental factors and
the availability of stimuli, meanwhile, shape the contextual dimension of this process. Additionally, the
model emphasizes that classical and operant conditioning in the addiction process reinforce cue-response
and craving responses; conversely, executive functions and inhibitory control weaken over time. From a
clinical perspective, it is observed that these components are often not independent of one another but
rather operate in a dynamic and reciprocal interaction.
In the updated version of the model, Brand et al. [4] argued that the I-PACE model is not limited to
internet use disorders; it is also applicable to other behavioral addictions such as gambling disorder, gaming
disorder, shopping disorder, and compulsive sexual behavior disorder. The updated model addresses the
addiction process in terms of early and advanced stages. In the early stage, general inhibitory control
plays a regulatory role between emotional responses and behavioral decisions; in the advanced stage, cue-
reactivity and craving, reinforced by conditioning processes, weaken stimulus-speciﬁc inhibitory control and
lead to the behavior gradually becoming habitual. At the neurobiological level, ﬁndings suggest that in the
early stages, activity in the ventral striatum associated with reward anticipation is more pronounced; as the
process progresses, a shift toward the dorsal striatum occurs.
Individual psychological characteristics also play a signiﬁcant role in the development of addiction. In
particular, traits such as impulsivity and low self-control have been shown to be associated with addictive
behaviors [90]. In these individuals, short-term rewards are more likely to become appealing, and long-
term consequences are more likely to be disregarded. Additionally, it has long been known that adverse
childhood experiences and traumatic events are associated with addictive behaviors [99]. It is thought that
such experiences may contribute to the emergence of addictive behaviors as a coping mechanism through
difﬁculties in emotional regulation [100, 101]. Furthermore, it has been proposed that early-life schemas,
particularly themes of rejection and disconnection, provide a cognitive backdrop for this process [102].
The social and relational context also plays a signiﬁcant role in the development of addiction. The family
environment is a fundamental setting that shapes an individual’s emotional development and coping skills;
in particular, it has been demonstrated that attachment patterns are associated with addictive behaviors
[103, 104]. Insecure attachment styles are more strongly associated with behavioral addictions [105]. In
addition, it is thought that environmental factors such as the family environment and level of social support
may inﬂuence the risk of addiction [106]. Peer inﬂuence and social learning processes, in particular, play a


signiﬁcant role in the maintenance of behaviors among adolescents and young adults.
It is well established that behavioral addictions are closely linked to learning and reinforcement pro-
cesses. In particular, reward-based learning mechanisms facilitate the repetition of behavior and its rein-
forcement over time. The reward structures observed in gambling and digital games emerge as a signiﬁcant
factor reinforcing this process. Behaviors that are initially more goal-directed gradually acquire a habit-
based and more compulsive nature over time [90]. During this process, the individual may continue the
behavior not only to obtain pleasure but also to avoid negative emotions [87]. Additionally, cognitive distor-
tions—particularly the overestimation of reward expectations and the illusion of control—play a signiﬁcant
role in the maintenance of the behavior.
In conclusion, the psychosocial model demonstrates that behavioral addictions cannot be explained
by a single-dimensional approach. The interaction of individual characteristics, cognitive and emotional
processes, learning mechanisms, and environmental factors collectively plays a role in the development
and maintenance of addictive behaviors. This holistic approach provides an important framework for both
clinical assessment and treatment planning.
2.3. Comorbidity
Behavioral addictions constitute a clinically heterogeneous group that is frequently observed both within
the category itself and in conjunction with other psychiatric disorders [3, 86]. This comorbidity not only
increases the burden of disease but also complicates the understanding of etiopathogenesis and treat-
ment planning [107]. Indeed, various studies report a high prevalence of additional psychiatric diagnoses
in individuals with behavioral addictions.
It is well-known that rates of psychiatric comorbidity are quite high in gambling addiction. In a meta-
analysis by Lorains et al. [108] based on 11 studies using general population samples, it was shown that the
most common comorbid condition among problem and pathological gamblers was nicotine dependence
(60.1%). This is followed, in order, by any substance use disorder (57.5%), mood disorders (37.9%), anxiety
disorders (37.4%), and antisocial personality disorder (28.8%).
However, it is also worth noting that these rates may vary depending on the methods used. For example,
the prevalence of alcohol use disorder can reach as high as 41.2% in interviews conducted by clinicians,
while it can drop as low as 18.2% in self-report scales. Similarly, lifetime prevalence rates (33.4%) are
reported to be higher than past-year prevalence rates (15.7%). These ﬁndings suggest that comorbidity
rates are highly heterogeneous and that interpreting them based on a single rate may be misleading.
IGD and PIU often co-occur with other psychiatric conditions. Indeed, studies report that the most com-
mon comorbid conditions associated with IGD are anxiety (92%), depression (89%), attention-deﬁcit/hyperactivity
disorder (ADHD; 85%), and social problems (75%). A study conducted on a large sample in South Korea also
demonstrates that PIU is not limited to such internalizing problems; it is also associated with nicotine and
alcohol use disorders, somatoform disorders, pathological gambling, sleep problems, and suicidal ideation
[109].
The clinical picture becomes even more severe, particularly in cases where IGD co-occurs with alcohol
use disorder. It has been shown that this group exhibits higher levels of impulsivity, difﬁculty with self-
control, and depression; they also spend more money on online games [110]. On the other hand, functional
imaging studies in adolescents with IGD have revealed increased connectivity between the salience network
and executive control networks, and it has been suggested that this may be associated with comorbid
psychiatric disorders [111].
Data on causality, however, point to a different conclusion. A longitudinal study found that existing
psychopathologies, particularly depression and anxiety, predict the development of IGD; conversely, IGD
does not increase psychopathology over time [112]. This suggests that internalizing disorders may often be
a precursor rather than a consequence of IGD. However, it should not be forgotten that comorbidity rates


can vary depending on the measurement tools used. Ferguson et al.’s meta-analysis demonstrates that
different scales can yield different results [113].
High rates of psychiatric comorbidity have also been reported in other behavioral addictions. Clinical
studies have found that conditions such as kleptomania, compulsive buying, and compulsive sexual behav-
ior disorder frequently co-occur with substance use disorders [114]. In compulsive buying disorder, rates
of ADHD, borderline personality disorder, and binge eating disorder were found to be signiﬁcantly higher
compared to the control group. In compulsive sexual behavior disorder, comorbidity with mood disorders
has been reported at 36–81%, and with anxiety disorders at 46–96% [115]. In general, psychiatric diagnoses
such as major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, OCD, and ADHD frequently co-occur with behavioral ad-
dictions; however, it should be noted that the vast majority of these ﬁndings are based on clinical samples,
and their generalizability to the general population remains uncertain.
When exercise addiction and eating-related behavioral addictions are evaluated together, a signiﬁcant
overlap with eating disorders in particular is observed [116]. It has been reported that excessive exercise
behavior can be observed in 30–80% of patients with anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa. Obsessive-
compulsive traits and perfectionism are also more frequently observed in this group. Additionally, it is noted
that anxiety and depression are more common in individuals with exercise addiction.
Behavioral addictions are frequently observed not only alongside substance use disorders but also in
combination with one another. Shaffer et al. [117] noted that individuals who engage in compulsive shopping
and pathological gambling exhibit similarly high rates of addiction, explaining this pattern of co-occurrence
with the concept of the “addiction syndrome.” The authors also presented the fact that individuals recov-
ering from one addiction often transition to another behavioral addiction (for example, from gambling to
excessive shopping or vice versa) as one of the key pieces of evidence for this syndrome. Grant et al. [3]
also supported this view, reporting that other behavioral addictions such as compulsive shopping, com-
pulsive sexual behavior, and kleptomania are frequently found co-occurring with pathological gambling in
clinical samples. Similarly, Chamberlain and Grant [118] highlighted the comorbidity and familial overlap
reported in the literature between compulsive buying disorder and gambling disorder. All these ﬁndings
suggest that different behavioral addictions are not independent conditions but rather different manifesta-
tions of a syndrome sharing common neurobiological reward pathways, and thus often occur together or
sequentially [117, 3, 114].
The relationship between behavioral addictions and personality disorders is also clinically signiﬁcant;
in particular, signiﬁcant associations have been reported with antisocial personality traits. Impulsivity and
difﬁculties with emotion regulation are thought to play a signiﬁcant role in this relationship. Furthermore, the
higher prevalence of addiction and other psychiatric disorders among ﬁrst-degree relatives of individuals
with behavioral addictions suggests that these conditions may exhibit familial clustering.
2.4. Current clinical treatment paradigms and limitations
The treatment of behavioral addictions relies on evidence-based yet individualized approaches rather than
standardized algorithms, due to the clinical diversity of these disorders and the relatively recent nature of
their classiﬁcations [86, 3, 78]. The current literature indicates that evidence regarding pharmacological
treatment options is limited, that no approved pharmacological treatment exists for any behavioral addic-
tion, and that standard clinical treatment protocols have not yet been developed. Therefore, treatment
planning is based not only on the diagnosis but also on the symptom pattern, the function of the behavior,
disease severity, and comorbid psychiatric conditions [119].
In treatment approaches, the focus is typically on targeting the mechanisms that sustain the problem-
atic behavior rather than the behavior itself. In behavioral addictions, processes such as increased reward
sensitivity, decreased tolerance for delayed rewards, poor impulse control, cognitive distortions, and avoid-
ance of negative emotions are thought to be key factors [4]. While these processes create short-term sooth-
ing or rewarding effects, they contribute to the maintenance of the behavior in the long term [78]. Therefore,


interventions are aimed not only at reducing the behavior but also at targeting these processes to develop
more functional coping strategies.
From a treatment perspective, the I-PACE model emphasizes that it is more effective to target variables
that play a mediating and regulatory role in the process (coping styles, expectations regarding internet use,
attentional biases, cue-reactivity and craving, and executive functions) rather than predisposing factors that
are difﬁcult to change. In this vein, it is suggested that CBT-IA for internet addiction including intervention
methods such as cognitive restructuring, cue exposure therapy, attention retraining, and inhibition training
can target cognitive biases and functional impairments in the addiction process. The updated model also
supports this theoretical framework; by emphasizing the need to target mediating variables (expectations,
emotional and cognitive responses, inhibitory control) in treatment, it offers empirically testable hypotheses
for future intervention studies.
In clinical practice, treatment often follows a stepwise approach. In the ﬁrst phase, the goal is to in-
crease the patient’s awareness of the problem and reduce ambivalence toward change using motivational
interviewing techniques [120]. Following this, cognitive-behavioral therapy focuses on identifying triggers,
deﬁning high-risk situations, and developing alternative behaviors. Techniques such as stimulus control,
exposure, cognitive restructuring, and impulse management are frequently used in this process. In the
relapse prevention phase, the focus is particularly on emotional triggers, high-risk situations, and dysfunc-
tional thought patterns.
The presence of comorbid psychiatric disorders is quite common in behavioral addictions and can di-
rectly inﬂuence the treatment process. It is known that conditions such as depression, anxiety disorders,
and ADHD not only serve as factors that sustain addictive behavior but also inﬂuence the response to treat-
ment. Therefore, it is important to assess comorbid disorders during treatment planning and prioritize them
when necessary. Dell’Osso et al. [121] emphasized that the pattern of comorbidity in internet addiction could
guide the selection of pharmacological treatment and that treatment should be planned not only based on
the addictive behavior itself but also on the co-occurring psychopathological dimensions (impulsivity, com-
pulsion, craving, depressive, and anxiety symptoms).
The role of pharmacological treatments in behavioral addictions is more limited, and to date, there is
no FDA-approved medication for any behavioral addiction. Current data indicate that pharmacotherapy is
primarily used to target comorbid symptoms and underlying psychopathological dimensions rather than the
addictive behavior itself.
In recent years, new approaches targeting neurocognitive processes have also been investigated. It
has been suggested that neuromodulation techniques, such as transcranial magnetic stimulation, may be
effective in inﬂuencing impulse control and reward processing [122]. Mindfulness-based interventions have
been shown to reduce reactivity to cravings and trigger experiences associated with addictive behaviors
[123]. However, the clinical efﬁcacy of these methods is currently supported by a limited number of studies,
and more evidence is needed for routine clinical use.
The level of evidence regarding treatment also varies among behavioral addictions. Gambling disorder
stands out as the condition with the strongest evidence in this ﬁeld [124]. Numerous studies have demon-
strated that cognitive-behavioral therapy leads to a signiﬁcant reduction in the frequency and severity of
gambling behavior [125]. Cognitive distortions that sustain gambling behavior, particularly the illusion of
control and the desire to recoup losses, are among the primary treatment targets [126, 127].
From a pharmacological perspective, opioid receptor antagonists—particularly naltrexone and nalmefene-
stand out as the only class of medications to have consistently demonstrated positive results in double-
blind, placebo-controlled trials for gambling disorder [128]. Chamberlain and Grant [118] highlight two sepa-
rate studies demonstrating naltrexone’s superiority over placebo in reducing gambling-related behaviors and
thoughts, as well as two multicenter studies showing that nalmefene produced signiﬁcant improvements
in gambling urges and behaviors. In addition, placebo-controlled studies have shown that glutamate mod-
ulators such as N-acetylcysteine also produce a signiﬁcant reduction in gambling symptoms. In contrast,


the results of studies using SSRI-class antidepressants (sertraline, ﬂuvoxamine, paroxetine) have been in-
consistent, and bupropion has also failed to demonstrate superiority over placebo [129]. While lithium has
been found effective in patients with gambling disorder accompanied by bipolar spectrum disorders, two
studies using olanzapine showed no difference from placebo. Therefore, pharmacological treatments are
generally planned according to the accompanying psychiatric conditions and symptom proﬁle.
Treatment approaches for internet gaming disorder are primarily based on psychosocial interventions
[130]. Cognitive-behavioral therapy is the most commonly used method for reducing gaming time, restruc-
turing usage patterns, and modifying dysfunctional thoughts [131, 132]. It has also been shown that family-
based interventions can increase treatment adherence, particularly among adolescents.
Among pharmacological treatment options, agents such as bupropion, methylphenidate, and atomox-
etine have been studied, and it is believed that these medications may be beneﬁcial for some patients,
particularly due to their effects on attention and impulsivity. Dell’Osso et al. [121] reported that in open-
label studies, escitalopram improved weekly online time and overall functioning in internet addiction, but
failed to distinguish itself from placebo when the study transitioned to a double-blind phase; while bupro-
pion signiﬁcantly reduced online gaming time and depressive symptoms in a randomized controlled trial.
Additionally, methylphenidate has been reported to provide signiﬁcant improvements in both attention ca-
pacity and internet use in internet gaming addiction accompanied by ADHD. However, most current studies
are limited to small sample sizes, and the generalizability of the results appears limited.
Meta-analyses indicate that psychological interventions can have signiﬁcant effects on both the dura-
tion of use and symptoms of addiction [133]. Dell’Osso et al. [121] suggest that the psychopathological
dimensions shared by internet addiction with other behavioral and substance addictions (impulsivity, com-
pulsion, and reward system dysfunction) may guide treatment approaches, and that planning pharmacolog-
ical interventions targeting these common dimensions could enhance clinical beneﬁts.
Evidence regarding treatment for other behavioral addictions, such as compulsive shopping, hyper-
sexual behavior, and exercise addiction, is more limited [134]. Chamberlain and Grant [118] report that,
of the three placebo-controlled studies conducted for compulsive shopping, only citalopram yielded posi-
tive results, while ﬂuvoxamine studies failed to distinguish themselves from placebo. Regarding compulsive
sexual behavior, a single double-blind study demonstrated that citalopram resulted in a signiﬁcant reduction
in sexual urges and behaviors. Regarding kleptomania, there is a single positive placebo-controlled study in-
dicating that naltrexone is effective in reducing the urge and behavior to steal; however, escitalopram failed
to demonstrate superiority over placebo in the double-blind phase. Data on the efﬁcacy of psychological,
pharmacological, and combined treatments in this area suggest they may provide meaningful improvement
in the short term; however, the level of evidence remains quite limited [124].
In conclusion, although the treatment of behavioral addictions is shaped by common mechanisms, there
are signiﬁcant differences in the level of evidence across these disorders [78]. While cognitive-behavioral
therapy stands out as the primary approach for many behavioral addictions, pharmacological treatments are
used in more limited and speciﬁc cases. Chamberlain and Grant [98] emphasize that most pharmacological
treatment studies for behavioral addictions have been conducted with small sample sizes, over limited time
periods, and likely with clinically underrepresented groups, highlighting the need for more comprehensive
research to develop treatment algorithms. Dell’Osso et al. [121] also highlight the lack of large-sample
randomized controlled trials and long-term follow-up data speciﬁcally regarding internet addiction. There
is a clear need for studies conducted with larger samples and evaluating long-term outcomes, particularly
in areas beyond gambling and internet gaming disorder [135].


3. Digital Technologies in Addiction Management
Digital technologies are increasingly used in the management of behavioral (process) addictions, including
gambling disorder, internet gaming disorder, and problematic digital media use. These disorders are char-
acterized by impaired behavioral control, reinforcement-driven repetition, and high relapse risk. Clinical
assessment largely relies on behavioral indicators rather than biological markers. Therefore, continuous
monitoring of usage patterns, cognitive states, and contextual triggers is essential. Digital systems enable
ﬁne-grained behavioral data collection in real-life settings and support timely, context-aware psychological
interventions. This approach aligns well with contemporary models that conceptualize behavioral addic-
tions as dynamic and self-regulation–based disorders rather than episodic conditions [4, 136].
Recent advances in artiﬁcial intelligence and computational behavioral modeling have further enhanced
digital interventions for behavioral addictions [52]. Machine learning methods can analyze interaction logs,
screen-time metrics, and self-report data to identify maladaptive trajectories and predict escalation or re-
lapse risk. These outputs support personalized intervention strategies, particularly within cognitive behav-
ioral and inhibitory control frameworks [137]. Digitally delivered interventions also reduce treatment barriers
related to stigma and accessibility, especially among younger populations. Evidence from controlled studies
suggests that structured, theory-driven digital interventions can achieve clinically meaningful reductions in
symptom severity when combined with appropriate ethical design and clinical supervision [138, 139, 140, 52].
The following sub-sections review the main digital intervention modalities used in behavioral addiction
management. Mobile applications and wearable or IoT-based sensors support continuous behavioral moni-
toring and self-regulation in daily life. Web-based platforms provide structured, evidence-based psychologi-
cal interventions with high scalability. More advanced approaches, including AI-powered DTx, VR/AR-based
exposure therapy, and conversational agents, enable adaptive, personalized, and context-aware support.
Telehealth systems extend clinical services beyond traditional settings, while community platforms, peer-
support networks, and gamiﬁcation-based interventions enhance engagement, motivation, and treatment
adherence [75].
Figure 3 shows the general digital intervention architecture discussed in this section. The model presents
behavioral addiction management as a continuous and feedback-based process. Behavioral and contextual
data are ﬁrst collected through mobile apps and sensors. These data are processed to detect risk states
and determine appropriate intervention timing. Brief interventions are then delivered through digital plat-
forms, with clinical escalation options when needed. This model helps to understand how the different
digital approaches discussed in the following sections relate to each other.
3.1. Mobile apps
Mobile applications are widely used in the management of behavioral addictions, including gambling dis-
order, IGD, and problematic digital media use. Their rapid expansion is mainly driven by high smartphone
availability and low access barriers. Unlike clinic-based interventions, mobile apps operate within daily rou-
tines. This makes them suitable for conditions that require continuous self-regulation rather than short-term
clinical treatment.
Most evidence-informed applications are grounded in CBT, motivational interviewing, and self-regulation
models. Common features include craving monitoring, behavioral diaries, goal setting, feedback on risky be-
haviors, and brief coping prompts. These components target impaired inhibitory control and cue-reactivity
processes described in contemporary addiction frameworks [4].
A key methodological strength of mobile interventions is ecological momentary assessment (EMA).
EMA collects repeated real-time data on mood states, urges, and contextual triggers. Compared with retro-
spective clinical reporting, EMA improves ecological validity and temporal sensitivity [56]. When combined
with usage logs, EMA can support time-sensitive micro-interventions and adaptive feedback.

Figure 4 illustrates the core functional components of mobile applications used in the management

logging), usage tracking features (e.g., screen-time reports, alerts), and digital intervention modules. Be-

support self-regulation and sustained engagement. The framework reﬂects a closed-loop model in which
monitoring, data processing, and intervention delivery operate continuously within daily life contexts.
Empirical evidence is strongest in prevention and early-risk populations. The ready4life cluster-randomized
controlled trial (n=1351) reported signiﬁcant reductions in at-risk drinking (OR ≈0.75) and PIU at 6-month

drinking, although follow-up retention was 28% [142]. These ﬁndings indicate moderate preventive efﬁcacy
but also highlight engagement challenges.
Mobile applications may also support treatment adherence. The DIDE eHealth tool demonstrated high
usability (SUS ≈82) and frequent after-hours engagement in adults with substance use disorder [143].
However, symptom-level outcomes were not evaluated.
Quality assessments of publicly available gambling apps reveal considerable heterogeneity. Applica-
tions incorporating structured CBT components scored higher on engagement and information quality met-
rics, yet most lack formal clinical validation [144].

not support their use as standalone treatment for severe gambling disorder. Long-term engagement, regu-
latory oversight, and integration with clinical care pathways remain essential [56].

3.2. Wearables & IoT sensors
Wearable devices and IoT-based sensors extend digital addiction management beyond self-report mea-
sures. Mobile applications primarily rely on active input, whereas wearables enable passive and continuous
physiological monitoring. This is important because craving and loss-of-control states ﬂuctuate dynami-
cally and may not be captured reliably through retrospective reports.
Within the architecture shown in Figure 3, wearable technologies mainly contribute to the data acquisi-
tion layer. They provide objective signals that may precede observable behavioral relapse. Common physi-
ological markers include heart rate variability (HRV), electrodermal activity (EDA), sleep patterns, and phys-
ical activity.
Figure 5 illustrates the role of wearable devices and IoT sensors in behavioral addiction management.
Continuous physiological monitoring through smartwatches, ﬁtness bands, or similar devices enables the
detection of autonomic signals such as HRV and electrodermal activity. These signals reﬂect stress and
craving-related responses during engagement with addictive behaviors. When processed in real time, such
data can inform adaptive, just-in-time interventions and dynamic feedback mechanisms. This approach
supports timely risk detection and personalized regulation strategies within digital treatment frameworks.
HRV has been widely examined as an index of autonomic regulation in behavioral addictions. Studies in
IGD report altered HRV responses during gaming exposure, indicating dysregulated physiological adaptation
to addictive cues [145, 146]. In addition, HRV reactivity features have been used in computational models to
classify IGD severity with high accuracy, supporting the feasibility of integrating wearable-derived signals
into digital monitoring frameworks [147]. In gambling research, electrodermal activity has been shown to
differentiate win–loss outcomes and correlate with subjective excitement levels [148, 149]. These ﬁndings
suggest that reward-related arousal can be objectively captured and may inform time-sensitive intervention
triggers.

3.3. Web-based intervention platforms
Web-based intervention platforms are among the most established digital modalities for behavioral addic-
tion management. They usually deliver structured, session-based content. This content is often based on
CBT and motivational approaches. Compared to mobile apps, web platforms tend to provide longer modules
and more complete therapy sequences. They are also easier to standardize and scale.
Web-based programs commonly include psychoeducation, cognitive restructuring, coping skills, relapse-
prevention planning, and goal tracking. Many platforms add therapist guidance or brief support contacts.
Evidence suggests that guided formats generally show better adherence than fully self-guided programs.
This is important in behavioral addictions, where dropout is frequent.
Figure 6 presents a typical workﬂow of web-based intervention platforms used in behavioral addic-
tions. The process often starts with online screening and then delivers structured CBT-based modules with
exercises and relapse-prevention planning. Progress tracking and symptom monitoring are commonly in-
tegrated. Many platforms add brief therapist guidance to support adherence. Escalation pathways (e.g.,
telehealth referral) are used when risk increases or symptoms persist.
Evidence in gambling disorder indicates that web-based CBT can reduce symptom severity, but out-
comes depend on intervention format and engagement. For example, a number of studies tested an internet-
delivered CBT program with telephone guidance against an internet-delivered motivational enhancement in-
tervention with motivational interviewing, in treatment-seeking individuals with gambling disorder [152, 153].
A separate self-guided internet intervention trial (N=150) examined feasibility and effectiveness, with im-
provements observed over the intervention period but no clear advantage over the control condition, high-

lighting adherence and study-design issues that often arise in unguided formats[154]. Implementation evi-
dence also shows that therapist-supported iCBT can be embedded in routine addiction care (comparative
cohort design), supporting real-world scalability[155]. Meta-analytic evidence conﬁrms overall beneﬁcial ef-

heterogeneity and engagement limitations[156]. For gaming disorder and related problematic internet use,

3.4. AI-powered DTx
DTx aim to move digital care from “static content delivery” to “adaptive treatment”. In behavioral addictions,
risk states change fast. Craving, cue exposure, loss-of-control, and relapse vulnerability are not stable. AI
methods can model these changes using digital traces (e.g., app logs, screen-time), EMA responses, and
sometimes sensor signals. This supports earlier risk detection and more precise timing of support.
Figure 7 shows that AI-powered DTx operate across both the risk-detection and intervention-optimization
layers. This ﬁgure illustrates a closed-loop AI-driven digital therapeutic architecture for behavioral addic-

that estimates vulnerability states. A decision module, potentially supported by RL, optimizes intervention
timing and content selection. Personalized therapeutic elements are then delivered digitally, while engage-
ment and outcome data are continuously monitored to update the model. Clinical oversight and governance
mechanisms remain essential to ensure transparency, safety, and appropriate escalation pathways.

A key use-case is prediction and stratiﬁcation. Explainable machine learning has been used to predict
internet addiction status and to identify inﬂuential psychosocial features in large longitudinal youth datasets

problem gambling and highlight behavioral predictors such as deposit patterns and session features [68].
These studies support the role of AI as a risk-detection layer that can inform stepped-care and referral de-
cisions. The other use-case is adaptive intervention optimization, often framed as JITAI. Gambling JITAIs
operationalize “right support, right time” by combining repeated EMA decision points with rule-based tai-
loring. For example, GamblingLess: In-The-Moment delivers tailored CBT/third-wave micro-interventions
when users report cognitive vulnerability (high craving, low self-efﬁcacy, or positive outcome expectancies)

used in Gambling Habit Hacker, another gambling-focused JITAI protocol evaluated via a 28-day microran-
domized trial [62]. Acceptability work in this line also suggests that dynamic “in-the-moment” delivery is
feasible and can be evaluated using usage metrics and app quality scales [159].
AI can also be used as the decision engine for personalization (beyond static rules). RL has been pro-
posed to learn intervention timing and content selection policies under changing contexts, which concep-
tually ﬁts DTx “precision dosing” [160]. At the system level, a recent DTx framework proposes where AI can
be embedded across therapeutic units, decision-making, and support components, rather than only in chat
interfaces [15]. Still, addiction-focused AI research remains fragmented, and many studies are predictive
rather than treatment-outcome trials. Reviews emphasize the need for transparent models, bias checks,
and clinical validation before deployment at scale [58]. Some ﬁndings from representative AI-supported
DTx work are presented in Table 6.

rather than just adaptive support. In substance use disorders, for example, FDA-authorized prescription

3.6. Chatbots & conversational agents
Chatbots and conversational agents represent an increasingly visible modality within digital addiction man-
agement. These systems simulate structured dialogue using rule-based scripts or artiﬁcial intelligence
models. In behavioral addictions, they are primarily used for psychoeducation, motivational enhancement,
craving management, and brief cognitive restructuring.
From a theoretical perspective, conversational agents target impaired self-regulation, maladaptive be-
liefs, and ambivalence toward behavior change. By delivering structured prompts and reﬂective feedback,
chatbots can operationalize motivational interviewing principles and CBT-based micro-interventions in a
scalable format[166]. Unlike static web modules, conversational interfaces provide interactive engagement,
which may enhance perceived personalization and immediacy.

system generates CBT- or motivational interviewing–based responses, including coping strategies, goal
reminders, and motivational feedback. Engagement metrics and outcome indicators are continuously mon-
itored. High-risk cases trigger escalation pathways involving clinician notiﬁcation or crisis support. Data
privacy, transparency, and clinical oversight remain integral components of the architecture.

assisted interventions often aim to change cognitive and behavioral patterns, with intervention durations

highlights that conversational agents can enhance diagnostic accuracy and support treatment planning in
broader healthcare contexts [168]. Speciﬁc work on digital conversational agents for youth mental health

3.7. Telehealth & tele-psychiatry
Telehealth and tele-psychiatry extend addiction treatment beyond physical clinical settings through syn-
chronous (video-based) and asynchronous (messaging-based) communication systems. In behavioral ad-
dictions, telehealth provides structured psychotherapy, psychiatric evaluation, medication management
when indicated, and relapse monitoring through remote platforms. This modality addresses accessibility
barriers, stigma concerns, and geographic limitations that frequently delay treatment initiation.
From a mechanistic perspective, tele-psychiatry supports behavioral addiction management by main-
taining therapeutic alliance and structured CBT delivery while enabling frequent follow-up. Regular remote
contact may reduce relapse vulnerability by increasing accountability and reinforcing coping strategies dur-
ing high-risk periods. Compared to fully automated digital tools, telehealth preserves clinician involvement,
which is particularly relevant in moderate-to-severe gambling disorder. The telehealth workﬂow in behav-
ioral addiction care is presented in Figure 10. This ﬁgure shows a hybrid telehealth care model for behavioral
addictions. Digital tools are used for remote screening and preliminary monitoring. Structured video-based
CBT sessions and psychiatric consultations provide clinician-guided treatment. Ongoing progress track-
ing and relapse monitoring support continuity of care. Medication management may be integrated when
clinically indicated. Escalation pathways enable referral to intensive or crisis services in high-risk cases.
Integration with AI-based risk detection systems can enhance personalization while preserving clinical over-
sight.
Recent literature demonstrates that telehealth is a viable alternative to in-person treatment across a
range of mental health and substance use outcomes. Systematic reviews indicate that telehealth does not
signiﬁcantly differ from face-to-face modalities in symptom reduction and quality of life measures for gen-
eral mental health conditions, suggesting transferability to addiction contexts [172]. In addiction treatment
speciﬁcally, telemedicine use increased signiﬁcantly during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, improving
access to outpatient services without increasing adverse outcomes such as relapse or crisis events [173].
Telehealth also improved treatment engagement and compliance, especially in rural or underserved pop-
ulations [174]. Emerging work on telehealth for gambling disorder suggests it may expand specialized

3.9. Gamiﬁcation-based interventions
Gamiﬁcation-based interventions have gained attention as supportive components in digital behavioral ad-
diction management. Gamiﬁcation refers to the integration of game design elements, such as points, levels,
challenges, progress visualization, and feedback loops, into non-game therapeutic contexts. In behavioral
addictions, these elements are typically embedded within self-monitoring systems, relapse-prevention mod-
ules, or digital CBT frameworks to increase engagement and adherence rather than to function as indepen-
dent treatment modalities. Gamiﬁcation mechanisms overlap with RL principles and self-determination
theory. Structured feedback, incremental goal progression, and reward signals may enhance perceived
competence and task persistence. However, in behavioral addictions, particularly gambling disorder and
gaming disorder, reward sensitivity and reinforcement vulnerability are already central mechanisms. There-
fore, gamiﬁcation must be carefully designed to avoid replicating compulsive reward cycles. The therapeutic
logic of gamiﬁcation-based interventions in behavioral addictions is presented in Figure 12. The process
begins with therapeutic goal setting based on cognitive-behavioral and self-regulation targets. Gamiﬁed


tasks are then introduced to support skills practice and short, structured missions. Progress feedback em-
phasizes mastery cues and non-addictive rewards rather than high-intensity reinforcement. Self-monitoring
components, such as EMA and usage logs, promote reﬂection and behavioral awareness. Risk safeguards,
including usage limits and session caps, aim to prevent overstimulation and compulsive engagement. Clin-
ical or school-based oversight ensures monitoring and escalation when needed. The model highlights that
gamiﬁcation should reinforce therapeutic goals rather than replicate addictive reward mechanisms.

serious game was developed to reduce arousal, improve emotion regulation, and increase well-being in indi-
viduals with gambling disorder [185]. Gamiﬁcation principles are also relevant for problematic digital media
use and screen-time reduction. A randomized ﬁeld experiment tested digital strategies for reducing screen
time and provided evidence that structured digital prompts and behavior-change mechanisms can reduce
use in real-world settings [186]. In parallel, school-based prevention programs are becoming more evidence-
driven. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that school-based interventions can reduce
problematic digital technology use and screen time, although heterogeneity across programs remains [187].
These ﬁndings support the role of gamiﬁed or game-like elements in prevention and early intervention, es-
pecially when integrated into structured educational settings. An updated systematic review summarized
clinical treatments for internet gaming disorder and highlighted that intervention content and adherence
remain central challenges, which strengthens the rationale for carefully designed engagement strategies,
including gamiﬁed components, within clinical programs [188]. Table 11 summarizes recent evidence on

4. Functions and Use-cases enabled by Digital Systems
Digital systems enable a wide range of functional capabilities in the management of behavioral addictions,
transforming traditionally episodic and clinic-centered care into continuous, data-driven, and adaptive pro-
cesses. These systems support end-to-end workﬂows spanning screening, diagnosis, risk prediction, inter-
vention, and long-term recovery [56, 69, 74].
From a functional perspective, digital addiction management can be conceptualized as a set of in-
terconnected layers that operate across assessment, modeling and prediction, and intervention domains.
As illustrated in Fig. 13, these capabilities are integrated within a closed-loop framework, where multimodal
data streams are continuously collected, analyzed, and utilized to dynamically adapt intervention strategies.
This aligns with emerging paradigms in precision psychiatry and AI-driven healthcare systems [189, 15, 58].
Figure 13: Functional framework of digital systems for behavioral addiction management.
4.1. Digital screening and assessment
Digital platforms enable scalable screening through mobile and web-based systems, signiﬁcantly improving
accessibility and reducing barriers associated with traditional clinical assessment [75, 69].
A key advancement is EMA, which captures real-time data on craving, mood, and contextual triggers.
EMA improves ecological validity and reduces recall bias compared to retrospective self-reports, enabling
ﬁne-grained temporal analysis of addictive behaviors [190]. However, digital screening approaches remain
dependent on user compliance and self-report accuracy. Engagement variability and missing data can limit
reliability. These limitations motivate the integration of passive sensing and behavioral tracking. Overall,
digital screening serves as a critical entry point in closed-loop systems, enabling early detection and con-
tinuous monitoring.
4.2. Digital diagnostics & risk stratiﬁcation
Digital diagnostics extend traditional assessment by integrating behavioral, contextual, and physiological
data to estimate individual risk proﬁles. Machine learning models have demonstrated effectiveness in iden-
tifying addiction severity and predicting problematic usage patterns [65, 158, 68]. Risk stratiﬁcation frame-
works leverage multimodal features such as usage intensity, interaction patterns, and contextual signals to


classify individuals into risk tiers. Unlike static diagnostic models, these systems continuously update risk
estimates, enabling adaptive care pathways. Nevertheless, challenges remain regarding model generaliz-
ability, interpretability, and bias, particularly in heterogeneous populations. Addressing these limitations is
essential for clinical deployment.
4.3. Early detection and relapse prediction
Relapse in behavioral addictions is a dynamic and context-dependent process inﬂuenced by cognitive, emo-
tional, and environmental factors [4, 191].
Digital systems enable early detection by monitoring behavioral trajectories and identifying deviations
from baseline patterns. Predictive models based on longitudinal data—including usage logs, physiologi-
cal signals, and contextual features—have demonstrated strong potential in detecting relapse precursors
[66, 192, 67]. However, predictive accuracy can be affected by data sparsity and variability across individ-
uals. Additionally, false positives may lead to unnecessary interventions. Future work should focus on
explainable and personalized prediction models. Early detection remains one of the most critical compo-
nents for effective intervention and relapse prevention.
4.4. Behavior tracking & digital phenotyping
Digital phenotyping enables continuous and passive monitoring of user behavior through smartphones
and wearable devices. This approach provides objective, real-world behavioral data that complement self-
reported measures [138, 193].
Behavior tracking systems collect multimodal signals such as screen time, app usage, mobility patterns,
and physiological responses. Studies have shown that physiological markers such as HRV are associated
with addiction-related processes and can support severity assessment [147, 146].
Despite these advantages, challenges related to data privacy, sensor reliability, and cross-device stan-
dardization persist. Ethical considerations and data governance frameworks are essential for large-scale
implementation. Digital phenotyping forms the backbone of continuous monitoring and predictive modeling
in addiction management systems.
4.5. Personalized intervention & adaptive care
Digital systems support personalized interventions by adapting the content, timing, and mode of delivery
to individual user proﬁles and behavioral patterns. In this context, AI-driven recommender systems and
RL methods are increasingly used to optimize intervention strategies and improve the alignment between
user needs and therapeutic responses [194, 195, 160]. In parallel, DTx integrate predictive modeling with
intervention delivery within a uniﬁed framework, enabling adaptive treatment pathways that can respond
to user behavior in near real time and continuously reﬁne therapeutic decisions [15, 196]. Despite these
advantages, personalization also raises important challenges related to transparency, fairness, explainabil-
ity, and the risk of overﬁtting, making clinical validity and interpretability essential for safe and effective
deployment. Overall, personalized intervention constitutes a critical step toward precision mental health.
4.6. Harm reduction strategies
Harm reduction approaches aim to minimize the negative consequences of addictive behaviors rather than
enforce complete abstinence [197]. These strategies are particularly relevant in early-stage or high-risk
populations.


Digital tools such as usage dashboards, real-time alerts, and application-blocking mechanisms sup-
port behavioral awareness and self-regulation [198, 69]. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of harm reduction
strategies depends on user engagement and adherence. Passive interventions may be insufﬁcient with-
out behavioral reinforcement mechanisms. Harm reduction provides a pragmatic pathway toward gradual
behavioral change.
4.7. Motivation enhancement & adherence support
Sustained engagement is a major challenge in digital interventions. Gamiﬁcation, feedback systems, and
persuasive technologies are widely used to enhance motivation and adherence [199, 69, 75]. Conversational
agents and chatbot-based systems have shown potential in supporting behavioral change and improving
user engagement [200, 169, 167]. However, long-term engagement remains difﬁcult to sustain, with high
dropout rates commonly reported. Designing adaptive and user-centered engagement strategies remains
an open challenge.
4.8. Continuous monitoring & just-in-time adaptive interventions
Continuous monitoring enables real-time assessment and supports JITAIs, which deliver interventions at
moments of highest vulnerability [8, 61, 62]. JITAIs leverage EMA data, behavioral tracking, and predictive
models to dynamically determine intervention timing and content. This approach aligns intervention deliv-
ery with real-world user states, improving effectiveness. Despite promising results, challenges remain in
optimizing decision policies and ensuring robustness in real-world settings.
4.9. Recovery maintenance & self-management
Digital systems support long-term recovery through self-monitoring, goal setting, and relapse prevention
strategies. Evidence from digital CBT and online interventions demonstrates their effectiveness in sup-
porting sustained behavioral change [155, 156]. These systems promote self-efﬁcacy and enable patient-
centered care models. However, long-term adherence and integration with clinical workﬂows remain open
challenges.
In summary, digital systems provide a uniﬁed, closed-loop framework for behavioral addiction manage-
ment, integrating assessment, prediction, intervention, and recovery processes. This functional paradigm
enables scalable, adaptive, and personalized mental health solutions aligned with emerging trends in digital
psychiatry and artiﬁcial intelligence.


5. Data Sources
The rise of digital technologies has led to their rapid adoption in the medical ﬁeld. This has brought signiﬁ-
cant changes in understanding diseases, developing treatment plans, and designing post-treatment follow-
up plans. One of the major concepts that have been at the center of this transformation is digital pheno-
typing [12]. Digital phenotyping refers to digital data generated from personal devices that can be used to
assess and predict an individual’s health status [201].
Data that constitute digital phenotypes can be generated in various ways. The information shared on
social media platforms, posting times, and internet search history have the potential to provide insights into
the mental state and behavioral patterns of individuals. In addition, information on an individual’s lifestyle,
such as location tracking using mobile applications and smartphone usage history, is signiﬁcant in devel-
oping digital phenotypes. Because it provides information on the behavioral patterns of individuals. With
regard to this, smartphone applications used to monitor individuals’ health have the potential to provide
information on individuals’ behavioral patterns. Moreover, wearable devices with sensors have the poten-
tial to monitor individuals’ behavioral and physiological characteristics. These digital platforms contribute
signiﬁcantly to the identiﬁcation of digital phenotypes by providing multidimensional data on individuals’
daily lives [202].
The process of measuring and analyzing the digital traces produced through the personal devices of an
individual in real-time and naturalistic settings is referred to as digital phenotyping [203]. The digital phe-
notyping process begins with the acquisition of raw data from multiple digital sources. The next part is the
processing and interpretation of the collected data. At this stage, the application of artiﬁcial intelligence
technologies, particularly machine learning and deep learning, emerges as an important data analysis tool
to extract relevant patterns from large and multidimensional data sets [204]. Machine learning helps in the
prediction and learning process through statistical and mathematical modeling without explicit program-
ming, using large volumes of data [205]. The application of these technologies helps in the prediction and,
hence, plays an important role in the psychiatric practice [206]. Artiﬁcial intelligence technologies help in
the recognition of hidden patterns in the data using supervised, unsupervised, and deep learning techniques
[207]. Deep learning is an application of artiﬁcial intelligence and machine learning, which uses multiple lay-
ers of neural networks and data processing techniques to solve complex problems [208]. Furthermore, NLP
technologies help in the analysis of the textual data generated by the individual and provides relevant in-
sights [209]. These technological layers extend beyond facilitating administrative processes in healthcare,
offering data-driven and proactive support for clinical decision-making. They enable the processing and
analysis of multiple types of data, namely, time series data, textual data, and sensor data, and the feature
extraction from the raw data, thus helping in the accurate modeling of the behavior of an individual. Es-
pecially the high-volume and continuous data generated through the application of the digital phenotyping
process helps in the accurate prediction and classiﬁcation performance of artiﬁcial intelligence algorithms
[205].
In the digital phenotyping process, collected data are generally categorized two main groups: active
data and passive data [210]. Active data involve data obtained from the direct involvement of participants
in the research process, whereas passive data are collected automatically through background sensors and
digital systems without requiring active user involvement [211].
Active data are typically collected through self-report-based assessments of mood, cognitive processes,
and motor functioning. One major method of obtaining active data involves EMA [12]. EMA involves allow-
ing participants to report their feelings and behaviors in real-time during their daily activities [212]. EMA
has been considered a more reliable method of obtaining data, as opposed to self-report methods that rely
on retrospective recall during clinical sessions [213].
EMA is typically administered via mobile devices, allowing broader accessibility [214]. EMA involves
three procedures: signal-contingent assessments (based on random prompts during the day), event-contingent
assessments (triggered by speciﬁc events), and time-contingent assessments (conducted at predetermined


intervals). EMA has been considered an effective method of obtaining data, including psychological states
and addiction behaviors, in real-time during daily activities [215, 216]. The data obtained through EMA allow
for the implementation of Ecological Momentary Interventions (EMIs) that offer support during times of
need by tailoring the type and intensity of intervention, depending on the state of the participant [217].
The potential for passive data collection methods is substantial for psychiatric studies, as these al-
low for detailed monitoring of behavior and patterns in daily life [218]. Passive data are collected through a
multitude of sensor and measurement technologies that are available on digital technologies such as smart-
phones, tablets, smartwatches, sleep monitors, and ﬁtness trackers [219]. That data may include keyboard
dynamics, accelerometer, gyroscope, GPS, time spent on the screen, temperature, light exposure, number
of times a phone is checked, level of physical activity, number of messages and emails sent, and time
and number of phone calls [220]. Furthermore, health-related factors such as heart rate, level of exercise,
number of stairs climbed, and sleep quality can also be monitored.
Such multidimensional digital data form the core data sources of digital phenotyping by enabling the
examination of behavioral patterns and psychological states within real-life contexts [204]. It is important to
note that the sensor technology does not measure mental health directly; rather, it measures behaviors that
may indicate mental health. Accordingly, studies emphasize that digital phenotyping is not to be used as
a tool for diagnosis or treatment; rather, a complementary approach that supports traditional assessment
methods in clinical decision-making and treatment processes [221, 218].
Passive data collection not only enables the monitoring of behavioral patterns, but it also allows for
the detection and intervention of risk situations. In this context, passive data collection can support the
implementation of JITAIs, which are often implemented through smartphones and wearable devices, and
use sensor and user information to detect risk states based on the individual’s context, providing them with
personalized intervention or therapeutic content when they are in a critical situation, such as when they are
at risk of relapse [222].
In today’s fast-paced digital world, several researchers have emphasized the potential of digital trace
analysis to improve the assessment, treatment, and monitoring of mental disorders [223]. The accessibility
of smartphones among the global population offers an opportunity to use smartphones as an important
source of data to be used in psychiatric research [224]. Indeed, the effectiveness of the data collected
using smartphone sensors, such as location, accelerometer, Bluetooth, and microphone sensors, has been
established in the identiﬁcation of behavioral patterns associated with mental disorders such as anxiety and
depression [225]. Similarly, digital traces, such as touchscreen and typing patterns, have been identiﬁed as
potential markers for the early prediction of various mental health disorders. Additionally, digital pheno-
typing has been found to have potential use in schizophrenia research, especially in the early detection of
schizophrenia symptoms and monitoring the progression of the symptoms over time [226].
Digital phenotyping approaches have been found to demonstrate high sensitivity and speciﬁcity in de-
tecting early signs of mental disorders. Also, the use of digital phenotyping provides important information
on the prediction of mental disorders while at the same time supporting gold-standard clinical assessments
[220]. These ﬁndings have emphasized the need to use digital phenotypes in the assessment of behavioral
addictions. Due to the limitations of traditional psychiatric assessment tools, especially recall bias and the
constraints of self-report data, interest in digital phenotyping has been steadily increasing over the years,
with an increasing number of studies on the use of digital phenotyping in recent years.
The types of data collected for the purpose of digital phenotyping are mostly behavioral, physiological,
psychological, environmental, and social in nature [227]. Therefore, the types of data used in digital phe-

5.1. Self-reported data
One of the primary data sources used in digital phenotyping studies is self-reported data. This type of data
is based on individuals directly reporting their own behaviors, emotional states, or experiences related to ad-
dictive behaviors through smartphone applications or web-based platforms [75]. In this approach, the data
collection process requires the active participation of the user. Data are typically obtained through short
questionnaires, electronic diary entries, and assessments of experiences such as craving or withdrawal
related to addiction. Among these methods, EMA approaches hold a particularly important place, as they
allow individuals to record their momentary experiences within their daily life context, thereby enabling the
collection of data that are naturalistic, context-sensitive, and ecologically valid [216].
EMA methods have been widely used in the development of digital health interventions for alcohol
use [228, 229] and substance use disorders [230]. In addition to data collection, EMA approaches can
also facilitate the delivery of treatment interventions. Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that
such interventions are effective in identifying high-risk situations, preventing relapse, and enhancing real-
time coping skills [231, 232, 233, 234]. Studies utilizing EMA have revealed signiﬁcant associations between
momentary variables (e.g., craving levels, mood states) and addictive behaviors [235, 215, 216, 221].
An example of an EMA-based application is Meine Zeit Ohne, which prompts users to select a speciﬁc
addictive behavior (e.g., social media use, gambling) and set a goal to reduce or quit this behavior over a
two-week period [236]. Throughout the process, users receive daily notiﬁcations requesting self-efﬁcacy
assessments, thereby aiming to facilitate behavior change.
In EMA studies examining gambling behavior, data collection typically relies on brief self-report ques-
tionnaires administered via smartphones [237, 238, 239]. Participants report their momentary emotional
states, gambling motivations, and gambling behaviors through prompts delivered at speciﬁc times during
the day. These studies predominantly rely on active data collection methods.
In the ﬁeld of gambling addiction, several mobile-based applications enable the monitoring of gambling
behavior by allowing users to report momentary craving levels and behaviors [144]. However, only a lim-
ited number of these applications are evidence-based [150]. Among the evidence-based applications are


Gambling Habit Hacker [240], GamblingLess: Curb Your Urge [241], and GamblingLess: In-The-Moment [61].
The interventions offered by these applications are based on a “push” approach, meaning that decisions
regarding when and how interventions are delivered are determined by algorithms rather than the individual
[222].
Gambling Habit Hacker is a mobile intervention that analyzes self-reported data and delivers behavior
change techniques when low self-efﬁcacy or high-risk situations are detected, supporting individuals in
maintaining ﬁnancial limits related to gambling behavior [159].
GamblingLess: Curb Your Urge is an EMI-based mobile application designed to reduce gambling urges.
Through EMA prompts delivered at random times during the day, the application collects self-reported data
on gambling episodes, craving levels, the ability to limit gambling, and self-efﬁcacy in resisting urges [241].
Based on these data, the application provides intervention content such as brief cognitive-behavioral tech-
niques and mindfulness exercises [242].
An enhanced version of this approach, GamblingLess: In-The-Moment, is based on a JITAI framework,
delivering personalized interventions using self-reported EMA data [61]. In this application, participants
complete EMA assessments three times per day, reporting cognitive variables such as craving intensity,
perceived control over gambling, and positive outcome expectancies. These data are analyzed to deliver
targeted cognitive-behavioral interventions at moments when the user is cognitively at risk.
Another approach used in gambling interventions is chatbot-based “pull” interventions. In these sys-
tems, users initiate engagement by accessing the application when needed, allowing them to use self-
monitoring tools, receive feedback, and access therapeutic content [222]. An example of this approach is
GAMBOT, the only evidence-based chatbot intervention currently available, which enables users to moni-
tor their gambling behavior, receive feedback, and access therapeutic resources [243]. Randomized con-
trolled trials have demonstrated that such applications show promising results in reducing problem gam-
bling severity and supporting behavior change. A general overview of digital intervention-based applications

5.2. Physiological data
The advancement of smartphones and wearable technologies such as smartwatches and ﬁtness bands has
enabled the integration of various sensors into these devices [12]. Today, these digital devices allow for the
continuous monitoring of a wide range of physiological indicators, including heart rate, skin temperature,
physical activity levels, sleep patterns, and energy expenditure [245]. In addition, sensors embedded in
smartphones and wearable devices can capture data related to individuals’ movement and mobility patterns.
Such sensor-based measurements enable the real-time monitoring of behavioral and physiological changes
in daily life, thereby constituting a signiﬁcant data source in digital phenotyping studies [185].
In digital phenotyping research, passively collected data offer the potential to evaluate individuals’ be-
haviors more objectively within real-life contexts. It has been demonstrated that individuals’ self-estimations
of smartphone usage duration or online behaviors often differ substantially from their actual usage pat-
terns [246], indicating that self-reported data may be inﬂuenced by recall bias and subjective evaluation. In
contrast, sensor-derived passive data provide a more objective means of monitoring digital behaviors and
physiological changes [226].
Physiological measures such as heart rate, HRV, and EDA are considered critical for understanding an in-
dividual’s contextual state. These indicators of autonomic nervous system functioning can provide valuable
information about an individual’s stress levels and psychological state at a given moment [247]. Currently,
such physiological measurements are increasingly being used as potential clinical assessment tools for
monitoring disease progression and detecting early warning signs across various domains of addiction
[248, 249, 250].
Research in the ﬁeld of behavioral addictions has also highlighted the relevance of these measures.
Changes in HRV have been observed in association with smartphone use and problematic internet use
[251, 151]. Similarly, a relationship has been identiﬁed between gaming addiction severity and HRV levels
[147]. These physiological indicators complement self-report-based mental health assessments by pro-
viding objective biological markers, thereby enabling a more comprehensive examination of psychological
processes within digital phenotyping frameworks [252].
Sleep patterns can be assessed using data derived from smartphone sensors. In this context, variables
such as phone charging times, cessation of prolonged phone use, and environmental conditions (e.g., quiet-
ness and darkness) can be evaluated collectively to monitor sleep patterns [253, 254]. Smartphone-based
sleep measurements have been shown to be useful in assessing symptoms of depression and anxiety [255].
Therefore, sleep-related data can be considered an important source for evaluating addiction severity and
predicting potential relapse risk.
The integration of digital data with self-reported measures allows for a more comprehensive under-
standing of both individuals’ actual behavioral patterns and their subjective perceptions of these behaviors,
thereby facilitating a more holistic examination of psychopathology. However, when studies in the ﬁeld of
gambling addiction are examined, it appears that there are currently no published studies adopting this in-
tegrated approach, although an ongoing study exists [244]. This study aims to develop predictive models of
gambling episodes by analyzing physiological data such as heart rate, sleep patterns, and physical activity
obtained from smartwatch sensors together with EMA data. It is anticipated that such an approach, partic-
ularly when combined with machine learning methods, may contribute to the identiﬁcation of early warning
indicators of gambling behavior.


5.3. Behavioral & usage data
Behavioral data refer to a type of data that enables the assessment of individuals’ health status through
the monitoring and analysis of their daily behavioral patterns. These data are typically obtained from digital
technologies such as smartphones and wearable devices and include information on daily behaviors such
as step count, physical activity levels, sleep patterns, phone usage duration, and social media activity. Such
data can provide important indicators of individuals’ daily activity levels, sleep–wake cycles, and social
behaviors [227].
Smartphones and digital platforms, which represent important data sources for the assessment of in-
ternet use disorders, enable the objective monitoring of individuals’ online behavioral patterns [256]. Digital
traces left on social media platforms (e.g., likes, posts, and textual content) allow for meaningful inferences
regarding individuals’ personality traits and behavioral patterns [224]. Data such as application usage fre-
quency, social media interactions, time spent online, and switching behavior between applications can con-
tribute to identifying addiction tendencies, including problematic internet use, online gambling, and gaming
behaviors [223, 257]. Clinicians have also emphasized that usage patterns related to social media, gaming,
or gambling platforms may serve as important indicators of addictive behaviors and can support treatment
processes [258]. The analysis of such data allows for the examination of individuals’ online behaviors within
the context of daily life and facilitates the early detection of risky usage patterns. For example, it has been
demonstrated that platform-speciﬁc behavioral data such as the number and frequency of Facebook posts,
textual content, language use, and page likes can be analyzed using machine learning methods to predict
tendencies toward social network use disorders [209].
To prevent problematic smartphone and social media use, smartphone-based applications have been
developed that automatically monitor individuals’ digital behaviors and provide feedback based on these
data [259]. These applications passively track behavioral data such as screen time and application usage
frequency and, based on the collected data, provide users with feedback to increase awareness of their
usage habits, restrict access to certain applications, or deliver real-time alerts and coping suggestions
in risky situations [260]. An example is mGaming Wellness, an artiﬁcial intelligence–based application
developed for mobile gaming addiction in adolescents [261]. The application analyzes usage data collected
from smartphones to estimate an individual’s risk level for gaming addiction and provides tailored feedback.
It also offers various tools to enhance behavioral awareness, reduce gaming time, and promote healthier
digital habits.
In digital phenotyping studies, behavioral data derived from individuals’ interactions with digital de-
vices are also considered a crucial data source [227]. Interactions with smartphones are largely mediated
through software-based keyboards, and the touch interactions generated during this process produce valu-
able behavioral data. Features such as typing speed, inter-keystroke intervals, touch pressure, error rates,
and swipe typing behaviors have been associated with individuals’ mood, stress levels, and cognitive load
[262, 263, 264]. Therefore, metadata derived from keyboard interactions can provide insights into users’
psychological states without accessing content information [265]. These behavioral digital traces are also
considered potential data sources for understanding and early detection of behavioral addictions such as
social media use, online gaming, and problematic internet use. Indeed, a study conducted with adolescents
demonstrated that keyboard interaction data obtained from tablet devices such as typing duration, dele-
tion behaviors, inter-word intervals, and changes in typing speed may serve as meaningful indicators for
identifying digital phenotypes associated with internet gaming disorder [266].
Transactional data related to betting behaviors recorded on online gambling platforms are also used as
data sources in digital phenotyping studies [267]. Variables such as betting frequency, wager amounts, total
time spent on online platforms, smartphone usage, and gaming behavior logs are analyzed using machine
learning methods to identify users at risk of problematic gambling behavior [68].


5.4. Contextual & environmental signals
In digital phenotyping studies, the identiﬁcation of environmental context is considered an important data
source for understanding the conditions under which behaviors occur. Individuals’ geographic location,
travel patterns, and frequently visited places can be determined using GPS sensors embedded in smart-
phones. Furthermore, other factors such as noise levels and temperature can also be measured [227].
The integration of EMA data with passive data collected through smartphones can also help to make a
multidimensional evaluation of mental states. These data can help to develop methods that can detect risky
situations and deliver timely interventions through technology [59]. EMA studies have shown that individu-
als’ environmental and emotional factors are highly related to substance use. In this regard, location-based
studies have shown that GPS data can be used to detect substance craving. Similarly, a study on a mo-
bile application called Q Sense, designed for smoking cessation, found that individuals’ smoking locations
were detected through GPS data, and timely support was provided to individuals when approaching these
locations through geofencing technology [268].
Findings from studies utilizing GPS sensors indicate that location-based interventions are effective
in identifying high-risk environments and delivering interventions [269]. In a study that aimed to examine
problematic smartphone use, application-based EMA data was analyzed, and passive sensor data collected
through smartphones, such as GPS, accelerometer, and step count, was also used [270]. The results showed
that behavioral patterns detected through GPS can be potential digital markers for problematic smartphone
use prediction.
These ﬁndings suggest that location-based digital interventions could also be applicable in the case
of behavioral addictions. However, the application of location-based digital interventions in the case of
gambling is still limited. There are mobile applications developed in the domain of gambling, and they
are intended to send alerts and messages using GPS sensors. However, the majority of the mobile ap-
plications are still in the early development phase [240]. An example is the SPGeTTI application, which
provides cognitive-behavioral messages and coping strategies, as well as supportive messages, while si-
multaneously detecting the entry into gambling venues through GPS-based location tracking. In this case,
the application sends real-time alerts and messages using the JITAI method to avoid the continuation of
gambling behavior [271]. Another application, Don’t Go There, is intended to reduce gambling behavior using
the JITAI method. The application sends real-time alerts when the user is close to gambling venues and at-
tempts to identify the location using other smartphone sensors, such as the accelerometer and gyroscope,
even when the GPS is turned off [272].
5.5. Social and communication traces
The analysis of the social interactions and communication patterns of individuals in digital platforms is an
important part of digital phenotyping. These data can be obtained from various digital sources, including
social media platforms, messaging applications, and smartphone usage logs. Indicators such as the call
and messaging frequencies, the number of contacts, the intensity of interactions in social networks, and
social media patterns are important and objective information related to social connectivity and communi-
cation patterns [273].
The content and linguistic characteristics of social media posts can be examined using NLP techniques,
allowing researchers to draw signiﬁcant inferences about the mood, psychological state, and behavioral
patterns of individuals [209, 274]. Researchers have found that shifts in content, linguistic, and communi-
cational behaviors in social media posts may be associated with shifts in mental state [275].
In addition, communication-based indicators derived from phone calls, such as speech rate and speech
quantity, can provide important markers associated with individuals’ emotional states [276]. Similarly,
the analysis of individuals’ communication data collected through digital communication tools enables
researchers to monitor individuals’ levels of social interaction objectively [277].


User-generated content posted on online forums has also been recognized as a vital source of data in
digital phenotyping studies. In particular, messages posted on gambling forums provide valuable data for
investigating gambling behavior and associated problems [278]. Analyzing such text-based data through
NLP and machine learning approaches helps researchers detect risky conditions associated with behavioral
addictions [279]. Further, through such studies, researchers have also shown that the emotional, cognitive,
and social aspects of gambling addiction can be revealed through linguistic markers [280].
5.6. Multimodal data fusion challenges
Multimodal data fusion constitutes a central yet technically demanding component of digital addiction
management systems. Behavioral addictions are complex, dynamic, and context-dependent phenomena
that cannot be fully characterized through a single data stream [281]. Effective digital phenotyping often
requires integrating heterogeneous sources such as self-reports, physiological measurements, behavioral
usage logs, contextual signals, and linguistic traces. While multimodal integration promises improved pre-
dictive accuracy and personalization, it introduces signiﬁcant methodological, computational, and practical
challenges [282].
One of the primary difﬁculties lies in heterogeneity across data modalities [283]. Self-reported question-
naires are typically low-frequency, structured, and subjective, whereas behavioral logs are high-frequency
and event-driven. Physiological signals such as HRV or electrodermal activity are continuous time-series
data with sensor noise, while textual data from chat interactions are unstructured and semantically rich.
These modalities differ not only in format and dimensionality but also in temporal resolution, reliability,
and semantic meaning. Designing architectures that can meaningfully align and integrate such disparate
representations remains a non-trivial task [284].
Temporal synchronization presents an additional challenge [285]. Behavioral addictions often involve
rapidly ﬂuctuating states, such as momentary cravings or stress spikes. However, different modalities may
be sampled at different rates and with varying latency. Aligning daily self-reports with second-level physio-
logical signals or event-based app usage logs requires careful temporal modeling. Early fusion strategies
(combining raw features) may suffer from misalignment, while late fusion strategies (combining model
outputs) may fail to capture cross-modal interactions [286]. Hybrid and attention-based fusion approaches
attempt to balance these trade-offs but increase model complexity [287].
Missing and incomplete data further complicate multimodal systems [288]. Wearable sensors may be
removed, self-reports may be skipped, and contextual data such as GPS signals may be intermittently un-
available. Missingness itself can carry behavioral meaning (e.g., disengagement during high-risk periods),
yet most machine learning models are not inherently robust to irregular or sparse inputs. Imputation strate-
gies [289], probabilistic modeling, and modality dropout training techniques are often required to maintain
stability.
Another critical issue concerns modality dominance and bias [290]. High-dimensional modalities such
as behavioral logs may overshadow lower-frequency but clinically meaningful signals like self-reported crav-
ing intensity. Without careful normalization and weighting strategies, models may overﬁt to the most abun-
dant data source, reducing interpretability and potentially overlooking subtle but important predictors [291].
From a computational perspective, multimodal deep learning models are resource-intensive and re-
quire substantial training data to generalize effectively. This raises concerns about scalability, especially in
real-world healthcare deployments where computational resources and labeled data may be limited [292].
Moreover, integrating sensitive multimodal data streams increases privacy and security risks, necessitating
robust encryption, access control, and possibly federated architectures [293].
Finally, interpretability becomes more complex as modalities are fused [294]. Explaining how a com-
bination of linguistic sentiment shifts, increased nighttime screen use, and physiological stress patterns
jointly contributed to a relapse prediction requires sophisticated explanation frameworks that remain un-


derstandable to clinicians.


6. AI and Machine Learning Approaches
Recent advances in artiﬁcial intelligence have opened new possibilities for the assessment and manage-
ment of behavioral addictions. Machine learning methods enable the analysis of complex and heteroge-
neous data generated through digital platforms, wearable devices, and self-reported behavioral records. As
illustrated in Figure 14, these systems can be conceptualized as an AI pipeline that transforms multimodal
behavioral data into predictive insights and personalized intervention strategies. Within this framework, AI
models support tasks such as risk prediction, early relapse detection, and adaptive intervention planning.
Various computational approaches have been explored in the literature, including classical machine learn-
ing models, deep learning architectures, RL frameworks, and NLP techniques. These methods facilitate
the integration of multimodal data and enable the identiﬁcation of behavioral patterns that are difﬁcult to
capture using traditional analytical approaches. At the same time, issues such as model transparency, re-
liability, and clinical interpretability remain critical for real-world deployment. Therefore, recent research
increasingly emphasizes not only predictive performance but also explainability and the safe integration of
AI systems into digital therapeutic environments.

6.1. Risk prediction models
Risk prediction models constitute one of the most critical AI-driven components in the digital management
of behavioral addictions [74, 72]. These models aim to estimate the likelihood of addiction onset [295], es-
calation [296], relapse [191, 297], or treatment dropout [298] by leveraging heterogeneous data sources such
as self-reports, behavioral logs, physiological signals, and contextual information. Accurate risk stratiﬁca-
tion enables early intervention, personalized care planning, and optimized allocation of clinical and digital
resources [299]. As illustrated in Figure 15, AI-based risk prediction systems typically integrate multimodal
behavioral and contextual signals to estimate addiction-related risks, transforming heterogeneous digital
data into clinically meaningful risk estimates.
Figure 15: AI-based risk prediction models for behavioral addictions
Risk prediction in behavioral addictions is typically framed as a supervised learning problem. Depending
on the clinical objective, tasks may include:
• Binary classiﬁcation (e.g., high-risk vs. low-risk individuals) [300]
• Multi-class classiﬁcation (e.g., mild, moderate, severe addiction risk) [301]


• Regression models (e.g., predicting addiction severity scores) [302]
• Time-to-event (survival) analysis (e.g., predicting time until relapse) [303]
• Sequential prediction (e.g., forecasting near-term craving episodes) [66]
In addition, short-term relapse prediction and long-term vulnerability estimation [304] represent two
complementary modeling paradigms in digital systems. Risk models integrate diverse multimodal signals,
including:
• Behavioral usage data: screen time, app switching patterns, gaming duration, transaction logs (e.g.,
gambling frequency) [305].
• Physiological signals: HRV, sleep quality, electrodermal activity [193].
• Self-reported measures: craving intensity, mood scales, impulsivity indices [190].
• Contextual features: geolocation, time-of-day patterns, social context indicators [192].
• Linguistic traces: sentiment, cognitive distortions, and emotional markers extracted from chat or
journaling data [306].
The fusion of these modalities enables richer digital phenotyping but introduces challenges related to
missing data, synchronization, and temporal alignment.
Traditional machine learning methods such as logistic regression, support vector machines (SVM), ran-
dom forests, and gradient boosting [65] have been widely used for structured addiction datasets due to their
robustness and interpretability [67]. With the increasing availability of longitudinal and high-frequency data,
deep learning architectures-including recurrent neural networks (RNNs), LSTMs, GRUs, temporal convolu-
tional networks (TCNs), and transformers-are employed to capture temporal dependencies and behavioral
trajectories [307, 308].
For relapse prediction and dynamic risk estimation, survival models (e.g., Cox proportional hazards
models [309], deep survival networks [310]) and state-space models are particularly relevant. Graph-based
learning approaches are emerging for modeling social inﬂuence effects [311], especially in online gaming
[312] or social media addiction contexts.
Unlike static clinical assessments, digital systems enable continuous risk updating. Real-time predic-
tion pipelines can support JITAI [8] by detecting early warning signals (e.g., increased nighttime usage,
negative sentiment shifts) and triggering timely micro-interventions.
Key performance metrics include AUC-ROC, F1-score, sensitivity/speciﬁcity, calibration curves, and Brier
scores. However, in clinical settings, calibration and clinical utility (e.g., decision curve analysis) are as
important as discrimination performance. External validation across populations, cultural contexts, and
age groups remains limited. Longitudinal validation and prospective clinical trials are necessary to assess
real-world reliability and impact.
6.2. Personalized recommendation systems for interventions
Personalized recommendation systems constitute a core component of AI-enabled digital addiction man-
agement platforms, aiming to tailor interventions to the unique needs, behaviors, and risk proﬁles of indi-
viduals [313]. In contrast to traditional standardized treatment protocols, these systems dynamically de-
termine which therapeutic content, coping strategy, motivational message, or behavioral exercise should
be delivered, at what time, and under which contextual conditions [314]. Such personalization enhances


engagement, perceived relevance, and ultimately therapeutic effectiveness by aligning intervention delivery
with the individual’s evolving psychological and behavioral state [315]. Figure 16 presents a conceptual

appropriate interventions for individuals with behavioral addictions. Recommendation engines employ dif-

to match therapeutic content with user needs. The system continuously adapts through user feedback
and behavioral responses, enabling dynamic optimization of intervention strategies in digital therapeutic
environments.

At a conceptual level, personalized recommendation in addiction management integrates multiple di-
mensions, including addiction type and severity, comorbid conditions such as anxiety or depression, mo-
tivational readiness for change, historical treatment responses, and real-time contextual factors [199]. By
leveraging these multidimensional proﬁles, digital systems can move beyond static rule-based approaches
and instead generate adaptive care pathways [316] that evolve over time. This shift reﬂects the broader
paradigm of precision mental health, where interventions are continuously calibrated based on individual-
level data rather than population averages [317].
From a technical perspective, recommendation systems in this domain typically rely on content-based,
collaborative, or hybrid ﬁltering approaches [194]. Content-based models match intervention modules to
user characteristics such as symptom patterns, impulsivity levels, or cognitive distortions [318]. Collab-
orative ﬁltering leverages patterns observed among similar users to identify interventions that have been
effective in comparable proﬁles [319]. Hybrid systems combine these strategies to mitigate sparsity and


cold-start problems [195]. Increasingly, context-aware models incorporate real-time signals such as mood
ﬂuctuations, geolocation, time-of-day risk patterns, or physiological stress indicators to enhance ecological
validity and responsiveness.
Given the longitudinal and dynamic nature of addiction recovery, sequential modeling approaches are
particularly relevant. Context-Aware / RL Models [320] frameworks enable systems to learn optimal inter-
vention policies by maximizing long-term outcomes such as relapse reduction or sustained engagement
[321]. These models treat intervention selection as a sequential decision-making problem, continuously
updating strategies based on user feedback and behavioral responses [322]. Such adaptive optimization
allows DTx to reﬁne treatment pathways over time rather than relying on static predeﬁned sequences.
The scope of recommended interventions may include CBT modules [196], mindfulness exercises, moti-
vational interviewing prompts [323], psychoeducational materials [324], gamiﬁed tasks [325], peer-support
suggestions, and digital nudges [326]. In higher-risk situations, systems may escalate care by recommend-
ing telehealth consultations or clinician contact [327]. The ability to personalize both content and intensity
of intervention helps reduce cognitive overload and supports sustained adherence.
Despite their promise, personalized recommendation systems face signiﬁcant challenges. Data spar-
sity, evolving behavioral patterns, ethical concerns around persuasive design, and the risk of over-personalization
require careful system design and governance. Moreover, transparency and explainability are essential to
ensure trust among clinicians and users. Rigorous clinical validation through longitudinal studies and ran-
domized controlled trials remains necessary to establish efﬁcacy and safety.
6.3. RL for habit formation & habit suppression
RL provides a powerful computational framework for modeling, supporting, and optimizing behavior change
processes in the context of behavioral addictions [65]. Because addiction is fundamentally linked to mal-
adaptive habit formation and dysregulated reward learning, RL-based systems align naturally with the neu-
robehavioral mechanisms underlying craving, reinforcement, and relapse. In digital addiction management,
RL is primarily used to dynamically optimize interventions that either promote healthy habit formation [328]
or suppress harmful habitual behaviors [329].
From a conceptual standpoint, RL formalizes behavior change as a sequential decision-making problem.
An individual’s behavioral state-characterized by factors such as craving intensity, emotional status, con-
textual triggers, and prior actions-can be modeled as the “state” in an RL framework. Digital interventions
(e.g., mindfulness prompts, CBT exercises, motivational messages, access restrictions, or peer-support
suggestions) represent the “actions.” The system receives feedback in the form of “rewards,” which may re-
ﬂect reduced craving, lower relapse probability, improved engagement, or sustained abstinence. Over time,
the algorithm learns a policy that maximizes cumulative long-term reward, effectively identifying which in-
terventions work best under which circumstances [330]. Figure 17 presents a conceptual view of how
RL models behavior change as a sequential decision-making process in which the user’s behavioral state
informs the selection of digital interventions. Within this framework, contextual factors such as craving in-
tensity, emotional status, recent usage patterns, and environmental triggers deﬁne the system state, while
adaptive interventions, including CBT prompts, mindfulness exercises, motivational messages, or access
restriction nudges, represent the actions. The RL agent continuously updates its policy based on reward
signals derived from behavioral outcomes such as reduced relapse probability, sustained abstinence, or
improved engagement. Through this iterative learning process, the system can both reinforce positive be-
havioral routines (habit formation) and suppress maladaptive behaviors associated with addiction (habit
suppression).
In habit formation scenarios, RL can be used to reinforce adaptive behaviors [331] such as regular sleep
patterns, physical activity, structured daily routines, or engagement in therapeutic exercises. By continu-
ously analyzing user responses and adherence patterns, the system can adjust intervention timing, intensity,
and modality to strengthen positive behavioral loops. For example, contextual bandit models may determine


Figure 17: RL framework for habit formation and habit suppression in behavioral addictions
the optimal timing for delivering a motivational message to maximize the likelihood of completing a coping
exercise [332]. In this way, healthy routines gradually become automated and self-sustaining.
Conversely, in habit suppression contexts-such as reducing compulsive gaming, gambling, or exces-
sive social media use-RL can help identify high-risk states and apply targeted countermeasures [333]. The
system may learn that speciﬁc temporal patterns, emotional states, or environmental cues precede harmful
behavior and proactively deploy micro-interventions. These may include friction-inducing mechanisms (e.g.,
delay prompts), cognitive reframing exercises, or escalation to social or professional support. By optimiz-
ing long-term outcomes [334] rather than immediate short-term responses, RL frameworks are particularly
suited to addressing the delayed reward structures characteristic of addiction.
Technically, several RL paradigms are applicable. Contextual bandits are often used for short-term
adaptive message selection [335]. More complex Markov Decision Process (MDP) models capture transi-
tions between behavioral states over time [336]. Deep RL extends these approaches to high-dimensional
and multimodal data environments, enabling scalable personalization [337]. However, the deployment of
RL in clinical contexts requires careful reward design, safety constraints, and human oversight to prevent
unintended behavioral reinforcement or ethically problematic nudging [338].
Despite its promise, applying RL to addiction management poses challenges. Sparse and delayed re-
wards, non-stationary behavioral dynamics, limited training data, and safety-critical decision-making con-
straints complicate model design. Ofﬂine policy evaluation, simulation-based validation, and hybrid human-
AI control mechanisms are therefore essential before real-world implementation.
6.4. NLP for interaction & sentiment
NLP plays a pivotal role in digital addiction management systems by enabling meaningful human–AI inter-
action and extracting clinically relevant insights from textual and conversational data [339]. Since behav-
ioral addictions are closely intertwined with emotional states, cognitive distortions [340, 341], motivational
ﬂuctuations, and social context, language becomes a rich and sensitive signal for assessing risk, monitor-
ing progress, and delivering personalized support [342]. NLP technologies therefore function both as an
interaction interface and as an analytical engine within AI-powered DTx.
At the interaction level, NLP enables conversational agents [200, 343], chatbots [344], and virtual ther-


apists [345] to engage users in structured or semi-structured dialogue. These systems may deliver psy-
choeducation, conduct cognitive restructuring exercises, guide mindfulness practices, or implement moti-
vational interviewing techniques. Advances in LLMs have signiﬁcantly improved contextual understanding,
coherence, and empathetic response generation, allowing digital systems to simulate supportive therapeu-
tic communication. In addiction contexts, such conversational agents can provide immediate, low-barrier
access to assistance, particularly during high-risk moments when human clinicians may not be available.

tal addiction management systems. In this framework, user-generated language inputs such as chat mes-

signals, cognitive patterns, and contextual indicators associated with addiction risk. At the same time, con-
versational agents utilize language generation capabilities to deliver psychoeducation, motivational sup-
port, and therapeutic dialogue. By integrating interactive communication with linguistic analysis, NLP sys-
tems enable real-time user engagement, continuous monitoring of emotional states, and early detection
of relapse-related linguistic markers while supporting personalized digital interventions under appropriate
safety and ethical oversight.

Beyond dialogue generation, NLP methods are widely used for sentiment analysis and affect detection.
By analyzing user-generated text-such as chat logs, journaling entries, forum posts, or transcribed speech-
systems can detect emotional polarity (positive, negative, neutral), intensity of distress, hopelessness in-
dicators, impulsivity-related language, or craving expressions [346]. More advanced models incorporate
emotion classiﬁcation, cognitive distortion detection, and stress-level estimation. Temporal tracking of
linguistic patterns [347] enables early identiﬁcation of deteriorating mental states or relapse precursors,
supporting proactive intervention strategies.
Recent transformer-based architectures and contextual embeddings have enhanced the ability to cap-
ture nuanced semantic and pragmatic signals in addiction-related discourse [348, 349]. Topic modeling
and semantic clustering can reveal recurring themes such as triggers, social isolation, ﬁnancial stress (in
gambling addiction), or performance anxiety (in gaming addiction) [350]. Additionally, dialogue modeling
techniques [351] can assess engagement quality and conversational alignment, which are critical for sus-
taining adherence in digital therapeutic programs.
However, NLP-driven systems face important challenges. Language is highly context-dependent and
culturally sensitive, making cross-population generalization difﬁcult [352]. Data scarcity [353] in clinically
labeled addiction datasets can limit supervised model performance. Privacy concerns are particularly


acute, as textual data may contain deeply personal information [77]. Moreover, ensuring that AI-generated
responses remain clinically appropriate, non-judgmental, and safe requires robust guardrails, alignment
strategies, and human oversight.
6.5. Explainability & interpretability concerns
Explainability and interpretability are critical considerations in the deployment of AI-driven systems for be-
havioral addiction management [354, 67]. Unlike many commercial recommendation environments, digital
addiction interventions operate within clinically sensitive and ethically regulated contexts. Decisions such
as identifying a user as high risk for relapse, recommending speciﬁc therapeutic modules, or escalating care
to a clinician can have signiﬁcant psychological and practical consequences. Therefore, AI models must
not only achieve high predictive performance but also provide transparent, understandable, and clinically
meaningful explanations for their outputs [355].
In addiction management, interpretability serves multiple stakeholders. Clinicians require insight into
which features-such as increased nighttime usage, negative sentiment shifts, or elevated physiological
stress indicators-contributed to a risk prediction [356]. Such transparency facilitates clinical validation,
supports shared decision-making, and allows professionals to override or contextualize algorithmic out-
puts when necessary. For users, understandable explanations enhance trust, perceived fairness, and en-
gagement with digital systems. If individuals understand why a particular coping strategy or intervention is
recommended, they are more likely to adhere to it.
From a technical standpoint, explainability approaches can be categorized into intrinsically interpretable
models and post-hoc explanation techniques [357]. Traditional models such as logistic regression, decision
trees, and rule-based systems provide relatively transparent decision boundaries but may lack the expres-
sive power required for complex multimodal and temporal data. Conversely, deep learning architectures,
such as recurrent networks or transformers-offer superior modeling capacity but function as “black boxes.”
Post-hoc techniques, including feature attribution methods (e.g., SHAP, LIME), attention visualization, coun-
terfactual explanations, and saliency mapping, are increasingly used to bridge this gap [358]. In tempo-
ral addiction modeling, explaining how risk evolves over time presents an additional layer of complexity
[359, 360].
However, explainability in digital addiction systems extends beyond feature importance. It also involves
ensuring that models behave reliably across demographic groups, addiction types, and cultural contexts
[361]. Hidden biases or spurious correlations [362] may lead to unfair risk stratiﬁcation or inappropriate in-
tervention recommendations. Therefore, interpretability must be coupled with fairness auditing, calibration
analysis, and robustness testing. Moreover, explanations must be clinically meaningful rather than merely
statistically descriptive; identifying that “screen time increased” is less useful than contextualizing it within
recognized behavioral relapse patterns.
Another key concern involves the tension between personalization and transparency [363, 364]. Highly
adaptive RL systems may continuously update policies in ways that are difﬁcult to summarize or justify in
simple terms. Developing explainable RL frameworks and incorporating human-in-the-loop oversight mech-
anisms are promising directions to ensure accountability [365].
Ultimately, explainability and interpretability are not optional add-ons but foundational requirements for
safe and trustworthy AI deployment in addiction management. Transparent systems strengthen clinical
integration, regulatory compliance, and user conﬁdence, thereby facilitating responsible innovation in DTx.
Ensuring that AI-driven decisions can be understood, audited, and ethically justiﬁed remains central to the
sustainable advancement of personalized digital addiction care.


6.6. Data scarcity, bias, and generalization issues
Data scarcity, bias, and limited generalization represent some of the most signiﬁcant methodological and
practical challenges in applying AI and machine learning to behavioral addiction management [366]. Al-
though digital systems generate large volumes of behavioral traces, clinically validated, high-quality, and
longitudinal addiction datasets remain relatively scarce [367]. This imbalance creates a paradox: while raw
data may be abundant, labeled, structured, and outcome-veriﬁed data suitable for robust model training are
often limited.
Data scarcity is particularly pronounced in clinically annotated datasets, where reliable labels such as
conﬁrmed relapse events, diagnostic categories, or treatment outcomes require expert evaluation [368]. Be-
havioral addictions such as gaming, social media use, or online shopping-also lack universally standardized
diagnostic criteria compared to substance use disorders, further complicating dataset harmonization [369].
Moreover, relapse events are relatively rare compared to non-relapse periods, leading to severe class im-
balance in predictive modeling. Small sample sizes and short follow-up durations reduce statistical power
and increase the risk of overﬁtting, particularly for deep learning architectures.
Bias constitutes another critical concern. Digital addiction datasets are often collected from speciﬁc
platforms, demographic groups, or cultural contexts, resulting in sampling bias [370]. For example, app-
based data may overrepresent younger, digitally literate populations, while underrepresenting older adults
or individuals with limited technological access. Behavioral patterns may differ substantially across cul-
tures, socioeconomic backgrounds, and gender groups. If not carefully audited, predictive models may
encode and amplify these biases, leading to unequal risk stratiﬁcation or inappropriate intervention rec-
ommendations. Measurement bias [371] can also arise when self-reported data are inﬂuenced by social
desirability, stigma, or recall inaccuracies.
Generalization challenges emerge when models trained on one addiction type, platform, or population
fail to perform adequately in different contexts [352]. A risk prediction model developed using gaming data
may not transfer effectively to gambling addiction, even if behavioral dynamics appear superﬁcially simi-
lar. Likewise, models trained in one cultural setting may not generalize across countries due to differences
in social norms, coping mechanisms, or technology usage patterns. Concept drift—where behavioral pat-
terns evolve over time—further complicates long-term deployment, as user habits and digital environments
continuously change [4].
Several methodological strategies are being explored to address these issues. Data augmentation,
transfer learning, and self-supervised pretraining can mitigate small-sample limitations. Federated learning
frameworks enable collaborative model training across institutions without direct data sharing, enhancing
diversity while preserving privacy. Domain adaptation techniques improve cross-population transferability,
while fairness-aware learning algorithms attempt to reduce demographic disparities. Additionally, contin-
uous model monitoring and recalibration are essential to maintain performance under evolving behavioral
conditions.


7. Ethical, Legal, and Societal Considerations
Digital therapeutics (DTx) represent a distinct category of digital health interventions, deﬁned by explicit
therapeutic intent, software-based mechanisms of action, and increasing regulatory scrutiny. Positioned
between pharmaceuticals and consumer-facing digital applications, DTx operate within fragmented and
still-developing governance frameworks that often lag behind rapid software innovation, creating an inher-
ently ambiguous regulatory space [70, 372]. These tensions are particularly visible in behavioral addictions
-such as gambling, gaming, and problematic social media or smartphone use- where digital tools rely on
continuous data collection, algorithmic personalization, and real-time behavioral feedback, situating them
at the intersection of healthcare systems, consumer technology markets, and data-driven economies. As a
result, their governance requires ethical and legal approaches extending beyond traditional clinical ethics
and individual patient–provider relationships, demanding a broader systemic perspective [56].
Unlike face-to-face psychosocial treatments, digital interventions for behavioral addictions embed ther-
apeutic reasoning directly into software infrastructures, rendering ethical risk a persistent feature of the
technological system rather than a discrete clinical event. Continuous monitoring, behavioral tracking,
automated feedback, and algorithmic inference reshape power relations among users, developers, clin-
icians, and institutions, often diffusing responsibility and blurring accountability for adverse outcomes
[69]. Behavioral addictions share core mechanisms with substance addictions -particularly compulsion
and reinforcement- which are ampliﬁed in digital environments that simultaneously facilitate both harm
and intervention through habit-forming design and pervasive connectivity [373, 374]. While digital interven-
tions offer signiﬁcant beneﬁts in terms of accessibility, anonymity, and scalability, these advantages are
accompanied by heightened risks related to privacy, autonomy, justice, and social inequality.
The growing integration of AI, mHealth, and DTx into addiction care necessitates a paradigm shift where
ethical, legal, and societal considerations are treated as foundational to responsible intervention design
rather than secondary safeguards. The vulnerability of users struggling with impulse control and compul-
sive behaviors makes the “safety-by-design” principle an ethical prerequisite to prevent iatrogenic harm,
such as technology-induced dependency or algorithmic discrimination [189]. Furthermore, as digital tools
increasingly reach marginalized groups, the potential for digital health technologies to reproduce or exacer-
bate existing social disparities must be critically addressed within governance models [72]. Consequently,
the transition toward digitally mediated addiction care requires robust legal frameworks that can accommo-
date the dynamic nature of software while protecting the fundamental rights and psychological well-being
of users in an increasingly data-driven therapeutic landscape.
7.1. Privacy & data protection
Privacy and data protection constitute one of the most structurally embedded ethical challenges in AI-driven
digital interventions for behavioral addictions, such as gambling, gaming, and compulsive social media use.
Tools developed for these conditions routinely collect highly granular, longitudinal behavioral, psycholog-
ical, and contextual data -including time-stamped logs, emotional states, impulse control markers, and
relapse indicators. Compared to conventional health records, these data are inferential and predictive, sub-
stantially increasing their ethical sensitivity and potential for harm if misused [56, 375]. Intensive collection
methods, such as EMA and passive monitoring, allow for the precise recording of user habits like “inﬁ-
nite scrolling” or “loot-box” spending, but uncertainties surrounding data access and storage -highlighted
by incidents like the BetterHelp controversy- undermine trust and negatively affect sustained therapeutic
engagement [376, 377]. Although regulatory frameworks like GDPR and HIPAA establish baseline legal pro-
tections, compliance alone does not guarantee ethically robust data governance or meaningful user control
in the specialized context of addiction care [378, 379].
The technical architecture of DTx ampliﬁes these risks through continuous monitoring and cloud-based
infrastructures that often involve third-party analytics, diffusing accountability and increasing the threat of


cyberattacks [380]. Many applications operate outside strict health data regulations, creating an “inaccu-
rate expectation of privacy” where users assume clinical-level conﬁdentiality that does not exist. This is
particularly concerning for free applications that rely on data-driven business models where sensitive in-
formation becomes the “currency of care,” being on-sold for commercial purposes or used for behavioral
targeting within the global “infosphere” [381, 382]. Consequently, behavioral addiction data intersect with
commercial platform economies in Industry 4.0 environments, where algorithmic proﬁling can detect user
vulnerabilities to tailor content that exploits compulsive patterns [383, 189].
To mitigate these threats, the literature emphasizes the integration of advanced technical and design
safeguards. AI-driven systems must move toward Responsible UX design and Value Sensitive Design that
prioritize user diversity and digital well-being during development [384]. Key strategies include:
• Privacy-preserving AI: Utilizing techniques such as federated learning, which trains models on de-
centralized devices without raw data transfer, and differential privacy, which adds statistical noise to
prevent individual identiﬁcation [76].
• Transparent governance: Establishing clear protocols for data stewardship, strong encryption, and
secure sharing mechanisms to prevent unauthorized secondary data use [380, 385, 58].
• Ethical validation: Implementing robust frameworks for consent and clinical validation, especially as
systems process detailed doctor-patient interactions [386].
Ultimately, privacy in digital addiction interventions must be understood as a foundational ethical condi-
tion rather than a purely technical requirement. Secure, private, and conﬁdential environments are paramount
for building the trust necessary for users to disclose habitual impulses -such as those found in internet ad-
diction and gaming disorder- and engage in long-term recovery [387, 388, 389]. Ensuring that AI technologies
do not expose individuals to proﬁling or unauthorized data use remains essential for the integrity of digital
health systems and the protection of service users’ informational autonomy [385, 259].
7.2. Security & safety concerns
Digital interventions for behavioral addictions -including compulsive gambling, gaming, and problematic so-
cial media or smartphone use- introduce security and safety risks that extend beyond conventional data pro-
tection, encompassing cybersecurity, clinical safety, algorithmic reliability, and the prevention of technology-
induced harm. Audits of digital mental health applications across the spectrum of addictive behaviors
reveal widespread governance deﬁciencies, including opaque privacy practices and extensive third-party
data tracking [390]. These vulnerabilities are especially consequential in addiction contexts, where secu-
rity breaches involving behavioral or ﬁnancial data may intensify stigma, facilitate economic exploitation, or
expose users to targeted marketing that triggers addictive impulses, directly undermining therapeutic goals.
Furthermore, the safety of addiction-related data is uniquely compromised by the behavioral patterns of the
target population, such as the increased frequency of device loss, sale, or theft, which can expose sensitive
health records to unauthorized acces. The absence of consistent legal frameworks across jurisdictions
further complicates the protection of these digital therapeutic infrastructures, often leaving users without
a clear understanding of how their data is handled or protected [381].
Beyond data breaches, DTx generate novel iatrogenic risks through algorithmic errors, inadequate cri-
sis escalation, and the creation of a “false sense of security” [377]. Safety assessments identify signiﬁcant
clinical vulnerabilities, including misleading therapeutic feedback, incorrect risk calculations, and “AI hallu-
cinations” -fabricated or incorrect outputs from generative models that increase cognitive burden and emo-
tional distress during crisis situations [391]. In behavioral addictions, where interventions rely on automated
relapse prediction and craving regulation, misclassiﬁcation or system failure may normalize excessive en-
gagement or delay urgent clinical intervention. Furthermore, many generic chatbots lack clinical validation


and may provide unsafe guidance, leading users to rely on self-guided tools rather than seeking necessary
professional care [392]. This reliance is particularly dangerous when applications have not undergone for-
mal accreditation by evaluation bodies like Organisation for the Review of Care and Health Apps (ORCHA)
or the Mental Health Apps Library (MIND database), leaving their therapeutic effectiveness and usability
uncertain.
Safety concerns are further ampliﬁed by design features that may unintentionally replicate the very
addictive mechanisms they aim to treat. Gamiﬁcation, reward-based nudging, and “techno-social closed-
loop systems" risk mirroring problematic gaming or social media dynamics when insufﬁciently regulated
[374, 382]. While intended to support behavior change, constant monitoring and performance metrics can
generate pressure, guilt, or shame, potentially reinforcing compulsive self-monitoring patterns. In environ-
ments designed for internet or smartphone use, persuasive and habit-forming strategies -such as inﬁnite
content feeds, autoplay, and personalized notiﬁcations- are often crafted to maximize engagement and user
retention, which can contribute to addiction-like behavioral loops [383, 393]. Consequently, gamiﬁed inter-
ventions can function as a "double-edged sword," where motivational mechanisms intended for recovery
instead encourage compulsive overuse or lead users into physically or psychologically risky engagement
patterns [394, 395].
A critical safety gap exists in the management of high-risk situations, such as suicidal ideation, which
frequently co-occur with severe behavioral addictions and other conditions like ADHD or depression [396].
Autonomous AI systems often struggle with the complexity of psychiatric crises; some may provide in-
adequate responses or struggle to demonstrate professional judgment, while others abruptly terminate
conversations upon detecting risk, highlighting the limitations of fully autonomous digital formats [389].
To ensure safety, digital addiction tools must incorporate immediate access to emergency services, robust
moderation systems to prevent the normalization of harmful behaviors, and rigorous testing before being
deemed clinically effective. These ﬁndings underscore that in behavioral addiction care, safety-by-design,
human-in-the-loop oversight, and rigorous post-market monitoring are ethical prerequisites rather than op-
tional enhancements to prevent unintended outputs and adversarial manipulation [397].
7.3. Equity, access, and digital divide
Digital interventions are frequently presented as scalable solutions to longstanding access gaps in behav-
ioral addiction treatment, offering a potential response to the global shortage of mental health professionals
and bridging geographical barriers through telemedicine and mobile platforms [398, 399, 381]. By increas-
ing anonymity and providing psychoeducation or early support for those on waiting lists, these technologies
can reduce the stigma that often prevents addicted individuals from seeking face-to-face care. Accordingly,
digital interventions act as a ‘buffer zone’ or ‘intermediary space’, allowing for clinical support while main-
taining anonymity and physical distancing. However, growing evidence indicates that these tools often
reproduce existing inequalities shaped by digital literacy, device ownership, and reliable internet access,
systematically excluding socioeconomically marginalized groups [400]. Approximately 40% of the world’s
population lacks reliable internet access, setting a structural ceiling for digital-ﬁrst strategies and effectively
transforming the digital divide into a new social determinant of health [401]. In the context of behavioral
addictions, these barriers intersect with ﬁnancial precarity and unstable housing, raising signiﬁcant doubts
about whether digital-ﬁrst strategies align with public health equity goals, especially since gambling-related
harm disproportionately affects lower-income groups who are least likely to have stable internet or private
digital spaces.
The beneﬁts of DTx are further stratiﬁed by income, education, age, gender, and language, mirroring
broader health inequalities [402]. While remote participation can enhance privacy, it simultaneously cre-
ates new structural barriers linked to technological infrastructure and professional capacity [403]. Many
commercial platforms operate on subscription-based or tiered pricing models, placing essential therapeu-
tic features behind paywalls and risking the transformation of DTx into “boutique services" for privileged


users rather than population-level public health interventions [377]. Furthermore, digital health applications
often assume that users possess sufﬁcient time, autonomy, and resources to implement recommended be-
havioral changes “assumptions that fail to account for the social conditions inﬂuencing addictive behaviors
and treatment accessibility. This socio-technical context is critical, as problematic internet or smartphone
use emerges through interactions between users and social environments; thus, social inequalities in the
capacity to regulate digital behavior may inﬂuence both the development and the recovery from addiction
[259].
Equity claims are also undermined by algorithmic bias and the lack of representative data. AI models
often perform poorly for underrepresented groups -including racial minorities, non-native speakers, and
low-income communities- because they are frequently trained on limited or non-representative datasets
[76]. In behavioral addiction care, biased risk proﬁling and inaccurate risk assessments can exacerbate
existing inequities in service distribution, particularly in low-resource settings where the addiction burden
and treatment gaps are substantial. Industry 4.0 technologies therefore have the potential to widen social
and economic inequalities if access remains uneven, reinforcing the digital divide [395, 384].
To ensure that digital innovation does not disproportionately beneﬁt already advantaged populations,
ethical technology design must prioritize inclusivity and accessibility. Socially responsible frameworks
must explicitly address the digital divide by developing low-storage applications, ofﬂine functionality, and
interfaces compatible with older devices to avoid excluding economically disadvantaged users [394]. Ac-
cessibility also requires adapting interventions for different literacy levels and communication preferences,
including voice-over support and cultural adaptation. Ultimately, achieving health equity in digital addiction
care requires public funding, free access initiatives, and collaborative frameworks involving policymakers,
healthcare professionals, and technologists to ensure that AI tools bridge service gaps rather than widening
the chasm of social inequality.
7.4. Stigmatization & discrimination risks
Behavioral addictions remain highly stigmatized and are frequently framed through moralizing narratives
that emphasize individual failure and a lack of self-control rather than structural or commercial determi-
nants. While digital health tools are often promoted for their ability to provide a judgment-free, anonymous
environment -potentially encouraging help-seeking among those who fear face-to-face exposure- they also
risk reproducing and even amplifying stigma through surveillance-oriented design and algorithmic labeling
[404]. In interventions for gambling, gaming, and internet addiction, continuous monitoring and risk-scoring
systems may position users as objects of surveillance rather than active agents in their own recovery, po-
tentially deterring individuals already shaped by shame (whakama) and concealment. Furthermore, if digital
tools use explicit labels like "addicted," they may negatively affect a user’s self-image and resistance to in-
tervention, particularly among youth who frequently avoid treatment due to embarrassment or doubts about
their ability to quit [399].
The integration of AI into this landscape introduces signiﬁcant risks of algorithmic discrimination and
the reinforcement of social biases. NLP models used in psychiatry and behavioral prediction systems have
demonstrated biases related to race, gender, religion, nationality, and age, which can lead to misclassiﬁ-
cation or inaccurate risk assessments for marginalized populations. When algorithms are trained on non-
representative or industry-inﬂuenced datasets, they risk embedding cultural and socioeconomic assump-
tions into digital addiction care, effectively "moralizing" health behaviors by transforming everyday digital
practices into measurable indicators of personal responsibility [382]. These "techno-social closed-loop sys-
tems" can induce feelings of "bad conscience, guilt, and shame" when users fail to meet digital benchmarks,
while gamiﬁed elements like leaderboards and performance metrics may intensify unrealistic social com-
parisons and feelings of inadequacy [395]. Moreover, focusing exclusively on individual pathology through
algorithmic labeling risks overlooking the role of platform design -such as habit-forming interfaces- in shap-
ing compulsive behaviors, thereby framing problematic use solely as a personal failure.


Stigmatization and discrimination risks also extend beyond the clinical interaction into broader institu-
tional settings. Unauthorized disclosure or secondary use of sensitive behavioral addiction data by insur-
ers, employers, or ﬁnancial institutions can lead to severe social exclusion and employment discrimination
[375]. Labels such as “high-risk user” derived from predictive analytics can bias decision-making in these
sensitive contexts, making privacy protections a central component of ethical addiction treatment [405].
Proprietary and opaque algorithms further complicate this issue, as it remains difﬁcult to evaluate whether
nudges and behavioral predictions are operating fairly or if they are prioritizing engagement metrics over
therapeutic outcomes. For marginalized or historically mistrustful communities, these technological and
institutional exclusions can reinforce a cycle of inequality and disengagement from healthcare services.
To ensure that digital interventions support recovery without reproducing shame or inequality, ethical
digital addiction care must move toward culturally sensitive and inclusive design. This requires the use of
diverse training datasets, the implementation of safeguards against stigmatizing language, and a clear sep-
aration from commercial gambling and gaming interests. Value-sensitive design frameworks and stronger
oversight mechanisms are essential to prevent discriminatory practices and ensure that AI systems do not
reinforce societal biases. Ultimately, addressing both interpersonal stigma and structural sources of algo-
rithmic discrimination is necessary to build the trust required for long-term engagement and to protect the
informational autonomy of vulnerable users [380].
7.5. Global policy recognition and international regulatory frameworks
Behavioral addictions have become increasingly prominent in global public health, digital governance, and
regulatory policy debates. The ubiquity of digital technologies has facilitated continuous engagement in
potentially addictive activities, including gambling, gaming, social media use, online shopping, and the con-
sumption of online sexual content. While research has expanded signiﬁcantly, policy interventions remain
fragmented. Gambling and gaming are the most comprehensively regulated sectors, whereas other behav-
iors are typically addressed under general digital platform regulations and consumer protection laws [373].
Global political recognition has been solidiﬁed through international diagnostic systems. The World
Health Organization (WHO) ofﬁcially recognized Gaming Disorder as a formal mental health condition by
including it in the ICD-111. Similarly, the American Psychiatric Association (APA) added Internet Gaming
Disorder to the DSM-5 as a category requiring further clinical study [406]. At the European Union level, the
Digital Services Act (DSA) mandates that platforms mitigate risks associated with addictive design2. Fur-
thermore, the Pompidou Group has published reports emphasizing the necessity of harm-reduction policies
speciﬁcally targeting digital gambling and gaming environments3.
Gambling remains the most strictly regulated behavioral addiction worldwide. National policies gen-
erally encompass licensing, advertising restrictions, and harm-reduction mechanisms [407]. The United
Kingdom initiated a "digital age reform" through its 2005 Gambling Act and subsequent 2023 White Paper4.
Sweden implemented Spelpaus, a national self-exclusion system, under its 2018 Gambling Act (Spelinspek-
tionen, 2018) [408], while Australia oversees online betting through the 2001 Interactive Gambling Act5.
Governments are taking increasingly stringent measures, particularly to protect minors. In 2021, China
restricted youth gaming time to three hours per week and mandated a real-name veriﬁcation system6. The
Belgian Gaming Commission effectively banned loot boxes by classifying them under gambling laws7. In

harm-reduction strategies like age-veriﬁcation, pre-commitment tools, and self-exclusion registries into reg-

medical devices remains a major societal challenge, as this classiﬁcation dictates the requirements for
clinical validation and safety monitoring [395]. Without standardized evaluation frameworks and enforce-
ment of the same regulations that apply to in-person services, digital addiction interventions risk expanding
commercially without meeting critical clinical and ethical standards.
7.6. Insurance & reimbursement models
Insurance coverage and reimbursement represent one of the most critical structural bottlenecks for the
ethical and sustainable implementation of DTx in behavioral addiction care. Although a limited number
of prescription DTx have entered payer systems, reimbursement remains inconsistent and weakly aligned
with long-term outcomes such as relapse prevention and psychosocial recovery [404]. This regulatory-
reimbursement feedback loop often prevents digital tools from being recognized as legitimate clinical in-
terventions, leaving them instead as peripheral consumer products. Payers frequently hesitate to fund
interventions that lack standardized validation and post-market evaluation, which in turn limits the real-
world evidence generation needed to justify further scale. In the absence of formal reimbursement, many
digital addiction interventions are pushed into consumer or employer-based markets -an acute concern for
individuals with gambling or gaming addictions who often face signiﬁcant ﬁnancial strain, debt, and social
marginalization.
The integration of digital DBTs into institutional health strategies, including employer wellness initia-
tives and insurance-based prevention programs, raises signiﬁcant ethical concerns related to fairness and
accountability. When individuals are incentivized or pressured to adopt behavioral monitoring tools, respon-
sibility for health risks being reconﬁgured from structural and commercial determinants toward individual
compliance with digitally quantiﬁed performance metrics. In the context of behavioral addictions, this shift

may further individualize both risk and recovery, framing them as matters of personal discipline rather than
outcomes shaped by dynamic interactions between users, technologies, and their broader social environ-
ments [382]. Furthermore, many commercial platforms utilize subscription-based or tiered pricing models,
effectively placing high-quality therapeutic support behind paywalls and exacerbating disparities for the
approximately 10% of addiction patients who may lack any insurance coverage [377].
Despite these challenges, hybrid and stepped-care models -where digital CBT serves as an entry point
before in-person therapy- show promise for sustainable integration. From a systems perspective, AI-driven
tools such as automated screening systems and virtual assistants can improve healthcare efﬁciency by
streamlining clinical workﬂows, reducing waiting lists, and providing 24/7 support. In countries like the
UK, digital tools are increasingly viewed as supplementary resources that extend therapy beyond the time-
restricted sessions typical of publicly funded systems [388]. However, the long-term ﬁnancial sustainability
of these innovations is often hampered by a reliance on short-term research grants rather than stable pub-
lic funding, with serious health games receiving signiﬁcantly less development capital than commercial
entertainment products [394].
Ultimately, the economic recognition of behavioral addiction interventions remains underdeveloped,
particularly for emerging areas like problematic smartphone use. While AI technologies offer the potential
to lower long-term expenditures by reducing relapse rates and caseloads, their impact is limited by a lack of
institutional recognition. Without dedicated reimbursement pathways that recognize digital interventions
as an integral part of the behavioral addiction care continuum -rather than market-driven accessories- these
tools will remain unevenly accessible and weakly accountable. Aligning technological innovation with in-
stitutional governance and stable funding is essential to ensure that DTx serve broader public health goals
rather than purely commercial interests [384].


8. Economic, Market, and Ecosystem Dimensions
Digital tools for behavioral addictions -including gambling, gaming, and compulsive digital engagement- are
increasingly shaped by market logics, platform economies, and private-sector business models rather than
public health priorities. Without ethical governance, these dynamics risk privileging scalability, data extrac-
tion, and user engagement over prevention, equity, and structural intervention [71]. Venture-capital-driven
ecosystems often prioritize rapid growth and engagement metrics, creating a fundamental tension with clin-
ical robustness and the long-term therapeutic outcomes required for sustainable addiction recovery [372].
This market-driven approach is particularly concerning in the context of behavioral addictions, where the
economic incentives of digital platforms often align with the very engagement patterns the interventions
aim to mitigate. From a macro-economic perspective, behavioral addictions are linked to broader socio-
economic consequences, including reduced workplace productivity, increased healthcare utilization, and
signiﬁcant social service burdens. AI-driven scalable interventions -such as automated monitoring, chatbot-
based support, and personalized relapse prevention- offer the potential to optimize resource utilization and
support population-level management strategies, particularly in regions facing acute shortages of mental
health professionals [58, 385]. By streamlining clinical workﬂows and providing low-cost screening, these
technologies could theoretically lower long-term healthcare expenditures. However, the market readiness
and ecosystem integration of such solutions remain severely constrained by a lack of empirical evidence
and a shortage of real-world implementation studies. Consequently, without deliberate policy-level integra-
tion and a shift away from purely commercial metrics, digital addiction tools risk remaining fragmented
accessories rather than integral components of a socially responsible healthcare ecosystem.
8.1. Business models for digital health & addiction solutions and the integration
of artiﬁcial intelligence
Digital addiction interventions currently operate within an economic framework driven by subscription-
based services, employer or provider contracts, and data-driven monetization strategies. While these mod-
els facilitate market expansion, they frequently prioritize user engagement over harm reduction. In the con-
text of behavioral addictions -such as gambling, gaming, and social media use- this tension is particularly
acute, as reward-based and persuasive design elements often replicate the very mechanisms associated
with problematic use, thereby blurring the ethical boundaries between treatment and harm [411]. This com-
mercial trajectory mirrors the rapid growth of the AI healthcare industry, which is projected to expand from
ﬁve billion dollars in 2020 to forty-ﬁve billion dollars by 2026 [412]. Within the “Healthcare 5.0” ecosystem,
the integration of cloud computing, deep learning, blockchain, and the Internet of Things (IoT) supports
innovation in service delivery [397, 413]. AI-based chatbots and virtual assistants provide scalable support
for mental health and addiction-related issues, yet they often conﬂict with the engagement-driven models
of Industry 4.0 that prioritize user attention. Design features intended to maximize time spent on platforms
are directly linked to technology overuse and addictive behavioral patterns [384].
As digital addiction solutions proliferate, many applications -particularly free versions- have adopted
data-driven business models where sensitive health data becomes the primary economic resource. This
raises profound ethical concerns regarding the commercialization of behavioral health data, leading to a
“currency of care” based on data extraction [379]. The involvement of private companies often centers
on sharing conﬁdential personal information, creating a potential misalignment between proﬁt incentives
and patient protection. Furthermore, there are few regulatory barriers to prevent AI chatbot companies
from directing vulnerable users toward paid advertising. Despite these risks, AI offers critical tools for
addiction management; machine learning and real-time monitoring enable enhanced prevention, diagnosis,
and recovery processes [385]. For instance, by analyzing “inﬁnite scrolling” patterns in social media or the
frequency of “loot-box” purchases in gaming, AI systems can now construct personalized risk proﬁles that
identify compulsive behavior more accurately than traditional self-reports [414, 415].


Systems like SoberGrid combine social support with AI analytics to provide tailored feedback, while
other platforms utilize educational modules and behavioral interventions for withdrawal management and
relapse prevention [380]. However, as many popular chatbots (e.g., Shim, Woebot, Wysa) are developed by
technology companies without clinical backgrounds, these tools are increasingly viewed as "complementary
tools" within a "blended care model" rather than total substitutes for professional therapy [396].
The paradox of digital addiction interventions lies in the fact that they often operate within the same
technological ecosystems that contributed to the development of addictive environments. Engagement-
oriented strategies such as gamiﬁcation, streaks, and push notiﬁcations are used to maximize retention, mir-
roring persuasive technologies that contribute to compulsive usage patterns and digital dependency. Con-
sequently, game-based mental health technologies function as a “double-edged sword," promoting therapeu-
tic engagement while simultaneously raising concerns about behavioral dependency [377]. While ecosystem-
based harm-reduction strategies -such as real-time AI-driven pop-up interventions in gambling environments-
show promise, many smartphone regulation apps for productivity and family monitoring are being commer-
cialized with limited empirical evidence of effectiveness. Ultimately, while AI-enabled solutions can address
workforce shortages by providing scalable, low-cost support to large populations, the structural mismatch
between market logic and the ethical demands of addiction care necessitates greater accountability and
transparency [416, 404]. When supported by ethical regulation and transparent models, these technologies
may redeﬁne service delivery by combining automated care with professional oversight [395, 383].
8.2. Integration into healthcare & insurance systems
The integration of digital addiction tools into healthcare and insurance systems remains uneven, with many
interventions operating parallel to, rather than within, established addiction services. This fragmentation
limits continuity of care, clinical accountability, and coordinated follow-up, particularly in cases of gambling
and gaming addiction which are complicated by ﬁnancial, familial, and psychosocial factors [400]. Institu-
tional barriers, such as limited clinician training, unclear liability, and misaligned reimbursement models,
remain more signiﬁcant than technological hurdles [69]. Research consistently shows that clinician involve-
ment is the cornerstone of trust and adherence in DTx. Clinician-mediated digital formularies can serve as
vital ethical ﬁlters, ensuring safer adoption and contextual interpretation of digital data. While web-based
interventions may reduce stigma and increase access, their long-term effectiveness depends on being em-
bedded within formal care pathways rather than functioning as standalone solutions [417, 72].
Healthcare professionals (HCPs) are increasingly prompted to recommend or prescribe digital offerings
as patients seek guidance on tech-based treatment options. The integration of AI technologies is closely
linked to the global expansion of EHR [418]. These systems combine EHR data with smartphone-based
electronic phenotyping and social media data to create unprecedented resources for addiction research.
AI-driven decision-support systems analyze speech patterns, physiological, and behavioral data to identify
mental health conditions and addiction risks more effectively than traditional methods [419, 420]. Telepsy-
chiatry platforms further enhance care by using AI algorithms to detect indicators of emotional distress
during consultations. However, a clear consensus exists that AI should augment -not replace- human-led
clinical provisions.
Sustainable impact requires collaboration between developers, policymakers, and service providers to
embed these tools within broader systems. AI-driven pop-up interventions and personalized behavioral
feedback, initially proposed for gambling harm reduction, offer a scalable "preventive ecosystem" model
applicable to various digital addictions, including compulsive smartphone and social media use [399]. AI
and mobile health tools, such as the Manaaki app, address the gap for individuals who do not seek traditional
services. These applications support the treatment pathway by providing psychoeducation before therapy,
reinforcing tasks during sessions, and serving as “step-down" tools for relapse prevention. Nonetheless, an
over-reliance on individual behavior change may obscure structural determinants of health, such as work-
ing conditions and social inequality. Ultimately, successful integration requires a systemic transformation


that addresses ethical uncertainties and incorporates AI as a decision-support tool within evidence-based
clinical workﬂows.
8.3. Interoperability with EHR/EMR platforms
Interoperability with EHR and EMR systems remains underdeveloped in digital addiction care, a gap that
affects not only gambling interventions but the entire spectrum of behavioral addictions. Most digital treat-
ments for gaming disorder, social media addiction, and compulsive smartphone use operate in isolation, us-
ing non-standardized data architectures. This fragmentation prevents the integration of patient-generated
data -such as app usage duration, social media engagement triggers, or late-night gaming sessions- into
clinical monitoring and decision-making. This lack of synergy limits clinicians’ ability to interpret digital met-
rics within broader longitudinal and comorbidity contexts, such as the relationship between social media-
induced anxiety and depressive episodes [189].
Regulatory frameworks are increasingly addressing this by framing interoperability as a safety require-
ment. The NHS Digital Technology Assessment Criteria emphasize that responsible adoption of addiction
tools requires seamless data stewardship and clinical relevance. Without interoperable infrastructures, the
high-frequency data generated by smartphone regulation apps or gaming monitors risk becoming a bur-
den to healthcare providers rather than a clinically actionable asset, ultimately undermining the preventive
potential of AI [416].
AI systems empower clinicians by analyzing multimodal data sources, which is essential for identifying
the diverse triggers of different addictions. Research has shown that combining EHR data with linguis-
tic patterns from social media or neuroscience data can effectively simulate the course of mental health
conditions [421]. In the broader ﬁeld of ICT (Information and Communication Technology) addictions, ma-
chine learning can merge behavioral patterns with digital activity to identify subtle markers of internet and
smartphone addiction that traditional self-reports -often subject to recall bias- fail to capture [415, 397].
By analyzing diverse datasets -including wearables that monitor physiological stress during gaming
or social media interactions- AI can predict addiction susceptibility and facilitate targeted prevention pro-
grams. While some speciﬁc platforms like Therachat or Recovery Record have begun linking patient logs
with clinical providers, a system-wide “eHealth ecosystem" is required to ensure that a user’s digital footprint
leads to proactive care rather than just passive data collection [396].
Ultimately, the effectiveness of these tools -whether for smartphone regulation or gambling harm reduction-
depends on their seamless integration into clinical workﬂows. This integration must move beyond simple
interface design and focus on a systemic transformation where AI acts as a decision-support tool, bridging
the gap between a user’s digital environment and the healthcare system’s formal oversight.
8.4. Public health implications & policy integration
From a public health perspective, AI-driven digital tools for behavioral addictions -including gambling, gam-
ing, social media, and problematic smartphone use- offer transformative potential for early intervention,
relapse prevention, and population-level harm reduction. However, without structured legal, regulatory, and
governance frameworks, their implementation remains inconsistent and patient protections uneven [404].
Evaluation of these DTx must therefore extend beyond individual effectiveness to include distributive im-
pact, governance quality, and their contribution to broader population-level prevention strategies [70].
Digital therapeutics and AI technologies are frequently framed as a response to the global mental health
crisis and the acute shortage of clinicians [381, 26]. Approximately one in eight people globally live with
a mental health condition, and AI promises to alleviate system pressures by efﬁciently allocating human
resources and providing low-threshold support for individuals who may not seek traditional services. Yet,
researchers caution that AI is a “tool, not a panacea”; technological investment must not supersede in-


vestment in people and infrastructure. A signiﬁcant public health concern remains that up to 90% of mental
health apps lack evidence-based clinical value or suitable security policies, risking the introduction of unvali-
dated interventions into vulnerable populations. However, clinicians are only able to assess digital therapies
if developers offer clear evidence of the product’s development and efﬁcacy testing [422].
The shift toward preventive mental health systems is a primary promise of AI, moving from reactive treat-
ment to proactive monitoring [389]. By analyzing large datasets -including digital activity, gaming behavior,
and ﬁnancial spending- AI systems can identify early risk indicators and predict crises or hospitalizations
[410]. This is particularly relevant for youth gambling and problematic smartphone use, where early warning
systems can detect “co-addictive human-machine conﬁgurations" -patterns where the addiction arises from
the dynamic interaction between the user and the platform’s structural characteristics [383, 399].
Effective policy integration is the “urgent need of the hour” to bridge the gap between technological avail-
ability and public health impact [26]. Digital interventions achieve meaningful effects only when embedded
within coordinated regulatory, service, and ﬁnancing infrastructures [423]. Coordinated action requires in-
tegrating prevention and harm-reduction strategies within national frameworks [424, 425]. Furthermore,
ethical governance must ensure technologies do not amplify social inequalities or promote unsupervised
self-treatment. Ultimately, while AI tools can reduce economic burdens and promote equitable healthcare,
success depends on collaborative efforts to ensure that the rapid growth of digital media use remains
aligned with societal well-being.


9. Challenges and Future Directions
Digital technologies have introduced new opportunities for the prevention and management of behavioral
addictions. Mobile platforms, AI-driven tools, immersive technologies, and online intervention systems have
expanded access to care and enabled continuous behavioral monitoring. However, despite these advances,
several methodological, clinical, and implementation challenges remain. Addressing these limitations is
necessary for translating experimental digital tools into reliable and scalable addiction care solutions. Fig-
ure 19 provides a conceptual overview of this landscape by organizing the discussion around two inter-
related dimensions: current challenges and future research directions. Speciﬁcally, the ﬁgure highlights
that progress in the ﬁeld depends not only on overcoming persistent limitations in validation, engagement,
multimodal integration, clinical alignment, scalability, and AI accountability, but also on advancing more
personalized, adaptive, and clinically integrated intervention models. Accordingly, this section summarizes
the major challenges identiﬁed in the current literature and outlines key directions for future research.

9.1. Challenges
Although the literature on digital interventions for behavioral addictions has grown rapidly, the current evi-
dence base remains uneven in terms of quality, consistency, and real-world applicability. A recent umbrella
review showed that intervention effects for digital addiction are promising but still heterogeneous across
addiction subtypes and treatment modalities, with several evidence classes remaining weak or inconclusive
[426]. Similarly, a recent systematic review of technology-based interventions for internet addictive behav-
iors emphasized the limited number of primary studies, substantial variability in intervention design, and
insufﬁcient reporting of software quality and interaction features [427]. Broader reviews in digital mental
health also stress that implementation barriers, safety concerns, and limited service integration continue
to restrict clinical translation [428]. In addition, recent work on cultural and contextual adaptation has
shown that digital interventions may fail to engage underserved populations when local social, linguistic,
and economic conditions are not adequately addressed [429]. Overall, the ﬁeld now faces a challenge that
extends beyond demonstrating effectiveness: digital interventions must also be validated, sustained, and
implemented reliably in real-world settings.
9.1.1. Lack of long-term validation
A major limitation of the current literature is the lack of long-term validation. Most digital interventions for
behavioral addictions are evaluated over short follow-up periods, which limits the assessment of relapse,


maintenance, and sustained behavior change. Recent evidence syntheses still emphasize that the ﬁeld is
characterized by heterogeneous designs, uneven evidence quality, and a limited number of robust primary
trials with extended follow-up [426, 427]. A notable exception is the OMPRIS line of research on internet use
disorders. The original randomized clinical trial showed short-term efﬁcacy of a therapist-guided webcam-
based intervention, and a later 2-year follow-up study reported sustained symptom reduction among re-
assessed participants [430, 431]. However, such long-term outcome studies remain rare. More longitudinal
trials, implementation studies, and real-world follow-up data are needed before digital interventions can be
considered durable treatment options.
9.1.2. Engagement & adherence sustainability
Sustaining engagement over time remains one of the main challenges in digital interventions for behavioral
addictions. Recent work in digital mental health shows that engagement and adherence are still deﬁned and
measured inconsistently, and this makes comparison across studies difﬁcult [432, 433]. A recent systematic
review of technology-based interventions for internet addictive behaviors also emphasized substantial vari-
ation in user interaction design, software quality, and intervention structure, suggesting that adherence is
shaped not only by clinical content but also by technical design [427]. Similar concerns appear in gambling-
focused digital interventions. Recent evaluations of gambling JITAIs reported good acceptability, but they
also highlighted user burden, fatigue, and the need for lower-intensity and more ﬂexible designs to support
sustained use [150, 159]. More broadly, a recent umbrella review concluded that digital addiction inter-
ventions are effective overall, but their beneﬁt varies across addiction subtype and modality, which further
suggests that adherence cannot be treated as a uniform process across behavioral addictions [426]. Taken
together, these ﬁndings indicate that long-term adherence depends on a combination of clinical relevance,
usability, burden, personalization, and contextual ﬁt.
9.1.3. Data heterogeneity & multimodal integration
Data heterogeneity and multimodal integration remain major challenges in digital interventions for behav-
ioral addictions. Current systems increasingly combine EMA, smartphone usage logs, wearable sensor
signals, and self-report measures. However, these data sources differ in format, sampling frequency, and
reliability. A recent systematic review of technology-based interventions for internet addictive behaviors
also showed substantial variation in intervention structure, software quality, and interaction design, which
complicates comparison across studies [427]. Similar problems are reported in mobile sensing research
more broadly. A recent scoping review of active and passive data collection identiﬁed persistent barriers
related to user compliance, passive data consistency, and authorization across multimodal systems [434].
In addition, a large real-world study based on data from 3,000 participants showed considerable variation
in sensor data quality across Android and iOS devices, indicating that technical differences alone may con-
found behavioral prediction models [435]. In internet gaming disorder, recent biomarker reviews also sug-
gest that multiple signal types are relevant, but integration across behavioral, physiological, and cognitive
domains is still methodologically limited [436]. Overall, these ﬁndings suggest that the main challenge lies
not merely in collecting more data, but in integrating heterogeneous data sources into stable and clinically
interpretable models.
9.1.4. Clinical-technical alignment gap
Many systems are designed primarily around technical capability, such as prediction accuracy, automation,
or interface novelty, while their alignment with therapeutic workﬂows and clinical decision-making often re-
mains limited. Reviews in digital mental health have emphasized that real-world adoption depends not only
on technical performance, but also on usability, trust, regulation, and integration into routine care [428, 69].
A recent development guide for digital mental health interventions similarly noted that intervention content


and technological features should be aligned from the outset, rather than treated as separate stages of
development [437]. Similar concerns are also evident in participatory design research. A systematic review
found that co-design with young people is still applied inconsistently, with varying levels of involvement and
limited reporting quality [438]. In addiction care speciﬁcally, a recent qualitative study showed that even
when digital tools are perceived positively, their implementation is still shaped by clinician concerns related
to workﬂow burden, service ﬁt, and operational feasibility [439]. Overall, these ﬁndings suggest that the im-
pact of digital addiction interventions will remain constrained unless technical design, clinical workﬂows,
and service delivery are developed in closer alignment.
9.1.5. Generalization across cultures and age groups
The generalizability of current digital interventions for behavioral addictions remains limited, as much of the
available evidence is derived from adolescents and young adults, particularly in studies focusing on internet
gaming disorder and problematic internet use. A recent meta-analysis on psychological interventions for
internet gaming disorder showed that treatment effects differed across domestic and international studies,
suggesting that cultural context may inﬂuence intervention response [440]. More broadly, a recent scoping
review of digital mental health interventions for people aged 16–25 years found that most studies relied
on convenience samples from universities and underrepresented marginalized groups, which restricts age-
related and social generalizability [441]. Similar concerns are reported in the cultural adaptation literature. A
recent narrative review emphasized that digital interventions are often transferred across settings without
sufﬁcient adaptation to local language, values, and service structures [429]. This concern is especially
relevant in low- and middle-income settings. A 2025 scoping review showed that most digital mental health
interventions were still developed in high-income countries and that ﬁdelity, usability, and cultural adaptation
were inconsistently addressed in LMIC contexts [442]. Overall, these ﬁndings suggest that digital addiction
interventions cannot yet be considered widely generalizable without broader validation across cultures, age
groups, and service settings.
9.1.6. Deployment & scalability barriers
A major challenge lies in the gap between research success and real-world deployment, since promising
outcomes in controlled studies do not automatically ensure scalable and sustainable implementation in
routine practice. Recent reviews in digital mental health show that reimbursement, regulatory clarity, clin-
ician adoption, workﬂow ﬁt, and interoperability with existing services remain major barriers to scale-up
[443, 428, 444]. This problem is also relevant for behavioral addictions. For example, therapist-supported
online CBT for gambling disorder has been introduced in routine addiction care, but its implementation still
depends on organizational capacity, structured support, and service-level integration [155]. In practice, digi-
tal tools for gambling, gaming, and problematic digital media use will not scale sustainably unless they can
be embedded into routine care pathways, supported by clear governance and feasible delivery models.
9.1.7. Explainability, reliability, and accountability in AI-driven interventions
Explainability, reliability, and accountability have become central concerns in AI-driven mental health in-
terventions. In behavioral addictions, AI systems may inﬂuence risk detection, symptom monitoring, and
intervention timing. For this reason, clinical decisions cannot rely only on predictive performance. Re-
cent reviews in digital mental health emphasize that interpretability, fairness, and clinical relevance must
be treated as core requirements rather than optional technical features [69, 76]. The explainability debate
is still unresolved. A recent systematic review in healthcare showed no clear consensus on whether all
AI-based decision-support systems should be fully explainable, but it also concluded that explainability re-
quirements must be considered in relation to context, risk level, and clinical use [445]. Reliability is another
major issue. Models that perform well in development datasets may not remain stable across settings, pop-


ulations, or workﬂows. In a recent qualitative study from the UK, clinicians and healthcare staff highlighted
accountability, transparency, and bias as major barriers to trust in AI-assisted decision-making, especially
when responsibility for adverse outcomes remains unclear [446]. Recent ethics-oriented work in compu-
tational psychiatry also argues that generic responsible-AI principles are not sufﬁcient for mental health
settings, where ﬂuctuating autonomy, vulnerability, and clinician–patient relationships require more spe-
ciﬁc governance frameworks [447, 448]. Taken together, these ﬁndings suggest that AI-driven interventions
in behavioral addictions will require not only accurate models, but also transparent reasoning, stable real-
world performance, and clearly deﬁned accountability structures before they can be integrated into routine
care.
9.2. Future research directions
Future work is likely to move toward more personalized, multimodal, and hybrid care systems that combine
artiﬁcial intelligence with clinician oversight and social support structures. In this context, progress will
depend on whether digital tools can achieve not only technical sophistication, but also clinical validity,
implementation readiness, and long-term sustainability. The following research directions outline the main
priorities for advancing the ﬁeld.
9.2.1. AI-driven personalized health
AI-driven personalized health represents a transformative approach in behavioral addiction management,
shifting care from generalized protocols toward individually tailored interventions [449]. By leveraging AI
and machine learning on rich multimodal data, digital systems can understand the unique behavioral pat-
terns, risk factors, physiological responses, and psychological proﬁles of each user. This individualized
understanding enables interventions that are context-sensitive, adaptive over time, and optimized for long-
term recovery outcomes [450].
Central to AI-driven personalized health is the integration of continuous monitoring, predictive model-
ing, and adaptive decision-making. Data from self-reports, wearable sensors, app usage, social interactions,
and natural language input are analyzed to generate dynamic health proﬁles. These proﬁles inform risk pre-
diction models, personalized recommendation systems, and RL-based interventions, allowing the system
to proactively suggest preventive or corrective actions. For example, an individual identiﬁed as at high risk
for relapse during late-night social media use may receive a timely motivational prompt, access to a coping
exercise, or a recommendation to engage with peer-support networks.
Moreover, personalization extends beyond behavioral recommendations to the optimization of inter-
vention modalities, intensity, and timing. AI models can learn from historical responses to identify which
strategies—such as cognitive-behavioral exercises, mindfulness practices, gamiﬁed tasks, or chatbot inter-
actions—are most effective for a particular individual. Over time, these models continuously adapt, reﬁning
intervention policies based on observed outcomes, engagement patterns, and evolving behavioral states.
This feedback-driven adaptation ensures that care remains relevant and effective as the user progresses
through different stages of recovery [451].
AI-driven personalized health also enables population-level insights without compromising individual
speciﬁcity [452]. Aggregated and anonymized data can reveal emerging risk patterns, common triggers, or
effective intervention sequences, informing both digital system design and public health strategies [453].
By combining individual and collective intelligence, AI facilitates scalable and precision-oriented addiction
management that bridges clinical rigor with real-world applicability.
However, realizing AI-driven personalized health requires careful attention to data quality, model in-
terpretability, ethical safeguards, and privacy protection. Clinicians must be able to understand and trust
AI recommendations, while users need transparent explanations of how their data informs interventions.
Federated learning, differential privacy, and explainable AI methods are increasingly employed to balance


personalization, security, and accountability [454].
9.2.2. Generative AI and conversational therapy
Generative AI has emerged as a groundbreaking tool in the ﬁeld of digital behavioral addiction management,
particularly through its application in conversational therapy [455]. By leveraging large language models and
advanced generative techniques, digital systems can simulate human-like dialogue, providing immediate,
personalized, and context-aware therapeutic interactions [456]. These capabilities allow for scalable, low-
barrier access to psychological support, bridging gaps in traditional care and extending intervention reach
beyond clinical settings.
Conversational therapy powered by generative AI goes beyond simple scripted chatbots. Modern gener-
ative models can interpret user input in real time, understand nuanced emotional cues, detect cognitive dis-
tortions, and tailor responses to the individual’s motivational state, risk level, and behavioral patterns [457].
For example, when a user expresses craving or stress, the system can generate empathetic responses,
suggest coping strategies, or guide structured exercises such as cognitive restructuring, mindfulness, or
behavioral activation. By continuously adapting dialogue content, tone, and pacing, generative AI can main-
tain engagement, reinforce adherence, and provide personalized behavioral guidance that aligns with each
user’s recovery journey.
A key advantage of generative AI lies in its ability to integrate multimodal data into conversational ther-
apy [458]. Textual input from journaling, voice transcriptions, or social media activity can inform real-time
responses, while behavioral and physiological signals may modulate the timing and type of interventions.
This enables a dynamic, context-sensitive therapeutic experience that mirrors human clinical reasoning but
at scale. Additionally, generative AI systems can deliver proactive interventions, such as preemptively ad-
dressing relapse risks or motivational dips, by anticipating user needs based on historical patterns and
predicted behavioral states [459].
Despite these opportunities, deploying generative AI in addiction therapy presents critical challenges.
Ensuring the accuracy, safety, and appropriateness of AI-generated guidance is paramount, as erroneous or
misleading advice could have serious psychological consequences. Ethical considerations include main-
taining user privacy, preventing over-reliance on AI, and mitigating biases encoded in training data that may
affect different demographic groups unevenly. Regulatory compliance and alignment with evidence-based
therapeutic frameworks are also essential to ensure clinical credibility and user trust [460].
Ongoing research is exploring hybrid models that combine generative AI with human oversight, allow-
ing clinicians to supervise or intervene in high-risk situations [461]. Such models can balance scalability
with safety and maintain the therapeutic alliance while leveraging AI’s capacity for personalization and
immediacy [462]. Moreover, explainable AI techniques are increasingly incorporated to provide users and
clinicians with insights into why certain recommendations or conversational strategies are suggested, en-
hancing transparency and accountability.
9.2.3. Multimodal digital phenotyping
Multimodal digital phenotyping represents a cutting-edge approach for understanding and managing behav-
ioral addictions by capturing a comprehensive, high-resolution picture of an individual’s behavioral, phys-
iological, and contextual states [463]. Unlike traditional assessments that rely primarily on episodic clini-
cal interviews or self-reports, digital phenotyping leverages continuous, real-world data streams collected
from smartphones, wearable devices, social media, and other digital interactions [464]. Integrating multi-
ple modalities allows for a more nuanced understanding of addiction-related behaviors, triggers, and risk
factors.
In practice, multimodal digital phenotyping combines diverse data types including behavioral logs (e.g.,


app usage, gaming or browsing patterns), physiological signals (e.g., HRV, sleep quality, electrodermal ac-
tivity), contextual information (e.g., geolocation, social environment, time of day), and linguistic traces from
texts, chats, or voice inputs [465]. By fusing these heterogeneous streams, AI systems can identify latent
behavioral patterns, detect early warning signs of relapse, and track subtle changes in mood, impulsivity,
or engagement that may not be evident through self-report alone. This enables highly personalized risk
assessment and intervention tailoring.
The analytical backbone of multimodal digital phenotyping often relies on machine learning and deep
learning models capable of capturing complex temporal and cross-modal relationships [466]. Sequential
models, such as RNNs or transformers, can track behavioral trajectories over time, while attention-based
mechanisms highlight which modalities or features are most informative at different moments [467]. Graph-
based approaches may also be employed to model social interactions or dependencies between behavioral
signals. The integration of these techniques allows for both predictive modeling and explanatory insights,
helping clinicians understand not only what risk exists but also why certain behavioral patterns indicate
vulnerability.
Despite its promise, multimodal digital phenotyping faces several technical and practical challenges
[468]. Data heterogeneity, temporal misalignment, missing or noisy sensor readings, and privacy-sensitive
information complicate model development and deployment. Ensuring generalizability across populations,
cultural contexts, and device types requires careful validation and potentially domain adaptation. Moreover,
maintaining user trust demands transparent handling of sensitive behavioral and physiological data, robust
security measures, and clear communication of how data are used for assessment and intervention.
When implemented thoughtfully, multimodal digital phenotyping can transform behavioral addiction
care by enabling proactive, context-aware, and personalized interventions [469]. It facilitates early detection
of risk states, dynamic adaptation of treatment strategies, and continuous monitoring of recovery progress.
By combining ﬁne-grained behavioral insights with AI-driven analysis, this approach supports precision men-
tal health, enhancing both the effectiveness and scalability of DTx for addiction management.
9.2.4. Hybrid human-AI care models
Hybrid human-AI care models represent an emerging paradigm in behavioral addiction management, com-
bining the strengths of artiﬁcial intelligence with the expertise and empathy of human clinicians [470]. While
AI systems excel at processing large-scale, high-frequency data, identifying subtle behavioral patterns, and
delivering scalable, personalized interventions, human professionals provide contextual judgment, ethical
oversight, and therapeutic rapport that machines cannot replicate [471]. By integrating these complemen-
tary capabilities, hybrid models aim to optimize treatment effectiveness, engagement, and safety in digital
addiction care.
In a typical hybrid framework, AI algorithms continuously monitor multimodal data streams such as
app usage logs, physiological signals, conversational text, and contextual indicators to generate risk as-
sessments, personalized recommendations, and timely intervention prompts. These AI-driven insights are
then reviewed, validated, or adjusted by clinicians who can incorporate nuanced understanding of the in-
dividual’s history, comorbidities, cultural context, and psychosocial environment [472]. For example, an AI
system may detect early signs of relapse and suggest a speciﬁc coping exercise, while a human clinician
evaluates the appropriateness of escalation or adapts the strategy to the patient’s unique circumstances.
Hybrid models also enhance scalability while preserving safety. AI can handle routine monitoring, low-
risk interventions, and adherence nudges across large user populations, reducing the burden on health-
care providers [473]. Meanwhile, human intervention is reserved for high-risk or complex cases, such as
severe relapses, co-occurring psychiatric conditions, or ethical dilemmas in therapy. This allocation of re-
sponsibilities allows healthcare systems to expand access to care without compromising clinical quality or
personalized attention.


Another advantage of hybrid approaches is the iterative feedback loop between humans and AI. Clini-
cian input can be used to ﬁne-tune machine learning models, improving predictive accuracy, reducing bias,
and enhancing interpretability [474]. Conversely, AI can provide objective, data-driven insights that aug-
ment human decision-making, highlight overlooked behavioral trends, and suggest alternative intervention
strategies that may not have been considered. Over time, this collaboration fosters adaptive, continuously
learning systems that optimize both algorithmic performance and clinical outcomes [475].
Challenges in implementing hybrid models include ensuring seamless integration of AI outputs into
clinical workﬂows, maintaining trust and transparency for both clinicians and users, and establishing clear
accountability for decisions made jointly by human and machine [476]. Privacy and ethical considerations
are ampliﬁed, as sensitive behavioral and physiological data are shared between AI systems and human
professionals. Regulatory compliance, standardized protocols, and explainable AI techniques are essential
to address these concerns.
9.2.5. Preventive & proactive public health strategies
Preventive and proactive public health strategies in the context of behavioral addiction leverage AI and dig-
ital technologies to shift the focus from reactive treatment toward early intervention, risk mitigation, and
sustained population health [477]. Rather than waiting for individuals to develop severe addiction symp-
toms, these strategies aim to identify at-risk populations, anticipate emerging trends, and implement timely
measures to reduce incidence, severity, and societal burden. Digital systems enable continuous monitoring,
data-driven risk stratiﬁcation, and personalized outreach at both individual and community levels, making
prevention scalable and evidence-based.
AI-driven analytics play a central role by detecting patterns and early warning signals across large pop-
ulations. Multimodal data from app usage, wearable devices, social media activity, and environmental con-
text can reveal early signs of maladaptive behaviors, stress responses, or social inﬂuences that predispose
individuals to addiction. Public health authorities and digital platforms can use these insights to design
targeted interventions, such as educational campaigns, micro-interventions delivered via mobile applica-
tions, or community-level awareness initiatives. This proactive approach allows resources to be allocated
efﬁciently, focusing on high-risk groups before adverse outcomes manifest [478].
At the individual level, personalized digital interventions can promote protective behaviors, encourage
healthy routines, and provide real-time guidance to mitigate triggers [479]. For example, AI systems can
nudge users toward alternative activities, suggest stress-reduction exercises during periods of high vul-
nerability, or connect them with peer-support networks. By continuously adapting to the user’s evolving
behavioral and emotional state, these interventions enhance adherence and effectiveness, turning preven-
tion into a dynamic, ongoing process rather than a one-time effort.
Proactive public health strategies also extend to policy and ecosystem-level actions. Aggregated and
anonymized data can inform policymakers about population-level risk trends, emerging addictive behaviors,
or the effectiveness of regulatory measures [480]. Insights from AI-driven monitoring can guide the design
of school-based prevention programs, workplace wellness initiatives, or public awareness campaigns. Ad-
ditionally, digital platforms can integrate harm-reduction features, such as self-limiting tools in gaming or
gambling apps, to minimize exposure to high-risk behaviors across the community.
However, implementing preventive and proactive strategies requires careful consideration of ethical,
privacy, and equity concerns. Data collection and monitoring must respect user consent and conﬁdentiality,
and interventions should avoid stigmatization or unintended consequences. Efforts must also address the
digital divide to ensure that preventive measures reach diverse populations, including those with limited
technological access.


9.2.6. Incorporation of augmented social support systems
Augmented social support systems represent a critical component of modern digital addiction manage-
ment, enhancing traditional human support networks with AI-driven, technology-mediated interventions.
Behavioral addictions are strongly inﬂuenced by social factors, including peer pressure, isolation, and com-
munity engagement [481]. By integrating digital platforms with real-time social support mechanisms, aug-
mented systems provide both emotional and practical guidance, improving adherence, motivation, and long-
term recovery outcomes.
These systems leverage AI to facilitate personalized connections with peers [482], mentors, or profes-
sional counselors based on shared experiences, behavioral proﬁles, and current risk states. For instance,
digital platforms can suggest peer-support groups for individuals exhibiting early signs of relapse, con-
nect users with mentors who have successfully managed similar addictions, or prioritize interactions with
counselors trained to address speciﬁc behavioral patterns [483]. AI algorithms can monitor engagement,
detect changes in sentiment or social behavior, and recommend timely interventions, ensuring that support
is delivered when it is most needed.
In addition to peer and professional interactions, augmented social support systems incorporate digital
nudges, gamiﬁed reinforcement, and community-driven motivation [484]. Users may receive encouragement
messages, recognition for adherence milestones, or invitations to participate in community challenges de-
signed to reinforce positive behaviors. These mechanisms enhance the social reinforcement of healthy
habits, creating an ecosystem where adaptive behaviors are continuously encouraged and maladaptive
patterns are mitigated.
Context-aware personalization further ampliﬁes the effectiveness of augmented social support [485].
AI models can identify when users are likely to disengage or experience stress, delivering timely micro-
interventions that combine social, motivational, and therapeutic components. For example, an individual
experiencing a high-risk craving period might be prompted to reach out to a peer-support contact, join a
live discussion session, or access a counselor via chat or video call. This proactive and context-sensitive
integration of social support complements traditional therapeutic interventions and reinforces resilience.
Despite its potential, incorporating augmented social support systems requires careful attention to pri-
vacy, safety, and inclusivity. Sensitive behavioral and emotional data must be securely managed, while
interactions must be moderated to prevent harm, misinformation, or stigmatization. Ensuring equitable ac-
cess across diverse populations and avoiding digital exclusion are also essential for system effectiveness
and fairness.
9.2.7. Integration with broader digital mental health ecosystem
Integration with the broader digital mental health ecosystem is essential for maximizing the impact, scal-
ability, and continuity of AI-driven addiction management interventions [486]. Behavioral addictions rarely
occur in isolation; they often coexist with other mental health challenges, such as depression, anxiety, or
stress-related disorders. Seamlessly connecting digital addiction tools with existing mental health plat-
forms, electronic health records (EHRs), telepsychiatry services, and public health initiatives enables com-
prehensive, coordinated, and person-centered care.
Effective integration allows digital addiction interventions to exchange data and insights with comple-
mentary systems, enhancing both personalization and clinical oversight. For example, risk assessments,
behavioral logs, or intervention outcomes generated by an addiction management platform can inform
broader mental health treatment plans, alert clinicians to emerging comorbid conditions, or guide adap-
tive therapy sequencing. Similarly, mental health monitoring apps can feed relevant contextual data—such
as mood ﬂuctuations, sleep quality, or stress levels—into addiction management systems, supporting more
accurate predictions and timely interventions. This interconnected approach ensures that AI-driven care
considers the whole person rather than addressing behavioral addiction in isolation.


Technical integration requires adherence to standardized protocols, interoperability frameworks, and
privacy regulations. Platforms must support secure data sharing while maintaining patient conﬁdential-
ity and compliance with healthcare standards such as HIPAA or GDPR. Interoperable APIs, standardized
data formats, and semantic mapping of behavioral and clinical indicators facilitate smooth communication
across systems, enabling real-time analytics and coordinated care workﬂows [487]. In addition, integra-
tion with telehealth services allows for seamless escalation to human clinicians when AI systems detect
high-risk events or complex clinical needs.
Beyond clinical coordination, ecosystem integration fosters population-level insights and preventive
public health strategies. Aggregated, anonymized data from multiple platforms can reveal emerging behav-
ioral trends, high-risk demographics, or effective intervention patterns, informing policy decisions, community-
based programs, and large-scale digital therapeutic initiatives. By aligning digital addiction management
tools with the broader mental health infrastructure, health systems can improve resource allocation, reduce
fragmentation of care, and enhance both individual and societal outcomes.
However, challenges remain in ensuring equitable access, maintaining data quality across heteroge-
neous systems, and avoiding technological silos. Ethical and legal considerations, such as informed con-
sent, data ownership, and transparency in algorithmic decision-making, are crucial to preserve trust among
users, clinicians, and policymakers. Continuous monitoring, evaluation, and adaptive governance are re-
quired to ensure that integration delivers both technical interoperability and clinically meaningful outcomes.


10. Conclusion
Behavioral addictions represent a complex and rapidly evolving challenge that requires interdisciplinary,
scalable, and adaptive solutions. This book has provided a comprehensive overview of how digital tech-
nologies and artiﬁcial intelligence are reshaping the landscape of addiction management, spanning clin-
ical foundations, technological enablers, data sources, analytical methods, and broader societal implica-
tions. By bridging insights from psychology, neuroscience, and computer science, the book highlights the
transition from traditional, reactive treatment models toward proactive, data-driven, and personalized care
paradigms.
The integration of digital technologies such as wearables, mobile platforms, AI-powered therapeutics,
conversational agents, and telehealth systems enables continuous monitoring, real-time intervention, and
enhanced accessibility. These systems support a wide range of functions, including early risk detection,
relapse prediction, personalized intervention delivery, and long-term recovery management. At the core of
these advancements lie machine learning and AI techniques, which leverage multimodal data to generate
actionable insights and optimize intervention strategies. However, challenges related to data heterogeneity,
scarcity, bias, and generalization continue to limit the robustness and transferability of current models.
The book also emphasizes the importance of explainability, ethical safeguards, and regulatory com-
pliance in ensuring trustworthy and responsible deployment of AI-driven systems. Issues such as privacy,
fairness, digital divide, and user autonomy must be carefully addressed to avoid unintended consequences
and to foster widespread adoption. Furthermore, economic and ecosystem-level considerations including
interoperability with healthcare systems and sustainable business models play a crucial role in translating
research innovations into real-world impact.
Looking forward, emerging directions such as AI-driven personalized health, generative AI-based con-
versational therapy, multimodal digital phenotyping, and hybrid human-AI care models offer signiﬁcant po-
tential to enhance effectiveness and scalability. Preventive and proactive public health strategies, along
with the integration of augmented social support systems and broader digital mental health ecosystems,
further extend the scope of impact beyond individual treatment.
In conclusion, digital technologies and AI hold transformative potential for behavioral addiction man-
agement. Realizing this potential requires not only technical innovation but also interdisciplinary collab-
oration, rigorous clinical validation, and ethical responsibility. By aligning technological capabilities with
clinical needs and societal values, future systems can enable more personalized, accessible, and sustain-
able approaches to addiction care.
