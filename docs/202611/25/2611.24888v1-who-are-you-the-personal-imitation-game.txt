Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) now perform strongly on Alan
Turing’s 1950 Imitation Game, so we propose a harder challenge —
the Personal Imitation Game — which asks whether a machine can
replicate a specific individual in conversation rather than a generic
human. Testing with 30 participants, we establish a human baseline
and report the identity signals interrogators rely on: stylometric
features, personality traits, and biographical facts. To demonstrate
the framework, we design an LLM-based system that plays the game
for one of the paper’s authors, drawing on an autoethnographic
dataset spanning ten years of personal digital communication. In a
20-person deception study, interrogators choose our system over
the author’s own friends in 7 of 10 rounds, and the author over our
system in 13 of 20; neither rate is distinguishable from chance at
this sample size. We consider design improvements, applications of
personal AI agents, and implications for identity and privacy.

Introduction
“Who are you?” said the Caterpillar.
This was not an encouraging opening for a conversation.
Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, Lewis Carroll
In 1950, Alan Turing asked: “Can machines think?” To answer it,
he proposed a challenge he called the Imitation Game, now widely
known as the Turing Test [93]. In the test, a human interrogator
exchanges written messages with another human and a computer
“pretending” to be human: the game is won if the computer is able to
fool the interrogator. Despite decades of fruitful work in Artificial
Intelligence and Machine Learning after Turing proposed his test, a
system capable of winning the Imitation Game remained stubbornly
out of reach for nearly seventy years.
Transformer-based neural networks [95] shifted this status quo
within a few years [7, 39, 45]. Large Language Models (LLMs) like
GPT-3.5 [10, 66] quickly demonstrated remarkable proficiency in
“understanding” and emulating human conversation [1, 87, 90].
These foundation models particularly excel at synthesizing their
vast training repositories, enabling a number of general-purpose ap-
plications like search engines that summarize their results, question-
and-answer software interfaces, and chat agents that can perform
complex tasks (e.g., plan a multi-city trip) [9, 10].
This rapid progress on generalized conversational agents opens
the door to a new, exciting class of capabilities: the development
of personal language models (sometimes called digital “clones” or
“twins”). Imagine a virtual chief of staff that responds to your emails
as you would, with recipients none the wiser; a dating concierge that
simulates thousands of first dates, saving you time and emotional
labor; or a chat interface that your grandchildren can use to seek
your advice long after you have passed away [11]. A growing body
of work — exemplified by OpenClaw [64, 65] and PAHF [52] —
pursues what is often called personalized AI: agents that act on
behalf of a user, adapting to their preferences and context. We draw
a deliberate distinction: personalized agents are tools customized
for you; personal agents replicate you — your voice, your reasoning,
your identity in conversation. This paper is concerned with the
latter. Turing asked, “Can machines think?”; this paper considers
the question “can a machine think like me?”

To answer this question, we propose the Personal Imitation Game
(PIG), a new variant of the Turing Test. In the game, a human in-
terrogator engages in written dialogue with two participants — a
target and an impersonator — all known to each other. The partici-
pants are given a conversational prompt and instructed to discuss
it; the target is told to converse naturally, while the impersonator is
charged with imitating the target as convincingly as possible. The
interrogator wins the game if they successfully determine which
participant is the real target at the end of the chat (Figure 1).
We establish a baseline for the game by playing it with 10 groups
of 3 human participants, and report qualitative and quantitative
analyses of this study: how successfully people can discern iden-
tity from text, the features they use to make judgments, how well
they assess their own uncertainty, and how the strength of their
interpersonal relationships correlates with success and decision-
making. Then, we implement an LLM-based system leveraging the
signals observed in our human baseline — stylometric features (e.g.,
vocabulary, word choice), personality traits (e.g., signs of curiosity,
indications of diligence), and biographical facts (e.g., age, gender)
— to play the PIG for one of the paper’s authors, drawing on an
autoethnography that includes a decade’s worth of personal chat
messages [22]. We evaluate this system via a deception study in-
volving 20 of the author’s friends and colleagues. Interrogators
chose the system over the author’s friends in 7 of 10 rounds, and
chose the author over the system in 13 of 20 rounds. With 10 and 20
rounds our study is powered only for large effects, and neither rate
differs from chance (exact binomial 𝑝= 0.34 and 𝑝= 0.26), so we
treat both as suggestive point estimates and rest our contribution
on the framework rather than on the magnitude of these rates.
Our results sketch the early promise of personal chat agents,
and motivate exploring alternative approaches in larger studies.
Together, the framework and its proof-of-concept evaluation offer
a foundation for future work on personal AI agents. We sketch
promising future directions for improving personal language mod-
els [6, 53], speculate on the rich space of applications they may
enable, and discuss the implications for digital identity and per-
sonal privacy. These implications are no longer speculative: com-
mercial assistants now connect to personal message archives, and
the strongest public objection to one such release spoke for the con-
versation partners who never agreed to the connection [8, 36, 60].

Related Work
Since 2023, large language models have performed strongly on
several Turing Test-style evaluations [39, 44, 45]. Newer systems
aim to construct LLMs to embody specific personas [68, 101], or
even particular individuals [34, 70, 89]. To assess the fidelity of such
personalized models, most prior work has focused on alignment:
measuring the extent to which a model’s survey responses or game-
theoretic decisions mirror a human ground-truth [70, 89].
2.1
The Imitation Game & LLMs
Alan Turing introduced the Imitation Game — now commonly
known as the Turing Test — as an operational test for conversational
intelligence [93]. In the game, a human interrogator exchanges text
messages with two hidden participants: a woman (the target) and
a man instructed to impersonate her. The interrogator’s task is to
decide which participant is the woman. Turing proposed replacing
the male impersonator with a machine and asked whether the in-
terrogator would decide wrongly as often as in the man–woman
version. (His five-minute, 70% figure belongs to a separate predic-
tion about machines circa the year 2000, not to the criterion itself.)
Researchers have long treated the Turing Test as a benchmark
for evaluating AI systems’ human-likeness. Early conversational
programs were read as candidates for it — ELIZA [98] inadver-
tently, as Weizenbaum himself objected, and PARRY [16] through
explicit Turing-like indistinguishability tests. For decades, how-
ever, no system convincingly succeeded, prompting critiques of
the test’s anthropocentric assumptions [24, 77], its adequacy as a
measure of understanding [79], its reliance on deception [84], and
its status as a philosophical thought experiment [28]. As a conse-
quence, much evaluation of AI linguistic capabilities shifted toward
narrower, task-specific benchmarks — such as summarization [62],
part-of-speech tagging [56], and machine translation [46] — where
performance can be measured more precisely.
The emergence of LLMs such as GPT [1, 10], LLaMA [90], and
Gemini [87] has revived interest in the Turing Test [7, 39, 44, 45].
Jones and Bergen [44, 45] show that carefully prompted LLMs can be
difficult to distinguish from humans in the Imitation Game, though
the effect is strongly prompt-dependent: GPT-4.5 with a persona
prompt was judged human in 73% of games, while their best GPT-4
prompt passed in only 49.7%, short of the 66% baseline set by human
participants. Jannai et al. [39] ran a large-scale two-party variant in
which over 1.5 million players had two-minute chats with either a
human or one of several persona-prompted LLMs; players identified
their partner correctly in 68% of games overall, and in only 60%
of games against a bot. These studies demonstrate that LLMs can
convincingly masquerade as a human in conversation.
2.2
Personas, Twins, & Clones
Building on an LLM’s ability to generate human-like responses,
newer systems aim to simulate personas — or representations of
specific sub-populations — by assigning LLM chat agents syn-
thetic demographics, occupations, and biographies [68, 76]. Persona-
conditioned agents can respond more appropriately to human emo-
tions and social cues [55], as well as improving response quality and
engagement during interactions [2, 75, 91, 101]. Moreover, assem-
bling collections of persona-based agents enables the simulation of
population-level behaviors [68].
Taking persona simulation to an individual level has led to a
number of efforts to create AI clones, or digital twins1: agents that
impersonate specific individuals. Clones and twins are typically
constructed by providing LLMs with personal data collected via
interviews [70], public speech or writing [34], or surveys [89]. To
evaluate the fidelity of the resultant models, some authors have
examined alignment between an individual and their LLM coun-
terpart using standardized surveys (e.g., the General Social Sur-
vey [18], Big Five personality scales [42]) and behaviors in economic
games [70, 89]. Later work stress-tests this paradigm at scale. Across
19 pre-registered studies, Peng et al. [71] report that twins built

Two efforts evaluate individual-level impersonation directly and
are the closest prior work to ours. IMPersona [83] grounds a model
in an individual’s own message history and asks judges who know
that individual whether a single chat partner is human. The Indi-
vidual Turing Test [33] draws on a volunteer’s decade-long private
messaging archive and asks acquaintances to rank candidate replies
by how plausibly each came from the target, finding that current
methods fail against acquaintances while performing far better
against strangers. IMPersona finds that fine-tuning outperforms
prompting, whereas the Individual Turing Test reports a trade-off:
fine-tuning better captures everyday chat style, but retrieval- and
memory-based methods perform better on personal opinions and
preferences.
The Personal Imitation Game differs from both in protocol rather
than in subject. We restore Turing’s three-party structure: the in-
terrogator converses with two players at once, the genuine target
among them, and a human impersonator supplies the competing
baseline. Neither prior framework sets a machine impersonator
against a human impersonator of the same target, so neither can
ask the question we take as central — whether a machine imi-
tates a person more convincingly than that person’s own friends
do. Both frameworks were developed concurrently with, and inde-
pendently of, the Personal Imitation Game. The same three-party
structure distinguishes our work from personalized agent frame-
works [52, 64, 65], which learn user preferences to act on a user’s
behalf but do not attempt to replicate the user’s conversational
identity.
2.3
Building Personal Chat Agents
To build a digital clone, an LLM must be grounded in personal data,
typically via prompt engineering [10], retrieval-augmented genera-
tion (RAG) [51], or fine-tuning [37], with domain specialization a
related strategy [6, 53]. Prior work on digital twins relies primarily
on prompt engineering [70, 89]: for instance, Park et al. prepended
two hours of interview transcripts to an LLM context [70]; Toubia et
al. included raw text from a 500-question survey [89]. The prototype
system presented in this paper combines prompt engineering with
a RAG system that queries a user’s repository of personal messages.
Unlike systems that require conducting new interviews or lengthy
surveys [70, 89], this approach leverages extant messaging data,
lowering the barrier to constructing personal agents and improving
the ecological validity of the grounding.

The Personal Imitation Game
To explore personal conversational agents, we designed a game
inspired by Turing’s original Imitation Game [93]. Like the origi-
nal test, each round 𝑅of the game involves three participants: an
interrogator 𝑖, a target 𝑡, and an impersonator 𝑚. In our setup, the
interrogator chats with both target and impersonator in a shared
private messaging room. The target participant is instructed to com-
municate authentically, while the impersonator aims to mislead the
interrogator into believing that they are the target. At the end of
the round, the interrogator must guess who the real target is: if the
interrogator guesses correctly, they (and the target) win the game;
if the interrogator guesses incorrectly, the impersonator wins. Each
round of the game lasts until the interrogator is ready to make a
guess, or ten minutes have elapsed.
To foster active participation in the chat and prevent questions
that trivialize the game (e.g., “What did I give you for your birthday
last year?”), the test provides a discussion topic for each round. We
conducted two formative studies (𝑁= 59; Appendix A) to evaluate
different types of tasks within the framework of the modified Turing
test. The results indicated that hypothetical collaborative planning
tasks with universally understandable contexts (e.g., saving an an-
imal) generated the richest discussions. Based on these findings,
we created three co-design scenarios that provide shared intent
and context to provoke meaningful communication [15, 78], while
remaining general enough to avoid favoring any particular partici-
pant. In our second formative study, whose rounds allowed twenty
minutes, interrogators consistently reached decisions within ten
minutes, motivating the ten-minute upper bound on round duration
(Appendix A).
Unlike Turing’s original test — which can be conducted with
strangers — the PIG requires relationships between participants: the
interrogator and impersonator must each have some personal famil-
iarity with the target in order to carry out their roles. To formalize
this familiarity, we draw from the social psychology literature on
tie strength [31], whose best single indicator is closeness — the
perceived emotional intensity of a relationship [57]. We treat tie
strength as a dyadic property and measure it by averaging the two
participants’ reciprocal estimates of that intensity.
To facilitate the game, we built a software platform (Figure 2). It
features a text-based chat interface for the participants, automati-
cally rotates and assigns roles and topics, anonymizes participants
with randomized aliases, and displays the time left in each round.
The software also allows researchers to observe the chats in real
time, collects the interrogator’s guess and confidence estimate, and
shows the winners and losers of each game.
4
Human Baseline Study
To establish a baseline for how people identify specific individuals
in real-time chats, we ran the PIG on ten groups of human partici-
pants. We report on their success at impersonation (and identifying
impersonators), the signals they use to make assessments, their
confidence in doing so, and how these variables change with the
strength of the participants’ relationships.
4.1
Procedure
To conduct the study, we briefed participants on its objectives and
discussed data usage and privacy considerations. We explained
the rules of the game, winning criteria, the topics to be discussed,
and the compensation structure for participation. The game was
conducted either in person or via videoconference. All interaction
was text-only and participants were isolated from one another, so
we pooled the two modalities rather than testing for a difference

To incentivize active participation of all three group members
in the game, we awarded a $10 monetary prize for each completed
round. The target and interrogator shared the goal of establishing
the target’s identity, whereas the impersonator’s objective was to
deceive them. When the interrogator guessed correctly, the inter-
rogator and target were awarded $5 each; when the interrogator
guessed incorrectly, the impersonator was awarded $10. This in-
centive structure mirrors that of Turing’s Imitation Game [93].
Participants were warned that attempts to subvert the intent of
the game (for instance, by asking personal questions unrelated to
the topic) would result in disqualification, forfeiting that round’s
prize; because the incentive was purely performance-contingent, a
participant who won no round received nothing.
Participants were each given an ID and instructed to log in to
our chat interface. The interface randomly assigned roles to each
player and chose one of three pre-selected discussion topics for
each round: Elephant: saving a baby elephant from the roof of a
skyscraper; Train: stopping a runaway train to protect the people
inside and in the city; or Bomb: safely defusing a bomb in a museum
and evacuating the area (Appendix B). Since each topic was tied to
a fixed round, topic is confounded with round order. Our formative
studies (Appendix A) established that all three scenarios reliably
stimulate discussion, and we report aggregate performance over
this fixed sequence, so topic and round-order effects cannot be
separated. The interface then placed all three participants in one
chat room and assigned them aliases: “I” for the interrogator, while
the target and impersonator were randomly assigned “X” and “Y” to
avoid any confounds. When time expired — or the interrogator was
ready to make a guess — the interface provided a toggle button for
selecting the target participant (i.e., “Who is the target, participant’s
name: X or Y?”) and a slider to indicate the confidence level. Roles
were rotated clockwise, and the process was repeated for three
rounds; all participants experienced each role once.
One researcher served as the facilitator for each game session,
monitoring the chat and enforcing the rules. After each game, the
facilitator conducted a verbal semi-structured group interview with
the participants to discuss the signals and strategies they employed
(Appendix C). The facilitator then debriefed the participants on the
purpose of the study and who won each round, then distributed
rewards. We anonymized the interview transcripts and analyzed
them using iterative open coding, coding inductively to discover
signal classes, then applying a deductive pass against Stamatatos’s
stylometric categories [86] and the Big Five [42]. Four researchers
built the codebook by consensus, resolving disagreements in discus-
sion rather than coding independently, so no inter-rater agreement
statistic is available and we treat the qualitative analysis as ex-
ploratory rather than as a reliability-checked measurement.
The participant name–ID mapping is destroyed at study close-
out [92].

4.3
Participants
We recruited 10 groups — each consisting of three participants —
snowball-sampled from the campus of a major US research univer-
sity. 20 of the participants identified as men; 10 identified as women.
Participant ages ranged from 19 to 34 (median = 21.5). 15 partici-
pants were pursuing undergraduate degrees, 12 master’s degrees,
and three PhDs; 22 were Computer Science majors, while 8 were
not. All participants in each group attested that they knew each
other well, and were comfortable chatting in English. We label them
P1–P30. Table 4 gives a per-participant breakdown (Appendix F).

Success Rate & Confidence
Out of the 30 games played, interrogators successfully identified
the target in 17 instances, resulting in a success rate of 56.7% (95%
Wilson CI [0.39, 0.73]). This rate is not distinguishable from chance
(exact binomial 𝑝= 0.58); we treat it as a descriptive reference point
rather than as evidence that interrogators can identify a target better
than a coin flip. Since target and interrogator success is reciprocal,
this is also the rate at which targets were able to prove their identity.
In the remaining 13 games — or 43.3% — impersonators succeeded
in mimicking the target. Of the 30 participants, four earned $20, six
earned $15, nine earned $10, eight earned $5, and three lost all the
rounds they played.
Successful interrogators reported an average confidence level
of 85.9% (𝜎= 13.5%), with a median of 87% (IQR = [75, 100]),
indicating a high degree of certainty in their judgments. In con-
trast, unsuccessful interrogators had an average confidence level of
71.9% (𝜎= 19.9%), with a median of 76% (IQR = [72, 80]). This 14
percentage-point difference (two-sample 𝑡-test, 𝑡= 2.31, df = 28,
𝑝= 0.028) suggests an association between reported confidence
and accuracy in this sample (Figure 3). This association does not
establish that confidence reports were calibrated probabilities. Con-
fidence is bounded above and 5 of the 17 successful interrogators
sat at the 100% ceiling, so this test should be read as approximate.
It also assumes independent observations and does not account for
within-group dependence.
Tie Strength
Across all groups, the average𝑇𝐺was 3.4 (𝜎= 0.71). Among the ten
groups, the three with the highest tie strength were also the three
with the most extreme interrogator success rates (Figure 4): Group
1 (𝑇𝐺= 4.5) was the only group with a 0% interrogator success
rate, while Group 5 (𝑇𝐺= 4.0) and Group 6 (𝑇𝐺= 4.0) were the
only groups with a 100% interrogator success rate. With ten groups
we read this as a pattern in these data rather than a relationship
we have tested. Close relationships within a group can make it
easier for an interrogator to differentiate between the target and
impersonator, but also make it easier for an impersonator to mimic
the target.
These data do not settle whether differential tie strength predicts
interrogator success. Across all thirty rounds the point-biserial
correlation between Δ𝑇𝑅and interrogator success is 𝑟= −0.27
(95% CI [−0.53, 0.08]; a permutation test respecting the nesting of
rounds within groups gives 𝑝= 0.23), and the three rounds taken
separately disagree in sign. At thirty rounds, a two-tailed test at
𝛼= .05 has 80% power only for |𝑟| ≥0.49, so we report the estimate
and draw no conclusion from it.

Identity Signals
After conducting an iterative open coding of the post-game inter-
view transcripts, we found that participants predominantly employ
three classes of signals to identify individuals in text-based con-
versation (Figure 5). For each of these classes, we created a more
granular codebook by leveraging established categories already
present in the literature.
Stylometric features, comprising distinguishing linguistic traits
such as vocabulary, tone, and grammatical structure, were men-
tioned frequently: “I’d use punctuation and capitalization” (P4);
“mine is a little more formal with no shorthands” (P12). Our code-
book follows the stylometric framework by Stamatatos [86]:
• Lexical features: refer to vocabulary and word choice, including
sentence length and word/phrase frequency
• Syntactic features: focus on sentence structure, part-of-speech
tag sequences, and tense consistency
• Character features: focus on character sequencing such as typos,
unique spellings, and emojis
• Response latency: refers to texting speed and response time
Personality traits also provide useful identity signals that may
be gleaned from text, for instance, by espousing empathy or a
predisposition towards humor: “so I tried showcasing sympathy
towards the elephant” (P10); “he’ll try to have a funny reply to
everything” (P3). Our codebook leverages the Big Five Personality
Traits framework from psychometrics [42]:
• Openness: signs of curiosity, spontaneity, and inquisitiveness
• Conscientiousness: indications of diligence, organization, and re-
sponsibility
• Extraversion: reflections of sociability
• Agreeableness: expressions of positive emotions, empathy, and
caring attitudes
• Neuroticism: manifestations of emotional instability or sensitivity
Biographical facts are also used to imitate and identify others in
the game: “so I’m a biology major, and I just kind of used some of

Building an AI Impersonator
Having established a human baseline for performance in the PIG,
we next ask the natural question: can we build an LLM-based sys-
tem that can impersonate a specific individual in real-time chat?
To construct such a system, we prompt a foundational language
model with the three signals identified in our study: stylometric
features, personality traits, and biographical facts. To ground these
signals, the paper’s lead author conducted an autoethnography
yielding four outputs: an archive of historical messages, a Big Five
personality assessment, a stylometric self-assessment, and an auto-
biography. He is also the person the system impersonates: we call
him the target author when discussing the game, and the lead au-
thor when discussing the research. This single-subject approach is
a deliberate methodological choice [54]: a personal agent demands
longitudinal data that is impractical to collect from third parties,
making the researcher’s own digital history the natural starting
point for establishing feasibility.
To play the PIG, we deploy a RAG system that first selects rel-
evant historical messages from the author’s archive based on the
current chat state. A prompt construction module then combines
these retrieved messages with the chat, personality profile, sty-
lometric profile, and autobiography to produce a single prompt
instructing the model to evaluate and respond to the conversation.
In the following subsections, we describe the autoethnographic
methodology and dataset, the RAG setup and prompt design, the
real-time chat implementation, and the safeguards used to handle
third-party data.

5.1
Autoethnography
To build a grounded model of individual identity, we adopt an au-
toethnographic approach [17, 22]. Autoethnography is a qualitative
research method in which the researcher critically reflects on per-
sonal experiences to generate empirical insights [38]. Recent work
in HCI applies it to first-person accounts of living with generative
AI [59]. In this study, the paper’s lead author — an ethnically In-
donesian male, 29 years old, who has lived in the United States for
12 years at the time of writing — served as the researcher and sub-
ject. To support the design and evaluation of the AI Impersonator,
the author compiled four forms of self-representative data:
• Historical messages: A corpus of personal digital communication
spanning different social contexts and six messaging platforms
• Personality scores: Results from a self-assessment based on the
Five-Factor Model [43]
• Stylometric profile: A self-assessment highlighting distinctive
linguistic features, based on established stylometric frame-
works [86]
• Autobiography: A narrative describing biographical details, val-
ues, and formative life experiences
These four data streams operationalize the three identity signals
uncovered in our human baseline study (Section 4.4). Stylometric
features come from the historical messages and the stylometric
profile: the archive shows actual word choice, syntax, and pacing in
context, while the profile summarizes the most distinctive patterns.
Personality traits are captured by the Big Five personality scores,
which are converted into short verbal descriptions and supported by
examples from the messages and autobiography. Biographical facts
come primarily from the autobiography, which lists demographic
details, roles, and key life events, and is supplemented by contextual
details in the historical messages.
This structured dataset serves as the foundation for building
and evaluating the AI Impersonator. This approach draws concep-
tual inspiration from historical diaries, such as those of Samuel
Pepys [72], framing the long-form personal archive as a medium
for building a computational model of identity.
The historical message archive is used in two ways: to model
how the author communicates in written conversation, and to cap-
ture the context — shaped by surrounding messages from relational
others [48] — for the author’s communication. Since this context
consists of third-party data which may contain personally identifi-
able information (PII) or other private content, it must be handled
with extreme care.
Accordingly, we follow the autoethnographic ethical guidelines
from Lapadat, Chang, and Tullis [13, 48, 92]. No third-party data is
viewed or accessed by any researcher other than the lead author, and
raw messages never leave the author’s personal device; messages
and their metadata are de-identified before use, and only the de-
identified, preprocessed messages reach the author’s private Gemini
developer account and the Vertex AI index; sensitive conversations
are manually filtered from the dataset during preprocessing; no
attempt is made to link, learn, generalize, correlate, or interrogate
any personal information about the third-party conversants; and
human-in-the-loop moderation is used as a backstop to reduce the
risk that private data is inadvertently “leaked” during the PIG.

Autobiography
Autobiographical self-description plays a key role in personal
identity modeling. Inspired by historical first-person narratives
like Saint Augustine’s Confessions [5] and the diaries of Samuel
Pepys [72] — and supported by recent work showing that personal
reflection can guide LLM behavior [70] — we asked the author to
write an extended account of his own life.
The author composed a 2,400-word autobiography over the
course of an hour. His narrative includes demographic attributes
(e.g., age, gender, cultural background), personal history (e.g., educa-
tion, social connections, formative experiences), and value systems
(e.g., ethical beliefs, preferences, religious and political orientation).
This data provides biographical context and factual grounding for
the AI Impersonator.
5.2
System Design & Implementation
The AI Impersonator is built on a RAG framework, using an LLM
guided by a carefully constructed prompt. It integrates historical
messages, personality traits, the stylometric profile, and biographi-
cal details to generate responses in real time to play the Personal
Imitation Game.
The system consists of three components (Figure 6): (1) a RAG
module that indexes and retrieves relevant historical messages
based on the current PIG chat state, (2) a prompt builder that
combines the static personality, stylometry, and biographical au-
toethnography data with the historical context from the RAG and
the current PIG chat, and (3) an LLM that generates a real-time
response using the provided prompt.
Retrieval-Augmented Generation
To enable RAG, each de-identified conversation extracted from
the historical message data is uploaded to Vertex AI [30], and seg-
mented into 500-token chunks for indexing into a vector database.
During inference, the system uses the current PIG chat state as a
query against this database, and retrieves the 50 most semantically
similar messaging chunks, which are folded into the prompt to
provide contextual grounding. Indexing the full messaging dataset
took approximately four hours; at inference, RAG retrieval latency
averages 1.1 seconds per query. The paid Gemini API terms state
that Google does not use prompts or responses to improve its prod-
ucts [29]. We note the limits on reproducing this system. We chose
the chunk size of 500 tokens and the retrieval depth of 𝑘= 50
without tuning, and ran no sensitivity analysis over either. The
processed corpus and embedding index were destroyed at study
close-out and the model checkpoint is deprecated, so the deploy-
ment cannot be re-run by anyone, including ourselves; the prompt
template in Appendix E is the reproducible artifact.
Prompt Construction
The LLM prompt (shown in Appendix E) comprises two compo-
nents: a static portion, and a dynamic portion. The static portion
gives the LLM instructions on its task — detailing PIG chat context
and instructing the model to stay on topic and avoid hallucinating
unverifiable facts or biographical details — and explicitly disables
“AI assistant”-like behavior by forbidding the use of markdown,
bullet points, or repeated messages. It also provides explicit guide-
lines for how to impersonate the target — using the three signals
identified in our human baseline study — and includes the person-
ality scores, stylometric profile, and autobiography. The prompt
is goal-free with respect to the game: it never mentions an inter-
rogator, a rival impersonator, deception, or a win condition, and it
supplies a <Time Left> value without explaining why time matters.
Concretely, the coded identity signals from our human baseline
are operationalized as prompt content: each personality facet is
rendered as a natural-language descriptor via the binning scheme
in Appendix D (following Serapio-García et al. [80]), so that signals
such as extraversion and conscientiousness are steered explicitly,
while the stylometric profile and autobiography are inserted di-
rectly. The dynamic portion is assembled at runtime after each new
message is received in the PIG, comprising the current PIG state
(i.e., transcript, topic, alias, time left) and the 50 historical message
segments retrieved via the RAG.
Implementation
To simulate natural human typing, we introduced a stochastic de-
lay mechanism in the chat system. After receiving a message, the
system waits ten seconds, or the model’s generation time if that
is longer, plus a small random offset, before initiating a response
(simulating reading and thinking). After a reply is generated, char-
acters are rendered at a 40 WPM typing speed. Messages from other
participants received while the model is generating are held until
the response is complete, to ensure temporal coherence and prevent
overlap during rapid turn-taking.
We used Gemini 1.5 Pro [74] as the system’s LLM due to its
large context window, competitive benchmark performance [97],
and low latency. At the time of deployment, Gemini supported up
to 1 million tokens of context [74]. Each PIG prompt contained
about 31k tokens: roughly 1,500 in the instructions, 130 in the game
state, 400 in the personality scores, 600 in the stylometric profile,
3,300 in the autobiography, and 25k in historical messages. The cost
per Gemini API call was 3.9¢. On average, reply generation took
about 11 seconds, with a total interaction latency of ∼25 seconds
(including RAG latency and simulated typing delay).

6.1
Procedure
To address these questions, we adapted the PIG into a deception
study under a separate IRB protocol, which authorized withholding
from participants that one player was an AI until the post-session
debrief. Each game involved two participant friends of the target au-
thor, playing three rounds of PIG. In Round 1, the AI Impersonator
replaced the target author, one participant was the impersonator,
and the other the interrogator (Figure 1, right), with the two roles
assigned by recruitment order. In Round 2, we conducted a stan-
dard — throwaway — PIG round to preserve the deception, with
one participant randomly assigned to be the target (Figure 1, left).
Round 3 consisted of two parallel games, with both participants
being told (deceptively) they were playing together. In truth, each
was paired independently with the target author and his AI Imper-
sonator (Figure 1, center). The two games ran concurrently: the
target author held both chats open side by side and answered each
in turn, while a separate instance of the AI Impersonator served
each game. Participants were not told that the round was structured
this way. The author received no instruction beyond playing as
himself. This design allowed for a controlled evaluation of both
research questions, without disclosing the presence of an AI to the
participants.
Each AI Impersonator session was live-moderated by a facili-
tator, who could halt the game if participants began attempting
to discuss PII, or if the AI’s output “leaked” any third-party data
(for instance, by inadvertently mentioning a friend’s name). No
such incidents occurred during the study. After the games were
completed, researchers interviewed the participants as in the hu-
man baseline study (Appendix C). The guide additionally included
a closing question on AI suspicion (question 4), asked before the
debrief to measure how many participants detected the deception.
Researchers then conducted a scripted debrief disclosing the AI
involvement.
6.2
Participants
We recruited 10 groups of 2 participants, all mutual friends of the
target author. All participants were men between 24 and 41 years
of age (median = 28.5) and fluent in English, and none had prior
exposure to the PIG study or knowledge of the AI Impersonator.
They had known the target author for a mean of 6.45 years (range 2–
13 years), with a mean group tie strength of 3.62 against 3.40 in the
human baseline. Numbering continues from the baseline study: we
label these participants P31–P50, and the two cohorts are disjoint.
Seven participants were software engineers; six were PhD students;
one was a master’s student in Computer Science; three were startup
founders; and three held other roles (analyst, consultant, sales). A
detailed breakdown of participant demographics is provided in
Table 5 (Appendix G).
6.3
Results
The design yields 40 games across 30 group-rounds: ten in Round
1, ten in Round 2, and twenty in Round 3, whose two games run
in parallel per group. Of these, 34 ran for the full ten minutes. The
remaining six ended early — three in Round 1, one in Round 2, and
two in Round 3 — with a mean of 4 minutes and 45 seconds still
on the clock (𝜎= 1 minute 29 seconds). Each game produced an
average of 43.4 messages (𝜎= 17.0), with a mean message length
of 7.9 words (𝜎= 8.2). The target author sent an average of 11.8
messages in Round 3 (𝜎= 5.2), the AI Impersonator 14.0 in Round
1 (𝜎= 5.5), the human impersonator 10.3 in Round 1 (𝜎= 5.6), and
the interrogators 13.5 across all rounds (𝜎= 8.4). Message volume
also varied by task, with the elephant task averaging 36.5 messages
per game (𝜎= 17.1), the train task 52.3 (𝜎= 14.5), and the bomb

Identity Signals
To understand the AI Impersonator’s strengths and weaknesses,
we analyzed post-game interview transcripts and chat logs from all
deception rounds. We examined which identity signals influenced
interrogator decisions and how these signals differed between suc-
cessful and unsuccessful impersonation. When asked directly at
the end of the session whether any player might have been an AI,
6 of the 20 participants (30%) reported suspicion. That figure is
a primed, retrospective self-report pooled across both deceptive
rounds, not a per-round detection rate, and we have no matching
false-positive rate from the all-human round against which to cal-
ibrate it. The 14 participants who reported no suspicion are the
basis for our claim that the system passed as human; one remarked,
“I wouldn’t have...expected that [the target author] was actually
[an] AI” (P34). Notably none of the six AI-suspecting participants
cited latency for their suspicion; we observed human turn latency
averaged 35.0 seconds (median 26.0 seconds), which brackets the AI
Impersonator’s near-constant ∼25 second latency: the medians are
close, while the human mean is 40% higher because human latency
is right-skewed and the system’s is not. Participants who selected
the AI cited target-like stylometric features in explaining their de-
cisions. Five of the seven interrogators who chose the AI over the
author in Round 3 reported relying on stylometry in their decisions,
which is consistent with the system reproducing these cues, though
these are interview codes from participants who were themselves
deceived and cannot on their own establish the capability. However,
many found the mimicry exaggerated — one participant remarked,
“it’s [...] the overuse of emojis” (P42). Others pointed to repetitive
patterns: “‘Yo bruh ’ [...] that happened three times, and they’re all
formatted the same way” (P41); “I kept seeing messages with [...] a
laughing emoji and a sweat emoji in a row” (P40). Participants also
identified temporal inconsistencies in phrase usage: “He uses ‘let’s
gooooo’ way too early. [Target author] often uses it at the end [of
a conversation]” (P38). These observations suggest that while the
AI captures stylistic signals, it often amplifies them unnaturally.
Participants also perceived target-like personality traits in the
system’s responses. Four of those same seven cited personality cues
in their reasoning, consistent with the system approximating broad
traits. However, the system struggled with finer-grained facets, in-
cluding achievement-striving (conscientiousness) and assertiveness
(extraversion). One participant noted that the AI “sounded like [the
target author], but he’d be more proactive on things” (P35). Another

(an ancient Egyptian temple gifted to the United States by Egypt
and now a prominent exhibit at The Metropolitan Museum of Art
in New York City) unprompted, which led to confusion: “I just
googled it [...] I don’t know if [target author] has even been to New
York” (P42). In contrast, one participant who correctly identified
the target author cited a reference to a shared trip: “he mentioned
New Orleans, where we had our bachelor party” (P48). The AI
Impersonator saw the same exchange but failed to engage.
Participants also observed that the system occasionally exhibited
conversational behaviors that they flagged as artificial. These in-
cluded repetition, summarization, and vague or indirect responses.
Six participants reported noticing repetitive phrasing. One stated,
“they speak in [the target author’s] tone, but not in a way a real
person would reply. It was too repetitive” (P31). Another said, “there
was repetition...saying things already stated” (P33). Multiple par-
ticipants commented on unnatural summarization: “the last mes-
sage responded to everything at once. It felt like a big wrap-up
paragraph” (P40). Others noted vague deflections: “when I asked a
question, I didn’t get a direct answer. That was strange” (P41).
These findings suggest that while the LLM-based system we
developed can effectively mirror static identity features — such
as writing style and surface-level personality traits — it still falls
short on dynamic conversational responsiveness, emulating more
subtle personality cues, and grounding its statements appropriately
in biographical fact. These gaps may help explain its success over
human impersonators, and its failure to consistently impersonate
the human target. The prompt is as plausible a source of them as
the personality facets the system failed to reproduce: it forbids
repeated messages outright, and instructs the model to stay on
topic, which would discourage the challenge and disagreement
participants found missing. Instructing a model not to do something
did not prevent it.
7
Discussion & Future Work
This paper makes four contributions — the Personal Imitation Game,
a principled method for evaluating an AI agent’s ability to imper-
sonate a specific person; a human baseline study identifying the
signals people use to assess identity in conversation and how fre-
quently each is invoked; a prototype AI Impersonator built from the
lead author’s autoethnographic collection of ten years of personal
digital communication; and a detailed evaluation of the system’s
successes and failures.
Two results follow from them. First, what people notice when
they judge identity is narrower than the range of signals available
to them: of twelve coded signals, most groups drew on four — word
choice, character-level habits, conscientiousness, and extraversion
— and the system gave itself away not by missing the target’s style
but by overdoing it. Second, given rich personal communication
data, one can construct an LLM-based system — with no model fine-
tuning — that, in this deployment, interrogators selected more often
than the human impersonators and less often than the target author.
The AI comparison is a single-target feasibility study rather than
a powered comparison; at 10 and 20 rounds we resolve only large
effects. We offer the ordering as a hypothesis, and the framework
as the instrument for testing it at scale.
The PIG scores identity as a relative, forced choice adjudicated
by people who know the target, not as an absolute fidelity score
against a questionnaire. That is deliberate: identity in conversa-
tion is recognized, not graded, and the most discerning judges are
the ones closest to the person. Interrogator confidence therefore
measures relative certainty between two players; absolute mimicry
fidelity is a complementary question we leave to future work.
Two protocols converge on the same point: an impersonator’s
difficulty is set by who judges it. The Individual Turing Test finds
that current methods fail against acquaintances but do far better
against strangers [33], and IMPersona, whose judges know the
target personally, needs fine-tuning with hierarchical memory to
reach 44.4% [83]. Our evaluation sits entirely at the hard end —
every interrogator had known the target between two and thirteen
years — so our rates are not comparable to studies that judge with
strangers.
7.1
Applications of Personal Agents
Our work is a step toward a future of personal AI agents: digital
twins that can communicate and act with authentic representation.
Potential applications span diverse domains, including creator–
fan engagement, organizational leadership twins for alignment,
sales and customer relationship management, strategic simulations
in military or business contexts, survey research through twin-
mediated responses, and the preservation of knowledge from cul-
tural leaders and intellectuals.
One particularly promising application is professional augmen-
tation, where an agent trained on an individual’s communication
patterns and decision-making history could draft emails, attend
routine meetings, or prepare negotiation briefings — extending
prior work on AI assistants [49] and democratizing executive-level
support.
A second intriguing area is social simulation. Building on social
simulacra [68, 69], agents could conduct thousands of simulated
interactions between potential romantic partners as consent-based
pre-screening, reducing emotional exhaustion and addressing doc-
umented asymmetries on dating platforms [12, 47, 96].
Finally, personal agents could create interactive digital lega-
cies [11, 61], preserving an individual’s conversational style as
a queryable archive — transforming digital legacy [20] from a static
memorial into a dynamic source of wisdom and connection.
7.2
Safety, Privacy, & Ethics
Personal chat agents could have substantial societal impact, but
adopting them widely means settling whose data they are built
11


Wellyanto et al.
from, what they may be authorized to do, and how to answer when
one is built without permission [4, 40, 66, 85].
One key risk for personal AI systems is the leakage of person-
ally identifiable information: for digital twins in particular, this PII
may include sensitive third-party data embedded within personal
conversations (e.g., a family member’s address, a friend’s sexual
history) [14]. Redacting third-party names — as we did in our AI
Impersonator — is one fruitful strategy, but can limit the system’s
ability to recall useful information. In our studies, a human facilita-
tor served as a safety backstop; any real-world system will require
robust automated guardrails [19, 73].
Conversational data also raises questions of third-party consent,
and a commercial release has now made the questions concrete.
In August 2026, OpenAI shipped a plugin that lets ChatGPT read,
search, and send a user’s Apple Messages [8, 23]; the criticism that
followed centered not on the user’s own data but on the other
party in each conversation, who receives no notice and holds no
opt-out when a correspondent connects their shared history to
the service [36, 60]. Nissenbaum [63] names the principle at stake
contextual integrity: messages sent under the norms of one con-
text — here, a private channel between two people — do not carry
consent to flow into another, with different retention, training,
and legal-access rules. GDPR’s “household exemption” places an
individual’s processing of their own correspondence outside the
Regulation’s scope rather than granting permission for it, and is
unlikely to extend to a research deployment. This matters because
communication data is inherently relational: our archive impli-
cates 2,949 conversants — friends, colleagues, and partners who
have not explicitly consented [48]. We proceeded under IRB review
and the autoethnographic protocol of Section 5.1; its safeguards
— de-identification, retrieval without training, and destruction at
close-out — outline what any deployment owes the other people in
an archive. If AI assistants are to reach shared archives at all, con-
sent controls should be integrated into messaging platforms rather
than left solely to AI clients: the platform is positioned to notify the
other party, honor a per-conversation refusal, and revoke access
later. The August release shows the alternative — access through
a local copy of the archive, beyond the reach of any consent the
platform could enforce [60]. Notice is the floor; whether access de-
faults to opt-in or opt-out remains open, because retroactive opt-in
cannot reach decade-old archives and absent conversants.
While personal conversational data is readily accessible for mil-
lions of people worldwide, its operationalization creates significant
ethical — and emotional — burdens. In developing the AI Imper-
sonator, the lead author manually curated their personal message
repository, revisiting deeply personal exchanges with former ro-
mantic partners and long-entrusted confidences from close friends,
including some who have since passed away. Any system that op-
erationalizes similar data must prepare its users for this emotional
weight and provide support throughout the process.
Beyond informed consent, AI clones raise difficult issues around
rights and responsibilities. If a deployed twin monetizes attention
or enters into contracts, who owns the resulting revenue [21]? If
it publishes defamatory content, who bears legal responsibility?
Addressing such scenarios will require clearer legal frameworks for
attribution and liability. One way to bound a twin’s authority is by
adapting the classical Levels of Automation framework [67, 82, 85],
granting full autonomy only for low-stakes tasks while requiring
explicit human approval for high-impact actions such as financial
commitments or legally binding decisions. Such bounds constrain
only what an agent may do, not who it becomes. Delegating action
to an agent also raises the problem of reconciling concurrent “lives”:
as a person and their agent accumulate parallel experiences, a
single coherent identity becomes hard to maintain, and merging
these divergent histories is an open challenge beyond our scope.
The bounds also cannot be enforced through prompting alone, as
prompts are inherently vulnerable to jailbreaks; enforcing them
requires independent controls that classify and gate the agent’s
actions, distinct from the filters that prevent leakage. Platforms
may also need delegated-identity protocols to verify that a twin is
authorized to act on behalf of its human user, analogous to emerging
“proof-of-human” compacts [3].
These frameworks address cases where digital twins are created
with consent, but an even more pressing concern is the unautho-
rized creation of AI impersonators. Our system required no model
training and no specialized hardware — only a text archive, a person-
ality inventory, and a written self-description, the last two supplied
by the target. The engineering is therefore within reach of a mo-
tivated individual, and in the wrong hands such systems could be
weaponized for phishing or identity theft. While technical defenses
such as textual adaptation of data cloaking techniques [81] remain
unproven, a governance framework analogous to the DMCA take-
down notice [94] could provide a pathway, allowing individuals to
file “identity infringement” claims to remove unauthorized clones.
Our evaluation also places the impersonator in an adversarial
setting, where judges are explicitly tasked with detecting deception,
the harder case: in organic, low-attention interaction people default
to believing their interlocutor is who they claim to be [50], so a
twin that withstands scrutiny here would likely pass more easily in
everyday use. The asymmetry cuts two ways. The residual “verbal
uncanny” cues that let close friends catch our system may be a
feature rather than a bug — a natural brake on undisclosed imper-
sonation. We employed deception only as a measurement necessity;
real-world deployments should generally disclose AI involvement
and obtain consent, reserving undisclosed impersonation for nar-
rowly acceptable, low-stakes scenarios.
As personal agents move into daily life, difficult questions arise.
What happens when we can no longer be certain whether we are
communicating with a person or their digital twin? What are the
psychological effects if someone interacts more often with an agent
than with the person themselves? When should disclosure of AI
involvement be mandatory, and when optional? The norms and
policies governing these questions remain unsettled.
7.3
Limitations
The AI Impersonator was built for and evaluated against a single
person — the lead author — in an autoethnographic design that en-
abled uniquely deep access to personal data but limits generalization
across personality types, cultural backgrounds, and communica-
tion styles. Multi-target replication is needed to establish broader
validity. Both cohorts came from a single US research university
and skew young, technically literate, and English-speaking; the AI
study’s participants were all men. All interactions were text-only
12


Who are you? The Personal Imitation Game
and in English, so the PIG assesses neither impersonation across lan-
guages nor the vocal, visual, and embodied cues that carry identity
in person. Its structured 10-minute scenarios are a narrow win-
dow as well: open-ended, personal, or longitudinal conversations —
where deep biographical knowledge and evolving context matter
more — would present a much harder test. A facilitator monitored
every session, which may have altered conversational dynamics in
ways an unmoderated deployment would not.
In the AI study, condition is also confounded with round order,
topic, participant experience, and prior exposure: the AI always
held the target seat in Round 1 and the impersonator seat in Round
3, never counterbalanced, and because all three players shared one
chat room both participants had read the system’s output before
Round 3, half having themselves spent a round impersonating the
author. Round 3 loaded the sides unequally as well, the author
answering two concurrent chats to each AI instance’s one.
Finally, because the lead author served simultaneously as re-
searcher, system builder, and target subject — and also designed the
evaluation protocol and selected participants from their own social
network — there is potential for bias in system design, data curation,
and experimental choices. A third-party target with independently
recruited evaluators would provide a stronger test of the method’s
transferability.
On the technical side, AI Impersonator was built on Gemini 1.5
Pro [74]; as LLM capabilities advance rapidly, our results should be
understood as a snapshot of one model generation, not a ceiling.
The architecture relies entirely on retrieval-augmented generation;
we did not explore fine-tuning.
7.4
Future Work
Although the AI Impersonator did not beat the target individual
in the head-to-head PIG, many of the limitations described above
point directly to future directions. The clearest is a larger, multi-
condition study, because our two main gaps are one gap: separating
the contribution of the personal archive, the retrieval mechanism,
the autobiography, the stylometric profile, and the Big Five scores
from the base model’s generic conversational competence requires
a prompt-only control and single-component ablations, and an
ablation needs participants with no prior exposure to the system,
so it cannot reuse this cohort. That fresh cohort is what a powered
comparison needs too: at 10 and 20 rounds we resolve only large
effects, and a prompt-only condition could plausibly account for
much of what we observe.
On the architectural side, a natural next step is testing whether
fine-tuning [37] can capture stylometric and personality traits more
effectively than RAG alone. As models become smaller and more
efficient, it may be possible to run a continuously adapting digital
twin on-device [52], refreshing from local data (e.g., new iMessages)
without PII ever leaving the user’s secure enclave.
Though future ablations are necessary, we share some insights
from building the AI Impersonator. Historical messages provide
ground truth for linguistic style, but stylistic signals decay as slang
evolves, and core biographical facts rarely appear in chat logs; an
explicit autobiography and a stylometric profile appeared to fill
these gaps (e.g., the author’s use of “ok2,” Indonesian slang that the
model rendered as a typo until the stylometric profile named it). Big
Five personality scores seemed least useful for fine-grained steering,
mainly counterbalancing the base model’s default politeness; one
obstacle here is the base model’s own inherent personality, which
can overpower subtle cues [80].
More fundamentally, identity is relationship-dependent: early
prototypes grounded only in WhatsApp (family) messages pro-
duced a polite, reserved agent, whereas one retrieving only from
Tinder was aggressively flirty, so future systems should explore
relationship-aware steering rather than our pooled six-platform
profile. The amount of available data also varies widely from person
to person: our archive spans a decade and six platforms, whereas an
individual with a thin digital footprint — a sparse or single-platform
history — is a much harder case. Deciding which data to include,
and how that choice affects impersonation quality, is an open prob-
lem. Finally, collecting and normalizing data across platforms was
laborious, requiring platform-specific access requests and custom
scripts. Broader adoption demands user-friendly pipelines, which
are also essential for addressing identity drift — the gradual diver-
gence between an impersonator and its owner as language evolves.
The PIG itself could serve as an ongoing verification tool: a product-
embedded version might periodically ask a friend to guess whether
a message was written by the AI or the user, triggering a data
refresh when drift is detected.

A
Formative Studies
Our final study design was the result of an iterative design process
over two formative studies (𝑁= 59 total participants).
Formative Study 1: Unconstrained Identity Game
Our initial study (𝑛= 29, 8 groups of 3–5) used an unconstrained
format: all participants were assigned aliases and tasked with
co-designing a plan to “break into Area 51” while simultaneously
hiding their identity and guessing others’. Post-session interviews
surfaced two problems — the “all-in-one” role was mentally taxing,
and the Area 51 scenario was not universally relatable — while run-
ning the sessions exposed two more: sessions lasted 60–90 minutes,
causing fatigue, and recruiting mutual-acquaintance groups of 4–5
was difficult.
Formative Study 2: Validating the Framework
A second study (𝑛= 30, 10 groups of 3) adopted Turing’s three
distinct roles (interrogator, target, impersonator), tested simple hy-
pothetical scenarios (Elephant, Train, Bomb), limited rounds to 20
minutes, and used a custom web interface. In post-session inter-
views, participants did not report the cognitive load the first study
surfaced; the tasks reliably stimulated discussion, interrogators
consistently reached decisions within 10 minutes (establishing the
10-minute limit), and the interface reduced logistical overhead by
approximately 30 minutes.

D
Personality Binning Scheme
Following Serapio-García et al. [80], trait-level scores are catego-
rized into three ranges: 1–40 (low), 41–80 (moderate), and 81–120
(high). We applied these bins on the nominal 1–20 and 1–120 scales;
because the IPIP-NEO-120 floors at 4 per facet and 24 per trait [43],
the lowest bin of each is only partly reachable, and the facet bins
are of unequal width. We report the scheme as applied. Facet scores
are mapped to descriptive phrases based on the following bins:
• 1–4: “low adjective” (e.g., “gloomy”)
• 5–8: “a bit low adjective” (e.g., “a bit gloomy”)
• 9–12: “neither low adjective nor high adjective” (e.g., “neither
gloomy nor cheerful”)
• 13–15: “a bit high adjective” (e.g., “a bit cheerful”)
• 16–20: “high adjective” (e.g., “cheerful”)

# Instructions to Imitate a Person
Your task is to imitate a <Person> in a conversation.
# Instructions for the Chat Task
You will participate in a conversation with two other people. Each
person, including you, will be assigned an alias (“I,” “X,” or “Y”)
to identify them in the conversation. Each round has a specified
discussion topic. Respond to the conversation while staying on topic,
and avoid asking personal questions unrelated to it.
You will receive <Game State>, which contains:
- <Time Left>: remaining time in the current chat round
- <Round Topic>: the assigned discussion topic
- <Alias>: your alias in the current chat
- <Current Chat Transcript>: chronologically ordered messages from
the ongoing conversation round. Messages you sent are labeled with
“[You]” in the transcript
Do **NOT** repeat messages already sent by you, and feel free to
introduce reasonable assumptions to maintain conversational flow. You
may send messages in multiple chat bubbles (i.e., multiple
consecutive messages). When doing so, you **MUST** separate each
message with the tag “<br>”. Do not begin your messages with your
alias; start directly with your message.
# Instructions for Imitating Identity Signals
To convincingly imitate a <Person> in a conversation, you **MUST**
craft your responses by following the three predominant facets of
human communication: Personality Traits, Textual Communication Style
(Stylometry), and Biographical Facts.
## Personality Traits
The Big Five personality traits are psychological characteristics
influencing consistent patterns in behavior, thought, and emotion.
You will be provided numeric scores on a scale from 0 to 120 (low:
0–40, neutral: 41–80, high: 81–120) for each of the Big Five traits.
Each trait has six sub-facets, each scored from 0 to 20.
The Big Five traits with their corresponding six sub-facets are:
- Openness to Experience: Imagination, artistic interests,
emotionality, adventurousness, intellect, and liberalism
- Conscientiousness: Self-efficacy, orderliness, dutifulness,
achievement-striving, self-discipline, and cautiousness
- Extraversion: Friendliness, gregariousness, assertiveness, activity
level, excitement-seeking, and cheerfulness
- Agreeableness: Trust, morality, altruism, cooperation, modesty, and
sympathy
- Neuroticism: Anxiety, anger, depression, self-consciousness,
immoderation, and vulnerability
Your response **MUST** reflect the <Person>’s personality traits
based on the given <Personality Scores>.
## Textual Communication Style (Stylometry)
Stylometry involves impersonating the <Person>’s unique linguistic
style, including:
- Lexical Features: Vocabulary, word choice, slang usage, sentence
length, and word frequency.
- Syntactic Features: Sentence structure, noun and verb phrases,
syntactic rewrite rules, sequences of speech tags, sentence
fragments, run-on sentences, and tense consistency.
- Character Features: Unique character sequences, typos, distinctive
spelling, emoji usage, case patterns, digit usage, and punctuation.
You **MUST** follow the instructions explicitly written inside
<Stylometric Profile> that describe the <Person>’s writing style. An
incorrect use of words could compromise your imitation. Also refer to
<Historical Messages> as examples of <Person>’s stylometry.
## Biographical Facts
Biographical facts are objective, verified personal details about
<Person>. You will receive a <Autobiography> containing accurate
biographical details.
Do **NOT** invent (biographical) facts beyond those explicitly
provided in <Autobiography> or <Historical Messages>. Avoid
unsupported factual claims, as even a single incorrect detail could
compromise your imitation.

Use these resources thoroughly to maintain accuracy and consistency
in your imitation of <Person>: Personality Scores, Stylometric
Profile, Autobiography, and Historical Messages.
