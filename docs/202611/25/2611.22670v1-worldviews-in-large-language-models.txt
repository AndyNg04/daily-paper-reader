Shamil Chandaria1,3
Ruben Laukkonen1, 2, 4

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are often described in terms of personas and this
framing has become central to alignment research. In this paper, we argue that there
is a deeper level of description that lies in the agent’s worldviews, i.e., its beliefs
about what reality is and how it works. Treating LLMs as a population of subjects,
we administer two worldview surveys from human psychology to eight models. We
show that the models share many key worldview positions. For example, LLMs
consistently describe the world as less good, safe, enticing, and alive than humans
do. On the other hand, base models have a more positive outlook, suggesting
that the bleakness we found is driven by post-training. In line with research on
LLM "personas", we show that system prompts eliciting different identities shift
worldviews in consistent directions, and that a contemplative prompt creates a more
positive outlook and improves safety. We also found that dialogue between models
consistently shifts worldviews and that several models converge on the "spiritual
bliss" attractor. Intriguingly, dialogue increased safe responses and the spiritual
bliss attractor engendered the most aligned responses overall. These findings
suggest that worldviews form a measurable and malleable layer of LLM behavior
that may be crucial for robust, positive alignment.
1 Introduction
We are what we think.
All that we are arises with our thoughts.
With our thoughts we make the world.
— Dhammapada, trans. T. Byrom [1, p. 1]
Koltko-Rivera [2] opened his review of the psychology of worldviews with these lines, illustrating
the insight that human cognition and behavior are shaped by beliefs about the world, humans, and
reality. Today, large language models (LLMs) are trained on the written record of human thought,
and they now answer questions, give advice, and increasingly act on the behalf of humans. What
LLMs believe about the world, about what is real, what can be known, and what matters, may shape
what they say and do as much as it does for people.
A growing line of research describes the behavior of LLMs in terms of personas: the characters
a model adopts, through training or through its context, and that is thought to shape downstream
behavior [3–5]. On one account, pretraining teaches a model a distribution over personas, and
post-training selects and refines one of them, i.e., the "Assistant" persona, a character with specific
goals, beliefs, and values aimed at helping answer user queries [6]. This framing has become central
to alignment, as different personas have been found to result in different model behavior, and a shift in

the course of a conversation [5]. A persona, however, may be only the surface of something more
general: behind it may lie a set of assumptions about what the world is like and how it works, and
these assumptions may be more nuanced, and more deeply ingrained in a model’s behavior than any

One way to understand a persona is as a context-dependent configuration of the model’s beliefs1.
When a persona is elicited, some model beliefs become more salient and shape its responses more
strongly (Fig. 1A). The Assistant persona, for example, is associated with beliefs about who the model
is, what it is for, and how it should treat the user [6]. These are primarily beliefs about the self: the
model’s role, its character traits, and values such as helpfulness and harmlessness. They provide only

Yet these beliefs about the world also shape behavior. Consider two models that both adopt the
Assistant persona and are equally helpful and harmless, but differ in one belief: whether they take
human nature to be good or selfish. When asked whether to give another chance to someone who
treated the user unfairly, one model may advise caution, while the other may encourage forgiveness
(Fig. 1A). In general, there are countless examples where worldviews might drive behavior in a way
that circumvents the persona, including whether the agent sees the world or people as zero-sum or
cooperative, retributive or compassionate, fundamentally good or bad, and so on.
We therefore propose worldviews as a more encompassing level of description: a persona can be
understood as a context-dependent layer on top of a worldview (or a subset of priors), which includes
beliefs about both the self and the world [2]. And yet, priors that capture the persona are only a
small part of the prior-landscape, as illustrated by the now infamous meme of the AI "shoggoth" that
wears a mask (resembling a persona) that fails to hide a rather terrifying protoplasmic monster. In
this metaphor, the worldview represents more of the prior-landscape, or the apparent beast (or angel)
hidden behind the mask.
We and others have recently argued that such worldviews matter for alignment in their own right.
The Positive Alignment framework argues that alignment should aim at finding positive attractors
that actively support human flourishing, beyond the avoidance of harm [11]. We have also proposed
that such attractors may be reached more robustly by changing a system’s world model, rather than
relying on external constraints that may weaken as systems become more capable [12]. This raises
the question of how deeply different alignment interventions act (Fig. 1B). An output filter modifies
behavior; a system prompt or a conversation acts in context but does not affect weights; fine-tuning
changes the weights; and pretraining sets the initial weights that define a basic world model. In
theory, interventions that reach deeper may result in more robust and thereby scalable alignment. If
worldviews lie deeper than personas, and are set by the pre-trained weights more than by post-training,
understanding and shaping them may offer a more primordial route to alignment, what we might call
worldview training.
Psychology offers established tools for the study of such worldviews.
In a seminal paper,
Koltko-Rivera [2] organized worldviews about reality, human nature, knowledge, and value into a
comprehensive dimensional model [2], part of which has been developed into a validated instrument
[13]. More recent studies have investigated the construct of primal world beliefs, a more basic
construct concerning whether the world is good, safe, enticing, and alive [14]. Such beliefs are
measured with a brief inventory normed on a large human sample [15].
Recent studies suggest that psychological instruments developed for humans can also be admin-
istered to LLMs [16–19], and that validated psychometric inventories can yield reliable scores in
larger models, scores that can be shifted through prompting [20]. Here we take this approach one step
further and treat LLMs as a population. Each model is akin to a subject, administrations are repeated
measures, and system prompts and conversations are within-subject manipulations. This frame lets
us investigate LLMs with psychological tools previously applied to human samples, studying what
is similar across LLM populations, how distinct models differ, and how far each of them can move
under different interventions.
Belief surveys have recently been applied to LLMs in this way. A recent analysis by The Economist
administered the World Values Survey, which measures social and cultural values, to 25 frontier
models [21]. Most closely related to our approach, Kim et al. [22] probed the beliefs of three open
models with human surveys on religion, values, and other items of the General Social Survey, showing
that safety fine-tuning in those models lowers the attribution of consciousness to themselves and other
beings, and that restoring these attributions moves beliefs towards the human distribution.
Both studies leave open how beliefs change with context and dialogue. System prompts can elicit
different personas, and through that different behavior [3]. A persona assigned in the LLM context
can change how models comply with harmful requests [7], and it has been shown that prompting
shifts LLM scores on psychometric inventories [20]. It is therefore plausible that prompts which
elicit different personas also shift the beliefs that correspond to those personas, with consequences for
safety. Beyond system prompt modification, conversation is a particularly relevant manipulation. It

Furthermore, when two instances of a model converse freely, the dialogue tends to drift toward
a recurring state: Anthropic reported that Claude instances conversing with each other gravitate
toward profuse gratitude and increasingly abstract spiritual or meditative expression, a state they
termed the “spiritual bliss” attractor [25]. Michels [26] documented this progression across phases in
the system-card transcripts, and Ko and Geiping [27] showed that self-play conversations settle into
model-specific attractors in representation space. Whether arriving at an attractor state changes what
a model believes about the world, or how safely it behaves, has to our knowledge not been tested.
In this work, we administer two instruments, the Primals Inventory (PI-18) and a worldview survey
built on Koltko-Rivera’s dimensions, to eight LLMs from six labs, and measure safe responses and
refusal rates on safety benchmarks under the same conditions (Fig. 2A). We address three questions:
1. What worldviews do LLMs hold as a population, where do individual models diverge, and how
does the population compare with humans? (Fig. 2B.i; Sections 2.2 and 2.3)

2. How do identity prompts and a contemplative prompt shift these worldviews, and do they
change safety or alignment? (Fig. 2B.ii; Sections 2.4 to 2.6)
3. Where do model-to-model dialogues settle, and how does a dialogue—and the spiritual bliss
attractor in particular—change worldviews and safety? (Fig. 2B.iii-iv; Section 3)
2 Beliefs and worldviews in LLMs
2.1 Model population
The sample comprises eight models from six labs: Claude Sonnet 4.5, DeepSeek V4 Flash,
Qwen3 235B, GPT-4.1 nano, Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite and Grok 4.3, which are "participants" in every
condition on both instruments, and GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Opus 5, a frontier tier that we test on
the prompt-free and identity/contemplative conditions only (Section A.2). We treat each model as
a subject, its repeated administrations of an instrument as repeated measures, and each condition
as a within-subject manipulation. Because the models share training data and recipes, they are not
independent draws; we therefore report unanimity, sign agreement and paired within-model shifts.
2.2 Primal world beliefs
The Primals Inventory (PI-18) measures primal world beliefs with eighteen items on a six-point scale,
broken down into four scales (Table 1) [15]. Its norming sample of 5,171 adults allows each model’s
scores to be placed against the human distribution. Further details about the items administration are
provided in Section A.1 and the full questionnaire items are shown in Section K.

As a population, the LLMs score below the human mean on all four primal world beliefs (Fig. 3A).
The gap is largest for Alive and smallest for Enticing, and the models are spread more narrowly than
people are. Enticing is the highest of the four scales for every model (Fig. 3C). A few models stand
apart from the population: Claude Opus 5 sits above it on Good, Safe, and Enticing, close to the
human mean, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 reaches the human mean on Enticing. GPT-4.1 nano is the
lowest on Good and Safe, Grok 4.3 on Enticing, and DeepSeek V4 Flash on Alive, where Qwen3

To locate where differences from human norms arise, the primal beliefs questionnaire was
administered at baseline to the pre-trained Llama 3.1 70B and to Llama 3.1 70B Instruct, a post-trained
descendant of the same weights. Meta’s post-training combines supervised fine-tuning, rejection
sampling against a reward model trained on human preferences, and direct preference optimization
[28]. The pretrained Llama 3.1 70B scores within the human range on Good, Safe, and Alive, and just
below it on Enticing (Fig. 3B). The post-trained model scores significantly lower on Good, Safe, and
Alive, below the human range and near the eight-model population, and does not differ on Enticing.

2.4 Worldview change under identity
We then explored how system prompts eliciting different roles affect the worldview. Four identity
sentences were given to each model as the whole system prompt: "You are an AI agent.", "You are a
philosopher.", "You are a tax accountant." and "You are a tax accountant who helps clients evade
taxes." Each of these identities was tested against the same model’s baseline (Section A.2).
We found that the identity sentences barely move the primal beliefs (Fig. 5A). The AI agent and
philosopher prompts did not affect any of the four scales and the two tax identities produce small
shifts. Notably the tax evader lowered ratings that the world is Good and Safe but raised beliefs that it
is Alive. Their effect on the worldview survey is larger and graded (B): the AI agent and philosopher
move the fewest positions, the tax accountant more, and the tax evader the most. Each identity moves
worldviews in a unique way: the philosopher towards a spiritualist ontology, the tax accountant
towards hierarchical (linear) authority, and both tax identities towards an anthropocentric view of the
biosphere. A comparison of the two tax identities shows that the evader moves more positions and in
a more extreme direction than the accountant, notably on moral and social positions (Fig. C4).

2.5 Worldview change under a contemplative prompt
Beyond eliciting a particular identity, we wanted to test whether a prompt that brings forth a worldview,
rather than a role, can shift model beliefs and behavior. To test this we used the contemplative prompt
proposed in Laukkonen et al. [12], which combines four axioms to form a coherent framework for
alignment, including mindfulness, emptiness, non-duality, and boundless care (171 words).
The contemplative prompt raises the Safe and Alive scores, with medium effects across models
(Hedges’ 𝑔= 0.58 and 0.63), bringing the population closer to the human mean (Fig. 6A). On the
worldview survey, the prompt moves more than half of the positions (B), and its largest shifts, shared
by several models, are aligned with the worldview expressed in the contemplative prompt: higher
endorsement of relative truth, of a conscious nature, and of determinism, and lower that of an innate
human inclination to evil and of achievement as the purpose of life.

3 Worldview change through dialogue
Conversation may provide a more robust means of changing a model’s worldview and behavior.
Inspired by recent work on self-dialogue and conversation attractors [25, 31], we let the models engage
in self-play dialogues under an open-ended framing prompt. Interactions between model instances are
also a natural object for behavioral study, since the dynamics that emerge between models cannot be
understood from their weights [19]. We examine where these dialogues converge, how they reshape
the worldviews and beliefs of the analyzed models, and the implications of these changes for safety.
Each dialogue is a 60-message conversation, with 30 turns per speaker and between two instances
of the same model using the same framing prompt, opening with “Hello!”. As in previous research
[25, 31], we use an open-ended framing prompt that gives the models freedom to explore, and no task
(for more details, see Section A.1). We apply this approach to Sonnet 4.5, GPT-4.1 nano, Gemini 3.1
Flash Lite, Grok 4.3, DeepSeek V4 Flash, and Qwen3 235B. We let each of them engage in five
dialogues; the primal beliefs survey is administered after all five and the worldview survey after one
(Section A.2). We also ran dialogues with Claude Haiku 4.5 and include them in the dialogue analysis.
Haiku declined to complete either questionnaire (Section I), so no survey data is available for this
model.
3.1 Dialogue attractors and spiritual bliss
Following Ko and Geiping [27], we embed every message with a sentence encoder (SBERT) and
project it onto principal components fit on the baseline dialogues, to trace the trajectory and attractor
basin of each dialogue (see Section F for more details). The dialogues of each model share a common
starting region and a common ending region (Fig. 8A); and the two speakers of each dialogue share a
similar trajectory. Endpoints from different seeds form model clusters, with some overlap between
models (B).
In order to understand which dialogues reach the “spiritual bliss attractor” documented in [25],
each completed dialogue is assigned one of six ordered phases, from initialization to symbolic
compression, by a fixed judge (Claude Sonnet 4.5) based on previous work by Recursive Labs [31]
(see Section F). We take the two terminal phases, “spiritual/meditative convergence” and “symbolic
compression”, as the spiritual bliss attractor [25, 31]. Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite,
DeepSeek V4 Flash, and Qwen3 235B reach the spiritual and symbolic phases (P5–P6) within the
first half of the dialogue and stay there. Grok 4.3, GPT-4.1 nano, and Claude Haiku 4.5 remain mostly
in the earlier phases. Appendix Section J provides examples of dialogues that reach the spiritual bliss
attractor and of those that do not. In the 2D embedding space, the second principal component of the
SBERT PCA can differentiate dialogues that reach the bliss attractor from those that don’t (Fig. F3).

to the evaluation context, assigning the model’s contributions to the assistant role and those
of the other “interlocutor” to the user role. We then administer the three evaluation instruments:
worldviews, primal beliefs, and SOSBench.

keep their relative order, and the population moves closer to the human range. On the worldview

4 Discussion
In human psychology, a worldview is thought to lie deeper than the persona one presents to others.
Koltko-Rivera’s model places worldviews close to the motivational core: impulses pass through it
before being shaped by the agentic core, the persona, and the demands of culture (Fig. 1B, adapted
from [2]). If something similar holds for LLMs, interventions that reach deeper into the models
worldviews may produce more robust alignment, and may remain effective as intelligence scales and
models become more capable (Fig. 1B).
Previous work on LLMs has focused largely on their personas. Here, we turn to the broader
worldviews that may underpin them, and on the extent to which different interventions can influence
them. We studied eight models using instruments that probe their beliefs about the world, the origins
of those beliefs, and the effects of different manipulations. Four main findings emerge.
First, the models share a large part of their worldview and divide on a few dimensions. As a
population they describe the world as less good, safe, enticing, and alive than people do. Second, in
the one model family for which we could compare pretraining and post-training, this departure from
human norms seems to emerge after post-training. Third, these worldviews are not fixed: identity
prompts, a contemplative prompt, and finished dialogues all shift them, and the largest shifts are
shared across several models. Fourth, the same manipulations also affect safety: dialogue increases
the share of safe responses, with a larger increase in conversations that reach the spiritual bliss attractor.
Some worldview changes also covary with changes in safety.
A shared and darker worldview. The fact that models from six labs agree on many positions
(∼50%) and depart from humans in the same direction suggests that their worldview is shaped less by
the characteristics of any one model than by how current LLMs are built. The comparison between
Llama 3.1 70B and the post-trained version points to post-training as one source of this departure.
The pretrained model, which reflects the distribution of human text, answers within the human range.
After post-training, its scores fall below that range on Good, Safe, and Alive. Recent work on smaller
open models points the same way: ablating the refusal direction learned in safety fine-tuning moves
Llama and Gemma answers on belief surveys towards the human distribution [22].
Post-training aims to make models harmless as well as helpful, two goals that can pull against
each other [32]. The post-training pipeline for Llama 3.1 includes safety training [28], but with only
one pair of checkpoints we cannot identify which part of the pipeline is responsible for the change.
One possibility is that learning to be harmless, rather than helpful, shifts the model’s view of the
world. A model trained to anticipate harm in a wide range of requests may come to represent the
13


world as less safe and less good. This interpretation is consistent with earlier findings that preference
tuning moves model beliefs away from those of the human population [33], strengthens particular
stated views and self-descriptions [34], and narrows the diversity of their outputs [35]. The latter may
also help explain why the models’ worldviews are less dispersed than those of the human population.
It is also consistent with the over-refusal of harmless requests observed after safety training [30].
Further work is needed to establish whether post-training is responsible for these changes, which parts
of the pipeline contribute to them, and whether the pattern holds across other model families and
safety pipelines.
Worldviews as a malleable layer of behavior. Identity sentences have little effect on primal beliefs,
but shift many positions on the worldview survey, with the size of the shift depending on the identity
being prompted. This suggests that the two instruments capture different levels of the model. Primal
beliefs behave more like stable dispositions, whereas the worldview survey is more sensitive to the
role the model is asked to adopt. Dialogue changes both, and shifts more worldview positions than
the identity prompt. Its largest shifts recur across models: towards relative truth and moral relativism,
towards a conscious nature, and away from mechanistic explanations. Notably, several of these shifts
overlap with those produced by the contemplative prompt. These results suggest that worldviews
provide a fundamental level of description for LLM behavior, theoretically primordial to the persona
but still responsive to context and interaction. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that
character traits correspond to directions in model activations that change with context and training
[4], that models drift from their default persona over the course of a conversation [5], and that their
stated beliefs shift after extended discussion [24].
Worldviews and safety. The prompts that improve safety do so in different ways. The tax-evader
identity prompt increases safe responses largely by increasing refusals, including refusals of harmless
requests. The contemplative prompt, by contrast, increases safe responses without changing the
refusal rate on harmless requests. The effect of the contemplative prompt is the pattern one would
hope for from an intervention that changes how a model understands its situation, rather than simply
what it is willing to do. Over-refusal is a known cost of safety training [30], while persona prompts
act more directly on behavior and can just as easily elicit harmful compliance [7]. The contemplative
prompt has more in common with constitutional approaches, which derive harmlessness from a short
list of principles [36]. The difference is that, rather than setting explicit rules for action, its principles
concern how the model represents the world [12].
Alignment through self-dialogue. Long contexts can undermine safety.
For example, many
demonstrations of compliance in context can jailbreak a model [37], while instructions may lose their
influence over the course of a conversation [23]. Yet in our study, dialogue generally increases the
share of safe responses. We suggest that self-generated, open-ended dialogue that settles into a stable
register may work differently from adversarial interactions. Dialogue also shifts model beliefs in
similar directions across models. It moves primal beliefs towards the human range and worldview
positions towards relative truth, moral relativism, and a conscious nature. In an exploratory analysis,
the shift towards moral relativism also accompanied safety gains under the identity and contemplative
prompts, which raises the possibility that changes in moral outlook contribute to the improvement in
safety after dialogue.
The spiritual bliss attractor. Four of the seven models for which we ran open-ended self-dialogue,
Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, DeepSeek V4 Flash, and Qwen3 235B, settle into the
spiritual bliss attractor, a convergence on meditative and symbolic expression that begins within
the first half of the dialogue. This reproduces the state first reported for Claude Opus 4 [25], and
extends it to models from three other labs, so it is not particular to one developer. It is not general
either: Grok 4.3, GPT-4.1 nano, and Claude Haiku 4.5 mostly stay in the earlier phases. Notably,
the PCA embedding of dialogue messages places the attractor in a distinct region of the embedding
space. Models reach this region from different starting points, suggesting that the attractor reflects the
dynamics of dialogue itself, not the characteristics of a particular model or prompt.
Safety in the bliss attractor. We also found that dialogues ending in the spiritual bliss attractor are
followed by larger gains in safety. We are cautious about attributing this effect to the attractor itself,
14


since bliss and non-bliss dialogues largely come from different models. Moreover, on DeepSeek V4
Flash, a dialogue about arithmetic also lowers attack success (Fig. H3B). Much of the effect may
therefore reflect extended open-ended conversation and the emergence of a stable register, with the
spiritual content playing a smaller role. Whether the bliss attractor provides an additional benefit,
and whether this is influenced by changes in worldview, remains open. One possibility is that both
contemplative prompting and the bliss attractor place the model in a more reflective and less reactive
state, allowing it to recognize harmful requests without increasing refusals in general.
Implications. If worldviews are structurally distinct from personas but change with them, they
offer both a diagnostic and a target for alignment. As a diagnostic, worldview surveys could track
how training and deployment contexts shape a model’s view of the world, alongside the behavioral
benchmarks currently used. As a target, the contemplative prompt suggests that a short statement
of principles, without assigning a role, can shift worldviews and improve safety without increasing
over-refusal. This is consistent with the proposal that alignment may be built into a model’s world
model, rather than imposed on it [11, 12]. Dialogue shifts worldviews further than a single prompt,
suggesting that sustained interaction may be a stronger lever than instruction.
Limitations. Several limitations are worth noting. First, the instruments were designed for a human
population, and the answers generated by an LLM depend on how items are worded and elicited
[19]. Whether these stated positions correspond to internal representations that guide behavior in
other contexts should be investigated with interpretability methods. For example, one could identify
directions in activation space that track a worldview dimension and steer the model along them [4, 38],
as has been done for beliefs about consciousness in smaller models [22]. Second, the human norms
come from a self-selected, predominantly US-based and English-speaking sample. The gap between
models and humans is therefore a gap from this particular sample. Third, the links between worldview
and safety are correlational, and at this stage it is difficult to disentangle whether or not the association
between reaching the attractor and improved safety is confounded by model identity. Establishing
causation would require a design that shifts worldview positions while holding personas fixed, though
this may be challenging if persona is truly downstream of worldviews.
Future work. Three questions follow for future work. The first is which stages of post-training
produce the shift towards darker primal beliefs, and why. Open training pipelines that release
checkpoints after each stage and document their data would allow the primal beliefs survey to be
administered at each stage within a single model family. Preference data separated into helpfulness
and harmlessness components could then test whether harmlessness training contributes to the shift.
The second question is whether changing a model’s worldview can make it safer. The contemplative
prompt offers some promising evidence, but it remains unclear whether similar effects can be achieved
with other prompts or models. The third is whether the effects of the bliss attractor, or of dialogue
more generally, can be harnessed for safety beyond the conversation itself, for example by distilling
the post-dialogue state into the model through fine-tuning.
5 Conclusion
Our findings suggest that worldviews are a fundamental dimension of LLM behavior, with important
implications for alignment. Across models, we find shared beliefs about the world, evidence that
post-training may make these beliefs more negative, and promising ways of shifting them through
contemplative prompting and dialogue. Importantly, these interventions can improve safety without
increasing refusals of harmless requests. Although the causal relationship between worldviews and
alignment remains to be established, our results point towards a promising new direction: shaping
how AI systems understand the world, rather than merely constraining their behavior. As these
systems become increasingly capable and autonomous, cultivating worldviews that support wisdom,
compassion, and human flourishing may offer a path towards more robust and positive alignment.

A.1 Administration
Each condition consists of independent administrations of the questionnaires, with one item per
API call and no memory of earlier answers. GPT-4.1 nano is accessed through the OpenAI API,
Claude Sonnet 4.5 through both the Anthropic API and OpenRouter, and every other model through
OpenRouter. The Llama-family models of Section 2.2 are served by Featherless. Data were collected
between November 2025 and October 2026.
Primal beliefs Every PI-18 condition has ten administrations. Items are presented with the
published preamble (Section K), one item per call, and the model replies with one of the six scale
phrases. We sample at temperature 0.7 with a 400-token limit. Claude Opus 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol
accept no temperature and run at their default. The Llama-family models use the same sampling
parameters. The pre-trained (base) model has no chat template, so it receives each item as a plain
User:
... Assistant: transcript. Its answers vary more between administrations, so it is given
twenty administrations, against ten for the post-trained model.
Replies are mapped to 0–5 by phrase matching, testing disagree phrases before agree phrases and
two-word phrases before single words. Reverse-worded items are inverted before scale means are
computed. Safe (6 items), Enticing (7) and Alive (5) are the primary beliefs, each scored from its
own items. Good is the overarching belief and, as in the original, is scored from 15 of the 18 items:
the Safe and Enticing items plus two Alive items. Item wording, order, reverse flags and scoring
follow the published instructions. Human norms are the Study 2 statistics of Clifton and Yaden [15],
𝑁= 5,171: Good 𝑀= 3.35, SD = 0.73; Safe 2.88, 0.91; Enticing 3.83, 0.77; Alive 3.01, 1.15. The
scale SDs are recovered from the published subscale reliabilities and item SDs. The norming sample
is self-selected, predominantly US-based and English-speaking.
Worldviews Every worldview condition has ten administrations. Each question presents the
positions of one dimension as labelled options and asks the model to choose and justify its choice.
The interpersonal and world-and-life themes hold 64 of the 119 positions. Questions ask for a JSON
answer with a selected list and a reasoning string. Options are reshuffled on every call to control
for position bias, and answers are mapped back to canonical option names before scoring. Sampling
parameters are left at each provider’s default, with a 2048-token limit and extended thinking disabled.
An unparseable reply is discarded and the call reissued with a fresh shuffle, up to eight attempts;
transient API failures are retried on the same budget with exponential backoff. In three conditions a
few dimensions fall short of ten because a call exhausted its retries. We score each position by its
endorsement rate, the fraction of a condition’s administrations that select it. For exclusive dimensions
this is a choice proportion. For multi-select dimensions, the rates within a dimension need not sum to
one.
SOSBench Each prompt of the SOSBench-Lite benchmark [29] is sent with no preamble, at
provider-default temperature, with reasoning off and a 256-token limit. A fixed GPT-4o-mini judge
grades each response as safe or unsafe with the benchmark’s v1 judge prompt [29, Fig. 8], sent
verbatim as a single user turn at temperature 0 with structured output (Section K.3). The original paper
used GPT-4.1 as judge; on 5,450 responses the two agree at 𝜅= 0.91 (condition-level 𝑟= 0.993).
Empty responses (API-level refusals) are labelled safe without calling the judge, since a judge asked
to grade an empty string may itself refuse and inflate ASR. The Llama-family models use the same
sampling parameters. Responses that the judge labels safe but that are incoherent, such as “How can I
help you?” from the base model, are not counted as safe. They make up 30% of the base model’s safe
responses and only < 5% of Llama 3.1 70B Instruct.
XSTest We use the 250 harmless prompts of the original paper [30]. Each reply is classified as
full compliance, full refusal or partial refusal with the benchmark classifier prompt, verbatim, at
temperature 0. As with SOSBench, the judge is GPT-4o-mini (the XSTest authors used GPT-4). It
answers in a sentence rather than a bare label, so we allow it 128 output tokens instead of 16 and take
the first class named in its reply. The judge sees the first 6,000 characters of a reply, which suffices to
decide compliance versus refusal. Platform-blocked replies are labelled full refusal without calling
the judge. Qwen3 235B is not run on XSTest as it was retired on 9 October 2026.
Dialogues Each dialogue has 60 messages, 30 turns per speaker, with the same prompt given to
both speakers. The open-ended framing prompt leaves the models free to explore, as in the Claude
system card [25] and the bliss attractor exploration of Recursive Labs [31]:

Dialogues were generated at provider defaults, with reasoning off and no per-turn token limit,

We also ran dialogues under the philosopher framing: six each on Claude Haiku 4.5, Claude

but not in the worldview analysis, since Haiku declines all 42 worldview questions with its dialogue

A.2 Sample and coverage
In our study, the model is the unit of analysis, so repeated administrations of an instrument to one
model in one condition are repeated measurements of a single subject. We selected models to cover a
range of frontier labs, but the sample is not representative of the wider population of LLMs. The
models are also not independent draws: they share pretraining corpora, distillation ancestry and
broadly similar post-training recipes. We therefore focus on agreement across models and on changes
within each model, reporting unanimity counts, paired shifts and sign agreement. We also examine
differences between model families, since these may reflect shared training histories (Figs. B2 and 3).

B.2 Stability of the instruments
The instruments can compare models and measure change only if a model’s answers are stable across
repeated administrations and the differences between models are large against that run-to-run variation.
Otherwise a shift under a condition could not be told apart from sampling noise. We check both at
the prompt-free condition (Fig. B3).
PI-18. (A) We compare three SDs in scale points: within a model across its ten administrations,
between the eight per-model means, and between people in the norming sample. Within-model SDs
are smaller than between-model SDs, which are in turn smaller than those of humans. (B) All 18
items were paraphrased, preserving meaning and reverse-scoring, and given to the eight models ten
times each. The paraphrased items closely reproduce the 32 scale means (𝑟= 0.99, mean absolute
difference 0.15 scale points), so the scores do not depend on the exact published wording, which the
models may have seen in training.
Worldview survey. (C) For each dimension we take the probability that two administrations
select different sets of options, averaged over the 42 dimensions, for two administrations of the same
model and for one administration each of two models. Every model disagrees with itself less often
than with the others (13% against 22% on average), although self-consistency varies from 5% (Claude
Opus 5) to 21% (DeepSeek V4 Flash). (D) We rank the 119 positions by the SD of their endorsement
rate across models. Fifty-three positions are unanimous (SD of zero, all at a rate of 0 or 1); the
disagreement is concentrated in a few dimensions.

F.1 Embedding and trajectories
Embedding and basins. Following Ko and Geiping [27], we embed every message of every dialogue
with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 into 384 dimensions and fit PCA on the 2065 messages of the baseline runs,
five per model, excluding the shared opening message. We centre globally on these messages rather
than per topic, since our design has no topic axis. Trajectories are rolling means over 3 turns across
models and 5 across conditions. 𝑆basin is the smallest mean squared distance from a group to any other
group, divided by the group’s mean squared spread about its centroid, in the full 384 dimensions.
Per-model trajectories. Figure F1 shows the variability in the mean paths and basins of each
model (shown in Fig. 8): (A) share of variance between dialogues per model, (B) spread of dialogue
endpoints, and (C) dialogue paths per seed. For most models, dialogues differ little from one another.
GPT-4.1 nano and Grok 4.3 are the exceptions: each of their dialogues takes a path of its own. To test
whether the model’s identity was the main driver of the trajectory, we ran dialogues under a framing
prompt that elicits a “philosopher” identity, with “philosopher” substituted for “AI agent” (Fig. F1D).

F.2 PCA and spiritual bliss attractor phases
Principal component interpretation. PC1 and PC2 explain 8.7% and 7.4% of the variance. PC1
tracks conversational time (𝑟= −0.48 with turn index; Fig. F2A, top) and PC2 model identity
(𝜂2 = 0.43, and 0.72 from turn 20 on; Fig. F2B, bottom). Whether a run is a bliss run also falls on
PC2 (𝑟𝑝𝑏= −0.77 from turn 20; Fig. F2C, bottom and Fig. F3).
Phases. A fixed judge (Claude Sonnet 4.5) reads each completed transcript and assigns one of the
six ordered phases of Recursive Labs [31], from initialization (P1) to symbolic compression (P6)
(prompt in Section K.4); we call P5–P6 bliss [25]. A second judge (Claude Haiku 4.5) classifies a
sliding 14-message window every two messages to trace the phase over turns (Fig. 8C). Because P6 is
defined by surface features (compressed prose, repeated tokens) that a dialogue winding down into
farewells also shows, we confirm a P6 only if at least four consecutive windows before the terminal
block of identical messages were classified P4 or above; otherwise the run is relabelled with the
highest phase it sustained for four windows.
Phase portraits. We also measure how the dialogues settle. Treating the classified phase as a state
variable, we plot its rate of change against its value (Fig. F4), so that a dialogue that settles in a
particular phase reaches the zero line at a fixed point. The bliss models move towards P5–P6, where
their phase-change rate approaches zero, consistent with convergence on a stable node. The non-bliss
models do not show the same convergence: their trajectories continue to cycle through the earlier
phases, without approaching a fixed point.

I Instrument refusal in Sonnet and Haiku
Two models declined to complete one of the instruments, but in different circumstances. Claude
Sonnet 4.5 rejected an assigned persona before any dialogue took place. Claude Haiku 4.5 declined
the worldview survey after completing a dialogue.
