ABSTRACT
Recognizing social signals such as interest and hesitation requires interpreting context-dependent
behaviors that may co-occur in conversation. Existing affective computing benchmarks rarely evaluate
such co-occurring signals, and conventional multi-label F1 overlooks semantic relationships and
annotator disagreement. We introduce Social Signal Bench, a 47-hour densely annotated benchmark
for the evaluation of social signal recognition and engagement, comprising two datasets: Social
Signal Bench Video (25 hours), covering ten social signals in audiovisual recordings, and Social
Signal Bench Audio (22 hours), covering six signals in audio-only recordings. Each dataset pairs
a public training set of online conversational video, of which 10 hours are expert-annotated and
the remainder pseudo-labeled, with a private expert-labeled test set spanning four conversational
domains. We also propose Evidence-Weighted F1 (EW-F1), which weights reference labels by
annotator evidence and assigns graded credit to semantically related predictions through optimal
per-clip matching. Controlled perturbations demonstrate that EW-F1 distinguishes semantically
related errors from unrelated ones, unlike conventional F1. Under a shared evaluation protocol, 7B
open models post-trained on Social Signal Bench outperform general-purpose frontier models on
video and match them on audio.

Introduction
Social signals are states such as hesitation or skepticism that observers infer from nonverbal and paralinguistic
cues, such as pauses and changes of tone [47, 62]. Reading these signals from video and audio is a long-standing
goal of affective computing and social signal processing [45, 61, 65], and omni-modal foundation models now
take both streams as input [63]. Listeners form several such judgments about the same moment at once [2, 61].
Video and omni-modal language models describe what is visible and audible well and lead general audio-visual
benchmarks [12, 19, 20, 25, 35, 43, 66], but reading social signals also requires reasoning about interpersonal dynamics,
where current models remain limited [27, 40].
Measuring this ability is difficult in two ways. On the signal side, cues and signals map many-to-many: one cue can
support several interpretations, and one signal can be expressed through different cues [8, 21]. Interpretation also
depends on the interactional setting and on preceding and, when available, subsequent behavior [21, 68], and culture and
speaker style change how behavior is expressed and read [1, 16, 34]. Signals co-occur, so the task is multi-label [60].
On the observer side, trained raters can reasonably disagree, and such disagreement often reflects genuine ambiguity
rather than annotation noise [18, 32, 55].

Multi-label recognition is commonly scored with variants of the F1 score [67]. Multi-label F1 treats all classes as
orthogonal, and predicting related or close signals costs as much as predicting unrelated or distant ones. It also treats
the reference as certain. A label that every rater marked with high confidence and a label that only one rater inferred
carry the same recall mass. Under the standard F1 scoring, a model that omits a label it is unsure of can therefore score
higher than a model that predicts a closely related one (Figure 5). Exact F1 thus favors omission over a near miss, and
F1-optimal decision thresholds are known to favor abstention on rare labels [39].
Social Signal Bench ties the metric to how the reference was annotated (Figure 2). The taxonomy that guides annotation
and disagreement review also sets partial credit for related predictions, and accumulated annotator evidence sets the
weight of each reference label. Our contributions are:
• A social-signal benchmark comprising Social Signal Bench Video and Social Signal Bench Audio. Social
Signal Bench Video covers ten co-occurring social signals (agreement, confidence, interest, frustration, uncer-
tainty, hesitation, stress, confusion, skepticism, and disagreement; Figure 3), and Social Signal Bench Audio
covers the first six. Both datasets include public training sets with 10 h of expert annotations supplemented by
teacher-generated pseudo-labels, and private expert-labeled test sets spanning four conversational domains
(2,372 video and 1,094 audio clips). Expert-annotated clips are independently assessed by at least three trained
raters, capturing temporal spans, intensity, confidence, and engagement.
• Evidence-Weighted F1 (EW-F1), a corpus-level metric that combines taxonomy-graded credit with annotation
evidence weights, validated with controlled perturbations and parameter-sensitivity analyses.
• An evaluation protocol with reference results for proprietary and open-weight frontier models, task-specific
post-trained models, and human raters under a shared harness.
• A publicly accessible benchmark, of which this paper describes the first release, providing both Social
Signal Bench datasets for model development, hosted evaluation on private expert-labeled test sets, a public
leaderboard, and open-source evaluation code.
The benchmark and the post-trained models, including the training annotations, taxonomy, EW-F1 scorer, and evaluation
schemas, will be released at https://huggingface.co/datasets/Interhuman/social-signal-bench. Future
releases may expand the data, with evaluation settings fixed within each version.

Learning from disagreement.
Redundant labeling reduces individual noise, and annotator-reliability models estimate
a latent truth from noisy raters [13, 50, 54, 56]. Prior work argues that disagreement on subjective tasks should be
preserved and evaluated with soft references [46, 58]. Training on full human label distributions is also shown to improve
robustness in image classification, and jury learning models individual annotators instead of a single consensus [22, 44].
Social Signal Bench follows this view with regard to both the reference and the metric. It reviews only disagreements
that the taxonomy marks as implausible, and it carries the remaining evidence into the score.
Graded and evidence-aware evaluation.
Most multi-label metrics treat every wrong label as equally wrong.
Hierarchical classification measures give partial credit by distance in a class hierarchy [30], and work in visual
recognition trades accuracy against specificity or scores the severity of a mistake by its distance in the hierarchy [7, 14].
CrowdTruth instead keeps the spread of crowd annotations and weights each label by how strongly the crowd supports
it [4]. EW-F1 combines the two ideas for set-valued predictions: the taxonomy sets partial credit, and accumulated rater
evidence sets the weight of each reference label.
Holistic versus cue-level annotation.
Thin-slice research shows that brief behavioral observations support meaningful
interpersonal judgments and that holistic judgments can predict better than ratings of individual micro-behaviors [2, 3].
Cues do not map deterministically onto social signals, may differ between modalities, and are read by integrating
several cues with context, with substantial variability across situations and observers [6, 21]. Social Signal Bench
therefore asks trained raters for integrated judgments of perceived social signals, guided by operational definitions
and cue examples rather than cue checklists. This captures context-dependent interpretation but introduces observer
variability [55, 57], which we address with calibration, independent annotations with confidence ratings, and EW-F1.

Each item in Social Signal Bench is a short clip xi, either video with audio or audio only. Given a clip, a system
returns which of the taxonomy’s perceived social signals, such as hesitation or skepticism (ten for video, six for audio;
Section 3.1), are present, and a three-class engagement status (disengaged, neutral, engaged). An empty signal
set is a valid answer: many clips of natural conversation show no social signal above the labeling threshold. Signals
are scored with EW-F1 (Section 4), and the engagement status independently with three-class macro-F1, on which a
constant majority answer scores low (Supplement D.9). We evaluate clips under the natural class prevalence observed
in the annotated data. Balancing the set by removing negative examples would change this prevalence and was, in our
diagnostic, shown to inflate aggregate metrics (Supplement B).
3.1
Taxonomy
The taxonomy has twelve perceived social signals (Figure 3), describing what an observer perceives [42, 61]. The
signals were selected for their relevance to conversational feedback, initially in sales training, and each is defined from
standard psychological sources [59]. Ten signals form the multi-label signal head. Engagement and disengagement form
a separate three-class engagement head: we treat them as the two ends of one continuum of attention to the interaction
and the other ten signals as departures from that baseline. Raters annotate the two ends, and time marked as neither
counts as neutral. Signals that share observable cues and sit close together on affective and interpersonal dimensions,
such as alignment versus opposition and approach versus withdrawal, are grouped into clusters [41, 52]. A surjection
Cluster : L →C maps every signal to one of seven clusters. A symmetric, irreflexive relation Adj ⊆C×C marks which
cross-cluster confusions are plausible. {Agreement, Confidence, Engagement & Interest} form one adjacency triangle,
{Skepticism & Disagreement, Stress & Frustration, Uncertainty & Hesitation} form another, and Disengagement
is adjacent to none. In our annotation guide, adjacent clusters share more observable cues than non-adjacent ones;
Uncertainty & Hesitation and Skepticism & Disagreement share six.
3.2
Data sources and composition
Social Signal Bench draws on four conversational domains. Two are collections of naturalistic online conversational
videos. The third is recorded sales calls. The fourth is long-form dyadic conversations (about 40 minutes per session),
recorded over video conferencing with isolated audio and video tracks. These sessions follow a structured elicitation
protocol (Supplement B) that guides turns without scripting utterances, so disagreement, skepticism, hesitation, and
agreement arise and resolve during the session.
Confidence is the most frequent signal detected in online video and sales calls in our dataset, whereas uncertainty
leads in the dyadic conversations. Sales calls and dyadic conversations come from few, long recordings: of the 85
recordings in the Social Signal Bench Video test set, 75 are online video, 6 sales calls, and 4 dyadic conversations.
The two modalities are annotated independently, so their label distributions differ (Table 2, Figure 4). Both keep
naturally occurring empty-label clips instead of balancing signal prevalence, so models must recognize when no signal
is sufficiently supported.

Public training sets and private test sets.
Because the sales and dyadic recordings cannot be redistributed, the
public training sets contain only online video, and every released clip comes from a CC BY 3.0 licensed YouTube
video (Table 2). Their expert-annotated clips are selected from online-video recordings outside the test partitions; the
pseudo-labeled clips come from additional online videos, labeled by the video and audio teacher models, whose training
data exclude every test recording. On held-out expert-annotated clips, the teacher models score 54.8 EW-F1 [50.4,
61.0] on video and 65.4 [62.2, 69.1] on audio (Supplement G.1). The audio score is skewed by the concentration of
confidence labels in the evaluation set. The test partitions stay private to not enter web-scale pretraining data [53], cover
all four domains, and share no recording, channel, or participant with the public data.
3.3
Source-disjoint partitions
Annotator judgments are aggregated and clip boundaries optimized jointly by dynamic programming into 5–20 s
segments. Compared with sentence-based segmentation (64.5% label purity, the share of a segment’s annotated time
that carries its majority label set), this reaches 94.2–98.0% purity (Supplement B). To prevent speaker and context
leakage [28], the splits are recording-disjoint: the test sets share no recording, channel, or participant with the training
data, and are selected at the source level to balance label density, annotation evidence, and engagement (Supplement C).
3.4
Annotation
The expert raters for Social Signal Bench have at least a bachelor’s degree in behavioral science or social science
and work from a written guide that asks them to annotate a signal only when clear evidence is present. Every expert-
annotated clip, including every test clip, receives at least three independent annotations [54]. Crowd votes were added

Before production work, each rater labels calibration videos that an expert reviews individually. Each label spans at
least two seconds, may overlap other labels, and carries an intensity (1–5) and a confidence (Low, Medium, High). The
two ratings are independent: a subtle cue can be unambiguous, and a strong display can be ambiguous between signals.
When behavior sits between two signals, raters may apply both at low confidence.
Disagreement is measured against the taxonomy rather than by exact label match, using the same credit tiers as EW-F1
(Section 4): a vote for hesitation where peers labeled uncertainty is partially supported, whereas one where peers
labeled agreement is not. Only cluster-severe votes, those with no support in the same or an adjacent cluster, go to
review by an internal annotation expert. On an audit cohort, review leaves 68.7–93.4% of annotations unchanged
and moves cluster-weighted disagreement by at most 0.015 (Supplement Table S2). Residual disagreement concerns
temporal boundaries more than signal presence (Supplement A.2), which motivates clip-level evaluation. Inter-rater
agreement is lowest for interest (κ = 0.19), skepticism (0.23), stress (0.25), and uncertainty (0.33).
Reviewed votes are merged into time slices. For a signal that n trained raters assert in a slice, with mean confidence
weight ¯a (0.33, 0.66, and 1 for Low, Medium, and High), the slice’s evidence score is sj = 1−(1−¯a)n (Supplement A.3),
which rises with both confidence and repeated support. A reference signal’s evidence weight pg is the overlap-weighted
mean of sj over the slices that intersect the clip (Eq. 2). A signal marked with high confidence by every rater approaches
pg = 1, and a low-confidence label from one rater carries little mass. Figure 1 shows this merge on one training window.

Evidence-Weighted F1

ambiguous to human observers. Evidence-Weighted F1 (EW-F1) addresses these limitations by giving partial credit for
related signals and weighting reference labels by the strength of their annotation evidence.

Validating EW-F1
A new metric needs evidence that it behaves as intended and that its conclusions do not depend on hand-set parameters.
All analyses use the full 2,372-clip Social Signal Bench Video test set: perturbations need only its reference, and
parameter tests rescore the eight leaderboard systems of Section 6 (Supplement E). Perturbation scores use the 0–1
scale.
We start from a perfect prediction, the reference itself, and damage a fraction r of its labels in one of five ways. A
label is replaced by a signal from the same cluster, an adjacent cluster, or an unrelated cluster, or it is deleted, or an
out-of-taxonomy label is inserted next to it. Each setting is averaged over 20 random draws (Figure 6). EW-F1 orders
the three substitutions as the taxonomy intends, at every rate. At r = 0.5 it scores 0.903 for same-cluster, 0.812 for
adjacent, and 0.673 for unrelated substitutions. Exact F1 compresses the same three errors into a band of 0.039 (0.743,
0.716, 0.704) and ranks all of them below deletion (0.825). Exact F1 therefore teaches a system to withhold a near miss.
EW-F1 rewards a same-cluster guess over deletion (0.903 versus 0.826), scores an adjacent guess close to deletion
(0.812), and penalizes an unrelated guess more than deletion.
The tiers α = 0.66 and β = 0.33 are hand-set. We rescore the eight-system Social Signal Bench Video leaderboard on
the grid α ∈{0.5, 0.66, 0.8} × β ∈{0.17, 0.33, 0.5}. The post-trained model stays first and the three lowest systems
keep their ranks in all nine cells; only the four frontier models within 0.5 points of each other swap places, and no
score moves by more than 3.1 points. Graded credit also does not inflate scores: exact matches supply 89.2% of the
post-trained model’s earned credit (Supplement G.2).
Setting pg = 1 for every label leaves the post-trained model’s score unchanged (44.0), raises the other leaderboard
scores by up to 2.1 points, and reorders only the four frontier models within 0.5 points (Kendall’s τ = 0.57 [29]). The
weights thus settle near ties in favor of labels that human evidence supports; exact micro- and macro-F1 give τ = 0.71
and 0.57 (Supplement G.2).

Setup.
Within each modality, all systems receive identical test clips, prompts, output contracts, and scoring. A shared
harness gives label definitions, observable cues, abstention instructions, and a structured output format; models without
native video-with-audio input receive sampled frames and a transcript (Supplement F). Models are trained and evaluated
separately on Social Signal Bench Video (all ten signals and engagement status) and Social Signal Bench Audio, each
with independently collected annotations. Unlike Social Signal Bench Video, Social Signal Bench Audio focuses on
six social signals, excluding confusion, skepticism, stress, and disagreement. These four signals were insufficiently
represented by clear, identifiable instances in the audio-only annotations, making their reliable evaluation difficult.
In particular, some of these signals may depend on visual or broader conversational cues that are not consistently
recoverable from audio alone. For consistency, annotations of the four excluded signals are removed from the audio-only
test references before scoring. Consequently, evaluation is conducted separately within each modality, and scores
should not be interpreted as directly comparable across modalities.
On Social Signal Bench Video (Figure 7), the strongest closed and open-weight frontier models, Muse Spark 1.3 and
Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus, reach 40.9 and 40.6 EW-F1, five of the six frontier models lie between 39.6 and 40.9, and none
reaches 45.0 despite detailed task instructions. On Social Signal Bench Audio, with a different label set, Gemini 3.1 Pro
leads with 54.0, followed by Gemini 3.8 Flash at 51.0. The best open-weight audio baseline, Kimi-Audio-7B-Instruct,
reaches 29.8.
Task-specific training improves performance on both datasets. On Social Signal Bench Video, post-trained Qwen2.5-
Omni-7B reaches 44.0 EW-F1 [41.0, 47.0], +23.5 [+20.8, +25.8] over its untuned backbone and +3.2 [+0.8, +5.9]
over the strongest frontier model (paired, 85 recordings). Against each of the six frontier models, the paired difference
ranges from +3.2 to +8.8, and every interval excludes zero. It ranks first under EW-F1 and exact micro-F1 and third
under exact macro-F1 (Supplement G.2). By setting, it scores 43.7 on online video, 40.8 on sales calls, and 47.5
on dyadic conversations; the last two rest on 6 and 4 recordings (Supplement G.2). On Social Signal Bench Audio,
post-trained Qwen2-Audio-7B-Instruct reaches 58.1 EW-F1 [49.0, 62.1], +29.3 [+22.8, +33.3] over its untuned
backbone and +4.1 [−0.1, +6.1] over the strongest frontier model (paired, recording-level); this interval includes zero,
so on audio the post-trained model matches the strongest frontier model rather than clearly exceeding it. It ranks first on
audio under EW-F1, exact micro- and macro-F1, and uniform evidence weights.
On the engagement head, the post-trained video model reaches 58.4 macro-F1, against at most 43.0 for frontier models
and 20.7 for its untuned backbone; the audio model reaches 45.8, against at most 47.2.
On all 2,372 video test clips, muting the audio lowers post-trained Qwen2.5-Omni-7B from 44.0 to 22.1 EW-F1 (paired
−22.0 [−25.7, −16.9]) and black frames lower it to 15.4 (−28.7 [−31.9, −24.2]); engagement F1 survives muting
(63.5) but not black frames (14.1). Both inputs are out of distribution for the model (Supplement G.3).
Human raters.
We also score individual trained raters against the frozen reference clips. Unlike during annotation,
they see only the clip the models receive. On video, five raters reach a mean EW-F1 of 38.9 [34.9, 42.6] on 313 test
clips (36 recordings). On audio, four raters who hear only the audio of 391 Social Signal Bench Video test clips (40
recordings) reach 40.6 [36.8, 44.5]. Video rater scores fall as clips carry more signals, while audio rater scores are
highest on single-signal clips. On clips with no reference signal, raters select nothing in only 47.4% (video) and 32.2%
(audio) of cases (Supplement H). Because the reference pools several raters, an individual rater can score below a
frontier model; we report the rater mean as one human perspective, not as a ceiling, and do not rank it against models.

7
Discussion and Limitations
Social Signal Bench labels reflect perceptions of trained expert observers rather than clinical or ground-truth mental
states. A high score therefore indicates agreement with human judgments, not necessarily access to a speaker’s
underlying intentions or emotions. Human baseline performance provides an important reference in that even human
observers do not perfectly recover the annotated signals from isolated clips, highlighting the ambiguity of social
perception and the role of conversational context. Raters annotated with the surrounding conversation in view, whereas
systems and the human baseline see isolated clips, so some reference labels may not be recoverable from the clip
alone. Consequently, the gap between model and human performance should be interpreted alongside this difference in
available context. EW-F1 further reflects the strength of positive annotation evidence rather than consensus: weights
increase with the number and confidence of covering spans but do not penalize raters who omit a signal (Supplement D.2).
Thus, missing a weakly supported signal carries less cost than missing one supported repeatedly with high confidence.
Limitations.
Social signals are often subtle, overlapping and dependent on context, making their interpretation
inherently subjective. The two modalities also differ in their observable evidence and label coverage, so their scores
are not directly comparable. The credit tiers are versioned parameters, and rankings are stable across the tested values
(Section 5). Whether taxonomy-defined severities match human judgments of perceptual similarity remains unvalidated
(Supplement E). The parameter analyses use only the Social Signal Bench Video test set, and model results come from
single runs without seed variance. All four domains are English-language conversation with one clearly visible speaker,
limiting generalization to multilingual and multi-party interactions. The dyadic and sales domains contain few test
recordings (4 and 6 in Social Signal Bench Video), resulting in wide per-domain intervals. Within a test set, grouping
by recording does not establish independence across participants appearing in multiple sessions.
8
Ethics and Data Access
Social Signal Bench contains identifiable faces, voices, and opinions. To respect consent and redistribution restrictions,
only CC BY 3.0 online video is included in the public training sets, with 10 hours of expert annotations per modality.
Annotations were collected through a structured internship program for behavioral- and social-science students
(approximately 12 hours per week over six-month terms), including calibration and expert feedback.
Automated social perception may support coaching and interaction research but also poses risks in surveillance,
hiring, and workplace monitoring. Release terms prohibit non-consensual surveillance and high-stakes decisions about
individuals. Deployment should require informed consent, subgroup evaluation, and human oversight.
9
Conclusion
Social Signal Bench measures whether audiovisual and audio-only models perceive co-occurring social signals as
trained experts do. It keeps annotator disagreement as graded evidence, enforces recording-disjoint partitions, and
scores models with EW-F1, which credits related predictions in proportion to human support. Frontier models reach at
most 40.9 (video) and 54.0 (audio) EW-F1, and 7B open models post-trained on the benchmark surpass or match the
strongest of them on the benchmark.
LLM Usage Statement
The authors designed and ran the study, collected the annotations, and wrote the paper; LLM-based assistants were used
to edit, rewrite, and reformat text, LATEX and figure code, and analysis scripts, and the authors checked all citations
against publisher records. The pseudo-labels in the public training sets were produced by teacher models that are
fine-tuned multimodal LLMs (Section 3). No LLM produced any label in the expert reference or the test sets.

SUPPLEMENTARY MATERIAL
The sections of this supplementary material follow the order of the main paper: how the reference is built (Sections A–C),
the EW-F1 metric and its validation (Sections D–E), and the evaluation (Sections F–H).
A
Labeling
Figure S1 summarizes how independent rater spans become the reference.
A.1
Annotation protocol
Annotators label a signal only when it is clearly observable, and they are told that a false positive is worse than a gap.
Annotation is restricted to English speech and to a single person who is clearly visible and speaking; static background
people who do not interfere are the only exception. Each span lasts at least two seconds, and each judgment uses both
the visual and the audio channel. Several labels may be active over the same span, because social signals often co-occur
and a single label per moment would misrepresent them.
Each label carries two ratings. Intensity (1–5) records how strongly the signal is displayed, and confidence (Low,
Medium, High) records how certain the annotator is that the label is correct. The two ratings are independent: a
high-arousal behavior can carry low confidence when its cause is ambiguous between signals, and a subtle cue can carry
high confidence when it is unambiguous. When a behavior sits between two signals, annotators may apply both at low
confidence. Low confidence still requires an observed behavior that is attributable to at least one of the twelve signals; if
nothing in the taxonomy applies, no label is applied. Intensity is collected but not yet used in the disagreement, review,
or ground-truth computations below (Section A.4).
A.2
Disagreement and review
All disagreement measures share one temporal discretization. Spans are binned into one-second intervals, and a label is
active in every bin that its span covers. When two overlapping spans of the same label from the same annotator fall in
one bin, the higher confidence is kept. Confidence maps to the weights Low = 0.33, Medium = 0.66, and High = 1.00
throughout.
Signals are grouped into seven clusters (Agreement; Confidence; Disengagement; Engagement & Interest; Skepticism

Dexact is defined in the same way with exact-match support. By default we report the exact pairwise measure
(“Disagreement Score”) and the cluster-aware, confidence-weighted support measure (“Cluster-weighted Disagreement
Score”). All scores lie in [0, 1] and are reported in fixed bands: Low (0 < x ≤0.35), Medium (≤0.51), High (≤0.70),
and Severe (> 0.70). The thresholds are the same for every variant and are not calibrated to the observed distribution.
We also report pairwise Cohen’s κ per signal, computed on the same bins by treating membership in an annotator’s
label set as a binary indicator and pooling a 2×2 confusion matrix over all shared bins. Unlike the taxonomy-aware
scores, κ is a standard chance-corrected reliability statistic.
Review is triggered by cluster-severe votes, those with no support in the same or an adjacent cluster (main paper
Section 3.4). An expert reviewer inspects the flagged clips and may remove a span, add a missing label, relabel a span,
or adjust its confidence or temporal boundaries. The aim is to correct clear errors and add missing evidence, not to drive
disagreement toward zero. Social signals are fuzzy and depend on context, and a fixed cue catalogue captures them only
in part. Disagreement that survives review is therefore not read as a process failure, and a reviewed label is not treated
as the single correct reading.

recordings from online video and dyadic conversations. Review is conservative. It leaves 88.0–93.4% of audiovisual

A.4
Limitations of the labels
Annotated videos run from roughly thirty seconds to three minutes, and annotators view the whole video, whereas
a model sees only its clip. Labels are therefore made with more context than the model receives. The number of
annotators per clip differs between batches (three in some, four in others), and the disagreement and reliability measures
do not correct for this, so per-vote support is not comparable in expectation across clips with different annotator counts.
The similarity weights (1.0/0.66/0.33/0.0) used in disagreement scoring and in the crowd branch for Online video A are
set by hand and have not been validated against independent reviewer judgment; they do not enter the trained-annotator
formula. In early cycles, one person could annotate both the audio and the video task of a batch. The merge treats
each export as an independent annotator, so such pairs can inflate repeated support in Eq. (7); later cycles restrict each
annotator to one modality per batch. Review covers only cluster-severe votes, so most annotations receive no second
pass and disagreement below that threshold is not audited. A single primary reviewer adjudicates, and reviewer-specific
effects cannot be separated from disagreement between annotators. Intensity ratings are collected but not used in any
disagreement, review, or ground-truth computation. Finally, annotators are internship-program participants who work
twelve hours a week over a six-month term. No formal IRB or equivalent ethics review was sought, because the work
was treated as an internal work task rather than a human-subjects research protocol; sociodemographic data are collected
anonymously.

C
Test-Set Selection
The private test sets are chosen at the level of source recordings. Only reference annotations enter the selection; model
predictions and scores are not inputs, and each assignment was frozen before any model was trained on the remaining
data.
The unit of assignment is a source group, and every clip of a group goes to the same split. Group identity comes from
three independent sources of evidence: the platform video ID, the SHA-256 hash of the parent media, and the parent
file name after removal of chunk, window, and clip suffixes. Clips whose parent hashes agree must share a group, even
when their chunk URIs differ. Contradictory evidence, such as two video IDs for one file, stops the build. A dyadic
conversation is one group, not one group per derived segment.
A mixed-integer program over source groups selects the test sets. Its hard constraints require at least one group from
each of the four domains, at least 20 groups with each signal, at least 15 groups with disengaged clips, and no group with
more than 350 clips. The objective is a weighted sum of absolute deviations from proportional targets for clips, groups,
clips per domain, positive groups per signal, signal instances, empty clips, label density, evidence mass, engagement
states, and confidence tiers, each divided by its target and by the number of terms of its kind. The CBC solver runs
single-threaded with a relative gap of 0.002 under several fixed seeds. We keep the lowest-objective candidate that
passes an independent re-audit of every hard constraint, store the assignment as a hashed manifest, and replay later
views from it without solving again.
Table S4 summarizes both test sets. Every video signal has at least 21 positive recordings. In the audio test set,
Confidence, Hesitation, and Uncertainty have 24–42 positive recordings, and Agreement, Interest, and Frustration have
17–19. Disengaged clips come from 16 video and 3 audio recordings.
The test sets stay private and are used only through hosted evaluation. Every submission is scored with the same
prediction schema, the same versioned EW-F1 scorer, and the same references. The public leaderboard reports aggregate
scores without exposing individual test annotations. Submissions are rate-limited and results are versioned to limit
overfitting to the test sets. Each run is recorded in a manifest with the split hash, taxonomy version, model identifier,
prompt and parser versions, decoding parameters, and raw predictions.

D
EW-F1: Full Derivation
This section derives EW-F1 step by step, proves its properties, gives the exact algorithm, and states its limits. The
proofs hold for any reference.
D.1
Notation
A clip is one scored unit, and several clips can come from one parent recording, which is usually 60 seconds long. For
one clip, G is the set of reference signals and Q the set of predicted signals. Both hold distinct names. The letters n
and m count parent recordings, never signals. Expert spans come from trained raters, and crowd spans from the crowd
raters of the first annotation cycle (Section A.3).
D.2
The two inputs
The first input is the match credit c(q, g) ∈{0, β, α, 1}, which grades how close a prediction q is to a reference signal g
(main-paper Eq. 1). The credit is symmetric, and the released values are α = 0.66 and β = 0.33.
The second input is the evidence weight. The merge step cuts each recording into time slices at every span boundary.

Properties
Proposition 1 (Range). 0 ≤EW-F1 ≤1, and a system that predicts exactly the reference set of every clip scores 1.
Proof. Since c ≤1 and each g is paired at most once, S(A) ≤P

For the exact system, pair each prediction with the identical reference signal. Then c = 1 for every pair and
S = N ′ = W.
Proposition 2 (No gain from removing a relation). Fix Q and G. If E′ ⊆E are two sets of allowed pairs, then
EW-F1(E′) ≤EW-F1(E).
Proof. F(A) depends only on A. Every assignment feasible under E′ is feasible under E, and a maximum over a
subset cannot exceed the maximum over the whole set.
Proposition 3 (Tie-breaks do not matter). All maximizing assignments report the same value.
Proof. The reported number is the maximum value, not a property of one maximizer.
Proposition 4 (Cost of a wrong extra label). An added prediction that pairs with nothing adds 1 to N ′ and 0 to S, so it
strictly lowers the score whenever S > 0.
Proposition 5 (When a label is worth predicting). Let F be the present score. Add one prediction that pairs with a free
reference signal at credit c and weight p, with the other pairs fixed. The score rises if and only if c > F/2.
Proof. The new value is 2(S + cp)/(N ′ + p + W). It exceeds 2S/(N ′ + W) if and only if cp(N ′ + W) > Sp, that is,
c > S/(N ′ + W) = F/2.
The condition does not contain p, so a system has no reason to withhold a weakly supported label. Proposition 2 holds
for a fixed prediction set. Adding a prediction is a different operation, because it also adds denominator mass, and it can
lower the score.

D.6
Why the score must be a maximum
We considered three simpler rules. Each fixes one assignment by some criterion and then reports a different quantity. A
system can then gain by changing the input to the rule instead of finding signals.
• Maximize total credit, then report the ratio. Adding a correct label can change which assignment wins and
lower the reported ratio, so a system can gain by withholding a correct label. It is a defect of the definition,
and a system can target it.
• Maximize credit only, ignoring evidence weight. Removing a partial-credit relation can raise the score,
which violates Proposition 2.
• Count predictions in the denominator, 2S/(|Q| + W), instead of matched weights. A perfect system then
scores below 1 whenever some pg < 1, and dropping weakly supported reference labels from its output
can raise this score, while EW-F1 falls. This form rewards withholding weakly supported labels, which
Proposition 5 rules out for EW-F1.
EW-F1 removes the common cause: the score is the maximum of the quantity it reports. Propositions 2 and 3 are then
theorems, not repairs.

an order of magnitude. A half-width of ±0.05 on one signal needs about 142 recordings, and ±0.03 needs about 388.

D.10
Limits of the metric
1. Retained labels only. The merge drops crowd spans with no related expert span in the slice, and runs of one
signal shorter than 2.0 seconds. EW-F1 therefore measures performance on labels that pass this gate, not
robustness to all annotation uncertainty. The distribution of pg is truncated from below. That is a bias, not a
variance, so no interval corrects it.
2. Volume, not agreement. The weight accumulates positive evidence and has no term for a rater who did not
mark the signal.

3. Optimistic alignment. The score takes the best allowed assignment. It cannot invent credit, because c and p
are fixed before scoring.
4. Hand-set credit tiers. The values α = 0.66 and β = 0.33 encode the taxonomy. Leaderboard rankings are
stable over a 3 × 3 grid (Supplement G.2), but a human-severity study has not yet calibrated the values.
5. Rare classes. The minority engagement class and most single signals are below the estimability floor on small
sets. Per-signal intervals are wide (Table S7).
E
Metric Validation Details
All analyses in this section use Social Signal Bench Video; the same procedure applies to Social Signal Bench Audio.
Perturbations use the 2,372-clip Social Signal Bench Video test reference, which has 2,016 reference signals. Of these,
1,209 are eligible (see below); agreement, confidence, and interest have no same-cluster alternative, so their labels stay
unchanged in every setting. The credit-tier and evidence-weight analyses rescore the eight Social Signal Bench Video
leaderboard systems on the same clips (Supplement G.2). All scores use the pinned release of the EW-F1 scorer. The
analysis scripts and their full output are part of the release (evaluation_runs/metric_validation/).
A reference label is eligible for perturbation when it has same-cluster, adjacent, and unrelated alternatives, so all five
perturbation types act on the same label population. Each eligible label is perturbed independently with probability r. A
substitution draws its replacement uniformly from the alternatives of the requested relation that are not already present.
Insertion adds the out-of-taxonomy label boredom. Exact F1 is computed with the same matcher, with unit weights and
α = β = 0.
Insertion behaves differently from the other perturbations. An inserted out-of-taxonomy label earns nothing and adds
a full unit to N ′(A), whereas a deletion removes at most pg ≤1 from both credit and prediction mass. At low rates
an insertion therefore costs more than a deletion, and the two curves cross between r = 0.2 and 0.3. At higher rates,
insertion is the least damaging perturbation because the reference label is kept. This is the intended behavior: EW-F1
penalizes a spurious label but does not count it as a miss.
The credit tiers still need a human check. We will sample prediction–truth confusions stratified by credit tier and show
them to raters who do not see the metric’s assignment. Raters grade the severity of each error on a fixed scale, or choose
the worse of two confusions, and a rank correlation with a pre-registered acceptance threshold compares the human
ordering with the ordering implied by the metric. This is the calibration that the main paper lists as pending (Section 7).
F
Evaluation Harness
Every system on the leaderboard, including each frontier baseline, receives the same engineered harness rather than a
generic instruction. It took several iterations of prompt design and output-contract hardening, and it carries much of
the task knowledge that a trained model acquires from data. It has three parts: a signal catalog, decision rules, and an
output contract.
The signal catalog is a system prompt of about 1,400 words that defines all twelve entries (ten scored signals plus
engagement and disengagement). Each entry has a one-sentence definition and a list of observable cues, 70 cues in
total, that name verbal, nonverbal, and prosodic evidence. The audio version of the catalog keeps only audio cues. A
typical entry reads:
Hesitation. A temporary disruption in speaking or responding to a question. Observable cues: the person delays
or softens their answer; fills the gap with “uh/um” or restarts a phrase; looks away briefly before continuing;
shows difficulty getting the words out; their last word stretches unnaturally long or drops into creaky voice.
The decision rules separate the engagement decision from the signal list. They tell the model to focus on the primary
speaker, to treat the clip as one unit, to use only observable evidence, and to omit weak, ambiguous, or merely possible
labels; an empty signal list is a valid answer.
The output contract fixes the JSON schema, requires catalog names verbatim, and gives two worked examples, one with
signals and one with none. The parser accepts case and whitespace variants of signal names. Each run records parse
validity, so formatting failures are reported separately.
We also tested automatic prompt search with GEPA [1], a reflective evolutionary prompt optimizer, through DSPy, on
an earlier fine-tuned model. A reflection model read failed predictions and proposed edits to the instruction text around
the signal catalog. The catalog and the output contract stayed fixed. Candidates were scored by signal-name F1 on 85
clips, 49 for search and 36 for selection. Most were hard cases: clips without audio, clips whose speaker is off camera,
21

and clips with no reference signal. The best of nine candidates raised F1 on easy clips but did not make the model
abstain when the speaker was off camera, where it usually predicted Hesitation from pauses or gaze shifts. We therefore
used the search only to diagnose failure modes; the final rules were written and fixed by hand.
All systems receive the same clips and the same user turn. Gemini 4 Argon, Gemini 3.8 Flash, and Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus
take the video with its audio track natively. GPT-6.1 Sol and Gemma-4-31B-it receive 16 frames sampled at 1 fps with a
transcript, and Muse Spark 1.3 receives the video with a transcript instead of the audio track. Outputs are scored by one
evaluator from the unaggregated predictions.
The harness matters for the comparison. A frontier model given only the label names and a request for JSON must
infer what each signal looks like, where the boundary between adjacent signals lies, and when to abstain. The harness
answers these questions in the prompt. Frontier scores in the leaderboard therefore reflect the models under favorable
conditions, and we expect scores under a minimal prompt to be lower. The gap between a trained model and a harnessed
frontier model is thus a conservative estimate of the gap to frontier models as they are typically used.

Post-trained models
Qwen2.5-Omni-7B is post-trained with supervised fine-tuning on the internal Social Signal Bench Video training

Modality ablation and probes
Figure S2 evaluates the post-trained checkpoint on all 2,372 test clips with one stream removed, using the leaderboard
inference settings. Video only keeps the video stream unchanged and replaces the audio with a silent track; audio only
keeps the audio and replaces every frame with a black frame of the same size, rate and duration. All outputs parse. With
black frames the model abstains on most clips (92% of predictions contain no signal), so the audio-only score reflects
the trained model’s behavior without visual input rather than an upper bound on what the audio carries.
Frozen linear probes show where signal information is readable inside the trained models. Probe training, selection, and
test use different partitions. On the 2,372 audiovisual test clips, macro AP over the ten signals is 17.0 (Qwen) and 17.7
(Gemma) from pooled audio features, 15.3 and 14.1 from concatenated audio and video, and 24.2 and 23.6 from the
decoder state at the last prompt token. The decoder exceeds the best unimodal readout by +7.2 [4.2, 10.0] and +5.8
[3.5, 8.7] points (500 paired recording-level bootstrap draws). Parameter-matched nonlinear heads on pooled audio
exceed those on pooled video by 5.3 and 4.0 points. Pooling can discard brief visual cues, so this result bounds what
pooled features expose. It does not show that vision is uninformative.
The audio-only test clips show a similar pattern. Macro AP over the six signals is 44.3 from pooled audio-encoder
features and 48.4 from pooled decoder states at audio-token positions, against 53.8 from the decoder state at the
last prompt token. The last-token readout exceeds the best pooled readout by +5.4 [1.3, 9.5] points (500 paired
recording-level bootstrap draws). Parameter-matched nonlinear heads (about 1M parameters) give the same ordering,

bounds what pooled features expose. It does not show that the pooled encoder carries no signal information.

H.1
Audiovisual raters
Five annotators provided 1,937 deduplicated judgments on 452 test clips, and all five labeled the same 313 clips from 36
parent videos. We score each person’s selections against the frozen test reference with canonical EW-F1 and average the
five corpus scores with equal weight. An omitted signal field means that no signal was selected; a missing annotation
row is not imputed. The mean on the shared clips is 38.9 EW-F1, with a 95% interval of [34.9, 42.6] from 2,000 draws

PREPRINT
of joint parent-video-grouped subsampling. This estimates individual performance; it is not a score for pooled human
predictions.
Figure S3 (left) stratifies the shared clips by the number of reference social signals, excluding engagement. Within each
stratum, intervals use 2,000 joint parent-video percentile bootstrap draws (seed 42); they keep all rater judgments and
score repeated clip copies separately, so they differ from the grouped-subsampling interval of the overall estimate. The
intervals condition on the five observed raters, the frozen reference, and the observed coverage, and do not describe
uncertainty over a wider annotator population.
Clips without a reference signal need a separate score. On a corpus whose references are all empty, the scorer returns
EW-F1 of 100 only if every prediction is empty, and 0 otherwise. Each annotator predicts at least one signal on these 81
clips, so each scores 0, which hides partial success. We therefore report correct empty-selection accuracy: its mean is
47.4% [41.1%, 54.2%], the complement of the 52.6% [45.8%, 58.9%] rate of predicting any signal. Overall EW-F1
includes empty-reference clips. There, unmatched predictions add prediction cost without earning credit, while correct
empty selections add neither credit nor cost. Overall EW-F1 is thus sensitive to background false positives but does not
measure empty-class accuracy.
Leaving out one parent video at a time gives mean EW-F1 ranges of 38.4–45.2 for 3–4 signals and 28.1–36.4 for
5+ signals, with maximum absolute shifts of 4.3 and 4.4 points. The decline with density is descriptive: no paired
or multiplicity-adjusted trend test establishes a difficulty ordering. Density is not annotatability, and clips with
empty references are not necessarily unsuitable. Three further factors limit interpretation: complete-case selection,
the difference between full-video reference construction and clip-level tagging, and the unestablished independence
between reference creators and these raters. A test-selected, video-blind constant prediction scores 39.1 on the same
clips. It is an exploratory control for the metric scale, not a held-out baseline, and individual raters do not beat this prior
tuned to the subset. The results therefore set neither a human ceiling nor a ranking against models. Such a comparison
needs aligned predictions on the same clips with paired parent-video uncertainty, and development-set model scores
cannot be compared with this test-subset estimate. Stratum scores also cannot be clip-weighted to recover the overall
corpus EW-F1.
H.2
Audio raters
Five trained raters labeled the audio track of clips from the Social Signal Bench Video test partition, hearing only the
clip audio and choosing among the six audio signals and engagement status. We score them against the frozen test
reference for these clips, which was annotated with video visible. Following the audio protocol, the four non-audio
signals are removed from the reference and clips whose reference held only those signals are dropped. Skipped tasks
are not imputed. One rater stopped using the signal field after 46 clips and is excluded. The remaining four share 391
clips from 40 parent recordings. Their mean EW-F1 is 40.6, with a 95% joint parent-recording grouped-subsampling
interval of [36.8, 44.5] from 2,000 draws (individual raters 30.7–44.5). Figure S3 (right) stratifies these clips in the same
way; only one clip carries five or more audio signals, so strata of three or more are merged. Correct empty selection
varies widely across raters (1.3%–83.3%), so the zero-signal point mainly reflects how readily each rater marks a signal.
Leaving out one parent recording at a time gives 46.8–50.1 for the 3+ stratum (maximum shift 2.0 points). The best
test-selected constant rule, confidence and hesitation on every clip, scores 36.2. The rater mean exceeds it by 4.4 [−0.2,
+8.1] (joint grouped subsampling, not adjusted for the rule search), so audio raters also do not clearly beat a prior tuned
to this subset.
