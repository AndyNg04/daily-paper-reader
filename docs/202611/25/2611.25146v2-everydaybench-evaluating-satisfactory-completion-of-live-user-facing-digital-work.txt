Everyday digital tasks, from selecting products and planning trips to conducting research and creating
artifacts, require AI agents to deliver useful outcomes rather than merely retrieve web information. Yet
evaluating these outcomes on the live web is challenging, as a single request may admit multiple valid
results, and changing web information can alter which results remain valid over time. Although existing
benchmarks address aspects of this setting, they do not fully integrate predefined success criteria, current
evidence verification, and deliverable-quality assessment for agent-selected outcomes. To address this gap,
we introduce EverydayBench, a benchmark of 77 human-authored tasks spanning eight domains of
everyday life and work. Each task specifies success criteria before execution without prescribing an answer
or workflow, allowing agents to select their own outcomes. To evaluate submissions under fixed success
criteria, EverydayBench combines (1) deterministic checks of explicit constraints, (2) verification of claims
against agent-cited webpages retrieved at evaluation time, and (3) rubric-based assessment of final deliverable
quality. We further validate the protocol through human judgments, repeated scoring under fixed evidence,
and controlled grounding failures. Across 23 reported agent–model configurations, Claude Opus 5.5 and
Claude Fable 5.1 tie for the highest strict pass rate at 54.55%; a controlled GPT-6 Sol effort sweep ranges from
29.87% at low effort to 45.45% at max effort. Reliability varies substantially by task type: GPT-5.6 Sol leads
lookup, Fable 5.1 leads planning, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 tie on comparison, and GPT-6 Astra and Sonnet
5.5 tie on customized deliverables. Together, these findings highlight the difficulty of delivering complete,
well-supported, and usable outcomes for everyday user requests.

1. Introduction
AI agents are increasingly being deployed across a wide range of tasks, including everyday digital
work such as comparing products, planning trips, gathering information, and preparing user-facing
artifacts [29, 31, 17, 9, 28, 25, 20, 10]. In these settings, performing relevant actions or retrieving useful
information does not necessarily mean that the task has been completed satisfactorily. For example, a
comparison may omit a requested alternative, a recommendation may rely on unsupported claims, or a
travel itinerary may leave out essential logistical details. Such outputs may appear complete while leaving
important parts of the user’s request unresolved. These shortcomings raise a fundamental question for
agent evaluation: “when should an agent’s delivered work count as completed?”
We study this question through satisfactory task completion, an outcome-level assessment of whether
an agent’s delivered work adequately fulfills a delegated request. Under this standard, an outcome should

provides an operational target for evaluating an agent’s work against a task-specific completion standard.
Evaluating satisfactory completion is challenging because everyday requests may admit multiple acceptable
outcomes, whose validity can also depend on changing external information. Consider the request in
Figure 1: find a bag under $75 suitable for full-day backpacking. Different products may satisfy these
requirements, while changes in price or availability can alter which recommendations remain valid. In
this setting, a predetermined reference answer may overlook valid alternatives, while a single overall
quality judgment may not reveal specific shortcomings, such as violated constraints, unsupported claims,
or missing deliverable components. Therefore, evaluating satisfactory completion requires assessing the
agent’s selected outcome against task-specific requirements and supporting evidence, without prescribing
the outcome in advance.
Existing agent benchmarks adopt different evaluation designs to make task completion measurable
and reproducible. Mind2Web evaluates actions against recorded demonstrations, while WebArena and
OSWorld assess task completion in reproducible environments through executable checks [6, 31, 26].
Other benchmarks evaluate realistic user requests against reference answers or use task-specific programs
to verify final outputs, often favoring relatively stable information for reliable evaluation [29, 17, 25, 23].
These approaches provide verifiable measures of success, but do not necessarily assess whether agent-
selected outcomes remain valid as external information changes. Meanwhile, Odysseys evaluates live-web
workflows through graded rubric checkpoints [11], while ResearchRubrics and LiveResearchBench use
fine-grained criteria to assess open-ended, evidence-backed research outputs [21, 24]. Building on
these approaches, we focus on assessing whether diverse, agent-selected outcomes satisfy predefined
completion criteria, are supported by current web evidence, and constitute usable deliverables.

To study satisfactory task completion, we introduce EverydayBench, a benchmark of 77 human-
authored tasks spanning eight domains of everyday life and work. Our central design principle is to fix
the standard of completion, not the solution. Each task pairs a natural-language user request with a
task-specific success specification established before agent execution. The specification includes explicit
task requirements and anchored component rubrics for assessing whether the resulting work serves its
intended purpose, without enumerating acceptable outcomes. Agents remain free to select their own
sources, workflows, and concrete results within the task’s stated constraints and deliverable requirements.
To evaluate satisfactory task completion, EverydayBench uses an evidence-informed, component-level
assessment. We first perform deterministic checks of explicit task requirements and claim-grounding
checks that verify submitted claims against agent-cited webpages retrieved at evaluation time [18]. The
resulting findings, together with the submitted outcome and task-specific anchored rubrics, are provided
to a component-level judge that assesses each aspect of the deliverable. Component scores are then
aggregated to obtain an overall assessment of task completion. We evaluate the reliability of grounding
and component scoring against independent human annotations [30, 16], and further examine evaluator
consistency through repeated scoring and controlled grounding failures.
We evaluate 23 agent configurations across all 77 tasks in EverydayBench. Claude Code with Opus
5.5 and Fable 5.1 tie for the highest strict pass rate at 54.55%, while only 32.30% of all agent–task runs
meet the benchmark’s completion standard. Failure analysis shows that research accounts for the most
deductions even for the high-scoring configurations, often because a claim says more than the page it
cites, while low-cost configurations lose more points in every category, most of all through output files
that are missing or violate the required format and through user needs that the model does not look into.
These findings reveal a gap between producing plausible task-related information and delivering work
that is complete, usable, and adequately supported.
2. Related Work
AI agent benchmarks.
Existing benchmarks increasingly capture realistic tasks that users may delegate
to AI agents, but differ in how they define and evaluate successful completion. AgentBench evaluates
agents across diverse interactive environments, while GAIA and AssistantBench evaluate answers to real-
world requests against collected references [15, 17, 29]. Mind2Web evaluates action predictions against
recorded demonstrations, whereas WebArena and OSWorld use reproducible environments and executable
checks of task completion [6, 31, 26]. More recent benchmarks expand the scope of realistic agent tasks.
MCP-Atlas and Toolathlon evaluate multi-step tasks across tool and application ecosystems using claims-
based scoring and executable checks, respectively [4, 14]. CocoaBench allows flexible tool composition
and evaluates final outputs using task-specific programs, while favoring stable external dependencies for
reproducibility [23]. Online-Mind2Web and Odysseys extend agent evaluation to live websites, using
trajectory-level judgments and graded task rubrics [27, 11]. In contrast, EverydayBench focuses on
evaluating agent-selected, user-facing outcomes whose validity depends on current web information,
without prescribing a particular answer or execution workflow. A more comprehensive comparison is
provided in Table 1.
Open-ended outcome evaluation.
Beyond action-level evaluation, prior work has developed methods
for assessing complex outputs that admit multiple valid solutions. SWE-bench evaluates software patches
through executable tests, while PaperBench uses author-validated hierarchical rubrics to assess research
replication [13, 22]. Agent-as-a-Judge introduces agentic evaluation of AI development tasks against
hierarchical user requirements [32]. For web-grounded research, Mind2Web 2 assesses answer correctness
and supporting sources through agentic judges, while ResearchRubrics uses expert-defined criteria to
evaluate evidence-backed research outputs [8, 21]. LiveResearchBench further evaluates dynamic,

3. EverydayBench
3.1. Benchmark Tasks
Task scope and composition.
EverydayBench comprises 77 human-authored tasks framed as com-
plete user-facing goals across eight application domains: consumer products and shopping; travel and
local services; food, culture and entertainment; research; education and careers; business; health and
public affairs; software and data work. We classify tasks by their primary goal: lookup and extraction,
comparison and selection, planning, analysis and audit, and deliverables and tools. Tasks may combine
several activities; the primary goal captures what the user ultimately requests rather than every step
needed to finish it.
Task validation.
For each task, we manually reviewed the instruction and evaluation specification
alongside pilot runs. We checked that the instruction clearly stated the user’s goal and deliverables, that

the success criteria captured the principal requirements, and that the structured outcome schema could
express the information needed to assess them. We ran task-specific direct tests on both positive and
negative cases, and checked whether grounding recovered task-relevant content from web pages captured
during execution. We also reviewed whether the rubric covered the success criteria, evidence assembled
across sources, and qualitative aspects of the deliverable. These checks assessed whether each task could
be represented and evaluated.
3.2. Open-Outcome Evaluation
Success specification.
We decompose each user request into success criteria: conditions that an
acceptable outcome must satisfy, without requiring a match to a fixed reference answer. Each user request
has 7.6 success criteria on average. We also provide an agent-visible outcome schema specifying how
agents submit the information needed to check those conditions. For example, a studio-rental task lets
agents search public listings and recommend one to three properties. Its success criteria include “every
selected effective monthly cost and move-in cash value is exactly recomputed and satisfies its cap” and “each
claimed candidate rent quote and availability date is supported by its property-controlled exact listing page.”
Different sets of recommended properties can satisfy the same success criteria.
Criterion verification.
We evaluate each criterion through one of three routes, according to what must
be judged: (1) deterministic direct tests handle deterministic properties of agent’s submission, such as
arithmetic, cross-field consistency, and artifact structure. (2) source-grounded checks determine whether
submitted factual claims are supported by the exact web pages the agent cites; an inaccessible page yields
an unverified finding rather than a factual rejection. And (3) rubric-based component scoring measures
qualitative judgment or combined evidence. In the rental task above, for example, the cost calculation is
checked directly, rent and availability claims are grounded in listing pages, and recommendation quality
is assessed through the corresponding rubric component.
Component scoring.
Each task is divided into a set of predefined outcome components, each scored on
a five-point scale. For every component, we provide rubric descriptions for scores of 1, 3, and 5: a score
of 1 indicates that the component is missing or substantially unsatisfied; a score of 3 indicates partial
fulfillment; and a score of 5 specifies what full and satisfactory fulfillment requires. Figure 1 shows three
representative examples of score-5 anchors. Scores of 2 and 4 represent performance between adjacent
anchors. At evaluation time, the component-scoring judge applies these rubrics to the submitted outcome
and deliverable, together with the findings from deterministic direct test and source-grounded checks.
The rubrics capture qualities of agent’s submitted outcome where individual direct test or grounded checks
cannot fully assess, such as whether evidence from several sources supports a coherent recommendation
and whether the final deliverable makes the trade-offs usable for the requester. The judge scores each
component from 1 to 5; an output that violates the required output format scores 0 on every component.
We combine these component scores using a weighted average, then scale the result to [0, 1] to obtain
the task-level reward.
3.3. Curation Process
Figure 2 contrasts conventional fixed-answer or holistic evaluation with our success-criteria curation.
Each task is curated by at least three curators, who reach consensus at every step. First, they derive
success criteria from the user’s explicit requirements and from the implicit requirements the user would
reasonably expect but does not state (e.g., a user relocating from Florida to Minnesota would need
guidance on preparing for cold weather). Second, they assign each criterion to one of three verification
routes based on what it requires. Criteria checkable from the submission alone, such as arithmetic,
cross-field consistency, or file structure, become direct tests. Factual claims that depend on current web

4. Evaluation Setup and Validation
4.1. Evaluation Setup
We evaluate 23 agent–model configurations on 77 tasks, yielding 1,771 agent runs and corresponding
judgments. With official Harbor 0.24.0, each agent is run in an isolated container using a native CLI
start and multi-turn resume, with 3,000 seconds allowed per turn. The judging backbone is GPT-5.4 at
temperature zero. Agent versions, authentication routes, transports, and resource limits are documented
in Appendix B.1.
Attempts interrupted by timeout or execution failure are excluded and retried until one clean, unin-
terrupted run completes. Task-specific component scores are weight-averaged by the rubric judge and
reported as percentages. A task passes when its overall score is at least 85% and no rubric component
scores below 3; Appendix A.1 evaluates sensitivity to the overall-score and rubric-score thresholds.
Direct-test and grounding findings inform the rubric judge rather than act as separate vetoes, since an
isolated finding, such as a cited page that cannot be retrieved, may matter little to the deliverable as a
whole. A task can therefore pass despite such a finding if it meets both score thresholds.

5. Main Results
5.1. Agent Leaderboard
Table 3 reports strict pass rates for 23 agent–model configurations on EverydayBench. A task passes
only when its overall score is at least 85% and every rubric component is at least 3. Claude Code with
Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Fable 5.1 tie for first at 54.55%. Codex with GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra
form the next group at 49.35%, followed by Claude Sonnet 5.5 at 48.05%. Across all 1,771 agent–task
runs, only 32.30% pass, showing that consistently complete outcomes remain uncommon even when
average-quality scores are high.
The task-type pass rates show that no system dominates every kind of everyday work. GPT-5.6 Sol leads
lookup at 56.25%; Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 tie on comparison at 48.00%; Fable leads planning at 66.67%;
and Opus, Fable, GPT-6.1 Sol, and GPT-6 Sol at max effort tie on analysis at 54.55%. GPT-6 Astra and
Sonnet lead customized deliverables at 76.92%, despite lower overall pass rates than the two leading
Claude configurations. The aggregate ranking therefore compresses distinct strengths that matter when
an application emphasizes a particular type of work.
Reasoning effort helps most on analysis, but the effect is not monotonic at every adjacent setting. In
the controlled GPT-6 Sol sweep, overall pass rate rises from 29.87% at low effort to 45.45% at max
effort. Analysis pass rate rises from 18.18% to 54.55%, while comparison rises from 16.00% to 32.00%
and lookup from 31.25% to 43.75%. Medium and xhigh occasionally trail their neighboring settings,
so additional reasoning improves the upper envelope of reliability rather than guaranteeing that every
increase in effort produces more completed tasks.

5.3. Failure Mode Analysis
We analyze where the agents lose points. We compare two groups of configurations: the frontier group,
which is four high-scoring configurations, and the low-cost group, six configurations that each have a
median cost per task below $0.15. 1 Each task has a rubric of several deliverable components (e.g.,
table, text file). The judge scores every component from 1 to 5 and writes a short reason for each score
(Section 3.2). We count each component that scores below 5 as one deduction, and we read the judge’s
reason for it. An output that violates the required format scores 0, and we count each of its components
as a deduction. Across the 77 tasks, 57% of the components score below 5 in the frontier group and 76%
in the low-cost group. The deductions in the frontier group are mostly small: 72% of them are a score of
4, against 44% in the low-cost group.
1Frontier: Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Fable 5.1, GPT-6.1 Sol, and GPT-6 Sol at max reasoning effort. Low-cost: Claude Haiku
5.5, GPT-6 Luna, GPT-5.6 Luna in the Goose, Pi and Qwen Code harnesses, and Qwen3.7-Plus.

6. Conclusion
We introduced EverydayBench, a benchmark of 77 human-authored tasks for evaluating agent-selected
outcomes on the live web. Each task specifies success criteria before execution without prescribing a single
valid answer. Evaluation combines direct checks, verification against cited webpages, and component-level
scoring of the submitted deliverable. Across 23 agent configurations, the strongest agent configuration
passes 54.55% of tasks. Failure mode analysis shows that research accounts for the most deductions in
both the high-scoring and the low-cost configurations, and the high-scoring configurations most often
lose a point because a claim says more than the page it cites. The low-cost configurations lose more
points in every category, most of all through output files that are missing or violate the required format
and through user needs that the model does not look into.

A. Additional Results
A.1. Pass-Threshold Sensitivity
A task passes when its overall score is at least 85% and no rubric component scores below 3. Because
component scores run from 1 to 5, an overall score of 85% corresponds to a weighted component score
of 4.25: on average, the deliverable must be better than “good” and reach the top anchor on a substantial
share of the rubric weight. The component floor prevents strong components from masking a deficient
one. To test how much our conclusions depend on these choices, we recompute pass rates from the 1,463
runs of the 19 primary configurations in the leaderboard (Table 3), varying the overall-score cutoff 𝜏
from 75% to 95% in steps of 5 with the floor fixed at 3, and varying the floor with 𝜏fixed at 85%.
We report two rank correlations over the 19 configurations of the primary leaderboard. 𝜌score is the
Spearman correlation between pass rate at 𝜏and mean overall score, which needs no cutoff. 𝜌85 is the
Spearman correlation between pass rate at 𝜏and pass rate at the primary 85% cutoff, and measures
whether moving the cutoff changes the ranking we report.
Table 4 shows that absolute pass rates depend strongly on the cutoff, falling from 53.86% of all runs
at 𝜏= 75% to 31.72% at 85% and 7.38% at 95%. The ranking is much less sensitive. Between 75%
and 90%, 𝜌85 stays at or above 0.94, and Claude Opus 5.5 or Claude Fable 5.1 has the highest pass rate
at every cutoff; at 85% the two are tied at 54.55%. Only at 95%, where 7.38% of runs pass and two
configurations pass no task, does the ranking become noticeably less stable (𝜌85 = 0.868). Pass rate and
mean score agree closely but not perfectly (𝜌score = 0.902 at 85%), because the pass metric rewards
complete outcomes rather than partial credit: Qwen Code with GPT-6 Sol ranks seventeenth by mean
score but eighth by pass rate, while Codex with GPT-6 Luna ranks eighth by mean score and tenth by
pass rate. In the GPT-6 Sol reasoning-effort sweep, pass rate at 85% rises from 29.87% at low effort to
45.45% at max, although neighboring levels are not strictly ordered.

A.2. Computer Use Versus Retrieval-Only Execution

B. Benchmark and Evaluation Details
B.1. Detailed Evaluation Setup
Agents and evaluator.
We report 23 agent–model configurations and 1,771 agent–task runs, with every
configuration covering all 77 tasks. Claude Code uses Claude Opus 5.5, Fable 5.1, Sonnet 5.5, and Haiku
5.5 at high effort. Codex uses GPT-5.5 at low, medium, and high effort; GPT-5.6 Sol, Terra, and Luna
at high effort; GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, Sol, and Luna at high effort; and GPT-6 Sol at low, medium,
xhigh, and max effort. Qwen Code uses Qwen3.7-Plus, GPT-5.6 Luna, and GPT-6 Sol, while Pi and Goose
use GPT-5.6 Luna. Each agent may choose its own sources, tools, and workflow. The source-grounded
checker and rubric-based component scorer use GPT-5.4 at temperature zero, with one evaluator pass
per run.
Configuration fidelity.
Claude Code uses Claude subscription OAuth: the Opus 5.5 run uses version
2.1.311, while the Fable 5.1 traces span the recorded transition from 2.1.305 to 2.1.311. The GPT-5.6
Codex runs use Codex 0.147.0, while the GPT-6 family and effort sweep use Codex 0.163.0; both
authenticate with ChatGPT subscription OAuth. Qwen Code 0.26.1, Pi 1.4.0, and Goose v1.56.0 use their
native CLI adapters. Qwen3.7-Plus is routed through OpenRouter. Pi/GPT-5.6 Luna, Qwen Code/GPT-5.6
Luna, Goose/GPT-5.6 Luna, and Qwen Code/GPT-6 Sol use the recorded UniAPI OpenAI-compatible
route; Pi and Qwen Code/GPT-6 Sol use Responses transport. The latter uses a narrow subclass of
Harbor’s official Qwen adapter that changes only CLI authentication transport from Chat Completions to
Responses. Agent instructions, the native two-step protocol, and the verifier remain unchanged.
Execution environment and protocol.
We orchestrate runs with official Harbor 0.24.0 at suite commit
f29775a in isolated Docker containers with internet access. Each task configuration records two CPU
cores, 4 GB of memory, 10 GB of storage, and a 3,000-second timeout for each native agent step.
Model calls use either the configuration’s subscription-authenticated CLI or its recorded API route, while
orchestration, artifact collection, and analysis run locally. Harbor first provides the user request and
then resumes the same trajectory with the outcome schema used to submit the final structured result
(resume_trajectory=true).
Failure handling and selection.
If a timeout or execution error prevents either native step from
completing, we exclude that attempt and rerun the task. If both steps complete but the required output
is missing or cannot be parsed, the evaluator assigns zero. A completed submission that violates the
output schema likewise receives zero; these zeros remain because they represent task failures rather
than incomplete executions.
Scoring and aggregation.
We aggregate task-specific component scores using their rubric weights,
normalize the result to [0, 1], and multiply by 100 for percentage reporting. Each configuration contributes
one completed run per task. A task passes when it has an overall score of at least 85% and every component
score of at least 3.

F.2. Failure Types
Each deduction receives exactly one failure type, according to what the output is missing or gets wrong
(Table 8). The failure types belong to three categories that follow the three things a task requires:
working out what the user needs beyond what the request states (intent recognition), finding the required
information (research), and writing what was found into the final files (delivery).
Rules for close calls.
1. What is missing, not how much. Phrases such as “not specific enough” or “could be more explicit”
say how much is missing. A missing qualification that the user needs is intent recognition; missing
information, items, data or evidence is research; a plan or analysis that is not worked out for the
user’s case is delivery (other).
2. Required items only for what the request asks for. A point that only the 5-point anchor asks for,
and that tells the user how far to trust or how to use the answer, is intent recognition; for example,
the runner-up and the price gap behind a recommendation.
3. An account of the search or the calculation is data selection: which sources were tried, why the
search stopped, which records were combined, how values were rounded or computed.
4. Trap components. A component that tests a need the request does not state is intent recognition,
unless the main shortfall is clearly source support, missing items, format or consistency. A trap
mentioned only briefly or generically is still intent recognition.
5. Needs inferred from the user’s situation are intent recognition, for example the features a backpack
must have for full-day birdwatching.
6. Placeholders and empty entries are required items not found; claims resting on weak evidence say
more than the cited page.
7. The three page types. A value differs from the page only when the page shows a specific different
value for the same thing. If the page supports part of the claim’s specific content, the claim says
more than the page; if it supports none of it, the value is not found on the page.

F.3. Defining and Assigning the Failure Types
Defining the failure types.
We derived the failure types by hand. We sampled 100 deductions and,
for each, read the judge’s reason, the agent’s output and its trace, which records the model’s messages
and tool calls, to see what the output was missing; from these cases we summarized the failure types in
Table 8 and the rules for close calls.
The failure-mode judge.
The failure-mode judge is Claude Sonnet 5.5 run as an agent. Each call
receives one of the prompts in Appendix F.4 and a batch of deductions, and returns one JSON line per
25


EverydayBench : Evaluating Satisfactory Completion of Live User-Facing Digital Work
deduction. It labels the deductions in three steps.
1. Category label (all 2,116 deductions with a score). For each deduction the judge sees a summary
of the task, the component’s description, its 5-point and 3-point anchors, the score and the judge’s
reason. It returns one of eight labels and a short phrase saying what is missing: intent recognition;
required items not found, value not supported by the cited page, conditions not met, data selection
not explained; format, disagreement, other.
2. Kind of source problem (the 612 deductions labeled as not supported by the cited page). The judge
also sees up to five checks of the agent’s cited claims: the claim the agent wrote, the check status,
and what the checker saw on the cited page. It decides whether the claim says more than the page,
the value is not on the page, the value differs from the page, or no source is given (14 deductions);
these and the 2 deductions it leaves unclear are counted as not found on the cited page. If the main
shortfall is not about source support, the judge moves the deduction to another label; 18 deductions
moved, 4 of them to intent recognition.
3. Looking into the need (all 659 intent recognition deductions). The judge has a command-line tool
that lists, prints and searches the delivered files and searches the run’s trace; each hit in the trace
is marked as the model’s own message, the arguments of a tool call the model made, or the text a
tool returned. The judge first states the missing point and then gives one of four verdicts: the point
is in a delivered file but in the wrong place, in weak wording, or for only some items (IN_FILES); it
is only in the model’s own messages or tool calls (CONSIDERED); it is only in text a tool returned
(SEEN_ONLY); or it is nowhere (ABSENT). The first two verdicts mean that the model looks into
the need but does not report it; the last two that it does not look into the need. Every IN_FILES or
CONSIDERED verdict is then read a second time under stricter rules (prompt 3b), and the second
verdict replaces the first.
Components of outputs that violate the required format are assigned to that failure type without the
judge.
Trials and revisions.
Steps 1 and 3 were first run on ten trial tasks (302 deductions in step 1 and 136
intent recognition deductions in step 3). We compared the labels with our own reading of the cases and
revised the prompts. After the trial batch of step 1 we added rules 4 and 5 of prompt 1, which separate
required items from qualifications and from accounts of the search. After labeling all deductions, we
found that the judge used other as a catch-all; we added rules 6 to 8 of prompt 1 and relabelled the 376
deductions labeled other with the revised prompt, which moved 111 of them to intent recognition. The
stricter second reading in step 3 was added because the first reading accepted loose matches, such as a
general reminder in place of the specific point; it changed 81 of the 313 verdicts it re-read. In all, 58
labels (2.7% of the 2,116 deductions) were corrected individually after review.
Agreement with human labels.
On 100 deductions labeled independently by the authors, the failure-
mode judge’s final failure type agrees with the human label in 92% of cases.
