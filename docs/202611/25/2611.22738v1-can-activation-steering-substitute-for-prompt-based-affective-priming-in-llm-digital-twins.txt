Can Activation Steering Substitute for Prompt-Based Affective
Priming in LLM Digital Twins?
Junyi Fan
University of Southern California
Abstract
Affective priming – asking participants to write about a recalled event, such as a time they felt
grateful or lacked control, and then measuring the induced state and its spillover onto related judgments
– is a standard way to manipulate emotion in the social sciences, and it also shifts the responses of LLM-
based digital twins. The writing task, however, requires generating a full text. We test whether activation
steering can replace it. Using Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on 100 personas in two domains (gratitude, lack of
control), we compare a real-prompt path replicating the original Twin-2K-500 Mega Study (J2) with an
injection path that skips the writing and adds a mean-difference direction, extracted from human writing,
to the residual stream at varying strengths. The real-prompt effects fall between the original human and
digital-twin effects in 5 of 6 comparisons. Injection shifts the manipulation check in a dose-dependent
way and matches the real-prompt effect at one strength in the gratitude domain (β ≈2.7), but overshoots
beyond it, on the proximal measure as well, and asymmetrically across the two directions. A structure-
matched No-Injection baseline separates two sources of difference: omitting the writing task raises the
proximal measure in both domains, whereas the injected direction itself moves a distal measure in the
lack-of-control domain. Substitution is therefore measure-dependent: validity established for one way
of inducing a state does not carry over uniformly to another.
1
Introduction
Social scientists often manipulate emotional states with affective priming: participants reflect on and write
about a recent event, and researchers test whether the induced state carries over to the responses that follow.
A prime prompt asks for an event tied to the target state and a baseline prompt for a neutral or opposite event.
A manipulation check confirms that the state was induced, and proximal measures (closely related to the
target state) and distal measures (more distantly related) show how far it spills over. In the gratitude version,
participants recall a specific time they felt sincerely grateful for someone’s kindness or help, or, at baseline,
their morning routine (Emmons and McCullough, 2003; DeSteno et al., 2014; Oguni and Ishii, 2024). In
the lack-of-control version, they describe an incident in which they had no control over the situation, or,
at baseline, one in which they were in control (Bukowski et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2017; Lembregts and
Pandelaere, 2019; Whitson and Galinsky, 2008). “Priming” here refers to this recall-based induction of a
state, not to the brief stimulus-presentation paradigms of cognitive psychology.
Whether such manipulations work on LLM-based stand-ins for people tests whether a simulated respon-
dent reproduces not only static traits but also state-level shifts. Persona-conditioned models can reproduce
subpopulation-level survey responses (Argyle et al., 2023), and “digital twins” built from rich personal data
predict individuals’ held-out answers with high accuracy (Park et al., 2024a; Toubia et al., 2025). The J2
sub-study of the Twin-2K-500 Mega Study (Peng et al., 2026) asked whether affective priming works with
digital twins. One thousand human participants and their twins were assigned to a prime or baseline writ-
ing condition in the gratitude or the lack-of-control domain, and then completed a manipulation check, a
proximal measure (Elevation; Fatigue) and a distal measure (an adapted Empathic Concern scale; Desire

for Predictability). Writing about gratitude or lack of control induced the intended states in twins, twins re-
sponded more strongly than humans, and proximal measures showed spillover while distal measures showed
small or inconsistent effects. This over-responsiveness is one of several systematic distortions documented
there, alongside cautionary work on LLM surrogates (Gao et al., 2025; Hu and Collier, 2024; Li et al., 2025)
showing that incidental pipeline choices can change simulated respondents’ behavior in ways unrelated to
the manipulation. This raises a question for any alternative way of constructing a simulated state: does
validity established for one method carry over to another?
The writing task is an obvious candidate for replacement. It requires the model to generate a complete
piece of writing, which adds inference cost and leaves open whether the effect comes from the content the
writing activates or from the act of writing itself. Activation steering offers an alternative. If concepts are
approximately linear directions in activation space (Park et al., 2024b), a direction computed from con-
trastive examples can be added to hidden states at inference time without changing the input (Turner et al.,
2023; Rimsky et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2023). The closest precedent, PsySET (Banayeeanzade et al., 2025),
evaluates steering of emotions and personality and finds finer intensity control than prompting at some cost
to output quality, with narrow mid-layer windows working best. Such evaluations score the steered model’s
own emotional expression. Whether injection reproduces the downstream consequences of an established
human-subjects manipulation has, to our knowledge, not been tested.
We implement two parallel pipelines on one open-weight model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct; Grattafiori
et al., 2024), using the J2 materials and instruments: a real-prompt path, in which the model completes
the writing task before answering, and an injection path, in which the writing is skipped and a direction
extracted from the real human writing is added at strength β. The real-prompt path is compared with the
original study’s human and digital-twin (GPT-4.1) results to provide an external calibration; the two paths
are then compared with each other on the same personas. We ask four research questions:
RQ1 How does the real-prompt effect of our small open-weight model compare with the human and digital-
twin results of the original study?
RQ2 Can injection induce a measurable, dose-dependent shift in the manipulation check?
RQ3 Is there an injection strength at which injection and the real prompt produce comparable effects?
RQ4 Does injection reproduce the proximal–distal and valence structure of real-prompt priming?
Contributions.
(i) A direct comparison of prompt-based and activation-injection induction of the same
state in LLM digital twins on a published human-subjects paradigm, with an external calibration against
the original human and twin results. (ii) Evidence that validity does not transport uniformly: injection
is dose-dependent and can be matched to the real prompt on the manipulation check, but overshoots be-
yond the matching strength, on the proximal measure too, and asymmetrically across directions. (iii) A
structure-matched No-Injection baseline that separates the effect of omitting the writing task from the effect
of the injected vector. Code, generation logs and analysis scripts are released at https://github.com/
JunyiTim/twin-activation-injection.
2
Method
2.1
Setup
Model.
All experiments use Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct in bfloat16 for generation, direction extraction and
injection. Generation uses sampling at temperature 0.7 (top-p = 0.9, top-k = 50), no fixed seed, and at
most 1500 (gratitude) or 5000 (lack of control) new tokens, set by the length of the question set.
2


Domains and materials.
We replicate the two J2 domains. In the gratitude domain the prime asks par-
ticipants to recall a specific experience in which they felt sincerely grateful (QID46) and the baseline asks
for their morning routine (QID47) (Emmons and McCullough, 2003; DeSteno et al., 2014; Oguni and Ishii,
2024). In the lack-of-control domain the prime asks for an incident in which they had no control over the
situation (QID31) and the baseline for one in which they were in control (QID32) (Bukowski et al., 2024;
Chen et al., 2017; Lembregts and Pandelaere, 2019; Whitson and Galinsky, 2008). Writing prompts and all
measure items are taken verbatim from the original instrument (Appendix D).
Personas.
Personas are the persona summary field of Twin-2K-500 (Toubia et al., 2025), about 2,000
words of demographics, percentile-scored psychometric instruments and a few verbatim answers, fetched on
demand through the public dataset API. We use the first 100 personas in the dataset’s order, identical in both
domains (Appendix C). Every persona is run through every condition, whereas in the original study each
participant was assigned to one condition; conditions are therefore matched on persona, which the analysis
exploits. No new human data were collected, and all human and original-twin results quoted are published
values.
Measures.
Each measure is the mean of its items on a 7-point scale. Following the original analysis
code, Fatigue items 3, 7, 8 and Desire-for-Predictability items 1, 4, 5 are reverse-coded (8 −x) before
averaging, and the lack-of-control manipulation check is analyzed on the raw scale (higher = more control).
The manipulation check is three emotion-intensity items (gratitude) or three sense-of-control items (lack of
control) (Greenaway et al., 2015). The proximal measure is Elevation (6 items; Schnall et al., 2010; Walsh
et al., 2022) or Fatigue (9 items; Sedek and Kofta, 1990; Bukowski et al., 2024); the Fatigue items refer to
“recalling and describing the event” and are only partly defined when no writing occurs. The distal measure
is an adapted Empathic Concern scale (2 items; Oliveira et al., 2021) or Desire for Predictability (8 items;
Webster and Kruglanski, 1994; Lembregts and Pandelaere, 2019).
2.2
Experimental Design
In each domain, two paths run on the same personas (Table 1). In the real-prompt path, a single generation
contains the writing task, the manipulation check, the proximal and the distal measure, as in the original
pipeline; it has a prime and a baseline condition. In the injection path the writing task is omitted and the
model answers the same questions while a direction vector is added to its hidden states at strength β. A dose
arm injects along the priming direction at four strengths (β > 0: Low 1.8, Moderate 2.5, High 3.2, Extreme
3.9); a single-point active-control arm injects the opposite pole of the same direction (β = −2.5); and a
No-Injection condition (β = 0) is run once per persona as the shared zero point. No-Injection has the same
prompt and output structure as the injection conditions and differs from them only in the vector, which lets
us separate the effect of the injected vector from the effect of changing the pipeline.

2.4
Parsing and Analysis
An 8B model is less reliable than the original study’s model at producing structured output, so free-form
responses are parsed by an extractive LLM judge (claude-sonnet-4-5; Banayeeanzade et al., 2025)
4


that returns the numeric answers or a failure marker, and parses with the wrong number of items are rejected.
Failed generations are retried (at most two attempts in the gratitude domain, three in lack of control), every
attempt is logged, and analyses use the final attempt of each cell when it succeeded (769 of 800 gratitude
and 753 of 800 lack-of-control cells; Appendix A).
Because every persona appears in every condition, comparisons are paired by persona: paired t-tests on
personas with successful generations in both conditions, with effect size dz (mean within-persona difference
over its standard deviation; Cohen, 1988). The comparisons in the text address the research questions;
Appendix E reports all 39 comparisons per domain with Holm-adjusted p-values and Welch’s test as a
sensitivity check. Each injection condition is also compared with No-Injection (Holm over 15 within-path
comparisons per domain). The strength at which the dose curve crosses the real-prompt effect is found by
linear interpolation, with a 95% percentile bootstrap interval (5,000 resamples of personas).

Results
All comparisons are paired by persona; p-values are uncorrected unless a Holm-adjusted value is given
(Holm over the 39 comparisons per domain of Appendix E); dz is the paired effect size. Per-condition
means and standard errors are in Appendix B.

3.2
Dose-Dependence of Injection (RQ2)
Figures 2 and 3 show the dose-response patterns. The manipulation check responds monotonically to injec-
tion strength in both domains. In the gratitude domain, Low is already significantly below the real prime
(t(99) = −8.48, p < .0001, dz = −0.85) and Extreme exceeds it (t(71) = 10.20, p < .0001, dz = 1.20).
In the lack-of-control domain the pattern runs in the opposite direction, and Low already exceeds the real
prime (t(95) = −7.46, p < .0001, dz = −0.76). Injection thus induces a measurable, dose-dependent
shift in the manipulation check (RQ2). Parsing success declines with strength, to 72% (gratitude) and 77%
(lack of control) at Extreme (Appendix Table 7), so results at Extreme are conditional on the generations
that remained parseable.

3.4
Proximal–Distal Structure and Sources of Difference (RQ4)
Real-prompt path.
It reproduces the original proximal spillover in both domains: prime exceeds control
on Elevation (t(99) = 6.58, p < .0001, dz = 0.66) and on Fatigue (t(98) = 6.20, p < .0001, dz = 0.62),
while the distal measure does not differ (gratitude dz = 0.03, p = .766; lack of control dz = 0.13,
p = .206), as in the original study.
Injection path against the real prompt.
The closest agreement is gratitude Elevation at Low, which does
not differ significantly from the real prime (t(99) = 0.37, p = .710, dz = 0.04; a non-significant difference
is not evidence of equivalence, and we ran no equivalence test). In the lack-of-control domain injection does
not match the real Fatigue effect at any strength (overshoot from Low, dz = 1.03).
Separating the pipeline from the vector.
Differences between injection-path and real-prompt conditions
mix the injected vector with the change of pipeline (no writing task, shorter prompt, fewer questions, differ-
ent output structure). Table 4 separates them. The No-Injection baseline contains no vector, so its differences
from the real control reflect the pipeline alone. It is higher on the proximal measure in both domains, with
the same sign: Elevation in the gratitude domain (t(99) = 4.71, p < .0001, dz = 0.47) and Fatigue in the
lack-of-control domain (t(96) = 7.43, p < .0001, dz = 0.75), where it is indistinguishable from the primed
condition (dz = 0.00). The within-path contrast (active-control arm minus No-Injection, same prompt
and output structure) isolates the vector. In the lack-of-control domain the in-control direction moves the
manipulation check in the intended direction (dz = 0.45) and lowers the distal measure (t(95) = −6.12,
p < .0001, dz = −0.63), a measure on which the real manipulation has no reliable effect; the proximal
measure does not change reliably. In the gratitude domain the active-control arm does not differ reliably
from No-Injection on any measure, so its differences from the real conditions there come mostly from the
pipeline. Within the injection path the dose arm raises the proximal measure strongly in both domains
(gratitude, Moderate: dz = 0.75; lack of control, Low: dz = 1.03) and moves the distal measure only in-
consistently across strengths (Appendix Table 10). Persona-level agreement between conditions is moderate
(Appendix B), and we do not interpret it as individual-level fidelity.

(RQ2) that can be matched to the real-prompt effect at one strength in the gratitude domain (β ≈2.7) but
overshoots beyond it, on the proximal measure as well, and asymmetrically across directions (RQ3). It

Amplification, not reconstruction.
Twins over-respond to priming relative to humans in the original
study, and our real-prompt path reproduced that at an intermediate magnitude. Injection does not repro-
duce this tendency through another channel; it exceeds it, on the manipulation check and on the proximal
measure. This is more consistent with injection amplifying an existing property of the twin paradigm than
with reconstructing the state real writing induces. The in-control direction complicates the picture: it has
a small effect on its own manipulation check (dz = 0.28 against real control) yet lowers a distal measure
within the injection path (dz = −0.63 against No-Injection), which the real manipulation does not. One
possibility is that the contrastive corpus mixes topical and affective signal asymmetrically across poles; the
extraction also averaged over the instruction wording, which differs between poles, and the probe separated
the classes perfectly at every layer, as expected when topic and wording are available. These observations
are compatible with a direction that tracks topic and wording at least as much as affect.
Two sources of difference.
Because the injection path contains a structure-matched baseline, the vector
and the pipeline can be separated (Table 4). Omitting the writing task raises the proximal measure relative
to the real control in both domains regardless of the vector; the injected direction adds nothing reliable
beyond No-Injection in the gratitude domain but lowers the distal measure in the lack-of-control domain.
Which source dominates depends on domain and measure. The pipeline effect cannot be traced to one cause
here: the injection path omits the writing, shortens the prompt, drops questions and changes the output
structure, and the lack-of-control Fatigue items refer to an event that no longer exists. A neutral-writing
control matched to the real-prompt structure would isolate the writing task; we leave it for future work.
Limitations.
We use one base model, one extraction method and a three-layer window that was not chosen
by a discriminating criterion (the probe was at ceiling at every layer). The active-control arm was tested at
a single strength. The strength grid was informed by a three-persona pilot whose personas are in the main
sample, so the matching strength is approximate. We use paired tests rather than a single mixed-effects
model and no equivalence tests, so “no difference” means non-significance. The retry limit differed between
domains and results at Extreme are conditional on parseable generations. The direction was extracted from
writing by the same participant pool whose twins we simulated, and we did not manually audit the parsed
values. Finally, the paths differ in more than the vector, as discussed above.
Conclusion.
Activation steering can substitute for prompt-based affective priming on some measures, but
not uniformly: on the manipulation check a well-chosen strength matches the real effect in one domain,
whereas on the proximal and distal measures injection overshoots or shifts outcomes the real manipulation
leaves unchanged, partly because of the vector and partly because of the changed pipeline. Researchers
who use injection to induce states in digital twins should therefore calibrate strength separately for each
outcome and include a no-injection control that matches the prompt and output format of the injection path.
Next steps are a neutral-writing control, other models and extraction methods, and an extraction corpus that
excludes the simulated personas.

Additional Method Details
A.1
Direction Extraction and Layer Selection
Training examples.
Each example is a chat-formatted sequence consisting of the model’s default system
header, a user turn containing the writing instruction shown to the human participant (the prime or baseline
prompt), and an assistant turn containing that participant’s own writing. Write xi = (pi, wi) for instruction
and writing, with token length Ti. Let D+ and D−denote the prime- and baseline-condition examples, of
sizes n+ and n−. In the gratitude domain, n+ = 249 and n−= 257 (506 in total); in the lack-of-control
domain, n+ = 256 and n−= 236 (492 in total). Because the average over token positions (Section 2.3) runs
12


over all tokens of the sequence, including the instruction, the extracted direction mixes affective content,
topical content, and the wording difference between the prime and baseline instructions; this is deliberate,
since the real manipulation likewise conflates topic and affect, and our question is whether injection can
substitute for the real manipulation rather than whether it isolates a topic-free affective direction.
Layer selection.
For each candidate layer l ∈{10, . . . , 23} we train a logistic-regression probe (no in-
tercept; regularization strength chosen on the training split, C = 100 in the gratitude domain) to classify
prime versus baseline from ¯h(l)
i , using an 80/20 split (404 training and 102 test examples in the gratitude
domain), and record held-out accuracy Acc(l). We define L∗as the middle element of the set of layers
attaining the maximum accuracy. In the gratitude domain, held-out accuracy was 1.000 at every candidate
layer, so the probe did not discriminate among layers: L∗= 17 is the midpoint of the candidate range rather
than a data-driven maximum (the same held in the lack-of-control domain, again with L∗= 17). The per-
fect separability itself is unsurprising given that the prime and baseline sequences differ in their instruction
text and topic, and it should not be read as evidence that a purely affective signal has been isolated. Our
choice of a mid-depth window is consistent with PsySET’s finding that narrow mid-layer windows work
best (Banayeeanzade et al., 2025).
A.2
Choice of Injection Strengths
The grid was set after a small pilot on the first three personas (which are also part of the main sample). At
β = 2.0 the mean manipulation-check score was 4.44, below the level we were aiming for (about 6, near
the top of the 7-point scale); at β = 4.5 the first persona scored 7.00 but generation then failed to produce
parseable output and the sweep was halted. We therefore used four equally spaced strengths (step 0.7) from
1.8 to 3.9, spanning the pilot range while staying below the strength at which generation broke down. The
same grid was used in both domains. Because the grid was informed by the manipulation check, the location
of the strength that matches the real-prompt effect (RQ3) should be read as approximate.
A.3
Response Generation and Parsing
An 8B instruction-tuned model is much less reliable than the larger model of the original study at produc-
ing well-formed structured output, and its free-form responses vary in format. Our prompts also omit the
original pipeline’s trailing JSON format instructions (the general instruction announces a format but none
is appended), which further increases format variability. We therefore use an LLM-as-judge step, as in
PsySET (Banayeeanzade et al., 2025): Claude (claude-sonnet-4-5, maximum 1024 output tokens,
default sampling) receives each raw Llama response and either extracts the numeric answers into a fixed
JSON schema or returns a failure marker if any item is missing, unclear, or unanswered. The judge is purely
extractive; it is not asked to rate, repair, or complete responses. The code additionally rejects any parse
whose item counts do not match the instrument (3, 6, and 2 items for the gratitude-domain Q2–Q4, and 3,
9, and 8 items for the lack-of-control domain). The full judge prompt is given in Appendix D. We did not
manually audit the extracted values against the raw responses.
If a generation fails validation it is regenerated: each persona–condition cell receives at most two at-
tempts in the gratitude domain (an initial attempt and one retry) and at most three in the lack-of-control
domain, where the longer question set produced more parse failures. Every attempt, including failures, is
logged with the raw Llama output, the judge’s output, and the success flag. Figure 4 illustrates the pipeline.

A.4
Data Quality and Sample Size
Analyses use only the final attempt of each cell, and only when it succeeded (complete-case analysis).
We verified that the success flag coincides exactly with whether valid numeric scores could be parsed (no
flagged-successful row lacked parseable scores). The resulting sample size per condition is roughly 77–100,
falling at high injection strength because of parse failures (reported per condition in Appendix B), with a
final failure rate below 10% overall. Because failures concentrate at high strengths, results at Extreme are
conditional on the generations that remained parseable.
A.5
Statistical Analysis (Full Description)
For each condition we compute the mean and standard error of each measure. Because every persona ap-
pears in every condition, comparisons are paired by persona: each injection condition is compared with
the corresponding real-prompt condition, and the real-prompt prime and control conditions with each other,
using paired t-tests on the personas whose final generations succeeded in both conditions (so the number of
pairs is at most 100 and falls at high injection strength). We report t with npairs −1 degrees of freedom, the
paired effect size dz (the mean of the within-persona differences divided by their standard deviation; Co-
hen, 1988), and the correlation between the two conditions’ scores. The comparisons in the main text were
chosen to address the research questions, and the active-control analyses are exploratory. To guard against
selective reporting, Appendix E reports all 39 comparisons per domain (every injection condition, including
No-Injection, against both real-prompt conditions on all three measures, plus prime versus control), with
Holm-adjusted p-values computed over that full set. As a sensitivity check, the appendix also lists Welch’s
independent-samples t-test (Welch, 1947), which ignores the pairing and is therefore conservative. Because
the injection path contains its own structure-matched baseline (No-Injection), we also compare each injec-
14


tion condition with No-Injection (paired, with Holm adjustment over these 15 within-path comparisons per
domain, Appendix E); this contrast removes any difference between the injection and real-prompt pipelines.
The location of the injection strength that matches the real-prompt effect is estimated by linear interpolation
of the dose curve, with a 95% percentile bootstrap interval obtained by resampling personas (5,000 re-
samples). The real-prompt results are additionally compared descriptively with the digital-twin and human
results published for the original study, forming the basis of the RQ1 analysis.

Success rate declines monotonically with injection strength in both domains, dropping sharply at Ex-
treme. Because this decline reflects only whether a generation could be structurally parsed, it is an outcome-

regardless of any particular downstream measure. It parallels the widening standard errors at High and Ex-

domain, so shifts in that arm cannot be attributed to selective attrition.
B.3
Persona-Level Agreement
Paired scores are moderately correlated across conditions. Between real prime and real control, r on the
proximal measure is 0.50 (gratitude) and 0.60 (lack of control). Between the dose arm at Low and real
prime on the manipulation check, r is 0.32 (gratitude) and 0.63 (lack of control). We have no human or twin
benchmark for these values and do not interpret them as evidence of individual-level fidelity.

Implementation Details
Software and hardware.
Experiments were run in Google Colab on a single GPU; the runtime was re-
built between runs, so exact library versions may differ slightly across runs. Steering used the PsySET
code base (Banayeeanzade et al., 2025) (modified Llama implementation exposing a hook after mlp hs
hook point after each decoder layer’s MLP residual addition). Versions in the last recorded environment:
transformers 4.56.1, torch 2.11.0 (CUDA 12.8).
Extraction pipeline.
Hidden states were extracted for candidate layers 10–23 (14 layers) in batches of
8, averaged over all token positions of each chat-formatted example, in bfloat16 and cast to float32 for the
probe. Gratitude domain: 506 examples (249 prime, 257 baseline); probes were trained on 404 examples
and evaluated on 102. Held-out probe accuracy was 1.000 at every candidate layer in both domains, giving
L∗= 17 and an injection window of layers {16, 17, 18} in both. Lack-of-control domain: 492 examples
(256 prime, 236 baseline), i.e. 393 training and 99 test examples under the same split rule. As a reference
point for how much of this separability is lexical, a bag-of-words (TF-IDF) logistic-regression classifier
trained on the participants’ writing alone (no instruction text; 20 random stratified 80/20 splits) reached
held-out accuracy 1.000 in the gratitude domain and 0.77 (range 0.69–0.89) in the lack-of-control domain.
The activation probe is not directly comparable, since its input also contains the instruction wording, but the
gratitude result shows that the two sets of writings are already perfectly separable by vocabulary alone.

Generation.
Ancestral sampling at temperature 0.7; maximum new tokens 1500 (gratitude) and 5000
(lack of control); no fixed seed. Injection was implemented as forward hooks adding βˆv(l) to the output of
hook after mlp hs at layers 16–18, active for the entire call to generate (prompt prefill and every
decoding step).
Pilot.
β ∈{2.0, 4.5} on three personas: mean manipulation check 4.44 (n = 3) at β = 2.0; 7.00 for the
first persona at β = 4.5, followed by a parse failure, after which the sweep stopped.
