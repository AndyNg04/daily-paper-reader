Abstract
Conversational meme retrieval supports response selection in online communication, where relevance depends
on both contextual meaning and affective expression.
We study this task using two language-specific
collections from Douban and Imgflip, containing 98,506 meme records across 5,583 discussion topics. Thread
membership supplies weak relevance labels, while likes provide implicit ranking feedback. Automatically
extracted sentiment, facial-expression indicators, and cross-modal consistency features support an exploratory
analysis of engagement. We propose CARE, a Conversational Affective Response Retrieval framework
that combines an emotion-enhanced CLIP retriever with a Jina-based reranker. The retriever incorporates
free-form emotion descriptors through gated fusion and learns from multiple positive examples per query. The
reranker combines query–image interactions with emotion representations and is trained using engagement-
based ranking supervision. Under the reported topic-held-out protocol, CARE achieves Hit@10 of 24.03% on
Douban and 49.33% on Imgflip, compared with 23.16% and 41.07%, respectively, for fine-tuned CLIP. These
results support the utility of the complete two-stage system for recovering thread-associated memes. They
do not independently establish perceived social resonance or isolate the contribution of affect from reranker
training. The study provides a basis for further evaluation of affect-aware retrieval with human relevance
judgments and controlled comparisons.

Meme retrieval, Multimodal information retrieval, Affective computing, Conversational response

1. Introduction
Internet memes are used as responses that express amusement, frustration, agreement, and irony in online
communication (Shifman, 2013; Dynel, 2016; Yus, 2021b). An intelligent meme retrieval system must identify
candidates that fit a textual situation while accounting for how visual and textual cues communicate a stance.
Literal image–text similarity alone may miss this response function; conversely, matching emotion without
matching context can return inappropriate results.
Figure 1 illustrates this challenge. “Introverts doing a presentation” invokes anxiety and a change in
emotional state, while “Duolingo be like” relies on a shared interpretation of persistent reminders. Both cases
require contextual interpretation in addition to recognition of depicted objects. These examples motivate
affect-aware retrieval, but do not imply that emotion similarity is sufficient for relevance.
Vision–language models such as CLIP provide a useful semantic retrieval backbone (Radford et al.,
2021). Applying them to conversational memes raises three practical challenges: each context can admit
several responses; embedded text and expressive characters can convey meanings beyond visible objects; and

measured. Topic membership and likes provide weak supervision for retrieving thread-associated memes
rather than exhaustive judgments of conversational appropriateness.
We propose CARE (Conversational Affective Response Retrieval), a two-stage system that augments a
CLIP retriever with emotion descriptors and applies a Jina-based reranker with engagement-based supervision.
The exploratory regression examines facial-expression indicators and within-meme semantic and affective
consistency, together with sentiment and controls, whereas CARE uses a richer emotion-description vocabulary
for retrieval. The regression’s semantic and polarity-consistency variables are not explicit alignment objectives
in CARE. The contributions are:
• A conversational meme retrieval collection with 98,506 records across 5,583 topics from Douban and
Imgflip, together with automatically extracted semantic and affective attributes and a topic-based
evaluation protocol.
• A two-stage retrieval framework combining gated emotion-feature fusion, multi-positive contrastive
learning, and engagement-supervised reranking.
• An evaluation of the complete system and its reported component configurations on two language-
specific collections, accompanied by an exploratory engagement analysis and an explicit account of
weak-label and evaluation limitations.
2. Related Work
This section reviews prior work related to multimodal affect modeling for affect-aware conversational meme
retrieval. We first discuss how affect functions in meme communication, drawing on pragmatic and cognitive
2


accounts of meme interpretation. We then review multimodal affect modeling and meme understanding
benchmarks, followed by work on conversational and multimodal retrieval. Finally, we position our work by
clarifying how it differs from existing approaches.
2.1. Multimodal Affect in Meme Communication
Memes are not merely image–text composites but pragmatic acts embedded in digital discourse (Shifman,
2013).
Their meaning often emerges through implicature, exaggeration, irony, and shared contextual
knowledge (Grice, 1975; Sperber and Wilson, 1986; Yus, 2021b). Shifman’s framework of content, form, and
stance highlights that meme interpretation depends not only on what is represented, but also on how a
communicative position is affectively expressed.
Cognitively, meme understanding involves resolving cross-modal incongruity while preserving sufficient
coherence for interpretation (Yus, 2021a; Tanaka et al., 2022). Affect is central to this process. Emotional
arousal and contagion shape meme propagation (Hatfield et al., 1993; Ayele et al., 2023; Beskow et al.,
2020), while meme interpretation also depends on contextual cues to stance and emotional orientation (Yus,
2021b). Cultural context further modulates affective signaling, particularly in high-context communities
where implicit humor and nuanced cues play an important role (Zhang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2020).
Together, these studies suggest that multimodal affect provides observable evidence for how users interpret,
respond to, and socially resonate with memes. However, most computational approaches still focus on
semantic correspondence, leaving the affective basis of conversational meme response largely underexplored.
2.2. Multimodal Meme Benchmarks and Modeling
Research on meme understanding has developed from early sentiment-oriented tasks such as Memotion
(Sharma et al., 2020) to increasingly multilingual and culturally nuanced benchmarks. A major line of
work focuses on harmful content detection, including hate speech and misogyny (Suryawanshi et al., 2020;
Fersini et al., 2022; Bui et al., 2025; Hossain et al., 2022). Other benchmarks examine humor and persuasion
techniques (Xu et al., 2025; Dimitrov et al., 2024). Unified frameworks such as MemeLens (Shahroor et al.,
2026) further attempt to consolidate multiple meme understanding tasks.
These benchmarks have substantially advanced meme classification and interpretation, but most are
designed around predefined labels rather than conversational response retrieval. As a result, they rarely
provide thread-level query–response structures or socially grounded relevance signals needed to study how
memes function as affective responses in online interaction.
On the modeling side, vision–language pre-training models such as CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) and BLIP
(Li et al., 2022) establish joint embedding paradigms for image–text alignment. Multimodal large language
models (MLLMs), including GPT-4 (Achiam et al., 2023) and Qwen-VL (Bai et al., 2023), further improve
multimodal reasoning. However, multimodal models can still struggle with irony, metaphor, and culturally
specific humor (Xu et al., 2025; He et al., 2024).
Recent studies introduce emotional, knowledge-injected, or multi-view reasoning mechanisms to improve
meme understanding (Shah et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2026). KID (Li et al., 2026) grounds harmful meme
detection in background knowledge using label-constrained distillation and structured reasoning chains.
Its dual-head learning jointly optimizes semantic generation and classification. This work illustrates the
importance of contextual knowledge beyond literal image–text correspondence. Its output is a harmfulness
prediction, whereas CARE ranks candidate memes for a textual query; the tasks therefore require different
supervision and evaluation.
2.3. Conversational and Multimodal Retrieval
Pragmatic accounts explain how communicative intent is inferred beyond literal meaning (Grice, 1975;
Sperber and Wilson, 1986). Although these approaches have advanced pragmatic reasoning in language
understanding, their connection to multimodal retrieval remains limited. In conversational meme settings,
intent is expressed not only through text but also through visual affective tone, character emotions, and
cross-modal consistency. This makes meme retrieval closer to response retrieval, where relevance is socially
and contextually grounded rather than defined by one-to-one semantic correspondence.
3


Multimodal retrieval has progressed from shared embedding approaches such as VSE++ (Faghri et al.,
2018) to large-scale contrastive pre-training. Contemporary systems commonly adopt a two-stage architecture:
a bi-encoder for efficient coarse retrieval followed by a cross-encoder reranker for fine-grained interaction
modeling (Jina AI, 2025; Tian et al., 2025). This architecture is well suited to conversational meme retrieval,
where candidate responses must first be efficiently retrieved and then ranked according to their contextual
and affective appropriateness.
For meme-related tasks, prior work improves CLIP through retrieval-guided contrastive learning (Mei
et al., 2024) or robust adaptation strategies (Mei et al., 2025). More directly related to recommendation,
Zhang et al. (2025) present A benchmark for cross-modal emotion-infused internet meme recommendation.
This is a relevant antecedent for combining cross-modal retrieval with affective information. We include the
associated IEF comparator in the experimental evaluation and do not claim that introducing emotion into
meme recommendation is itself new.
MemeMatch (Le et al., 2026) provides a large-scale meme dataset and retrieval system with nearly one
million records from Reddit and Imgflip. It separates local context, including overlaid text and titles, from
global visual or template context, and enriches these representations with sentiment, emotion, topic, and
usage-intent annotations. It supports retrieval from natural-language or image queries. MemeMatch therefore
establishes a direct precedent for context-aware, affect-enriched meme retrieval, rather than only meme
classification.
CARE focuses on a thread-associated protocol in which textual main posts supply queries, multiple images
from a thread supply weak positives, and engagement supplies ranking feedback. Its evaluated pipeline
combines gated emotion descriptors, multi-positive contrastive learning, and supervised reranking. This
task and training configuration distinguish the present study’s focus from the representation and retrieval
capabilities described above; the existing results do not establish superiority over MemeMatch, which has
not been evaluated under the same protocol.
2.4. Positioning of This Work
CARE studies the use of automatically inferred emotion descriptors in a conversational retrieval pipeline.
Relative to MemeMatch (Le et al., 2026), KID (Li et al., 2026), and the emotion-infused recommendation
benchmark (Zhang et al., 2025), the scope of the contribution is the thread-associated task formulation and
the evaluated two-stage training configuration. Its application differs from meme classification because each
query can have several relevant candidates and the output is a ranked list. The architectural components
draw on established vision–language retrieval, gated fusion, and learning-to-rank methods.
3. Dataset Construction and Task Formulation
Modeling multimodal affect for affect-aware conversational meme retrieval requires datasets that preserve
both conversational context and response-level social feedback. Classification datasets address tasks such
as hate detection, sentiment labeling, and humor recognition, while retrieval-oriented resources such as
MemeMatch and the emotion-infused recommendation benchmark explicitly support search or recommenda-
tion (Le et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025). The present collection is organized around discussion topics to
study main-post queries, multiple associated memes, and engagement-based ranking.
To study this setting, we construct a large-scale benchmark comprising two language-specific meme
collections. The benchmark is designed to support one-to-many conversational meme retrieval, where a
main post serves as the query and the associated meme responses form weakly labeled relevance candidates.
Engagement signals, especially likes, are used as implicit feedback for ranking thread-associated memes.
3.1. Benchmark Overview
The benchmark consists of two independent language-specific collections:
• Douban (Chinese): A community-based discussion platform organized around thematic “Groups,”
characterized by context-rich conversations and culturally implicit humor.

and user-generated templates, characterized by high template diversity and rapid meme iteration.

3.2. Data Collection and Preprocessing
Data Sources. Douban (Chinese). Data was collected from June 1, 2023 to September 1, 2023 across six
meme-oriented communities. The raw crawl included post topics, textual content, comments, images, and
engagement metrics (likes and favorites), resulting in 68,943 topics and 819,507 comments and 331,431 meme
images.
Imgflip (English). Data was collected up to April 2025 across diverse meme streams. The raw corpus
included 125,124 topics, over 1.79 million comments, and 312,914 candidate meme images.
Preprocessing and Quality Control. To ensure multimodal validity and sufficient conversational context,
we adopt a standardized preprocessing protocol. Corrupted text, special characters, duplicate topics, and
duplicate images are removed, and posts without textual content are excluded.
To preserve conversational richness, only threads with a minimum number of comments are retained
(Douban: ≥10; Imgflip: ≥2). Low-engagement meme responses are further filtered on Imgflip using a
threshold of ≥2 likes. These thresholds are determined based on the scale of the raw corpus and the resulting
dataset size after filtering, balancing conversational depth, engagement representativeness, and sample
sufficiency for stable empirical analysis and retrieval evaluation.
Images are normalized to consistent formats, and incomplete or low-quality samples are discarded.
After preprocessing and filtering for valid multimodal availability, the final dataset comprises 1,836 topics
and 13,387 multimodal meme records for Douban, and 3,747 topics with 85,119 multimodal meme records
for Imgflip.
Semantic and affective annotations are extracted automatically using platform-specific vision–language
models, and consistency measures are derived from these annotations for empirical analysis and retrieval
experiments. Representative examples of raw metadata and corresponding automatically extracted multimodal
annotations are presented in Figure 2. These examples illustrate how conversational context, OCR text,
visual descriptions, affective attributes, and engagement feedback are jointly structured into retrieval-ready
records.
3.3. Task Formulation
We formulate the task as affect-aware conversational meme retrieval. Given a textual query qi
corresponding to the main post of a discussion thread, the objective is to retrieve meme responses that are
appropriate to the textual situation and its affective expression.
Unlike conventional one-to-one image–text retrieval, this task is inherently open-ended: a single conversa-
tional context may elicit multiple acceptable meme responses that differ in humor style, affective stance, and
pragmatic expression. Therefore, relevance is not defined as exact semantic correspondence, but as observed
membership in the same conversational thread.

3.4. Feature Engineering and Operationalization
To transform raw conversational meme records into structured variables, we implement an automated
annotation and representation pipeline that extracts multimodal affective and semantic signals for empirical
analysis and response ranking.
3.4.1. Automated Multimodal Annotation
We use Doubao Vision Pro for the Douban collection and Gemini Pro (Team et al., 2023) for the
Imgflip collection. Each platform-specific model performs OCR and produces visual descriptions, sentiment
labels, free-form emotion descriptors, and facial-expression labels in the same zero-shot annotation pass. The
multimodal annotation prompt is provided in Appendix A. The extracted attributes include:
• Textual Sentiment: sentiment polarity expressed in meme text (positive / neutral / negative);
• Image-level Sentiment: overall affective tone conveyed by the visual content;
• Emotion Descriptions and Facial Expressions: free-form emotion descriptors for retrieval and
facial-expression labels for regression, with the latter drawn from seven categories (happy, calm, angry,
sad, fear, disgust, surprise);
• Visual Description and OCR Text: textual representations of visual content and embedded meme
captions for semantic consistency estimation.
In this study, affect is an umbrella term for multimodal emotional signals, and sentiment denotes textual
or image-level polarity. The same multimodal annotation pass produces two distinct character-level fields.
For regression, seven facial-expression labels are converted into meme-level binary indicators: each indicator
equals 1 if the corresponding facial-expression category occurs for at least one character in the meme, and
0 otherwise. For retrieval, the original free-form emotion descriptors provide richer affective information,
including reactions inferred from the full meme context, and are not restricted to these seven facial-expression
categories.

4. Exploratory Analysis of Affect and Engagement
We examine the contributions of facial-expression indicators and within-meme semantic and affective
consistency to explaining engagement, alongside sentiment and control variables. This exploratory analysis
motivates affect-aware retrieval but does not exhaust the richer emotion-description vocabulary used by
CARE. Communication research distinguishes literal content from contextual and pragmatic interpretation
(Morris, 1938; Levinson, 1983; Grice, 1975; Sperber and Wilson, 1986). This motivates examining both
individual affective attributes and within-meme consistency, without treating either as a direct measurement
of response appropriateness.
The analysis asks whether affective variables and cross-modal consistency explain additional variation in
log-transformed likes beyond the available controls. Likes are an engagement proxy affected by exposure,
posting time, audience, and platform mechanisms.

Adjusted R2 increases from 0.051882 with controls to 0.056656 after sentiment and facial-expression indicators

is not statistically significant in the full Imgflip model, and several emotion coefficients differ across platforms.
These patterns motivate testing affective representations in retrieval, but they do not establish a universal
preference for emotional consistency. The regressions also do not evaluate query–response compatibility:
their consistency variables describe relationships within a meme.
5. CARE: Conversational Affective Response Retrieval Framework
We propose CARE (Conversational Affective Response Retrieval), a two-stage framework (Figure 4).
CARE-Retriever augments CLIP image embeddings with automatically extracted emotion descriptors. CARE-
Reranker combines query–image interactions with emotion features and uses engagement-based ranking
supervision.
Figure 4 organizes the workflow into offline annotation and dataset construction (Phase 1), sequential
retriever and reranker training (Phase 2), and inference with fixed parameters (Phase 3). In Phase 1,
raw records and automatically derived annotations are assembled into a structured annotated collection
Dann. Each record retains its query text, image reference, thread membership, source/comment role, and
engagement metadata, together with visual descriptions, available OCR text, character emotion descriptors,
sentiment labels, and consistency measures. Query–image pairs and emotion descriptors provide model inputs,
while thread associations and engagement metadata support weak supervision. The additional sentiment
and consistency variables are retained for exploratory analysis. The thread-organized training view D in
Algorithm 1 is derived from Dann; the two symbols denote different views of the same annotated collection.
The architecture uses the richer free-form emotion descriptors as affective inputs, rather than the seven
binary facial-expression indicators used in regression. Textual sentiment, image-level polarity, and the

5.2.3. LambdaRank Optimization
The source meme image is assigned the highest relevance label, taking precedence over the reciprocal-rank
labeling rule. For the remaining candidates in each training candidate list, candidates with matching query
text and positive like counts are ordered by decreasing numbers of likes. A candidate at positive rank r
receives a relevance label of y = 1/r, while all other candidates receive a label of zero. Ties in like counts
retain the original candidate order. The NDCG gain associated with a label y is defined as 2y −1.
To optimize the ranking order, we use a LambdaRank-weighted pairwise logistic objective (Burges et al.,
2006). For a pair of documents di and dj where di is more relevant than dj (i.e., yi > yj), the gradients are
scaled by the change in NDCG (∆NDCG) that would result from swapping their positions.
The pairwise loss is weighted by ∆NDCG:

larger effects on the chosen ranking metric. It relies on the weak relevance labels defined above.
6. Experimental Evaluation and Analysis
6.1. Evaluation Protocol
The Douban evaluation uses 885 query records randomly sampled from the test split, while the Imgflip
evaluation uses 1,000 query records. The full candidate pools contain 4,108 test images for Douban and
15,742 for Imgflip. During reranking, the reranker reorders the top 100 candidates returned by the first-stage
retriever. Results are averaged over the sampled query records.
Each query corresponds to the textual context of a main post and may be associated with multiple thread-
associated meme records, including the source meme and comment memes in the same thread. Therefore,
we adopt a multi-positive evaluation protocol. Unlike one-to-one image–text retrieval, conversational
meme retrieval is open-ended: a retrieval is considered successful if at least one meme from the ground-truth
response set Gq is retrieved in the top-K results.

differs from multi-positive recall, which measures the fraction of all relevant items recovered:

6.2. Dataset Splitting
To evaluate generalization across conversational contexts, we split the benchmark by topic rather than
by individual comment. Douban contains 951 training topics and 885 test topics, totaling 1,836 topics;
the test proportion is 48.20%. Imgflip contains 2,997 training topics and 750 test topics, totaling 3,747
topics; the test proportion is 20.02%.
15


6.3. Implementation Details
CARE uses Chinese-CLIP (Yang et al., 2023) for candidate retrieval on Douban and CLIP ViT-B/32
(Radford et al., 2021) on Imgflip, with jina-reranker-m0 for fine-grained response ranking.
• CARE-Retriever Training: The retriever is fine-tuned for 5 epochs with a batch size of 128. We use
Adam with a shared learning rate of 5 × 10−6 for the pretrained retrieval backbone and the affective
fusion parameters. The emotion extraction module and BERT parameters are frozen; the emotion
projection and scalar gate are trainable.
• CARE-Reranker Training: The reranker freezes the pretrained cross-encoder backbone and updates
only the scoring head and emotion adapter. It is trained with Adam using a learning rate of 1 × 10−6
and the LambdaRank objective (Burges et al., 2006) for up to 10 epochs. Numerical relevance labels
are constructed as described in Section 5.2.3. The source meme is assigned the highest relevance label
to preserve its role as the original visual context. The head-only ablation trains the Jina scoring head
without emotion features.
6.4. Baselines
We compare our method against the following retrieval baselines:
• VSE++ (Faghri et al., 2018): A classic approach learning a common embedding space with hard
negative mining.
• CLIP (Radford et al., 2021): Evaluated in both zero-shot and fine-tuned settings, using Chinese-
CLIP on Douban and CLIP ViT-B/32 on Imgflip.
• BLIP (Li et al., 2022): A unified vision-language pre-training framework with strong retrieval
capabilities.
• IEF (Zhang et al., 2025): An emotion-infused multimodal recommendation framework.
The baselines use image and text inputs, while IEF and CARE additionally use affective information. The
complete CARE system also includes a trained reranker. Consequently, the main comparison evaluates
complete systems rather than isolating emotion-feature fusion under matched architecture and training.
6.5. Main Results
Table 6 presents the retrieval performance on the Douban (Chinese) and Imgflip (English) test sets. Here,
CARE denotes our complete two-stage framework. All values are reported as percentages (%), and the best
results are highlighted in bold.
CARE achieves Hit@10 of 24.03% on Douban and 49.33% on Imgflip. Relative to the fine-tuned CLIP
values in Table 6, these are absolute increases of 0.87 and 8.26 percentage points, corresponding to relative
improvements of 3.8% and 20.1%. On Imgflip, CARE also exceeds the strongest non-CARE comparator,
BLIP, by 8.13 points at Hit@10. Across the two datasets, CARE attains the best result in fifteen of the
sixteen reported metric columns. CARE leads on the reported early-rank metrics, while fine-tuned CLIP
retains higher Hit@50 on Douban.
The gains are most pronounced at the top of the ranking, which is the operating range that matters for a
response-suggestion system. On Douban, Hit@1 rises from 10.40% to 13.63% (a 31.1% relative improvement)
and Precision@3 from 7.9% to 10.13% (+28.2%). On Imgflip, Hit@3 increases from 26.5% to 38.27% (+44.4%)
and Precision@10 from 8.16% to 10.77% (+32.0%). Because a deployed meme assistant typically presents
only a handful of candidates, lifting the first few ranks has greater practical value than marginal changes at
deep cutoffs, and the Precision@K results confirm that the promoted items are themselves labeled positives,
evidence that reranking surfaces new positives in the top ranks.
The comparison with IEF evaluates two affect-aware systems. At Hit@10, IEF underperforms fine-tuned
CLIP on both collections (for example, Imgflip: 33.47% versus 41.07%), whereas CARE reaches 49.33%.

Figure 5 presents two selected retrieval examples. For “Joker flames Batman,” CLIP retrieves flame

6.8. Cross-Platform Synthesis: From Engagement Signals to Retrieval Behavior
The exploratory regression and the retrieval experiments target different outcomes: variation in log-likes
and thread-associated ranking, respectively. Engagement is the regression outcome and also contributes to
reranker supervision. Their findings can inform the retrieval design, but the regression is not an independent
validation of the retrieval mechanism.
First, semantic consistency is positively associated with engagement in both full regression models
(Douban: 0.9354; Imgflip: 0.2599, both p < 0.001), and adding the cross-modal blocks raises adjusted R2
from 0.0519 to 0.0656 on Douban and from 0.1097 to 0.1178 on Imgflip. In the retrieval experiments, the
strongest comparators are semantic vision–language models. CARE uses the CLIP embedding as a residual
input and adds a weighted emotion representation. These findings motivate retaining semantic information
alongside affective features; they do not independently validate the gate or guarantee preservation of semantic
neighborhoods.
Second, the reported affective associations differ between collections. Affective consistency is significant
on Douban (β = 0.2414, p < 0.001) but not on Imgflip (β = 0.0064, n.s.); its base rates are 0.50 and 0.82,
respectively. Individual emotion coefficients also differ, with Disgust positive on Douban (0.3128) and Angry
negative on Imgflip (−0.0605). The reranker’s numerical margins over the head-only control likewise differ
between collections. These are different affective channels: facial-expression indicators and within-meme
consistency in regression, and richer emotion descriptors in retrieval. Their results should not be mapped
one-to-one or interpreted as proof that the gate adapts optimally to platform differences.
Dataset structure also matters when interpreting absolute scores. The Imgflip candidate pool is 3.83
times larger (15,742 versus 4,108 images), and its threads contain 22.72 memes on average versus 7.29 on
Douban. Under the evaluation protocol, hit rates depend on both candidate-pool composition and the size of
each positive set Gq. Language, platform conventions, collection period, and thread structure vary together
here. The two collections therefore provide evaluations in different settings, without isolating the causes of
their performance differences or demonstrating cross-platform transfer.
7. Discussion and Limitations
7.1. Principal Findings
The study yields four main observations. First, under the topic-held-out protocol, the complete CARE
system leads in fifteen of sixteen reported metric columns, with a Douban Hit@50 trade-off. Second, CARE-
Retriever exceeds fine-tuned CLIP at all evaluated cutoffs, and CARE-Reranker exceeds the trained head-only
control in both collections. These comparisons support the evaluated configurations without isolating every
component. Third, LambdaRank supervision combining likes and source-image priority improves Main-Post
Recall; on Imgflip, MR@10 more than doubles relative to retrieval alone. Fourth, the exploratory affective
associations and the reranking margins differ between collections. Their agreement motivates further study
of affect-aware retrieval rather than establishing a universal emotion rule.

7.2. Implications for Affect-Aware Retrieval Design
The results suggest design considerations for further evaluation. A semantic retrieval backbone can be
combined with a weighted affective representation, as in CARE’s residual fusion. Multi-positive learning
accommodates the thread-based label structure, although thread membership is incomplete relevance
supervision. The two-stage design restricts cross-encoding to the top 100 candidates; the Douban results
illustrate that gains at early ranks can accompany losses at deeper cutoffs. Likes provide scalable ranking
labels, but exposure and audience effects limit their interpretation as measures of response quality.
7.3. Practical Implications and Research Resources
CARE offers a potential design for response-suggestion applications. The retrieval backbone is fine-tuned
together with the emotion projection and gate, while the emotion extraction module and BERT remain
frozen. During reranker training, the pretrained cross-encoder backbone is frozen, and only its scoring head
and emotion adapter are updated. Restricting cross-encoding to the top-100 candidates limits the number
of reranker evaluations per query; latency, memory, and throughput are not measured here. Applying this
pipeline to stickers, emoji, or short-video replies would require further validation. The benchmark provides
98,506 thread-organized records across 5,583 topics and two languages, with automatic affective annotations,
engagement labels, and a topic-held-out evaluation protocol.
7.4. Semantic Relevance and Affective Compatibility
Emotion similarity is one component of relevance and is not sufficient on its own. Memes expressing
the same emotion can concern unrelated events, and an appropriate reply may convey a different emotion
from the query, as in reassurance or irony. CARE combines the semantic embedding with an emotion
representation and uses query–image interactions in reranking. Further work could examine calibrated
context and affect scores, query-conditioned fusion, and hard negatives that share emotion but differ in
context. These extensions could help distinguish semantic relevance from affective compatibility.
7.5. Limitations
Weak relevance and engagement labels. Thread membership is incomplete supervision, and likes are influenced
by exposure and audience composition. The source image is assigned the highest rank by design, so Main-Post
Recall gains measure recovery of the conversational anchor rather than superior comment-response selection.
The topic-held-out protocol separates training and test discussions, but does not establish isolation of
near-duplicate images or shared templates across topics. Human assessment of semantic relevance, affective
compatibility, and conversational usefulness, together with response-only evaluation, remains future work.
Automatic annotation. Retrieval emotion descriptors may draw on captions and contextual cues as well
as facial expressions. Regression instead uses seven facial-expression indicators, together with sentiment
and consistency variables. Both kinds of automatic annotation can contain errors. Naming the models and
releasing the zero-shot prompts in Appendix A makes the annotation procedure more transparent and
auditable, but does not guarantee deterministic outputs or annotation accuracy. An independent human
annotation audit remains future work.
Platform and statistical scope. Douban and Imgflip differ in language, platform conventions, and collection
period, so the separate evaluations do not demonstrate cross-lingual transfer or isolate cultural effects. The
exploratory blockwise regressions do not account for topic-level dependence or identify causal effects. The
results describe associations and ranking performance within the two reported settings; their generalization
to other platforms and periods remains to be evaluated.
21


7.6. Future Research Directions
Four directions follow directly from this study. The first is human-centered validation: collecting graded
judgments of semantic relevance, affective compatibility, and conversational usefulness, and adding response-
only evaluation that excludes the source meme. The second is architectural refinement along the path
identified above: query-conditioned gates, disentangled context/affect scores, and emotion-sharing hard
negatives. The third is cross-platform and cross-lingual transfer, including shared multilingual encoders and
explicit modeling of cultural affect differences. The fourth broadens the learning signal: temporal drift in
meme usage, larger platform networks, multimodal large language models as rerankers, and quasi-experimental
designs that move the engagement analysis from association toward causation.
8. Conclusion
We presented CARE, a two-stage conversational meme retrieval framework combining gated emotion
descriptors, multi-positive contrastive learning, and engagement-supervised reranking. Under the reported
topic-held-out protocol, CARE leads in fifteen of sixteen metric columns and achieves Hit@10 of 24.03% on
Douban and 49.33% on Imgflip. Its largest gains occur at early ranks, while fine-tuned CLIP retains higher
Hit@50 on Douban. The trained head-only comparison is consistent with an additional benefit from the
emotion-adapter configuration. The current experiments do not isolate every architectural contribution or
establish perceived conversational usefulness.
The thread-organized benchmark and evaluated pipeline provide a basis for further research on affect-aware
response retrieval. Human judgments of relevance and usefulness, matched complete-system comparisons,
query-conditioned fusion, and cross-lingual transfer are directions for future validation.

Appendix A. LLM Prompts for Affective Meme Extraction
Doubao Vision Pro is used for OCR, emotion annotation, and visual descriptions on Douban, while
Gemini Pro is used for these tasks on Imgflip.
Please use English to analyze the provided meme image and output the results in JSON format
containing the following seven parts:
1. Visual Description (description): Provide a detailed textual description of the visual content
of the meme, without providing interpretations.
2. Character Emotions (emotions, emotion_labels): In the same annotation pass, provide two
distinct character-level fields: a free-form emotion descriptor for retrieval and a
facial-expression label for regression:
- emotions: A list of concise English emotion descriptors inferred from the full meme context for
retrieval. These descriptors are not restricted to a predefined vocabulary and may capture
nuanced reactions such as amused, skeptical, sarcastic, disappointed, or worried.
- emotion_labels: A list of facial-expression labels for regression, selected only from these
seven lowercase English categories: happy, calm, angry, sad, fear, disgust, surprise. Select
the single best-fitting category based on each character’s facial expression; this label is
not a categorical equivalent of the richer contextual emotion descriptor. The label calm
denotes a neutral or relaxed facial expression and must not be used as a fallback for
uncertainty.
Keep the two lists aligned in reading order: entries at the same index must refer to the same
character appearance. Retain repeated descriptors and labels for different character
appearances. If no character emotion can be identified, return an empty list for both fields.
3. Image Sentiment Polarity (image_sentiment): Indicates the overall emotional inclination
expressed by the meme image. Values are:
- 1: Positive sentiment
- 0: Neutral sentiment
- -1: Negative sentiment
4. Theme (theme): Explain the theme or core idea that this meme image intends to convey.
5. Text Sentiment Polarity (text_sentiment): Indicates the emotional inclination conveyed by the
meme text, with the same values as above:
- 1: Positive sentiment
- 0: Neutral sentiment
- -1: Negative sentiment
6. Usage Scenarios (usage_scenarios): Describe the scenarios in which this meme image is typically
used.
7. OCR Text (ocr_text): Transcribe the readable text in the image in its original language,
preserving line breaks. Do not translate or invent text. Return an empty string if no text is
readable.
Please ensure the output strictly follows the following JSON structure:
{
"description": "...",
"emotions": ["amused", "worried"],
"emotion_labels": ["happy", "fear"],
"image_sentiment": 1,

Output only the pure JSON result without any additional explanation.
