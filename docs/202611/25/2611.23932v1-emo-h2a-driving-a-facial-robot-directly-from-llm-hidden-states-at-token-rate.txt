Emo-H2A: Driving a Facial Robot Directly from LLM Hidden States at
Token Rate
Yige Liu1, Jingcheng Huang1, Hao Sun1, Min Li1,∗, and Zhouping Yin1
Abstract— Diverse,
semantically
synchronized,
and
low-
latency facial expressions are essential for human-robot inter-
action. While large language models (LLMs) have endowed
anthropomorphic robots with fluent speech, facial expression
still struggles to follow the conversation. Motivated by this gap,
we present Emo-H2A (hidden-to-actuation), a paradigm that
drives a facial robot directly from LLM hidden states during
autoregressive decoding. Without a separate emotion label,
expression stays continuous and context-sensitive, and the face
moves with the unfolding reply. We instantiate this paradigm as
EmoManifold, a training-free method that extracts a valence–
arousal–dominance (VAD) coordinate from each hidden state,
projects it, and drives the face at token granularity. The
readout is checked on six language models, and the same
projection drives our 29-degree-of-freedom (DoF) facial robot.
When driven by this projection, the face shifts its expression
in the matching direction on 87.5% of within-reply valence
transitions—whereas holding a single label for the whole reply
leaves directional tracking near the 50% chance level—and first
moves in 0.96 s rather than waiting 10–26 s for the reply to
finish and a turn-level label to return.
I. INTRODUCTION
In face-to-face communication, humans perceive spoken
words and facial expressions as an inseparable composite.
To reproduce this natural affinity in embodied systems,
anthropomorphic platforms with high-DoF physical actuation
are actively being developed [3], [4], while modern LLMs
provide the linguistic engine for fluent dialogue. Yet, despite
these advances, modern conversational robots remain funda-
mentally desynchronized: the facial robot falls out of step
with the affective nuance of its own speech.
This desynchronization stems from treating emotion as an
extrinsic label rather than an endogenous signal. In existing
pipelines, an utterance opening with sympathy and closing
with encouragement is assigned a single categorical tag,
appearing only after the full reply has been generated [1],
[5]. Recent prompt-engineering and multi-stage scheduling
approaches attempt to develop intra-utterance trajectories by
matching generated segments to expression presets [2]. How-
ever, this strategy introduces complex prompt designs and
additional parsing passes that substantially increase inference
latency. As a result, conventional frameworks struggle to
balance response latency against affective granularity, high-
lighting the need to move beyond label-mediated emotion
generation.

Affective diversity presents another major hurdle. Tra-
ditional discrete emotion categories correspond to only a
handful of static motor templates, which fail to match the
rich and nuanced expressive capabilities of modern language
models. Consequently, an urgent need exists for a continu-
ous affective space capable of modeling fine-grained emo-
tional transitions. Recent interpretability studies find a low-
dimensional affective manifold in LLM hidden states, with
directions resembling valence, dominance, and arousal [6].
The emergence of this VAD-like geometry suggests that
LLMs can provide the representational foundation for con-
tinuous facial affect modeling.
To address these challenges, we introduce Emo-H2A,
a paradigm that couples the LLM’s autoregressive hidden
states at each generated token directly to facial actuator
commands. We instantiate this paradigm as EmoManifold,
an online framework that projects token-level hidden repre-
sentations onto a continuous VAD subspace derived from
the model’s unembedding vectors and maps the resulting
affective coordinates to physical motor commands. Physical
deployment on our 29-DoF facial robot demonstrates that
EmoManifold follows within-utterance affective shifts with
low initial response latency, achieving an 83.5% preference
rate from human observers over both a static expression held
for the entire utterance and a temporally shuffled control.
To foster further research, we will open-source our code-
base and evaluation benchmarks. In summary, our main
contributions are:
• Emo-H2A: a paradigm that maps the hidden state at
each decoding step to facial motor commands, without
a separate emotion label.
• EmoManifold: a training-free method that builds a
VAD subspace, projects each token’s hidden state onto
it, and drives the face from that coordinate.
• Physical evaluation: On our 29-DoF facial robot,
token-level actuation tracks within-reply transitions
above chance, where turn-level baselines stay near
chance, and observers prefer the token-level motion over
a static expression.

Within this generation, data-driven visual imitation became
prominent: Emo [3] trains self-supervised vision-to-motor
models on a 26-actuator facial robot to anticipate and
coexpress human facial movements, while X2C [4] maps
observed human faces onto 30 continuous actuator channels
on the Ameca anthropomorphic robot. Although these plat-

signals are driven by external visual mimicry rather than
conversational semantics. To align nonverbal behavior with
spoken dialogue on anthropomorphic robots, recent systems
integrate LLMs into the expression pipeline. FurChat [5],
SAFE [9], Nadine [10], and Mishra et al. [1] query or prompt
LLMs to attach discrete emotion tags or nonverbal cue
bundles to completed replies, whereas Expressive Furhat [11]
and Xpress [2] generate finer-grained gesture tags, intensity
levels, or keyframe schedules via structured prompting. How-
ever, all existing LLM-driven pipelines treat expression as
an extrinsic symbolic output generated or parsed after text
decoding, whereas Emo-H2A treats affect as an endogenous
signal read out at each decoding step.
B. Affective Representations in Expressive Robotics
How affective states are represented fundamentally deter-
mines the granularity and smoothness of robotic facial con-
trol. Historically, social robotics has relied predominantly on
discrete categorical models—such as Ekman’s basic emotion
categories [8]—to index static motor presets or turn-level di-
alogue classifiers [1], [5], [10]. While straightforward to cali-
brate on hardware, discrete labels impose coarse quantization
and abrupt transitions that cannot capture subtle within-
utterance emotional shifts. To enable continuous modulation,
expressive robotics turned to dimensional affect models from
psychology, notably Russell’s two-dimensional circumplex
model of valence and arousal [12] and Russell and Mehra-


bian’s three-factor VAD space [13], later operationalized at
scale by the NRC-VAD lexicon [14]. Early platforms such as
Kismet [7] interpolated postures within a designer-specified
three-dimensional space of arousal, valence, and stance. Yet
in contemporary LLM-based robots, continuous affective
coordinates still rely on post-hoc classifiers or explicit scalar
prompting [2]. Parallel mechanistic interpretability research
reveals that LLMs inherently encode linear affective geom-
etry within their latent spaces: Tigges et al. [15] extract a
linear sentiment direction from residual-stream activations
using contrastive sentence pairs, and Reichman et al. [6]
uncover a cross-layer emotional manifold that allows latent
emotion steering without altering surface semantics. Whereas
prior interpretability studies recover affective directions by
running forward passes over labeled or contrastive corpora
or by training auxiliary modules, EmoManifold reads its
VAD axes from the unembedding matrix, without training
an auxiliary module.
III. METHOD
The face changes with the tokens of a reply, instead of
waiting for one label (Fig. 1), and EmoManifold carries
each decoding step to a facial command before the reply
is finished (Fig. 2). This section first constructs the affective
axes (Section III-A); next, it projects each token’s hidden
state onto those axes (Section III-B); finally, it maps the
coordinates to actuator commands (Section III-C). The im-
plementation follows three principles:
• Expression-Rich: Use a continuous space of expres-
sions to capture slight affective changes.
• Token-Level: Update the face at a token rate.
• Direct-Readout: Derive the facial command from one
projection, and introduce no reasoning step that would
add latency.

facial actuator commands in two steps. First, the coordinates
are mapped through a basic-expression space into blend
weights (expression mapping). Second, the weights are fused
into servo commands with hardware-oriented smoothing and
rate limiting (hardware actuation).

IV. EXPERIMENTS
We design experiments to address three core questions:
• Q1. Within-Utterance Tracking: Does the physical
face reproduce the affective trajectory read token by
token from the hidden states?
• Q2. Response Timing: How much earlier does the face
move when driven during decoding rather than after the
reply?
• Q3. Affective Readout: Does the coordinate read the
situation, or only the words?
A. Experimental Setup
Physical evaluation uses the 29-DoF facial robot in Fig. 3,
controlled by an on-board Intel N100 industrial PC (4-
core CPU, no discrete GPU) over a serial port. Unless
noted otherwise, all experiments use Qwen2.5-0.5B with
offline affective axes and calibration parameters built from
50 English pole words per pole (Section III-A).
B. Main Comparison
1) Actuation Fidelity: This experiment evaluates physical
tracking of token-rate affective commands. Twenty replies
are generated once by Qwen2.5-0.5B and recorded in one
session under a fixed camera pose across five conditions.
EmoManifold updates valence, arousal, and the facial target
once per token. The four baselines share the identical reply
text: Mishra et al. [1] assign one of six categories to the
finished reply and hold it for the turn using their original
prompt; Xpress-Turn [2] holds one category and intensity
for the turn; Xpress-Story [2] synthesizes speech, splits the
reply at narrative or emotional turns, and plays one mapped
preset per segment from its word-level timestamp; and the
shuffled control replays EmoManifold’s per-token commands
in randomized order, preserving command amplitudes while
removing temporal order. Category calls use GPT-4o-mini for
the two turn-level baselines and GPT-4o for Xpress-Story.
While the robot acts out each reply, a fixed camera records
the face and Py-Feat [20] estimates frame-by-frame valence
and arousal independently of our pipeline. Because Py-Feat
is trained on human faces, we place its readings on the
commanded scale by fitting one linear map per dimension on
40 turn-level static segments from Mishra et al. and Xpress-
Turn, then freezing both maps across all dynamic trials. We
define the commanded trace as the token-level valence and
arousal trajectory sent to the actuators by EmoManifold, and
score the calibrated camera readout of all five conditions
against this same trace using a fixed 0.5-s actuation lag.
The primary metric is event-level directional agreement:
within each 2-s sliding window, an event is flagged when
commanded valence shifts by more than 0.3 or commanded
arousal by more than 0.2, and a hit is recorded if the
calibrated camera readout shifts in the same direction (50%
chance level). As a secondary metric, we report the R2

E. Robustness
This experiment tests whether the display pipeline pre-
serves within-utterance command order across smoothing
rates and prototype counts. We replay the pipeline on the 20
recorded command trajectories, read each frame-rate output
back onto the VA plane, and score it against the token-level
command using the same event thresholds and windows.
At the default setting (α=0.6, P=13), replay gives 84.4%
valence and 67.7% arousal agreement, closely matching the
camera measurements (87.5% and 69.6%) and validating
replay as a proxy for physical execution. Time-shuffled


commands passed through the same pipeline fall to 44%–
48% valence agreement (40%–45% arousal), confirming that
the metric remains sensitive to command order.
We then sweep nine settings formed by crossing three
smoothing rates (α ∈{0.3, 0.6, 0.9}) with three prototype
sets—the default P=13 and two P=7 constructions that each
retain a different prototype per emotion class—under the
same per-frame rate limit. Across all nine settings, valence
agreement stays within 75%–87% and arousal agreement
within 68%–74%, well above the shuffled range, showing
that temporal command order survives the display pipeline
across parameter choices.
F. Human Study
To evaluate human perception, we conduct two silent-
video questionnaires with 20 participants (10 items each)
assessing expression recognition and dynamic preference.
Each item displays the full sentence text before playing the
unsubtitled video; participants gave informed consent, and
all responses were anonymous.
In the recognition task, participants classify each video
into one of the seven basic expressions, compared against
hand-tuned static references recorded at the same camera
pose. EmoManifold achieves 85.5% accuracy versus 90.5%
for the hand-tuned references (Wilcoxon p=0.013), with both
showing identical confusion patterns between neighboring
pairs (fear–surprise and sadness–disgust) inherent to the
robot’s facial mechanics.
In the preference task, participants make a three-way
choice on sentences containing an intra-utterance emotional
shift, selecting which video best matches the emotional
progression: EmoManifold’s dynamic expression, a static
expression held for the whole sentence, or the shuffled-
order control. EmoManifold is chosen on 83.5% of trials,
compared with 11.5% for the shuffled control and 5.0%
for the static expression, with every participant preferring
EmoManifold over both alternatives (one-sided Wilcoxon
over participants, p < 10−6). Expression recognition stays
within five percentage points of the hand-tuned references,
and observers match the token-level video to emotional shifts
inside the sentence.
V. DISCUSSION
Collectively, our offline readout and physical hardware
evaluations demonstrate that continuous facial expressions
can be driven directly from LLM hidden states. The projected
coordinates correlate consistently with lexicon ratings across
six language models in both English and Chinese, and
respond to shifts in situational context even when surface
wording is held constant. When mapped through the FKCN
and expression presets onto the physical robot, these tra-
jectories are reliably recovered by external camera tracking
and preferred by human observers over both static turn-
level expressions and time-shuffled controls. Compared with
discrete emotion labels inferred only after a reply completes,
the principal advantage is temporal: affective dynamics are
rendered concurrently with language generation rather than
deferred until the end of the utterance.

studies use silent video, and the current system does not
drive audio-synchronized lip motion. Because mouth degrees
of freedom must serve both articulation and expression,
a naive superposition of the two can cause mechanical
interference. A direct next step is to apply the same
hidden-state readout to open-source speech-native models
and project affect along the audio-token stream, so facial
updates share the playback clock while lip articulation runs
as a complementary controller on that timeline.
Second, the face hardware and the interaction policy both
constrain what can be shown. Discrimination among neigh-
boring emotions is bounded by the robot’s physical DoFs and
preset expression basis. More critically, directly externalizing
an internal affective state can be socially inappropriate: nat-
ural human interaction often requires suppressing, softening,
or delaying expression, whereas the current pipeline renders
the signal immediately upon readout. We therefore plan a
lightweight, optional regulation stage between projection and
actuation—gain limiting, temporal hold, and an explicit off
switch—so deployment can choose when the internal signal
is shown, softened, or suppressed.
Third, the physical and human studies remain modest
in scale across corpus size, participant count, and tested
backbones. The closed-loop physical actuation (Section IV-
B) and the human study (Section IV-F) use only Qwen2.5-
0.5B. Because the subspace and context-sensitivity exper-
iments already show comparable geometry and sensitivity
on Llama-3.2-3B, we concentrated hardware and human-
evaluation resources on the deployment model. Repeating
the full closed-loop protocol on a second backbone, with a
larger participant pool, is the direct next measurement step
along the same evaluation pipeline.
VI. CONCLUSION
This paper shows that a facial robot need not rely on a
discrete emotion label: what a model will express in words
or audio is already computed in its hidden states before
the output is rendered. Emo-H2A takes this as its premise,
locating the relevant hidden states, extracting the affective
signal—whether by subspace projection, as in EmoManifold,
or by a learned network—and running actuation in paral-
lel with generation rather than after it. Continuous VAD
coordinates are read from an LLM’s hidden states during
streaming decoding, with the valence–arousal plane driving
physical expression at token granularity. Across experiments,
the training-free pipeline yields a context-sensitive readout
(∆V = −0.19 under sarcasm), tracks commanded intra-
utterance transitions on a facial robot (87.5% valence and
69.6% arousal directional agreement, against near-chance
baselines and with 83.5% human preference), and reduces
on-board first-update latency to 0.96 s, compared with 9.9–
93.5 s for local baselines. These results offer a practical


route beyond turn-level control toward fast, fine-grained, and
verifiable embodied affective interaction.
