Trajeform: Embedding Crowd Data
Marilena Lemonari
, Panayiotis Charalambous
, Yiorgos Chrysanthou
Abstract—Crowd simulation aims to generate agent behaviours
that are rich, natural, and appropriate to their surrounding
environment. Recent advances in Machine Learning have enabled
such behavioural patterns to be learned directly from trajectory
data, allowing simulation models to capture increasingly complex
crowd dynamics. However, the capabilities of these systems remain
strongly dependent on the type and diversity of the data used
during training. When evaluating how well crowd simulators
generalise to real-world datasets, most existing metrics focus
primarily on trajectory-level differences rather than behaviour-
level similarity. In this paper, we present Trajeform, a framework
that trains a reconstruction VAE (Variational Autoencoder) on
real trajectory data to learn a meaningful continuous embedding
that preserves behavioural characteristics of real crowds. The
trained encoder can then be applied to synthetic trajectories
generated by simulators and used to compare the resulting
embedding distributions. Additionally, the learned embedding
can be used to analyse supplementary data, such as trajectories
corresponding to a simulator’s principal behaviours or captured
data from controlled environments (e.g., cycling), providing
insight into their ability to span the real behavioural embedding
space. The framework compares data from three simulators
(CCP, modCCP, and RVO) against ground-truth datasets and
is evaluated quantitatively and qualitatively using latent-space
analysis, distributional metrics, and trajectory generation through
sampling from the aggregated posterior distribution. The results
indicate that the learned latent space captures meaningful
trajectory dependencies found in the real data, making the
framework useful both as an additional measure of synthetic
data believability and as a tool for informing the training of
future trajectory models.
I. INTRODUCTION
Crowd simulation aims to populate virtual environments with
realistic agent movements. Within this scope, the umbrella of
realism includes behaviour diversity, richness, and contextual
awareness, in that agents should not only move naturally, but
also behave in ways that are appropriate to the environment,
e.g., a university campus. Extensive research has achieved
advanced simulators for generating believable agents [1],
[2], [3], utilised in crowd authoring to optimise simulator
parameter spaces with respect to an authoring constraint [4],
[5]. However, most of these systems either rely on manual
parameterisation [1], or synthetic data [4]. Works that utilise
real data are often constrained by the amount and type of data
they use (single policy [6], controlled environments [7], [8]),
hence hindering generalisability. Therefore, it is imperative
to measure behavioural similarity by comparing differently
sourced agent behaviours with real data [9]. In this work, we
aim to quantify and formalise an embedding representation
to assess the quality of agent behaviours in comparison
with real ones. Such a tool enables comparing real and
simulated behaviours in the representation space, and informing
a selection of parameters spanning the real data features.
Measuring behaviour similarity properly, in the context of
crowds, is a challenging undertaking due to the conceptual
definition of a behaviour “match” and its non-deterministic
nature. Relevant works weigh hand-crafted salient features [10],
[5], perceptually validate realism [11], [12], or combine both
(perceptually-validated metric) [13]. However, these works still
focus on trajectory faithfulness, and are biased either on the
selection of trajectory features, or the expertise of user study
participants. Other works utilise entropy-based measures [14],
[15]. To compare simulated and real data, several systems [16],
[4] report metrics on trajectory features such as Deviation
From Goal (DFG), and Optimal Path (OP) [17]. Even though
these metrics are widely used and can give strong indications
regarding the distribution of trajectory features in the two
groups (generated and real), they are more tailored to trajectory
features in an aggregated manner (e.g., DFG for all agents
at every timestep); these partially capture behavioural aspects
(turning left and turning right contain the same behaviour but
lead to different paths) or limit controllability. We aim to
investigate learnable features to complement the assembly of
metrics used for comparing between two sets of behaviours.
In this paper, we introduce Trajeform, a trajectory-based
framework that learns an embedding space directly from real
crowd trajectory data. The learned latent distribution serves as a
baseline, against which synthetic trajectories (e.g., generated by
crowd simulators) can be compared. Synthetic trajectories are
projected onto the learned latent space, allowing their inferred
distributions to be juxtaposed with the real-data embedding
distribution. The framework preserves the properties of a
VAE latent space while encouraging behaviourally similar
trajectories to cluster together. Since the model is trained
through trajectory reconstruction, the decoder can additionally
be used to project latent embeddings back into the trajectory
space. Trajeform primarily aims to project crowd data onto
an embedding space tailored to real behavioural features. To
achieve this, the framework operates on canonicalised trajecto-
ries aligned during preprocessing, encouraging the model to
focus on behavioural characteristics beyond simple positional
attributes such as spawn location or orientation. Consequently,
the learned embedding space captures more behaviour-relevant
representations. Beyond measuring behaviour similarity, the
framework can also provide indications regarding how effec-
tively a simulator spans the real-data behavioural manifold,
while additionally informing simulator parameter selections
towards distributions more consistent with the arrangement
of real data in the latent space. We evaluate the proposed
framework through comparisons with established quantitative
metrics, complemented by graphical analyses of the learned
embedding distributions.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Trajectory-based Metrics
To compare simulations—either with synthetic or real
data—various quantitative metrics have been proposed. At the
macroscopic level, the Fundamental Diagram [18] is widely
used, relating speed, density, and flow to capture global
crowd phenomena. At the microscopic level, metrics focus
on per-trajectory features such as deviation from goals, path
optimality, and distance to nearest neighbours, which can
indicate behaviours like grouping. Another approach compares
distributions of trajectory features, such as speed and position.
Several crowd simulators leverage fundamental diagrams and
low-level features for validation [16], [1], [6]. However, such
metrics are more tailored to trajectory replication instead of
behaviour similarity and so they are often sensitive to outliers or
detailed motions [19], [20]. Some works benchmark evaluations,
but specifically for steering behaviours [21]. Charalambous et
al. [22] propose a method for outlier detection through a data-
driven approach, while Lerner et al. [23] perform database
matching. More recently, Wang et al. [9] employ Bayesian
inference to discover latent path patterns from trajectory data
for the analysis and comparison of crowd behaviours. Our
method is complementary to other techniques, and does not
perform inference on the trajectory space, but on a compressed
latent representation using an end-to-end architecture.
Perhaps closer to our method is Crowd Space [15] that
defines agent descriptors (Minimal Predicted Distance) and
trains a dimensionality reduction model to find the most suitable
simulator for a given real scenario. This method learns a
manifold based on the proposed agent-centric entropy [15],
[14]. In our method however, we do not specify the evaluation
metric, but instead, allow the model to learn features freely,
and derive a metric post-training, using our learned manifold.
Also, we employ a variational model, improving the sampling
and generative capabilities. Crowd Space aims to assess the
predictive accuracy of simulators to find the best simulation
method to use on new crowd scenarios. Our method focuses on
learning meaningful embeddings from real data, emphasising
representation rather than classification. Crowd Space in trained
on a larger set of real data that includes controlled and
dense environments [7], [24], whereas we aim at in-the-
wild behaviours. Finally, in Trajeform, we do not group our
trajectories according to their scenario source, e.g., University
Campus, but rather, learn a collective representation since
behaviours in two different scenarios could be the same.
B. Perceptual Evaluation
Apart from using quantitative methods to evaluate tra-
jectory and behaviour plausibility, perceptual evaluation is
also explored as a way to mitigate for the aforementioned
metric limitations. User studies have been implemented to
assess trajectory realism [25], [26], [27], [28]. O’Connor et
al. [29] conduct a study to determine whether users find crowd
behaviours to be more realistic with a Social Force model, and
its implications in psychophysical experimentation. Smaller
user studies have also been conducted as part of a larger
evaluation protocol of proposed optimisation models [4], or
authoring tools [30] asking users to report on believability
and usability. Still, user studies, especially in the domain of
virtual crowds, are highly dependent on the study design,
the experience of participants in the domain, and general
visualisation aspect of the shown stimuli, e.g., body animation,
clothing, facial animation, and environment quality [31], [32].
Daniel et al. [13] propose a quality metric that combines
comparison metrics with perceptual tools. Although existing
quantitative and qualitative evaluation approaches have been
proven useful in uncovering certain behavioural aspects of
simulated agents, in our method we compare directly with
real data, using learned features and so alleviating the need to
hand-craft quantitative metrics but also avoid the perceptual
biases. Our method can be used as an additional approach,
complementary to other metrics.
C. Trajectory Representation
Representing trajectories and trajectory-based behaviours
is also important for analysis and comparisons. Morphable
Crowds by Ju et al. [7] employs a data-driven method to build
a trajectory model and create behaviour prefabs, while GREIL
by Charalambous et al. [6] learns an inverse Reinforcement
Learning policy from real data. Pure trajectory information
usually has higher dimensionality p = (t, x, z) ∈(T, 2) and
contains low-interpretability features in terms of behavioural
characteristics, e.g., assigning behaviour labels to a distribution
of positional data is not straightforward. Therefore, multiple
works have attempted to transform pure trajectories to some
compressed representations, e.g., trajectory images [4], [33],
trajectory parameters [5]. Additional works employ learning-
based methods to encode trajectory information into high-
level parameters, e.g., combination of common behaviours [1],
and OCEAN personality attributes [34]. High-level trajectory
embeddings enhance explainability and assist comparison and
analysis, however, the extracted features are dependent on the
data used, which is often not sourced from real scenarios.
Conversely, approaches based on real data, lack controllability
and generalisability [35], especially if said data come from
controlled settings instead of in-the-wild. Here, we strive for
an embedding based on real, in-the-wild data, employing a
learning-based method to allow the model freedom to learn
appropriate behavioural and trajectory features that would be
challenging to define through traditional feature specification.


D. Crowd Data
Having crowd data is essential for improving simulation
systems, as it supports data-driven methods [23] and provides
training data for ML models. While both synthetic and real
data can be used, real datasets are preferable because they
capture complex, hard-to-replicate crowd dynamics. Such data
can be used for training (if sufficiently large) or for evaluating
new simulators [36], [15]. Crowd data typically consists of
agent trajectories, including at least 2D positions and often
orientations. Ideally, datasets would be paired, containing
ground truth annotations (e.g., behavioural descriptions), which
are crucial for supervised learning. However, collecting such
data is challenging. Trajectories are usually obtained either
through video tracking or motion capture, with data gathered
in controlled settings [7], [8] or in real environments ranging
from sparse to dense crowds [24], [37]. Due to the difficulty
of large-scale motion capture, most datasets rely on video-
based tracking. Widely used datasets include those from [38],
[39], covering environments such as a church yard, a shopping
street, and a university campus, as well as video-based datasets
like [40]. Despite these efforts, there remains a lack of
large paired datasets, limiting the ability of learning-based
approaches to extract behaviour patterns. As a result, many
works rely on synthetic data tailored to their specific needs.
III. OVERVIEW
A. Problem Definition
First, let us define some notations. Let T ⊆R2xT be the
trajectory space, where T is the maximum trajectory length,
associated with path duration which is measured in number of
frames - directly corresponding to duration (s) since timestep is
kept fixed. Also in the trajectory space, let τ t be the 2D position
at time t, with τ ∈T a trajectory point. We define τC to be
the set of category-specific trajectories, with C = {R, S, G}
denoting real, synthetic, and generated, respectively.
Our goal is to train an encoder Eθ : T →Z, subject
to parameters θ, that creates a mapping from the trajectory
space to the latent space Z ⊆RZ with latent dimensions Z,
where latent point z ∈Z. Given the trained encoder, real
data τR and simulated trajectories τf,h, we can obtain zR, zf
and zh via zi = Eθ(τi). If there are simulators f : Pf →T
and h : Ph →T , where Pi a simulator-specific parameter
space, suppose that f, h attempt to simulate the real data. Then,
comparing the distributions of zf and zh against zR, we can
provide an indication of how well they represent the real data
distribution. If τf,h are two different datasets to be used for
training a trajectory model, the latent distribution comparisons
help indicate how close the training set would be to the existing
real data - a measure analogous to class balancing. We theorise
that (i) our latent space meaningfully reflects the structure of
real data, and (ii) the properties of our latent space (continuous,
meaningful, preserving real behavioural features) make our
encoder more tailored to the real data. As part of this work, we
provide evidence of these claims via two sets of experiments
(Section VI).
We aim towards a smooth, continuous latent space, that is
generative but also has interpretable structure. This latent space,
achieved based on the trained encoder Eθ, can be used for:
• Graphical comparison of simulator data (e.g., CCP) with
the real data they attempt to simulate (e.g., commercial
street).
• Distribution comparison between the encoded simulated
and real data in the latent space.
• Graphical observation of the coverage of the embed-
ding space of differently-sourced data (e.g., principal
behaviours of CCP, or data from a controlled environment).
• Selection of training data of simulator-specific parameters.
B. Model
Our goal is to learn an embedding function subject to
trajectory information from real data. Considering the real
data size, we incorporate encoder/decoder modules that work
well on lower cardinality. Thus, we train a VAE on the task of
trajectory reconstruction, and define our learned latent space
as the desired manifold. We approach the training setup as
follows: (a) we train a VAE on reconstruction without explicitly
incorporating a prediction of the generated trajectory length,
and (b) learn an additional feed-forward model to predict the
path length [li; i ≤Lmax], from the latent space Z. We do this
since our data vary in path durations and so we help our decoder
generate more appropriate paths at inference time, by inputting
the predicted length in the form of a mask matrix. Hence, the
whole model is trained on the loss given in Equation 1, with
β = .1. The low value for β comes after experimentation,
trading off reconstruction quality and latent space faithfulness

C. Model Data
The model has been trained on a set of real data trajectories.
In this section, we give details regarding the data processing.
Real Dataset: We use existing tracked datasets corre-
sponding to trajectory data captured in a church yard, a
university campus, a commercial street, and outside a hotel,
henceforth referred to as Flock, Students, Zara, and ETH [39],
[38], [40]. The total number of trajectories for each dataset is
{24, 849, 531, 750}, respectively, sampled at a timestep = .4,
for uniformity. Before continuing to the preprocessing step,
we exclude Zara3 from our experiments dataset, and save it
for testing. Finally, the datapoints are padded according to the
maximum length, and a relevant mask is produced.
Data Preprocessing: Prior to training the model, we pass
the data through a preprocessing module whose purpose is to
canonicalise it in order to help the model learn useful features
and not be, for example, biased on the starting position. This
module includes: path segmentation, transformations, spatial
canonicalisation, and noise addition. More specifically, we
disregard paths with L < Lmin = 5, and segment paths with
L > Lmax = 104 to smaller valid parts. The transformations
include, translation to the (x,z) origin, rotation of the direction
of movement towards the right x-axis, and flipping of x and
z-axes to the first quadrant. For spatial canonicalisation, we
try both bounding the (x,z) space, and normalising according
to (z −zmean)/zstd. Finally, for noise augmentation we
randomly add noise ϵ sampled from a standard normal. The
choice for Lmax is justified by the boxplot in Figure 2 which
omits the outlier lengths for each dataset. It also shows an
example preprocessing module that includes segmentation and
transformation as mentioned. For a more detailed visualisation
of the preprocessing components, the reader is referred to the
supplementary material.
Fig. 2: Real Data Features.
We ablate the individual components of the preprocessing
module. The ablation experiments were conducted with the
same underlying model architecture and hyperparameters, for
fairness. We do not report on the model losses because the
scale is not comparable, e.g., in normalisation, lower losses
do not imply better model, or without translation, the model
achieves better performance because it learns to reconstruct not
according to the trajectory/behaviour features but according
to spawn. The final module includes the segmentation and
transformation operations, and thus is used in the inference
pipeline; note that padding and translation to origin are
mandatory. The final training set consists of ˜2k samples, and
maxLen of 104 frames.

IV. RESULTS
At inference time, we utilise the trained model to cluster
the real latents, and build a MoG distribution according to
the cluster centres and densities. We use this posterior to:
test on our control data (Zara3), infer principal trajectories
from the posterior means, and sample the distribution to
generate informed paths, hence crafting a latent similarity
metric between inference and training points.

C. Generations
Due to the variational component of our model, we can
sample the latent space and generate corresponding trajectories.
There are three main ways to sample the space: traversing the
space, sampling according to the prior (resembles standard
normal), or building some posterior distribution. Our aim is
to be able to assign meaning to the distribution of the real
behaviours in the latent space. Therefore, having the clustering
of Section IV-B, we can build a MoG with means equal to the
cluster centres and mixture weights proportional to the cluster
sizes, i.e., p(z) = π1 N(z | µ1, Σ1) + π2 N(z | µ2, Σ2) +
π3 N(z | µ3, Σ3). Supplementary material shows instances of
sampling 1k points for each method.
We can further operate on the latent space by leveraging both
the encoder and decoder modules. Specifically, assume that
we have a candidate trajectory (e.g., generated by a simulator)
that we wish to compare against a real trajectory counterpart.
The two trajectories are first projected through the trained
encoder onto the latent space, producing zs for the unseen
latent (synthetic trajectory) and z for the reference latent (real
trajectory). Their latent difference can then be computed as:
||z −zs||2.
Since the decoder is trained on real data, we can additionally
move from the latent space back to the trajectory space by
decoding the reference latent:
˜τ = D(z),
and subsequently re-projecting the reconstructed trajectory back
into the latent space:

V. USE CASES
In this section we explore three use cases. Mainly, we
compare simulator data that mimic the real, with the ground
truths through latent distribution comparisons to reveal be-
haviour similarity. Then, we test other data sources, e.g.,
principal simulator behaviours or captured data from controlled
environments, to inspect how well the span the latent space, and
lastly, use the distribution of the real latents to tune simulator-
specific parameters in order to guide simulations towards more
“real-data” balanced generations.
A. Behaviour Similarity
We show a use case of our framework, essentially showing
the latents of differently generated synthetic data and comparing
to see which is closer to the real data latent MoG.
Firstly, we gather synthetic data from three sources that
attempt to mimic the real data behaviours. Specifically RVO,
CCP, and MPACT [3], [1], [4]; for more details on the re-
spective simulators/methods, please refer to the supplementary
material. The parameters for RVO are set to match the real
data, whereas CCP denotes the behaviour produced when
manually setting a single CCP profile that best matches the
real data by inspection (both reported in [4]). The latter,
modCCP, refers to the modCCP behaviour profiles inferred and
optimised through the MPACT model. Henceforth, we refer to
the optimised modCCP weights as MPACT, for consistency
and readability; other references to modCCP imply controlled
profiles of the simulator itself - not optimised by the MPACT
model. Therefore, we expect that our metric based on MoG
latent distributions, is better for MPACT trajectories.
Each set of simulator-specific trajectories is passed though
our pipeline and juxtaposed against the real latents. Then, a
new posterior distribution can be defined according to the
new clustering, and is compared to the original. Figure 7
demonstrates the plotted latents for each inference dataset.
Figure 7 also shows the visualisations per-simulator, along with
the new distribution means. From the figures, we can observe
that the MPACT latents are closest to the real ones, while RVO
values tend to be confined and not access the whole latent
space. This could be due to the limited behavioural variability
of the simulator itself, since its target use is for generating

a latent metric. Users can also sample the learned space to

B. Embedding Space Coverage
We can also use the model on data that do not simulate
the real scenarios. In that case, the model is used as a way to
visualise how spread the inputs are in the embedding space.
For instance, inputting only goal, group and interaction-
specific behaviours from modCCP weights (e.g., {wg =
1, wgr = 0, wi = 0}), as shown in Figure 12, shows how
well the simulator’s principal behaviours can cover the real
behaviour space. We remind that this experiment references
modCCP and not MPACT, since the profiles used are controlled
(principal behaviours), and not optimised by the MPACT model.
From Figure 12, it can be seen that the grouping and interaction
behaviours have major overlap in the embedding space, which
is expected since the dynamics are similar, with the main
difference being in the environment context (proximity to POIs).
We observe then that these principal behaviours alone, do not
span the range of the real behaviours, and so a mixture of
them is necessary for obtaining a dataset that can be used, for
example, for training models on synthetic data.
Another functionality of our embedding is testing the data
projected far from the learned distribution. It is interesting
to consider trajectories from a controlled environment, e.g.,
people instructed to follow a single line [45], [46], which is
rare to find in in-the-wild data. Figure 9 shows that indeed the
embeddings are outside of the learned MoG, and also shows
that they are clustered together which is expected since this
corridor scenario “forces” behaviours to be very similar.

C. Guiding the Generations
Provided the posterior distribution of the real data, and a
corresponding empirical distribution based on data from a
specific simulator, e.g., MPACT [4], we can further explore
the functionalities of our system by editing the observed
distribution. Figure 11 shows an example of editing the MPACT
distribution, specifically shifting one of its inferred cluster
centres to coincide with that of the real data. Since the
PCA operation is reversible, we can inspect the latent space
visualisation (in 2D) and edit accordingly. Then, the new
informed distribution incorporates the new cluster mean, and
so we can sample from it (with the unedited cluster’s frequency
and covariance), as shown in Figure 11-right. The latent
space “trajeform” metric, decreases from 1.867 (as reported in
Table IV) to 1.254, implying that visually editing the observed
latents to have bigger overlap with the trained latents, translates
into higher distribution similarity with the real data.

Example of bringing one of its means to coincide with that of
the real data (left), and showing the points sampled according
to the edited cluster centre (right - yellow points).
Since operating on the learned space yields meaningful
outcomes, we can then use our model to “guide” the selection
of parameters in a different parameter space, say p ∈P, to
create a parameter set that is balanced subject to the real data
profiles. For this use case, we set P ∈R4 to be modCCP
profile space. We remind that modCCP is a simulator that has
been used to create the synthetic data to train MPACT - a
parameter prediction model. To collect the training data, one
could simply randomly sample the parameter space (Figure 14-
d). However, the real data do not often span the whole space
as can be seen from Figure 13-taken directly from the original
MPACT paper [4]. Note here, that for our visualisations we
use the same format as MPACT, for uniformity, i.e., converting
the goal, group and interaction weights (which sum up to 1)
to barycentric coordinates, and denote the connectivity weight
(from 0 to 1) via marker size.
Now, with our model, we can sample our posterior distri-
bution as in Figure 14-a. Then, we can estimate a mapping
M : Z →P. One way to do this is via nearest neighbour
search: since we have the ground truth weights of the modCCP
trajectories passed through our model, we can find the cluster
centres in P, and tune accordingly, as in Figure 14-b. We could
also learn a simple MLP (Multi-Layer Perceptron) parameter
model to predict this mapping, and tune as in Figure 14-c.
The parameter model is trained on mean square error loss,
on data created by passing modCCP generation (with known
weights) through our encoder, and getting the corresponding
latents in Z; the model yields training and validation losses
0.0484 and 0.0499, respectively, after 200 epochs. We can see
that in both cases the tuned points adhere by the real data
coverage of P, more than the random sampling. Still, it is
important to note a few important considerations. The plotted
points in Figure 13, taken from the original paper, correspond
to profiles per area and per frame-window. In our case, the
points correspond to the trajectories, as these are preprocessed
by our block, hence there are some discrepancies between
the two patterns. A user can trade-off the limited predictive
capability of the MLP parameter model with the reliability of
nearest neighbours, according to the intended application.

VI. EVALUATION
A. Self-Consistency Check
Prior to the qualitative and quantitative evaluations, we
perform a sanity-check experiment to verify the robustness
of the learned embedding space. More specifically, we assess
whether sampling latent vectors according to the learned
MoG distribution, decoding them into trajectory space, and
subsequently re-projecting the generated trajectories back into
the latent space through the encoder, results in latent points
that still adhere to the learned MoG distribution (Figure 15).
For sampling, we use 2k points to match the size of the
training dataset. From Figure 15, we confirm both visually
and quantitatively, through the obtained metric value of .705
(< 1), that the learned latent space is self-consistent.
B. Assessment Premise
Our aim in this evaluation is to investigate two claims, (i)
that following the guidance of our learned latent distribution
gives an advantage over no guidance for generation, and (ii)
our model encodes information tailored to the real data. Hence,
in order to determine the evaluation framework, we discuss
each claim and reach a comparison objective that will provide
evidence for our claims.
For (i), we use an existing set of trajectories, th, say from
simulator, h. We compute zR, zh via z = Eθ(τ), and sample Z
to get zG, where the distribution of our posterior (G) is closer to
the real latents than the distribution of zh (e.g., just the sampled
prior). Then we use the trained decoder Dθ : Z →T to get
decoded τG,S, where τS the decoded version of τh. Finally we
compare τG and τS, aiming to prove that the trajectories with
latents more faithful to the distribution of zR, are closer to
the real trajectories, than the other latents. We use both CCP
baseline and the prior as the sampled distributions, and so,
we compare the generated trajectories from our posterior, the
prior, and the CCP empirical distribution.
For (ii), let there be E′ an alternative encoder, e.g., MPACT,
then, pR = E′(τR). We sample from the distribution of pR to
get pG. We obtain τ ′
G via a known decoder D′. We compare our
obtained τG and τ ′
G to the real data. In practice this essentially
means comparing the trajectories decoded from two latent
distributions: our inferred MoG (τG), and the distribution of
the observed embeddings of another parameter space, MPACT
(τ ′
G).
Thus, we compare our posterior with MPACT (ii), prior and
other simulators (i). We emphasise that all comparisons are
made between trajectories generated through our decoder, to
ensure that we do not inherently compare simulator specifics,
but the embeddings of these simulated data against the real.
C. Quantitative Assessment
In order to quantify “closeness” to the real data, we sample
the latent distributions of five sources, i.e., prior, inferred MoG,
RVO, CCP baseline, and MPACT. We compute three types
of metrics on trajectories from our decoder for comparison
fairness; we want to exclude accumulated limitations of
different simulators. So, for each source, we sample their
distributions to obtain a set of trajectories and compare them
with the real ground truths. Specifically, we calculate: (a) our
latent metric described in Section IV-C, (b) the Maximum
Mean Discrepancy-MMD [49] values (statistical measure for
comparing distributions), (c) Sliced Wasserstein Distance [50]
(SWD), and (d) the energy distance of each set of generated data
against the real data. We use SWD because we do not have 1-1
matching of trajectories and want to measure them containing
similar behaviours. SWD measures the similarity between two
distributions by comparing their projections along multiple
random 1D directions and averaging the resulting Wasserstein
distances. The energy distance is a direct distributional distance

D. Qualitative Assessment
Next, we visualise some of the aforementioned results, for
qualitative inspection. Figure 17 shows the heatmaps illustrating
the difference in the correlation matrices of real and generated
data, for four sets of generated data, each associated with a
different empirical latent distribution. Correlation matrices [52]
are built by computing trajectory features, i.e., positions x
and z, speed, acceleration and angular difference, to find
whether correlations and dependencies between features are
preserved. Taking the difference from the real data matrix,
assess representativeness to the real data features. We observe
that our posterior returns the smallest differences with MPACT
being a close second. Finally, we plot boxplots of the trajectory
features (positions and speed) of the real data, and the four
sources (excluding the prior) in Figure 16, showing that our
MoG posterior and the MPACT generations are closer to the
real data, confirming the quantitative assessment observations.
VII. DISCUSSION
In this work, we learn an encoding that yields a latent
representation, embedding real data features, thus parameteris-
ing trajectory information. To do this, we utilised trajectories
captured in real life to train a VAE on the task of trajectory
reconstruction. We use the trained model encoder to build a
posterior distribution (MoG) based on the observed data (latents
of the real data), according to predicted cluster centres (k-
means clustering). Then we employ our method for embedding
synthetic data from various simulators to measure latent
distribution similarity. The feature space is aligned with the
real one, enabling comparisons between the two sets of latent
distributions. We can then inform the selection of latents, and
guide it towards a distribution of choice. This functionality
enables measuring behaviour fidelity in an aligned embedding
space which is useful for comparing synthetic data to real, as
well as for assessing whether new training datasets contain
behavioural features that adhere to those of the real ones.
Limitations and Future Work: Despite the new opportuni-
ties that our method offers, it has some limitations. Our model
does not take into account environmental information explicitly,
e.g., POIs, in its inputs, which limits our system’s ability to
assign specific semantic meaning to the latent space, e.g., action
labels. Also, it does not directly receive information regarding
neighbouring actions and group behaviours. But, these actions
are implicitly entangled into the input trajectories, since it falls
upon the simulator to produce paths that mimic the real ones,
including the spawns and densities of agents. Still, it would be
beneficial to further experiment with such aspects in the input
representation which, however, would require more data since
the task becomes more difficult.


Additionally, the amount of real data is limited and so
the model cannot capture the full range of behavioural
features that could emerge. The absence of a large corpus
of tracked trajectories captured in-the-wild affects both the
encoding power and choice of architecture. Experimenting
with more advanced model architectures, e.g., transformers,
would be interesting, which entails the creation of a larger
training database of real trajectories. Finally, our method is
not extensively assessed against a large variety of alternative
quantitative and qualitative metrics, an analysis that would
reveal both its strengths and weaknesses. However, the lack of
baseline state-of-the-art scores, benchmark evaluation metrics
and tasks, makes the proper formulation of such a protocol an
interesting but challenging direction for future work.
