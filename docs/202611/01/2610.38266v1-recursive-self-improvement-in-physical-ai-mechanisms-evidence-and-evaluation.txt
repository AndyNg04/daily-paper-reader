ABSTRACT
Embodied systems can update policies, world models, skills, and executable programs
from deployment experience, allowing one interaction to benefit later tasks. When a system also revises
and reuses its data-acquisition, feedback, or learning procedures, experience changes the process that
produces capability, introducing recursive self-improvement. This paper surveys research in physical AI
through the connection between persistent capability and its improvement process. We first explain the
functional distinction between learner updates and improver revisions, then compare policy learning,
imagined experience, memory and skill accumulation, and executable harnesses. These approaches
connect to revisions of curricula, rewards, and research programs in different ways. Contact validity,
recovery, retention, and resource costs determine whether the resulting gains accumulate during continued
deployment. An L0–L3 rubric distinguishes task-local adaptation, persistent capability gain, inherited
process revision, and verified improver gain. Representative benchmarks and within-study contrasts relate
these distinctions to existing results. We further propose common-data and interactive comparisons that
evaluate reference and revised improvers from shared learner starts, separating the use of experience
from its acquisition. This perspective connects the diverse methods, physical operating conditions, and
evaluation evidence underlying embodied RSI. A comprehensive list of studies in this survey is available
at https://github.com/nkd-lkz/physical-ai-rsi-survey.

I. INTRODUCTION
Robots encounter new objects, contact conditions, and task
combinations after deployment. A failed attempt exposes
a capability gap and can also supply experience for the
next improvement. Failed grasps can inform policy updates,
human corrections can become reusable skills, and recur-
ring failures can guide further practice. Self-improvement
methods incorporate this experience into continued learning
so that later tasks benefit from earlier interaction. RoboCat

Current work approaches self-improvement through pol-

changes in the learning process, and the additional gains
produced by those process changes. Embodied systems must
also obtain valid feedback, recover from failures, and retain
existing capabilities within limited robot time and human

With robot learning and deployment as the main setting,
this paper connects two strands of self-improvement in
physical AI: how experience becomes persistent capability,
and how it changes the process that produces capability.
The first covers policies and values, world models, memory
and skills, and executable harnesses. The second concerns
revisions to experience acquisition, feedback construction,
and learning procedures. Figure 1 summarizes their relation-

This paper makes four contributions:
• Conceptual distinctions and evidence criteria. We
distinguish task-local adaptation, persistent capability
gain, inherited process revision, and verified improver

• A synthesis of persistent and recursive mechanisms.
We organize methods by what experience changes,

• Physical conditions and controlled evaluation. We

Related surveys provide complementary perspectives on
self-evolving agents, embodied learning, and world–action
models. [7]–[9] Embodied self-evolution has been orga-

These surveys establish the components of self-evolution.

II. FOUNDATIONS AND CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK
Better performance after interaction can arise from adapta-
tion within the current episode, capability retained across
tasks, or changes to the learning process itself. We distin-
guish these cases through the object of change, its reten-
tion boundary, and its later use. The functional distinction
between learner and improver explains the mechanism;
capability gain, mechanistic recursion, and improver gain
identify the empirical claims; and the L0–L3 rubric connects
those claims to observable evidence requirements. These
distinctions also help interpret the convergence of several
research traditions, providing a common basis for comparing
their approaches.
A. LEARNER STATE AND IMPROVEMENT PROCESS
A robot learning to grasp provides an intuitive distinction
between capability and the improvement process. Updating
its grasping policy from failed trajectories changes its re-
tained action capability. If the system also identifies missing
slip experience, revises its sampling or feedback procedure,
and reuses that revision in later training, it changes the
process that produces capability. Policies, predictive models,
and reusable skills constitute the learner state x. Procedures

The learner–improver boundary follows the use of a

generate experience for its training procedure. The former
supports task execution directly; the latter participates in
capability acquisition. An executable skill can likewise en-

and code therefore do not correspond to fixed evidence
levels. The use of an edited component determines whether
it changes how the robot acts, how it continues to learn, or
both.

compared, the learner starts with the same policy, memory,
and normalization statistics. Update code, improver-specific
parameters, and meta-memory move with the improver.
If a world model is shared, its interface must identify
the function being replaced. Subsequent differences can
then be related to the improvement process rather than
unequal initial capabilities. An improvement round includes
an opportunity to acquire experience and propose updates;
its duration depends on the protocol.

B. THREE EMPIRICAL CLAIMS
These updates correspond to three empirical objects. Ca-
pability gain concerns whether the robot performs better
after an update. Mechanistic recursion concerns whether the
system revises and continues using the process that produces
updates. Improver gain concerns whether the revised process
produces better subsequent learning. In the grasping exam-
ple, higher success supports a capability gain, an inherited
sampling revision establishes a process change, and better
later policies trained from matched starts and resources
support an advantage of that revision. A process revision
can be effective or ineffective, so its mechanism and benefit
require separate observations.
Persistence must be measured across a declared boundary.
State retained between attempts within one episode differs
from state retained after an independent reset, across tasks,
or across deployments. Zeva and in-context world modeling
are useful boundary cases because their adaptation relies
on interaction history without establishing the same form
of long-term accumulation as a growing skill repository.
Test-time parameter updating likewise requires an explicit
account of what is reset before the next task. [21]–[25]
Experience thus supports two connected operations: train-
ing the learner and informing revisions to its improver.
Figure 2 shows these paths and their connection to physical
interaction. Independent outcome tests remain outside the
update loop so that their measurements provide an external
assessment of improvement.
Current-attempt correction makes the reset boundary con-
crete. RoboMonkey samples and verifies candidate actions;
CycleVLA predicts failure and backtracks at subtask bound-
aries; closed-loop multi-robot agents coordinate execution
through outcome verification and semantic correction. [26]–
[28] These operations can improve an ongoing attempt.
Their persistent training components, when present, should
be recorded separately from the inference-time correction
loop. The relevant observation is what survives when con-
text, environment, and task initialization are reset for a later
independent attempt.
C. A PROGRESSIVE L0–L3 RUBRIC
We use four evidence levels, L0–L3, to characterize a
specified system configuration and evaluation protocol. L0
concerns task-local adaptation within a reset boundary. L1
concerns retained capability gains in later independent at-
tempts. L2 concerns system-generated process revisions that
are inherited and govern subsequent updates. L3 concerns
the revised improver’s advantage in subsequent learning
under controlled conditions. The levels progress from task-
local change to persistent benefit, process inheritance, and
improver gain. Figure 3 illustrates these distinctions; Table 2
states the operational criteria.

claim. Mechanism type, human involvement, physical set-

D. DEVELOPMENT THROUGH INTERSECTING
TRADITIONS
The field develops through intersecting traditions rather
than successive replacements. Model-based control and
reinforcement learning made experience reusable through
value estimates and predictive models; World Models and
Dreamer exemplify this representational route. Open-ended
learning, exemplified by POET, altered the distribution of
problems encountered during learning. Program-generating
agents later made skills and control logic explicit and
reusable, as illustrated by Voyager and lifelong robot library
learning. These traditions address different bottlenecks and
continue to coexist. [29]–[33]
Recent work connects those traditions to foundation-
model policies, learned feedback, and agent-managed ex-
perimentation. The important shift is an expansion from
improving task solutions to making parts of the learning
infrastructure editable. It does not establish a chronological
sequence from L0 to L3: strong task-local adaptation can
appear after persistent skill systems, and digital recursion
can precede convincing physical validation. A historical
account should therefore track mechanisms, feedback, and
experimental scope separately. [2], [4], [6], [34], [35]
A continuing theme in policy learning is expanding the
experience available for improvement. Early autonomous
practice and recovery enabled repeated sampling. Generalist
robot policies reused experience across a wider range of
tasks. VLA post-training combines value estimates, correc-
tions, and learned feedback to make further use of successful
and failed trajectories. [36] These directions coexist with

III. MECHANISMS OF PERSISTENT
SELF-IMPROVEMENT
Persistent self-improvement turns experience from one in-
teraction into capability that remains useful later. Different
approaches retain that experience in different objects: poli-
cies and values influence action decisions, world models
support prediction and planning, memory and skills pro-
vide retrievable knowledge, and executable programs and
harnesses organize their use. Tracing experience through an
update, its retention, and subsequent reuse (Figure 5) reveals
the learning interfaces and scope of each approach. Several
objects often change within the same system, making their
division of labor and the effect of local updates on overall
task capability central to the comparison.
A. POLICIES AND VALUES
Policy learning directly changes how a robot selects and
executes actions. [70], [71] For a vision–language–action
model (VLA), post-deployment learning typically starts
from pretrained capabilities and uses autonomous trajec-
tories or corrective experience to adapt them to the task.
RECAP uses value-derived advantage information to or-
ganize heterogeneous experience, while LWD aggregates
deployment data from multiple robots. GR-RL and IG-
RFT combine offline preparation with online interaction
for difficult manipulation. These methods share the use
of execution experience to update decision capability, with
differences in experience organization, feedback, and update
location. [2], [3], [72], [73]
The update location determines the interface through
which experience changes actions. Q-Planning keeps the
behavior policy frozen while learning to evaluate and select
candidate actions; Q-VGM uses value signals to update an
action expert. The former retains a new basis for selection,

B. WORLD MODELS AND IMAGINED EXPERIENCE
World models supplement physical trial and error by pre-
dicting the consequences of actions. A robot can compare
predicted trajectories before execution or use generated
experience to train its policy. The role of the model therefore
determines where improvement occurs. RISE trains policies
with imagined experience, SILVR improves visual planning
from collected trajectories, and Motus2 connects policy,
simulation, and value interfaces. Policy-training approaches
convert predictions into retained action capability, planning
approaches use predictions at decision time, and joint sys-
tems exchange information between these roles. [48], [49],
[53]
Recent world-model methods further separate imagined
data, planning, and learned control. WMPO optimizes a
VLA using rollouts from a pixel-predictive model. RAW-
Dream adds task-agnostic imagination, a vision–language
reward source, and verification of unreliable imagined tra-
jectories. [91], [92] Imagine-RL instead keeps both the
VLA and world model frozen while a learned controller or
critic uses imagined visual and torque outcomes weighted
by prediction confidence. [93] MBDPO provides related
algorithmic context by aligning policy optimization with
trajectories searched in a latent world model. [94] The causal
role of prediction differs across these designs: it supplies
a training distribution, filters candidate experience, informs
value estimates, or supports search. Figure 6 separates fixed-
model use from model–policy co-improvement. The location
of persistent change must be established before attributing
a policy gain to improved modeling or a revised improver.
RISE and SILVR illustrate why the location of pre-
dictive learning matters. RISE separates future-observation
prediction from progress or value estimation, using their
outputs to train a policy in imagination. SILVR instead
updates a visual planner from self-produced trajectories.
In the former case, useful model changes must translate
into better policy training; in the latter, they must improve
the plans that execution subsequently attempts. A common
video-quality metric cannot resolve either causal chain. The
decomposition in RISE clarifies which component supplies
transitions and which supplies evaluation, but it does not
make their errors independent. A useful diagnostic dis-
tinguishes a mistaken predicted outcome from failure to
execute a sound plan, since these failures require changes
to different retained components. [48], [49]
VLAW and World-VLA-Loop feed new policy experience
back into model learning, making the training distribution
and predictive model mutually dependent. WoVR and VLA-
MBPO address the reliability of model-generated rollouts,
while VLA-RFT uses a world simulator with constructed
reward signals. The shared difficulty is distributional: a
stronger policy may visit states where the simulator is
least reliable, and a policy optimized against that simulator
may exploit precisely those errors. More realistic videos
are therefore insufficient evidence of more useful learning
experience. [50], [51], [95]–[97]
Coverage and supervision are as important as archi-
tecture. PlayWorld broadens dynamics experience through
autonomous play; human-video dynamics transfer priors
across embodiments; generative 3D worlds expand training
environments. Driving-oriented reward world models fur-
ther illustrate that the same vocabulary can hide different
feedback and deployment assumptions. Comparisons should
report how real evidence calibrates the model, which failures
remain unsupported, and what physical transfer was actually
tested. [52], [98]–[100]
The source of experience also determines which un-
certainties a world model can resolve. Autonomous play
supplies action-conditioned observations from the collecting
robot, whereas human-video priors offer broader behavioral
evidence with an embodiment gap. Generative environments
can vary scenes extensively while retaining the assump-
tions of their construction process. These resources are
complementary rather than interchangeable. The relevant
comparison asks whether newly acquired evidence corrects
an error that matters for action: an unseen contact mode, an
unreliable object transition, or a gap in task coverage. More
generated trajectories cannot by themselves compensate for
a physical variable that the model never observes. [52], [98],
[99]
Prediction can also guide the acquisition of further physi-

EmbodiRSI collects corrections and targeted demonstrations

tunes in reality. RoboCoach uses a world model to guide
subsequent skill learning. In addition to generating training
trajectories, these approaches influence which states or skills
the system practices. They bring experience allocation into
the improvement process, whose revision and inheritance
are examined in the next section. [46], [54]

C. MEMORY AND REUSABLE SKILLS
Experience can also persist as explicit knowledge retrieved
and invoked in later tasks. Compared with incorporating
updates into model parameters, memory and skill libraries
retain more direct units of experience, such as failure expla-
nations, preconditions, and executable programs. Voyager
and LRLL retain reusable program abstractions, LYRA
converts human corrections into code, and ViReSkill revises
and stores programs through visual failure diagnosis. A
repair acquired in one task thereby becomes a capability
available to later tasks while the foundation model can
remain frozen. [32], [33], [56], [57]
As a skill library grows, maintaining existing skills be-
comes as important as discovering new ones. Uni-Skill
uses available examples to fill capability gaps. EmbodiSkill
distinguishes incorrect skill content from an executor failing
to follow valid guidance before deciding how to revise it.
PRACTICE trains a separate skill learner to edit the library,
making the users and producers of skills separate objects
of study. The former determine how a task is executed;
the latter determine how new experience enters reusable
capability. [58], [59], [101]
A reusable skill moves the burden of generalization from
reproducing an entire trajectory to selecting and instantiat-
ing an abstraction. This can make a successful correction
available to later tasks, but the abstraction must preserve
the conditions under which the correction worked. LYRA’s
human corrections and ViReSkill’s visually grounded diag-
nosis represent different sources of evidence for a retained
program. EmbodiSkill adds another distinction: revising a
valid skill is inappropriate when the failure comes from not
following it. A useful comparison separates diagnosis, skill
editing, retrieval, and execution. Otherwise, a larger library
may receive credit for an improvement caused by a stronger
planner, while a retrieval failure may be misread as incorrect
stored knowledge. For physical tasks, the interface should
preserve prerequisites and expected outcomes so that reuse
can be checked against the current situation. [56]–[58]
The role of memory also depends on its temporal scope.
RoboSkill concerns persistent skill artifacts, the main proto-
cols of MemBodied and Zeva use within-episode informa-
tion for adaptation, and AGM explicitly maintains verified
task progress. Episode memory helps interpret the current
situation, progress records support continued execution, and
persistent skills serve later independent tasks. These uses
explain which capability improves when more information
is retained. [23], [102]–[104]
Self-directed play and evidence-managed knowledge re-
veal two further forms of persistence. RATs selects practice
tasks, executes and repairs code policies, and consolidates
successful behavior into a skill library that can be frozen
for downstream evaluation. [105] RoboHarn-Evo maintains
linked task-level and action-level physical knowledge, re-
vising applicability and retrieval eligibility from execution
evidence while retaining a fixed task–action schema and
fixed execution primitives. [106] In both cases, later be-

D. EXECUTABLE PROGRAMS AND AGENT
HARNESSES
Knowledge in a skill library becomes useful through the
system that executes it. A harness organizes the tool inter-
faces, context construction, skill routing, recovery routines,
and execution checks around a model. Revising it changes
how existing models and skills work together. ASPIRE
retains validated program repairs, SHAPER evolves skills
and planning context, and Zetta revises runtime assets
around a frozen policy. Harness VLA and HarnessPAI orga-
nize reusable skills and action interfaces. These approaches
extend persistent improvement to system orchestration. [4],
[5], [60], [107], [108]
Graph-as-Policy makes editable control structure explicit,
while PhyAgentOS and skill-graph assembly systems em-
phasize session boundaries, verification, and reusable inter-
faces. VASO adds formal checks over specified skill con-
tracts; CRISP and SERP illustrate correction and replanning
whose persistence must be established separately. CaP-X
supplies useful infrastructure for studying coding agents,
but the availability of an editing interface alone establishes
neither retained learning nor recursion. [109]–[115]
Executable artifacts make some failure causes easier to lo-
cate than a monolithic parameter update. A repaired branch
can change how a task recovers, and an edited graph can
change which skill follows an observation. Yet editability
creates dependencies: a new tool interface can invalidate an
older skill, while a routing change can alter which policy
failures are exposed. ASPIRE’s retained repairs and graph-
based control structures should therefore be analyzed at the
artifact level as well as through end-task success. The key
persistence test is whether an accepted change remains avail-
able in a fresh attempt with its required dependencies intact.
Only when that artifact also changes how later updates are
produced does the analysis move from this section’s learner
improvements to process revision. [4], [109], [111]
HarnessPAI illustrates two coupled but distinguishable
updates: executable programs remain fixed within a rollout
and evolve between rollouts, while successful trajectories
can subsequently train the wrapped action model. Its stored
repair knowledge supports reuse across tasks. These paths
motivate separate ablations for program improvement and
model post-training: a final system gain does not identify
their individual contributions, and persistence of an exe-
cution program does not by itself establish revision of its
updater. [60]
RoboFoundry and Physical Coding extend persistent arti-
facts to the organization of the execution system. Compared
with repairing one skill, a system-level edit can affect
context, tool invocation, and task recovery together. Poli-
cies, models, memory, and programs consequently form an
interdependent capability structure in which experience can
change execution through different entry points. A retained
program that also generates and selects later updates simul-
taneously serves as part of the improver. [61], [116]
RegenHarness makes a useful distinction between a pro-
posed modification, verified physical progress, and a re-
leased system version. Its design retains changes to context,
templates, routing, and recovery across missions, with re-
gression checks and versioned rollback. [117] This explicit
release boundary makes the edited artifact and its later use
inspectable. The deployment cases and the paper’s proposed
controlled RSI protocol nevertheless answer different ques-
tions: the former illustrate operation and evidence gating; the
latter specifies how a revised improver should be tested. This
distinction connects executable persistence to the inherited
process revisions examined next.
IV. FROM SELF-IMPROVEMENT TO RECURSIVE
IMPROVEMENT
The preceding mechanisms explain how experience changes
and preserves task capability; the learning procedures that
produce these changes can themselves become objects of
revision. When a system uses operational results to revise
experience acquisition, feedback construction, or learning
code, then reuses the revision in later rounds, experience
also changes how capability is produced. Figure 7 locates
the main revision points. Understanding these approaches
requires tracing both the modified component and its inher-
itance: acquisition and curricula control what is encountered
next, rewards and evaluators determine learning signals, and
training and research programs organize updates. What is
retained and how it enters subsequent learning determine
the scope of recursion.
A. REVISING EXPERIENCE ACQUISITION AND

to learn. [118] Teacher–student learning selects tasks using

intermediate difficulty. PAIRED and ACCEL extend this
control to environment generation and editing using regret-
related signals. These mechanisms can improve later policy
updates by changing which evidence is encountered. How-
ever, a changing curriculum does not by itself imply that
the curriculum-generating procedure has been revised. This
paper separates the selected tasks, the selector’s retained
state, and any inherited modification to its selection rule.
[119]–[123]

B. REVISING REWARDS AND EVALUATORS
Feedback methods differ in who supplies the task cri-
terion and how that criterion becomes an optimization
signal. Preference learning fits rewards to human compar-

a video–language model and a demonstration to construct
rewards, while RL-VLM-F derives comparisons from a
vision–language model. Vision-language rewards further

Task generation and feedback generation often need to
be designed together. Agentic Skill Discovery connects task
proposals, reward generation, skill training, and retention,
with an additional visual check of task completion. A new

and feedback agree. Acquisition, evaluation, and training

Reward code provides a concrete carrier for process
revision. Eureka generates and revises executable rewards
from training feedback, changing the optimization signal
used by later policies. DrEureka extends automated design
to training choices relevant to transfer, while reflective

curricula. A revised reward retained for the next training

Reward revision changes the objective seen by a down-

terminal score under the revised reward itself. Eureka’s exe-

a changed term can alter which intermediate behavior the
optimizer favors. DrEureka broadens the design problem to
transfer-related choices, where favorable simulated learning
still requires a hardware check. The comparison should

the training signal and record which constraints remained
fixed. If a reward change merely redefines failure as success,
internal optimization can improve without physical progress.

even though the outer reward-generation procedure remains
unchanged. [63], [141]
Learned rewards provide another representation of feed-

determines which trajectories the evaluator observes, and the
evaluator influences how the next policy is trained. [65],
[143]–[146]
Feedback adaptation now spans the construction of ex-

synthesizes failure trajectories to broaden the coverage of
a language-conditioned progress model. RAPID combines
parallel simulation, batched vision–language preferences,
and reward stabilization to reduce the cost of obtaining
learning signals. [147], [148] eVTA0 learns dense success-

updates the reward model as reinforcement learning collects
further data. [149] This creates a concrete pathway through
which policy-dependent evidence changes future feedback.
BEE takes a complementary approach to corrective super-

uses that information to constrain reinforcement learning
around a frozen VLA. [150] The comparison must therefore

part of later training. Holding the rollout set fixed can isolate

physical validity falls. The Red Queen Gödel Machine offers

external anchors and version boundaries. For physical AI,
reward calibration should be checked against measurements
or labels unavailable to the updating system. Independence
is a relationship among data, models, and decisions, not
merely the use of a differently named judge. [151]
C. REVISING LEARNING AND RESEARCH
PROCEDURES
When training and research programs become editable, a
system can change the method used to produce updates.
ENPIRE allows an agent to modify training code and policy-
learning choices, linking execution logs to subsequent policy
development. Compared with repeated fine-tuning under
a fixed algorithm, this exposes algorithm selection and
implementation to revision. Robot tasks, safety interfaces,
and reset conditions constrain the experiment, while the
revised program organizes the next learning round within
those conditions. [6]
A program can be both the artifact being improved and the
tool that produces further artifacts, a relationship especially
direct in digital-agent research. STOP edits an optimization
program that invokes a language model. Gödel Agent and
Darwin Gödel Machine expose agent code to modifica-
tion and evaluation. Dream-RSI revises exploration pro-
cedures using replayable search experience, while Escher-
Loop separates task and optimizer agents. These methods
offer references for constructing, retaining, and rerunning
improvement programs. Robotic applications additionally
connect them to physical experience, action interfaces, and
experimental constraints. [34], [35], [68], [69], [152]

D. INHERITANCE AND THE BOUNDARY OF A
RECURSIVE CLAIM
Revisions to acquisition, feedback, and training code con-
verge on a common relationship: accepted changes remain
active in later rounds. Connecting the parent program, the
experience that motivated a revision, the accepted version,
and its next update reconstructs this inheritance. Agenti-
cRobotics and harness-engineering work provide references
for organizing execution and version information. These
records distinguish a temporary repair from a reusable
process change in an implemented system. [157], [158]
Inheritance must also preserve the evidence needed to
interpret a revision. A successful final artifact does not
reveal whether an earlier rejected candidate supplied the
idea, whether a human repaired the environment, or whether
the accepted procedure was ever used again. An audit should
connect proposals and failures to the accepted version and
then to the next learner update. This is more informative
than counting self-editing calls, since many edits may be
inert or confined to current-task execution. Where a world
model or skill library serves both execution and training,
the record should identify which role changed. The same
stored artifact can support persistent capability without yet
establishing a revised improvement process.
RoboFoundry explicitly separates task-level repairs from
changes promoted to a persistent general system. General
edits are tested through replay of other tasks’ pre-repair
traces, using the model-based system as verifier of proposed
repairs. This makes the reuse scope inspectable, while leav-
ing development verification distinct from an independent
physical audit. This paper therefore asks whether a retained
edit improves future proposal generation, task execution, or
both, and whether that distinction is isolated by the reported
comparison. [61]
PhysicalRSI 1.0 combines a frozen foundation model with
an editable harness, using candidate generation, selection,
and inheritance for persistent harness revision. The key an-
alytical object is the function of an inherited edit: whether it
changes task execution, subsequent learning, or both. Final-
system leaderboard performance measures task capability;
improver gain requires a comparison of the subsequent
learning gains produced by the revised procedure. General
Robotics AutoEngineering further broadens the engineering
scope of editable robot systems. [62], [159]
V. PHYSICAL CONDITIONS FOR SUSTAINED
IMPROVEMENT
Accumulating long-term progress over successive updates
requires a robot to keep obtaining valid experience and
remain operational after each change. An action that appears
visually plausible may fail at contact, an unrecoverable oper-
ation may interrupt data collection, and a new skill may lose
its usefulness under environmental change or further learn-
ing. These conditions extend across experience acquisition,
update validation, and redeployment (Figure 8). Contact and
model validity affect the reliability of feedback; recovery
determines whether interaction can continue; and retention
determines whether gains accumulate. Update control and
full costs further constrain which changes the system can
attempt and how long improvement can continue.
A. CONTACT AND MODEL VALIDITY
Physical feedback is constrained by what can be sensed and
safely attempted. Visually plausible grasping need not es-
tablish force closure, secure contact, or successful insertion.
Model error can therefore affect both action selection and
the labels used to accept an update. Tests should distinguish
predicted success, observed completion, and independently
verified task outcomes. A model used for training should
be evaluated on failures and interventions, not only on
trajectories similar to its successful demonstrations. [50]–
[52], [160]
Contact tasks make the dependence between prediction
and feedback especially visible. A grasp image can show
geometry without revealing slip, and apparent alignment
in assembly can coexist with insufficient insertion depth.
A world model and reward model that rely on the same
visual cues may therefore accept the same physical error.
Object-state, force, or tactile measurements chosen for the
failure mechanism can test conditions that visual feedback
misses. The relevant criterion is whether the measurement
distinguishes the outcome the task actually requires.
Simulation can make experimentation reproducible, but
it does not remove the transfer obligation. DrEureka,
generative-world training, and TTT-Parkour illustrate differ-
ent ways to use simulation for physical policy improvement.

B. RECOVERY AND CONTINUITY
Learning stops before the next sample can be collected when
a robot drops an object, leaves its workspace, or enters a
state from which the task cannot continue. Recovery returns
such states to usable interaction conditions. MEDAL++
and RoboFuME incorporate recovery into autonomous prac-
tice, RoboClaw pairs forward operation with recovery, and
RADAR coordinates collection, filtering, and resets. These
mechanisms improve access to continued experimentation,
reflected in autonomous attempts, recovery success, human
intervention, and task performance. [38], [42], [167], [168]
Autonomous improvement depends on keeping the robot
in states from which further informative interaction remains
possible. Leave no Trace learns a reset policy; multi-task
reset-free learning uses relationships among tasks to con-
tinue practice without repeatedly restoring a single initial
state. The Ingredients of Real-World Robotic Reinforcement
Learning combines reward acquisition, policy learning, and
autonomous practice, while ARIEL uses prior data to reduce
the burden of autonomous task learning and sequencing.
MoReFree studies reset-free learning with world models.
These works motivate treating recovery as part of evi-
dence acquisition: two systems with the same nominal
rollout count may incur different intervention, reset, and
inaccessible-state costs. [169]–[173]
Recovery designs differ in their dependence on people
and environmental preparation. A robot can reduce manual
resets while still using a prepared workspace, a backup
controller, or expert corrections. Reset-free agile-driving
work illustrates the role of control and exploration condi-
tions, while RECALL retains expert recovery supervision.
Recovery also changes the states accessible in the next
round, affecting both operating costs and the distribution
of learning data. [128], [174]
Recovery also determines what the next experiment can
teach. Restoring only convenient states can produce a sta-
ble stream of trials while excluding precisely the failures

a policy needs to learn from. Conversely, treating every
abnormal state as another exploration opportunity can make
restoration slower or less reliable. The forward–recovery
organization in RoboClaw and the active recovery super-
vision in RECALL address different parts of this continuity
problem. Their contribution should be described through
the states they make available and the assistance needed to
reach them. Human participation is heterogeneous: demon-
strations, evaluator labels, timed action corrections, object
restoration, and safety monitoring consume different kinds
of effort. Eliminating one does not establish the absence of
the others, and their costs scale differently as the robot fleet
or task repertoire grows. [42], [128]
Execution-time recovery can preserve access to learning
even when it does not itself create a persistent update.
AgentChord anticipates failures in a task graph and mon-

Agentic AI uses typed skills and per-dispatch checks to
constrain multi-robot execution. [175], [176] Such mech-
anisms change the failures a learner encounters and the in-
terventions needed to continue. Evaluations should therefore
record both attempted learning updates and the recovery ma-
chinery surrounding them, including fixed fallback behavior,
human resets, and interventions that prevent an attempted
action from reaching the robot.
C. RETENTION AND DISTRIBUTION CHANGE
Once a robot can keep operating, long-term benefit depends
on whether new and existing capabilities coexist. CRL-
VLA and LifeLong-RFT study retention during sequential
updates, whereas Natural Continual Learners demonstrates
strong simple baselines under its conditions. These out-
comes reflect the joint effects of task overlap, representa-
tion, replay, and training objectives. Real-world continual
VLA experiments provide complementary settings: exter-
nally supplied task and demonstration sequences support
retention analysis, while autonomous experience acquisition
forms a separate dimension. [177]–[180]
Sustained improvement has two distinct failure modes:
losing earlier capabilities and losing the capacity to acquire
new ones. EWC constrains changes to important parameters,
while GEM uses stored examples to constrain updates
that interfere with previous tasks. Continual World makes
retention and forward transfer explicit evaluation targets
in robotic task sequences. Studies of plasticity loss show
why preserving old behavior alone is insufficient: repeated
learning can leave a network increasingly difficult to train.
The survey should therefore report old-task retention and
new-task learning separately. An apparent stable system
may simply have stopped adapting, whereas rapid new-task
improvement may conceal accumulated forgetting. [181]–
[185]
Forgetting also affects executable artifacts. A new skill
may conflict with an old interface, increase retrieval ambi-
guity, or encode a local shortcut. Library Drift and SkillFlow
show why library growth should be evaluated through use
and downstream benefit rather than artifact counts. Scientific
Amnesia further distinguishes preserving task behavior from
retaining knowledge about how to train the next system.
These digital findings motivate physical regression tests,
while their quantitative results remain specific to their
original environments. [186]–[188]
Retention must cover the whole deployed system. A
frozen policy can still lose an effective behavior if a new
selector stops choosing it, a memory entry overrides its con-
text, or a revised skill interface no longer calls it correctly.
An unchanged library may also become unreliable after
calibration or material properties drift. Rollback is therefore
more demanding than restoring one checkpoint: compatible
model, memory, tool, and controller versions may need to be
restored together. VLA retention studies and studies of skill-
library drift motivate complementary checks, since parame-
ter stability and artifact availability measure different things.
A sustained loop needs to distinguish forgotten competence,
inaccessible competence, and competence invalidated by
changed physical conditions before choosing whether to
retrain, repair retrieval, or recalibrate. [177], [178], [186],
[189]
Hardware wear, changed calibration, and environmental
drift make retained artifacts conditional. A formerly useful
grasp parameter or reward threshold can become incorrect
without any software edit. World-model-feedback adaptation
studies the triggering and stopping of learning under change;
SAGE connects risk to intervention. Sustained improvement
requires a policy for detecting invalid knowledge, revalidat-
ing it, and distinguishing adaptation to drift from learning
a transferable new capability. [129], [189]
Recent VLA studies show why forgetting must be in-
terpreted through the training protocol. Memory Anchors
prioritizes replay examples where conflicting tasks share
representations, aiming to preserve behavior at points of
interference. A separate study of pretrained VLAs reports
resistance to forgetting under its continual-learning and
replay conditions, including recovery of degraded skills.
[190], [191] These observations concern different task con-
flicts, pretrained representations, and replay supplies. They
motivate reporting the sequence of tasks, the retained data,
performance before and after relearning, and the cost of
recovery. A final average can conceal both transient regres-
sions and unequal practice on earlier tasks.
D. UPDATE CONTROL AND FULL COSTS
Accepting a new version also requires considering its effect
on real-time execution. Additional perception, planning, or
verification changes latency and computational competition,
so a component that performs better offline can behave
differently in deployment. VASO checks specified execution
conditions through declared skill contracts, while staged
deployment and rollback control the operational effects of
revisions. Formal checks apply within their abstractions and
assumptions; contact and sensing conditions also require
validation during execution. [112], [158], [192]

Update control introduces a trade-off between protection
and visibility. A fixed fallback controller can keep an
experimental policy within a restricted operating envelope,
yet frequent fallback may conceal deterioration of the policy
being improved. The deployed claim then concerns the
combined system, and intervention records are necessary to
attribute performance. Formal checks over skill contracts,
such as those considered by VASO, provide a different
kind of evidence from a favorable learned reward: their
scope depends on the stated abstraction and assumptions.
Neither should be generalized to unmodeled sensor or
contact failures. Preserving these boundaries keeps failed
updates identifiable and reversible, rather than letting re-
covery infrastructure make every candidate appear equally
successful. [112]
The learning objective also interacts with execution tim-
ing and motion quality. ARLI accounts for actions already
committed during slow policy inference, so the learner sees
the consequences of asynchronous execution. SmoothVLA
combines task-success reward with a trajectory-jerk term,
bringing motion smoothness into post-training. [193], [194]
Social DSRL studies learned diffusion steering for naviga-
tion under a performance-preserving adaptation objective,
with a hardware-in-the-loop setting in the inspected evi-
dence. [195] These cases show why physical improvement is
not exhausted by goal completion: timing, actuator-friendly
trajectories, and acceptable behavior during adaptation de-
termine whether a policy update can be used in continued
operation.
Improvement cost is a vector rather than a single to-
ken count: real robot time, simulated interaction, com-
pute, teacher demonstrations, human maintenance, resets,
failed trials, and hardware wear can trade off. Reporting
only successful retained trajectories omits the acquisition
attempts that produced them. Likewise, counting only the
final training run omits search and discarded candidates.
A claimed gain in sample efficiency is interpretable only
relative to the resources it reduces and those it increases.
These factors explain the distance between a single update
and long-term benefit. Incorrect feedback directs learning
toward ineffective behavior, recovery failures reduce avail-
able experience, and forgetting or hardware drift erodes
acquired capability. Figure 9 summarizes these distinct
failure paths. Evaluating sustained improvement therefore
involves both task performance and the conditions that keep
further learning possible.

C. CONTROLLED COMPARISON OF IMPROVERS
Task benchmarks measure system performance, while
before–after comparisons measure the effects of retained
updates. Evaluating the improver requires starting the ref-
erence and revised procedures from the same learner and
observing the subsequent gains each produces. We therefore
consider two complementary conditions. A common-data
test gives both improvers the same experience to compare
its use. An interactive test allows each to collect experience
under matched resource limits, comparing the joint effects
of acquisition and updating. Shared foundation models, tool
access, and independent test tasks help isolate the effect of
the procedure change.
Freeze both improvers before the final comparison and
keep their development and selection data separate from
held-out tests. The shared learner start controls differences
caused by co-adaptation in the original pairing. If further
revisions are allowed during testing, predeclare the complete
revision policy as part of the evaluated improver.
This distinction preserves the value of acquisition itself.
Common data controls experience differences when a revi-
sion changes optimization, while interactive conditions al-
low a revised exploration strategy or curriculum to influence
the data collected. Figure 11 contrasts these conditions, and
Figure 12 shows the pairing of learner starts with improver
versions.
The target quantity is subsequent gain, measured relative
to the shared starting learner. An improver comparison
should report the difference between those gains across
independent runs or task groups, with uncertainty and actual
resource use. Fixed-budget final performance, area under
a learning curve, and cost to reach a predefined threshold
answer different questions and should not be conflated.
Improvement rate is not established by dividing a single
final score by an arbitrary number of iterations.

E. SYNTHESIS OF THE CURRENT EVIDENCE
The approaches establish multiple routes from experience
to persistent capability: practice updates policies, prediction
expands usable experience, skill libraries retain reusable
behaviors, and harnesses organize their invocation. Revi-
sions to rewards and training programs extend change to the
process that produces capability. The common connection
is that an interaction can influence later tasks and even the
organization of later learning. Benchmarks and within-study
contrasts reveal different parts of this relationship; improver
comparisons from shared learner starts target the subsequent
value of the process revision itself.
Digital-agent research contributes designs for this latter
comparison. Dream-RSI and Escher-Loop evaluate pro-
grams that produce later solutions, while DGM analyzes
system modification through related ablations. Together with
robotic policy, memory, and harness results, these studies
provide methodological references. Further development of
physical RSI connects reusable process revisions to physical
interaction budgets, recovery, and retention, testing whether
their advantages accumulate during continued deployment.
[35], [68], [69]
VII. OPEN CHALLENGES AND RESEARCH DIRECTIONS
When experience acquisition, feedback, and learning pro-
cedures can all be revised, sustained improvement also
depends on their remaining compatible as they change
together. The preceding comparisons direct attention to this
continuity: feedback must remain grounded in physical out-
comes, acquired capabilities and improvement procedures
must survive further updates and transfer, and repeated
experimentation requires reliable recovery at an affordable
resource cost. Establishing progress also requires records of
process versions, interventions, and evaluation conditions so
that gains can be reproduced and attributed. The following
research directions develop these connected requirements.
A. GROUNDED FEEDBACK THAT SURVIVES SYSTEM
CHANGE
The first priority is to maintain a reliable connection
between internal feedback and physical outcomes as the
system changes. Reward models need failure coverage, tem-
poral context, calibration, and audits outside their training
loop. Proposals such as intrinsic robot rewarding can con-
tribute ideas, but proposed protocols must be distinguished
from completed online experiments. A useful research target
is not merely a higher reward-model score, but a feedback
mechanism whose errors remain bounded under the distribu-
tion induced by continued improvement. [65], [144], [231]
B. LASTING GAINS AND REUSABLE IMPROVERS
A useful improvement should survive later updates and
transfer beyond the failure that triggered it. This requires
studying skill retirement, conflicting memories, represen-
tation drift, and compatibility between a revised improver
and different learners. SkillSmith and Evo-Harness suggest
ways to manage external artifacts, while morphology-design
systems expose transfer across changing bodies. Physical
studies should test whether a learned acquisition, verifica-
tion, or update strategy benefits a new task or embodiment
after its own development feedback is frozen. [66], [232],
[233]

C. RECOVERY AND RESOURCE-AWARE
EXPERIMENTATION
Recovery should become part of the improvement objective
rather than invisible laboratory labor. Systems need to
reason about recoverable failure states, when to request
assistance, and when an experiment should stop. Automated
laboratories provide an adjacent physical setting in which
experiments have delayed outcomes and material costs.
They can inform provenance and scheduling, but successful
automated synthesis does not itself establish an improving
general-purpose robot or an improving research algorithm.
[42], [168], [234]
Resource-aware search must include the cost of discov-
ering the improver, not only the cost after discovery. Cheap
simulated search may shift work to model construction,
and fewer robot trials may require more demonstrations
or human maintenance. Future benchmarks should expose
these trade-offs as resource profiles rather than collapse
them into one score. They should also include unsuccessful
campaigns so that efficiency is not estimated only among
survivors.
D. REPRODUCIBLE PROCESS EVIDENCE AND REVIEW
LIMITATIONS
Research should publish versioned learner and improver
artifacts, acceptance decisions, intervention logs, and in-
dependent evaluation partitions. The verification gap in
autonomous research and guidance-sensitive benchmarks
such as ASI-Bench emphasize that finishing an experimental
workflow, producing a plausible paper, and establishing a
reproducible finding are different outcomes. Physical RSI
needs the same distinction, augmented with hardware state
and recovery records. [235], [236]
A concrete research target is an interchangeable im-
provement interface specifying inputs, editable components,
admissible evidence, and resource limits. This would en-
able cross-learner tests without undocumented engineer-
ing changes. Cross-embodiment transfer should distinguish
reuse of semantic knowledge, acquisition strategy, opti-
mization code, and control representation; extensive human
retuning changes the claim. Development evaluators may
evolve, but the reporting outcome must retain a common
interpretation. Changing success thresholds, task exclusions,
or failure definitions can otherwise mimic progress without
improving competence or improvement capacity.
The comparisons in this paper follow the retained ar-
tifacts, reset boundaries, and resource conditions reported
for each evaluation protocol. Differences in task distribu-
tions, human assistance, and cost accounting limit direct
comparisons across studies. The boundary between learner
and improver follows a component’s actual role in subse-
quent updates. A shared annotation protocol and inter-rater
agreement tests can further improve the reproducibility of
comparisons using the L0–L3 rubric.

VIII. CONCLUSION
Embodied self-improvement rests on turning interaction
experience into capability that remains useful later. Poli-
cies, world models, memory, skills, and harnesses provide
different routes for retaining and reusing that experience.
Recursive self-improvement further changes how data are
acquired, feedback is constructed, and updates are produced,
allowing inherited process revisions to govern the next learn-
ing round. This relationship connects otherwise separate
technical approaches while preserving their differences in
supervision, update objects, and physical conditions.
This paper relates capability gains, process inheritance,
and improver gains through the L0–L3 evidence rubric and
compares their mechanisms and experimental results. Long-
term progress in embodied RSI depends jointly on valid
feedback, continued interaction, retention, and affordable
improvement costs. Common-data and interactive compar-
isons of revised improvers can further distinguish a system
that has acquired better task capability from one that has
acquired a more effective process for developing capability.
