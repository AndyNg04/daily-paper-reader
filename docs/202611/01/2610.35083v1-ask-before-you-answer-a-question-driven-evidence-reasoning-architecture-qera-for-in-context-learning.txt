## Abstract 

AI assistants are increasingly asked to reason over a user’s own web traces: group chats, community threads, activity logs and revision histories. On CL-bench Life, a benchmark of such real-life context learning, frontier LLMs solve fewer than one task in five. We find that the request is not the specification: over 80% of the benchmark’s rubrics share less than 60% of their content words with the final request, as for a rule set two turns earlier or every record that meets the user’s criterion, and LLMs answering directly pass only 67% of these implicit rubrics against 81% of the others. We propose QERA, a Question-driven Evidence Reasoning Architecture that asks before it answers. QERA first turns the request into a requirement checklist and typed information needs, filtered by code for faithfulness to the user’s words; it then answers from the whole conversation, asks the same questions again with quotes verified by code, and applies a guarded local repair. It needs no training or rubrics and makes about four LLM calls per task. On all 405 tasks with four frontier LLMs, QERA attains the highest solving rate on every LLM (21.0% on average, against 18.2% for the strongest of three inference-time baselines and 14.6% for direct answering) and leads every baseline significantly when pooled across LLMs, while using fewer tokens than PSCI and 4–5% of the tokens of Ctx2Skill. Ablations on GPT-6-Luna show that the gain is settled before the answer, by a fixed answer protocol (+3.8 rubric points) and the request-specific questions (+1.6), and that it is concentrated on implicit rubrics, with checking afterwards refining mainly numeric requirements.

Existing inference-time methods for context learning recover it in other ways. CCA [26] compiles the context into typed rules with executable verifiers, and Ctx2Skill [29] distills a skill for each context by multi-agent self-play; both organize the knowledge of the context. PSCI [40] comes closest to asking: before answering, it induces a specification contract for the task, with the help of rubric-style demonstrations from other tasks, and then checks and repairs the answer against it. None of them turns the request into typed questions about the context (which records to enumerate, which facts to look up, which figures to compute) and reuses those questions to check the answer against quoted evidence. On CL-bench Life, the strongest of them changes from one LLM to the next, and Ctx2Skill spends 2.4M–3.1M tokens per task, because a context that serves a single request cannot amortize its self-play. Reference librarians have long held that a patron’s stated ques-tion is a compromised version of the actual information need, and that a good answer begins by negotiating the question [31]; search systems ask clarifying questions for the same reason [1, 38]. An assistant that reads a user’s traces cannot interview the user about every request, but it can interview the context. We propose QERA ,a Question-driven Evidence Reasoning Architecture that asks be-fore it answers (Figure 2). Its Ask stage turns the request into a requirement checklist (what must the reply deliver, including rules from earlier turns?) and typed information needs (what must be looked up, enumerated, counted, computed, compared or judged in the context?), which code filters keep faithful to the user’s words. The Answer stage writes the reply from the whole conversation, guided by these questions and a fixed answer protocol; the Check 

stage asks the same questions again, answered with quotes that code verifies; and a Guarded Repair patches only what fails. QERA needs no training or rubrics and makes about four LLM calls per task. We evaluate QERA on all 405 tasks of CL-bench Life with four frontier LLMs and four baselines, around three research questions: 

• RQ1 (Effectiveness and cost): Does asking before answering improve real-life context learning across LLMs, and at what cost relative to strong inference-time methods? 

• RQ2 (Asking vs. checking): Which stages produce the gain: the questions asked before the answer, or the evidence check after it? 

• RQ3 (Source of the gains): Which requirements and contexts benefit? Are the gains concentrated on implicit requirements, and do they hold across context lengths and types? Our contributions answer them in turn: 

• (RQ1) We propose QERA, a question-driven inference-time archi-tecture for learning from real-life context. It attains the highest solving rate on all four LLMs (21.0% on average, against 18.2% for the strongest baseline and 14.6% for direct answering), leads every baseline significantly when pooled across LLMs, and uses fewer tokens than PSCI and 4–5% of the tokens of Ctx2Skill. 

• (RQ2) A stage-wise ablation shows that the gain is settled before the answer: on GPT-6-Luna, the fixed answer protocol adds 3.8 rubric points and the request-specific questions 1.6 (89% of the gain), and QERA’s two-call draft already solves more tasks than every baseline on every LLM; checking afterwards refines mainly numeric requirements. 

• (RQ3) The gains are concentrated on implicit rubrics (2.5 points above the best baseline and 6.6 above direct answering, while all methods lie within 1.4 points of each other on the rest), and QERA ranks first in every context-length quartile and context category and in 20 of the 36 combinations of LLM and subcategory. 

## 2 Related Work 

Learning from context. In-context learning originally denoted learning a task from demonstrations in the prompt [3]. Context learning benchmarks widen it to learning new knowledge, rules and procedures from long, task-specific contexts at inference time [7], Ask Before You Answer: A Question-Driven Evidence Reasoning Architecture (QERA) for In-Context Learning 

and CL-bench Life moves it to real-life context such as chats, per-sonal archives and behavioral traces [6]. Long-context studies show that models use long inputs unevenly [2, 13, 19], multi-turn studies show that performance drops sharply when a task’s requirements are revealed over several turns [17], and instruction-following benchmarks test explicit, verifiable constraints [42]. Work on con-versational memory and personalization builds and evaluates as-sistants that store or retrieve a user’s history [18, 21, 24, 27, 35]. QERA neither retrieves nor stores: the whole conversation stays in every call, and the difficulty it addresses is recognizing what a request leaves implicit. 

Inference-time methods for context learning. Context engineer-ing adapts what a model sees without changing its weights [22]. ACE [39] and Dynamic Cheatsheet [30] accumulate strategies across tasks; Ctx2Skill [29] discovers skills for each context by self-play; CCA [26] compiles the context into typed rules with executable verifiers; PSCI [40] induces a specification contract, then checks and repairs the answer. CCA and Ctx2Skill organize the knowl-edge of each context. PSCI is closest to our work: it induces, before answering, a contract of the specifications a correct answer must meet, and then checks and repairs the answer, a shape that QERA’s later stages share. QERA’s questions differ: they are anchored to the user’s words and filtered by code, need no demonstrations or other tasks, and also ask what must be found in the context, as typed information needs whose findings are verified against the conversation and reused to check the reply. 

Asking, decomposing and verifying. Reasoning step by step [34] or through sub-questions and sub-tasks solved on the way to the an-swer improves LLM reasoning [15, 25, 32, 41], and ReAct interleaves reasoning with actions [37]. In information retrieval, systems ask users clarifying questions to recover underspecified needs [1, 38], and query expansion rewrites a query with generated text [9, 33]. QERA’s questions are addressed to the context rather than to the user or a retriever, they are typed so that enumerations and com-putations can be audited, and they are asked twice, before and after the answer. On the verification side, self-refinement and self-reflection [20, 28], chain-of-verification [5] and tool-interactive critiquing [12] revise answers after they are written, and program-aided reasoning delegates arithmetic to code [4, 10]. LLMs, how-ever, struggle to correct their own reasoning without external feed-back [14], and our RQ2 echoes this: once the right questions have been asked before the answer, most of the work is done.

The request is not the specification. We call a rubric explicit when at least 60% of its content words (names, numbers, quoted strings and words of five or more letters) occur in the request 𝑞 , and implicit 

otherwise (Appendix F). By this rule, 4,352 of the 5,333 rubrics it can classify (82%) are implicit. The rule is a lexical proxy, and our conclusions hold at every level of overlap (§4.4). Implicit rubrics test, for example, a rule the user set in an earlier turn, a later message that corrects a fact, every record that meets the user’s criterion, the person behind each claim, the figure that the user’s own numbers imply. They are also where frontier LLMs fail most: answering di-rectly, the four LLMs of our study pass 81.1% of the explicit rubrics but only 67.4% of the implicit ones (§4.4). The part of the specifi-cation that the request omits is in 𝐶 , and a solver has to recover it before it can answer well.

## 3.2 Ask: What Must the Reply Deliver, and What Must Be Found? 

One structured call asks two kinds of questions about the request, and answers neither. 

Requirement checklist 𝑂 : what must the reply deliver? The model splits the request into separate requirements 𝑜 1, . . . , 𝑜 𝑘 , one per thing asked (“compare A and B, then recommend” gives three). Each requirement records the user’s exact words that create it, a restatement, its scope (the people, items, period, units and any criterion or definition the user gave, copied word for word) and its kind (e.g., list, count, compare, infer, write). The checklist also records the answer format and its source, strings the user requires verbatim, exclusions with the user’s words that ground them, and, when the request edits an earlier reply, that reply’s headings and item layout. Rules the user set earlier in the conversation, such as formats, definitions, a language, or a remark like the chess-notation one in §1, are carried forward unless the last turn replaces them. Undefined words keep their ordinary reach: the checklist may not Qi et al. 

narrow the user’s criterion, add a threshold or an evidence standard, or ask for caveats the user did not ask for. 

Information needs 𝑁 : what must be found in the context? The model then lists the facts that a correct reply must find or derive in 

𝐶 , each as a typed question: lookup (a specific fact), enumerate (every item that meets the user’s criterion), count , compute (arithmetic on stated values, including the user’s own figures), compare , or judge 

(an inference from evidence). Each need names the requirements it serves and where in the conversation to start looking. A list or count requirement first gets an enumerate need that carries the user’s criterion verbatim over all of the materials; further needs ask for later corrections, updates or cancellations of the facts the reply will use. Across the four LLMs, the Ask stage produces 4.2 requirements and 7.8 needs per task on average; 47% of the needs are enumerations, 28% lookups and 13% judgments. 

Faithfulness filters Φ. A question the user never implied does harm: it makes the answer hedge, narrow its scope or change its format. Before anything is answered, code therefore checks the ques-tions against the user’s words. It drops exclusions whose grounding quote is in no user turn, required strings the user never wrote, caution clauses (“do not infer”) and brevity words no user turn asks for, date windows the user never set or that use dates the materials never print, unrequested output formats such as JSON, and templates whose headings the earlier reply lacks. Conversely, a list, count, timeline, summary or classification requirement that no enumerate, count or compute need serves receives an enumer-ate need over the whole set, in the user’s words (12–16% of tasks). Appendix B lists every rule. 

## 3.3 Answer: Generate from the Whole Conversation 

The draft ˜ 𝑦 is written in one call that sees the whole conversation; nothing is retrieved or extracted first, so no evidence is lost before generation. Two inputs steer the call. The first is the questions 

𝑄 , given as an advisory plan: the prompt says that the checklist may contain mistakes, that the user’s words and the materials are authoritative, and that anything the user asked for and the checklist missed must also be covered. The second is a fixed answer protocol ,the same for every task. Some questions arise in every request, whatever its content, and the protocol settles them once (Appen-dix A): (i) has every part of the conversation that bears on each part of the request been read, including later messages that correct, update or cancel an earlier one? (ii) does every requirement come with the specifics the materials give (names and IDs as written, numbers with units, full dates) rather than category words? (iii) is every qualifying item included with the requested fields, without a threshold or period of the solver’s own? (iv) does a yes/no, count or ranking question open with a committed verdict? (v) does every to-tal, share or rate show its inputs and operation? (vi) are attributions, the requested format, length and language, and every exclusion kept, without disclaimers or process commentary? Facts about the people and events come only from the conversation, and “now” is the date the conversation states, else the date of its latest message or record that has already happened. 

## 3.4 Check: Ask the Same Questions Again, with Evidence 

The draft is checked against the same questions in three steps. 

Measured facts. Code first measures word, sentence, paragraph and bullet counts, the user’s length and count limits, missing re-quired strings and headings, and excluded items that are mentioned. 

Question-wise check. One structured call then answers every question against the draft. For each requirement, it states a con-crete test in the user’s words and a verdict (pass, partial or fail); a pass must quote the draft text that satisfies the test. For each information need, it searches the whole conversation and returns findings, each a fact with an exact quote of under 25 words and its location. Enumerate and count needs are itemized, one finding per qualifying item with its identifier and value, and compute needs list every input. The status of a need (correct, missing, incomplete or wrong) states whether the draft contains every finding. The call also returns latent gaps (requirements from any user turn that neither the checklist nor the draft covers), stated facts that the materials contradict or never give, format violations judged with the code’s counts, a repair plan of at most twelve edits, and the parts of the draft to preserve. Three rules keep the check from doing harm: a count may be called wrong only when the check names the item missed, double-counted or wrongly included; an inference the user asked for that rests on stated evidence is not “unsupported”; and the check may never ask to hedge or soften a committed answer. 

Audit. Code then verifies the evidence: a finding whose quote is not in the conversation is marked so that the repair may not rely on it, and itemized findings are recomputed, so that listed values that do not sum to a stated result, listed items that do not match a count, and found items the draft never names are reported. 

## 3.5 Guarded Repair 

When the check or the measured facts flag anything, one more call patches the draft locally. It applies each fix with the facts and quotes from the check, skips fixes that the materials contradict or that rest on an unfound quote, keeps definite answers definite, propagates changed numbers everywhere, and keeps the draft’s structure and the parts marked to preserve. The guard Γ then keeps the patch only if it passes deterministic checks against the draft. The repaired reply must not be empty, nor cut off when the draft is not; it must not shrink below half of the draft or drop most of the draft’s specifics, unless a length fix was requested or the draft broke the user’s limits; and it must not introduce a year outside the conversation’s years, exceed a word limit or break a sentence, bullet or paragraph count that the draft met, drop a required string that the draft contained, or add excluded items or leaked labels. Otherwise the draft is kept. A final code pass restores quotations to their exact source text. 

Cost and scope. QERA makes three LLM calls when the check finds nothing to fix and four otherwise (3.8–4.0 per task on average, retries included; Appendix D), and it uses no training, demonstra-tions or rubrics. ## 4 Experiments 4.1 Experimental Setup 

Benchmark. We use all 405 tasks of CL-bench Life [6], which pose requests over 404 distinct real-life contexts and come with 5,348 rubrics written by annotators. The tasks fall into three cate-gories of 135 tasks and nine subcategories of 45: Communication & Social Interactions (private conversations, group conversations and meeting transcripts, community interactions), Behavioral Records & Activity Trails (digital footprints and daily-life records, game logs, self-tracking trajectories), and Fragmented Information & Revisions 

(creation and revision histories, personal and public information fragments). Contexts are 19.4K tokens long on average and up to 170.8K. 

LLMs. We use four frontier reasoning LLMs from four providers: Kimi K3 from Moonshot AI [16], Grok 4.7 from xAI [36] and GLM-5 from Z.ai [11], all served through Amazon Bedrock, and GPT-6-Luna from OpenAI (version 2026-09-22) [23], served through Azure OpenAI. Every call of every method uses high reasoning effort and the model’s full output limit, so that truncation never separates methods, and all calls of a method in a run use the same frozen model. 

Baselines. Vanilla sends the conversation to the model once. 

CCA [26] compiles the context into a typed representation of rules, output specification and data profile, generates executable verifiers, and corrects the answer when they flag violations; we run the au-thors’ released pipeline. PSCI [40], which reports the best results on CL-bench (28.1% solved with GPT-5.1), induces a specification con-tract from the context and from rotated rubric-style demonstrations, generates under it, checks the answer adversarially and repairs it; no code is released, so we re-implement it with the prompt tem-plates of its paper, verbatim, and draw its demonstrations from CL-bench [7], never from CL-bench Life. Ctx2Skill [29] discovers a skill for each context by multi-agent self-play and injects the selected skill at inference; we run the official code with every role played by the evaluated LLM. Ctx2Skill costs 2.4M–3.1M tokens per task on CL-bench Life, almost all of them in self-play, so on GPT-6-Luna we reuse the skills that the Grok 4.7 self-play selected for each context and run only the final generation on GPT-6-Luna (Ctx2Skill ∗); we charge it the full cost, the reused self-play plus the Qi et al.

## 4.3 RQ2: Asking Before vs. Checking After 

We build QERA up from Vanilla on GPT-6-Luna one stage at a time (Table 3, Figure 4): the answer protocol alone (one call), then the requirement checklist, then the information needs (the complete Ask stage, i.e., QERA’s draft), and finally check and repair (QERA). Each configuration re-runs only the calls its change affects; identical requests are served from the cache of the full run, so consecutive rows differ only in the added stage. For cost, this build-up runs on one LLM; the effect of checking is measured on all four. We also replace the check by a generic self-refinement that rereads the request and the materials, remove the measured facts and the audit, and remove the guard.

## 4.4 RQ3: Where Do the Gains Come From? 

The gains are on implicit requirements. On explicit rubrics, all five methods lie within 1.4 points of each other (81.1–82.5%), and QERA ties the best baseline ( −0.1 [ −1.9, +1.6] against PSCI; Figure 5a). On implicit rubrics, which make up 82% of the classified rubrics, QERA passes 74.0%: 2.5 points [+1.4, +3.6] above the best baseline (CCA) and 6.6 points [+5.3, +7.9] above Vanilla. The baselines also gain mainly on implicit rubrics, but by less (3.1–4.1 points over Vanilla). The pattern holds at every level of overlap: on rubrics Qi et al. 

with under 20%, 20–40% and 40–60% of their content words in the request, QERA leads the best baseline by 2.1, 2.7 and 2.3 points, each significant, and on rubrics with at least 60% it ties. The questions help where the request’s own words run out. 

The gains hold across context lengths. QERA is the best method in every context-length quartile, 1.2–1.8 points above the best baseline of that quartile (Figure 5b), which is CCA for the shorter half and PSCI for the longer half. All methods lose 1.1–4.3 points from the shortest to the longest quartile (QERA 2.6), in line with the bench-mark’s observation that difficulty is only weakly tied to length [6]. Because QERA keeps the whole conversation in every call, its ques-tions add focus without removing context. 

The gains hold across context types. QERA has the highest average in all three categories (Table 1). Its lead is largest in rubric pass rate in Communication & Social Interactions (76.8% vs. 74.7% for CCA), where requests refer to people and turns of a conversation, and in solving rate in Fragmented Information & Revisions (19.8% vs. 16.5%), where later versions supersede earlier ones; it is smallest in Behavioral Records & Activity Trails, as discussed below. Across the 36 combinations of LLM and subcategory, QERA has the highest rubric pass rate in 20, against 8 for PSCI, 6 for CCA, 3 for Ctx2Skill and none for Vanilla (one tie; Table 4). 

What the questions capture. The Ask stage makes two kinds of implicit requirement explicit: rules from earlier turns, as in the chess task of §1, which the checklists of Kimi K3 and Grok 4.7 carry forward (both solve it, while Vanilla writes chess notation with all four LLMs), and complete enumerations: 47% of the needs ask for every item that meets the user’s criterion rather than the first ones found. 

Where headroom remains. Numbers and counts remain the hard-est requirements: QERA passes 71.4% of the rubrics that mention numbers or counts, against 79.2% of the others (pooled over the LLMs). They make up 71% of the rubrics in Behavioral Records & Activity Trails (41% and 32% elsewhere), where QERA’s lead over PSCI is smallest (21.1% vs. 20.7% solved, 73.0% vs. 72.4% of rubrics). 

Answer to RQ3. QERA’s gains come from implicit rubrics, where it leads the best baseline by 2.5 points while tying on explicit ones, a lead that holds in every band of lexical overlap below 60%; QERA ranks first in every context-length quartile and context category and in most LLM–subcategory combinations. 

## 5 Conclusion and Limitations 

On real-life context, the request is not the specification: most of what a correct reply must contain is implicit in the conversation. QERA recovers it by asking before answering, with a requirement checklist and typed information needs kept faithful by code, and asks the same questions again with verified evidence. On all of CL-bench Life and four frontier LLMs, it solves the most tasks of the inference-time methods we compared, at a moderate cost (RQ1); its gain is settled before the answer, by a fixed answer protocol and the request-specific questions, rather than by the check after it (RQ2); and the gain lies on implicit requirements, across context lengths and context types (RQ3). For assistants that work over users’ web traces, inference is best spent on recovering what a request leaves unsaid before generating. 

Limitations. While QERA achieves consistent gains across LLMs and context types, we see several directions for future exploration. First, QERA is built on LLMs throughout: the same LLM writes the requirement checklist and the information needs, answers them and checks its own answer, and an LLM judge grades the result. Code keeps the questions faithful to the user’s words and verifies every quote, yet the underlying LLM can still hallucinate or misread messy context, for example by missing a rule stated turns earlier or by misjudging which records meet a criterion, and such errors carry into the answer. The quality of the questions may therefore depend on the capabilities of the underlying LLM, and different LLMs may ask somewhat different questions; stronger LLMs, or questions verified against the context, are likely to widen the gains. The judge, too, is an LLM: the benchmark validated it against human checks and other judges (§4.1), but individual verdicts can still be wrong. Second, asking and checking cost tokens: QERA uses about five times the tokens of direct answering, although fewer than PSCI and only 4–5% of Ctx2Skill’s. Because the gain is settled before the answer, where QERA’s draft alone already solves more tasks than every baseline, a check that runs only when the questions flag a risk could cut this cost further. Finally, our study covers text: chats, posts, logs and documents. Real-life traces also include photos, screenshots, voice messages and videos; extending QERA’s questions to such multimodal context is a natural next step. 

Ethics. We use CL-bench Life under its license, for evaluation only, and collected no new data; QERA processes a conversation only at inference time and stores nothing. Each stage sends the conversation to the LLM provider, so a deployment inherits the provider’s data-retention terms, which locally hosted LLMs avoid. Assistants that reason over personal traces raise questions of con-sent and data governance, which remain with whoever deploys them.

Answer (one call): the answer protocol. “Write your reply to the user’s last message now.”, the filtered checklist and needs as advisory input (§3.3), then (verbatim): Before writing, read every part of the conversation that bears on each part of the request: every document, log, record and earlier message, not only the first match, and every later message, entry or version that corrects, updates, cancels or reverses an earlier one (state the current value; give the earlier one too when the user asks about change). For list, count or compute requests, go through the materials item by item and do the arithmetic step by step; state the results exactly. 

• Deliver every requirement with the specifics the materials give: names, titles and IDs as the materials write them, numbers with units, full dates and times, each party’s position in their own key words, and the condition, reason or qualifier clauses; never replace a specific with a category word. A thorough, specific reply beats a vague one. 

• For itemized, list or per-item requests, include every qualifying item with its requested fields; brevity never drops them unless the user set a length or count limit. Keep record-level items at item granularity, in source order, unless the user asked for aggregation. Name records by the identifier the materials use (ID, title, full date) as well as any ordinal, and give the source of a claim when the user asks for evidence. 

• Yes/no, count, ranking and classification questions open with one sentence in the user’s key words giving the verdict (Yes or No; “Partly” only naming which part is which), count, winner or class, except inside text the user asked you to write. Show each requested per-item value next to its item, also when a total is given. Use the format the user asked for (table, prose, article, letter, JSON, exact template), with the requested fields in its columns or sentences, and head the sections the user names with those names (spelling corrected). 

• Commit to definite answers: state counts, rankings and totals exactly, never “about”, “approxi-mately” or “provisional”. For a top-N request always name N items (if there is a tie at the cutoff, name the tied items briefly). When asked to infer, judge, classify, estimate, choose or recommend, give the most likely answer plainly with its main evidence; do not add verdicts the user did not ask for. 

• Take the figures the user gives about their own situation (amounts, percentages, rates, dates) as stated unless the materials contradict them, and work out the concrete values they imply for each part of the answer (13% of $500 a month = $65; the rest = $435). For each total, difference, share, average or rate you report, give its inputs and operation in a short clause next to the result (8 / 145 = 5.5%) unless a length limit leaves no room; count each event once even when several places mention it, on the basis the user’s words name. 

• Include an item when it meets the user’s criteria in their ordinary meaning; add no threshold, time window or evidence standard of your own, and never drop a qualifying item because a requested field is not stated (write “<field>: not stated”). 

• When the checklist gives readings for a phrase, answer the first reading; give the result under another reading in one sentence only when it changes the answer (a different count, item set or verdict). 

• Say plainly, in one short clause where it first matters, the definition, criterion, time window or formula you applied when the question uses a term that needs one, and any value you inferred rather than read (a missing year) with the clue it rests on. Otherwise no disclaimers or process commentary: do not describe your research or how you resolved inconsistencies, and do not discuss data quality or what you can or cannot prove. Mention a limitation only when a requested item is entirely absent from the materials, in one short clause for that item. 

• Follow the requested format, structure, length and language; length and count limits the user set take precedence over completeness. When the user refers to an earlier reply’s structure, reuse its headings and item layout, apply the user’s changes, and keep its items unless the user removed them. Respect every exclusion: do not mention excluded items at all, not even to say they were left out, and never add a section, note or list of what you left out or why, unless a requirement asks about the exclusions. 

• Keep every attribution: each quote, claim and number names its speaker or source as the materials display it; never merge different people’s claims. Quote whole sentences unless the user asks for short quotes or a length limit applies; never change words inside quotation marks; cut only whole unrelated sentences, marked with an ellipsis. • Outside any text the user asked you to write (story, letter, post, statement), speak as the assistant: the user is “you”, and the participants in the materials are named in the third person. Inside such text, make the improvements directly: no drafting notes, editor’s notes or remarks on how it differs from the original. No HTML tags in markdown tables. 

• When the materials show a timestamp as concatenated display variants (e.g. “Today at 9:05 AMMonday, June 1, 2026 at 9:05 AM”), write the single absolute form; otherwise keep each timestamp in the source’s format, and never turn a relative or weekday label into a calendar date unless the materials give that date. Decode HTML entities. 

• Facts about the specific people, events, records and works come only from the conversation; never add outside facts, mechanics or causes the materials do not support. General knowledge may fill only what the conversation does not define: calendar arithmetic, the standard meaning of a term or convention the user uses, and (only when the user asks to estimate, project or price something the materials do not give) a typical value labeled as an estimate; none when the user restricts the answer to the provided text. “Now” is the date the conversation states, else the date of the latest message or record entry that has already happened (never a scheduled or planned future date), never your own clock. 

• Never mention a checklist or this process. Output only the reply to the user.  

> Check (one JSON call). Given the checklist, the needs, the code-measured facts and the draft, the model checks the draft “strictly, as a careful grader who knows only this conversation would” and returns:

• requirement_checks : per requirement, a concrete visible test in the user’s words; pass only with a short quote from the draft that satisfies it, otherwise partial or fail with the problem and the exact correction. 

• need_checks : per need, findings searched in the entire conversation (later corrections included), each with an exact quote under 25 words and its location. Enumerate and count needs go through the materials in order with one finding per qualifying item (identifier and value), never only the items the draft already has; count needs state how many items were listed, compute needs list each input and the result. Status correct only when every finding is visible in the draft. A count or total is wrong only when the problem names the item missed, double-counted or wrongly included, or the wrong input or step; a judgment only when a stated fact contradicts it. 

• latent_gaps : what the user’s words in any turn, or a constraint stated earlier (a budget, diet, date, person, something already done), require of this answer that neither the checklist nor the draft covers; never a preference of the checker. 

• unsupported : stated facts about the people, events or records that the materials contradict or never give, with the conflicting quote and the fix; a requested inference resting on stated evidence is not unsupported. 

• format_issues : violations of the user’s format, length, count, order, language, exact-string or exclusion words, judged with the code’s counts. 

• repair_plan : up to 12 concrete edits that change whether a requirement, need or format rule is met, each carrying the actual facts; preserve : the parts that are correct; verdict .

• Rules: judge only by the user’s words and the materials; never ask for caveats, disclaimers or hedges, or to weaken a committed answer; fixes must fit the user’s length limits; unconstrained style is not flagged.  

> Repair (zero or one call). Given the checklist, the actionable part of the check with the audit’s lines, the measured facts and the draft, the model revises the draft under these patch rules (verbatim):

• Apply every repair_plan item, fix every failed or partial requirement and every missing, incomplete or wrong information need with the facts and quotes in the check, and add the latent gaps; correct or remove the unsupported claims. 

• Skip a fix that the user’s words or the materials contradict, and any item marked optional or “consider”. Apply a code comparison only where the quote in the check shows the check’s value; never change a draft value on evidence marked [quote not found]. 

• Keep every definite answer definite: never turn an exact count, total, date or verdict into “about”, “approximately”, “may”, “appears” or “unverified”, and never add caveats, disclaimers or notes about sources, data quality, conflicts or what was left out, even if a fix asks for one. 

• When you change a name, number, date or verdict, change it everywhere it appears (opening sentence, table, summary) and update the totals, shares and rankings that depend on it. “Now” is the date the conversation states, else the latest message or record date that has already happened. 

• Patch locally: keep the draft’s structure, order, headings and wording wherever the check finds no problem, and keep every part listed under preserve. 

• Make each fixed requirement visibly satisfied in the reply itself; never rely on implication. 

• Keep the user’s format, length, count and language words: when the user set a limit, make room by tightening other text rather than exceeding it. 

• Keep quotations exact and every attribution; keep excluded items out of the reply. 

• No meta-commentary: never mention the check, the checklist, the draft, edits or corrections. 

## B Filters, Audit and Guard 

All rules are code; “user’s words” means all user turns, matched after normalization. A user message over 4,000 characters counts as pasted materials, and only its last 2,000 characters are read as the request (1 of the 405 tasks); length cues are read in the request and in earlier user turns under 2,000 characters. Filters Φ on the checklist. (1) Exclusions are dropped when their grounding quote is not in the user’s words, when a purpose-implied exclusion states no purpose, when they are caution instructions, or when they exclude missing values although the user asked to mark unknowns. (2) Caution clauses (“do not infer/assume/overstate”, “as an established fact”, “state the limitation”, “cautiously”, denials of proof) are cut from requirements, scopes and readings unless the user’s words contain them. (3) Brevity words (concise, brief, succinct, “short” before a reply noun, “keep it brief”) are cut when no user turn contains a length cue. (4) If the request names no time, a date window in a scope clause about all of the data is cut when it is narrower than the materials’ span of dates. (5) An earlier-reply template is dropped when that reply is missing or contains none of its headings or entities, or when the last message limits the form (“just list”, “nothing else”). (6) Required strings absent from the user’s words are dropped; the rest take the user’s spelling. (7) An unrequested JSON, YAML or XML format is reset to “not specified”. Filters on the needs. 

(8) Rule (2) applies to needs, and narrowing words the user never used (sustained, explicitly stated, confirmed, significant) are cut. (9) Date windows whose months never occur in the conversation are cut from a need and its “where”; rule (4) applies too. (10) Coverage : a list, count, timeline, summary or classification requirement that no enumerate, count or compute need serves gets a first need for “every item, unit or event the user’s words cover” for it, each with its identifier as the materials write it. 

Actionable check and audit. (11) Only items with something to act on reach the repair; a verdict alone never triggers one. (12) Findings and unsupported-claim items whose quote (12 or more characters) is not found in the conversation are marked “[quote not found]”; items relying on a year outside the conversation’s years are dropped. (13) Using only findings whose quote is found (or that have none), the audit reports the code’s sum when at least three listed inputs of a compute need miss its stated result (as a sum and as a mean, 0.5% tolerance) and the draft lacks that sum; a count that differs from the number of distinct listed items; for an enumerate or count need marked correct that serves a list requirement, the found items that the draft never names, when they are at most 15 and at most half of the items; and up to ten numbers or dates found for an item the draft names but whose value it lacks. A repair is called when any such line or actionable item remains, or when a required string is missing, an excluded item is mentioned or a label leaks. Guard Γ. The repair is rejected, and the draft kept, when it is (14) empty, or cut off mid-sentence while the draft is not; (15) under half of the draft unless a length fix was asked or the draft broke the user’s limits; (16) adds a year outside the conversation’s range; (17) keeps under 30% of the draft’s numbers and times when the draft has ten or more (with the same exceptions as (15)); (18) exceeds a whole-reply word limit (of at least 50 words; 10% tolerance unless stated as a maximum), or breaks a sentence, bullet or paragraph count, that the draft met; (19) is over 1.25 times a draft the user asked to keep brief while nothing failed; or (20) drops a required string that the draft contained, or adds a leaked label or an excluded item. A final polish restores quotations to their source text, sets required strings to the user’s spelling, removes HTML tags from tables, and removes bold and italics when a user turn bans them.
