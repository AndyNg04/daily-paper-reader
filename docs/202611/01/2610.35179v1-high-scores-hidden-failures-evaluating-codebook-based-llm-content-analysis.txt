Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) are increas-ingly used for codebook-based content anal-ysis in computational social science, where codebooks translate theoretical concepts into labeling schemes. While prior work reports strong predictive performance, it remains un-clear whether these systems produce reliable and robust text annotations. We conduct a large-scale empirical failure-mode analysis of LLM-based coding pipelines across four datasets and five models by systematically evaluating their stability, robustness, and conceptual validity under controlled perturbations, domain shifts, and optimization procedures. We uncover mul-tiple failure modes that are largely invisible under standard metrics, showing that high ag-gregate scores frequently coexist with severe label-level disparities, instability across runs, domain sensitivity, and shifts in label interpre-tation. Importantly, these failure modes persist under widely used optimization strategies and remain largely invisible under standard metrics such as accuracy and micro-F1. Our results demonstrate that current LLM-based coding pipelines cannot be treated as stable measure-ment tools without targeted validation beyond aggregate performance. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to annotate text data using natural language instructions, enabling scalable labeling for tasks such as dataset construction, qualitative coding, and content analysis (Törnberg, 2024; Abraham et al., 2025; Rytting et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2023). In computational social science (CSS), these in-structions are typically formalized as codebooks, structured annotation guidelines that map theoreti-cal concepts onto predefined labels. By in-corporating the codebook’s labels and their def-initions directly into prompts, recent studies re-port strong predictive performance and substantial agreement between human and LLM annotations, suggesting that LLMs could serve as scalable sub-stitutes for human coders (Rytting et al., 2023; Ramanathan et al., 2025; Ruckdeschel, 2025; Far-jam et al., 2025). However, the use of LLMs for codebook-based annotation introduces two sources of potential fail-ure. First, LLMs may fail to apply codebook in-structions consistently during inference. A grow-ing body of NLP research highlights prompt sen-sitivity (Shu et al., 2024; Mizrahi et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2023), where model predictions change un-der seemingly minor prompt variations, such as formatting choices, label ordering, or wording dif-ferences. Beyond prompt sensitivity, LLM-based coding pipelines may also exhibit broader robust-arXiv:2610.35179v1 [cs.CL] 26 Oct 2026 ness issues, including instability across repeated runs, inconsistent adherence to codebook defini-tions, and large disparities in performance across individual labels. Second, LLMs are increasingly used not only to apply annotation instructions but also to refine or optimize the prompts themselves. Recent approaches employ automatic prompt opti-mization or multi-agent prompting frameworks to iteratively modify prompts in order to improve pre-dictive performance (Abraham et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). While these methods can yield im-provements on standard evaluation metrics, they also introduce additional risks. For instance, modi-fying prompts that encode coding schemes can im-plicitly alter label definitions, amplify disparities in label-level performance, or shift the conceptual meaning of the constructs being measured (see Sec-tion 5). These issues are consequential in content analysis, where annotations function as measure-ments (Krippendorff, 2018). They feed down-stream statistical analyses (frequency comparisons, regressions, hypothesis testing), as well as qualita-tive interpretation, including conceptual and rela-tional analysis. This requires that performance is balanced across labels for frequency-based claims to hold (Egami et al., 2023), results are compara-ble across runs, models, and corpora for findings to replicate, and the labels must retain the theo-retical constructs the codebook was designed to measure (Halterman and Keith, 2025). In this paper, we operationalize the risks around faithful LLM coding by defining controlled experi-ments and complementary metrics. Then, we em-pirically demonstrate that current LLM-based code-book methods systematically fail in predictable ways. We organize our study around a set of fail-ure modes , spanning two stages of practical CSS pipelines: (1) inference failure modes , which arise when an LLM applies a fixed codebook (see Section 4), and (2) optimization-induced failure modes , which arise when an LLM is used to refine a codebook prior to inference (see Section 5). Our main contributions are: • A framework for systematic failure-mode analysis for LLM content analysis, identifying predictable breakdowns at both the inference and optimization stages. • Evidence across four datasets and five models that commonly reported performance metrics can misrepresent coding reliability by mask-ing these failures, along with a minimal val-idation protocol (see Section 6) to uncover such failure modes. • Two new expert-annotated datasets sharing a single codebook, enabling controlled domain-transfer evaluation, as well as code and evalu-ation pipeline to support reproducibility. 1

2 Related Work 

2.1 Reliability of LLM-Based Text Annotation 

In CSS, LLMs are increasingly used to generate annotations for downstream analyses, such as esti-mating prevalence or testing hypotheses (Gligoric et al., 2025; Baumann et al., 2025). It has been shown that LLM annotations vary depending on the design of the prompt, the domain, the task, or the choice of model (Aldeen et al., 2023; Koco´ net al., 2023). Therefore, naive use can produce un-reliable statistical estimates (Gligoric et al., 2025) and propagate to downstream statistical inference (Baumann et al., 2025). Within content analysis, research has increas-ingly explored how LLMs can leverage codebooks in zero-shot or few-shot coding scenarios (Rytting et al., 2023; Dunivin, 2025; Farjam et al., 2025). Prior work shows that incorporating codebook def-initions or examples can improve classification per-formance (Ruckdeschel, 2025; Xiao et al., 2023). Other studies highlight that prompt design choices and codebook components (e.g., “Negative Clarifi-cation” or “Positive Example”) can influence model predictions (Halterman and Keith, 2025). Some work also reports variability in LLM coding re-sults across repeated runs despite strong overall agreement with human annotations (Ramanathan et al., 2025), suggesting that stability remains an underexplored dimension of evaluation. However, existing studies primarily focus on aggregate per-formance metrics (Ruckdeschel, 2025; Xiao et al., 2023; Rytting et al., 2023; Dunivin, 2025; Farjam et al., 2025), leaving open questions about robust-ness, label-level disparities, and other potential fail-ure modes when LLMs apply codebooks. 2.2 Codebook Optimization with LLMs 

A complementary line of research explores how LLMs themselves can be used to optimize codebook-prompts for LLM inference. Abraham et al. (2025) propose Automatic Prompt Optimiza-tion (APO), starting with a human-labeled sub-set and an initial task prompt. The LLM repeat-edly generates semantically equivalent variations, evaluates them on the labeled data, and retains the best-performing prompts. Additionally, Zhao et al. (2025) introduce the multi-agent framework SCALE. SCALE simulates collaborative coding processes, including discussion and evolving code-books. In our study, we apply APO and SCALE, analysing their robustness, comparability, and im-pact on the quality of codebooks. While prompt optimization techniques focus on improving pre-dictive performance, their effects on robustness, and conceptual validity remain unexplored. In this work, we systematically analyse how codebook optimization interact with LLM-based annotation pipelines and show that performance improvements can coincide with amplified failure modes.

Overinterpretation Ruckdeschel (2025) identify overinterpretation as a recurring issue in LLM-based coding: models infer meaning beyond what is explicitly stated. Our quantitative results confirm this tendency. In two of the three datasets, macro-Precision falls below macro-F1 (Table 2), indicat-ing that models assign labels more liberally than the gold standard warrants. At the label level, precision lags behind recall in 17 of 24 labels, pointing to sys-tematic overprediction rather than isolated errors. For CSS workflows that rely on label frequencies or co-occurrence counts (Baumann et al., 2025; Gligoric et al., 2025), such overcoding inflates the apparent prevalence of concepts in a corpus and can distort substantive conclusions. 

Codebook-Specific Label Meanings A central assumption of codebook-based content analysis is that the model follows the provided definitions rather than defaulting to background knowledge acquired during pretraining. Ziems et al. Accuracy (replacing labels with pseudo-labels such as “Label 1”) and Swapped Label Accuracy (per-muting names and definitions). Building on this work, we introduce a third diagnostic, Label-Only ,in which inference uses only label names, without their definitions. The results (Table 2) reveal a con-sistent pattern across datasets: Label-Only achieves surprisingly high scores (Table 2), especially for VALUE CREATION SCIENCE , suggesting that mod-els lean heavily on label names. Stochastic Instability As Ramanathan et al. (2025) note, LLM (GPT-4) performance can vary across repeated runs. We observe the same effect with open-weight models when setting temperature above zero and not fixing a random seed. 3 Figure 2 shows the aggregated mean F1 scores over 15 such runs. The degree of instability is dataset- and label-dependent (Figure 2). For CCC, scores remain highly stable. V ALUE CREATION SCIENCE shows moderate run-to-run differences that are reduced by averaging three runs, while M INIMUM WAGE 

exhibits stronger variation, particularly for the low-support label TURNOVER. The pattern is con-sistent with the label-level disparities documented in Section 4.1: categories with small support are most vulnerable to both systematic underperfor-mance and stochastic noise. Our observed variance is modest compared to the greater instability re-ported by Ramanathan et al. (2025), likely because open-weight models allow transparent control over sampling parameters. Closed-source APIs, by con-trast, may introduce opaque configuration changes between provider updates, compounding run-to-run variability with silent shifts in model behaviour.

Domain Transfer Failure A critical assumption in codebook-based content analysis is that coding frameworks remain valid across new data sources within the same theoretical domain. We test this assumption by applying the V ALUE CREATION 

SCIENCE codebook, developed on academic publi-cations, to news articles covering the same business and economics topics. Both corpora share the un-derlying constructs but differ in register: style, vo-cabulary, and communicative conventions change. The results expose a paradox (Table 3). On one side, the per-label performance improves substan-tially in the news domain: micro-F1 rises from 44.89 to 62.22 and macro-F1 from 39.57 to 59.55, likely because news articles use simpler language and more direct formulations (qualitative examples in Appendix C). On the other side, this confidence does not extend to a holistic understanding of the coding task: the model fails to predict the full cor-rect label set for any single instance, resulting in an overall accuracy of 0.0. Such domain sensitivity poses significant risks for comparative analyses and theory testing in CSS. A codebook that performs well on one text type may yield systematically dif-ferent results on another, even when both sources address the same substantive questions. These in-consistencies can lead to spurious empirical pat-terns or failed replications.

5.1 Amplified Bias and Sensitivity Increasing Label Bias The label-level disparities observed during inference (Section 4.1) are ampli-fied when LLMs are used to optimize codebooks. Small modifications to definitions or examples can disproportionately shift performance across labels (see Table 4). When evaluation relies solely on aggregate metrics, such as micro-F1 , there is a dan-ger of endorsing adaptations that improve majority classes while harming minority ones. A concrete case illustrates the danger: adding positive and negative examples improves micro/macro-F1 , ac-curacy , and macro-Precision on both CCC and VALUE CREATION SCIENCE , yet simultaneously drives worst-label-F1 down to 5.67 and 11.32 re-spectively. Judged by aggregate metrics alone, these optimizations would be endorsed as bene-ficial. In practice, they systematically suppress minority categories, precisely the labels most vul-nerable to begin with. The problem is compounded by automatic methods such as APO, which opti-mize for micro-F1 by design and therefore have no mechanism to prevent minority-label degradation.

The absence of universal optimization rules motivates automated methods such as APO and SCALE, yet these introduce sys-tematic risks of their own. 5.3 Efficiency and Accessibility 

Beyond performance, optimization approaches must also be feasible for researchers in practice. We therefore compare the efficiency of different methods by counting the number of API calls re-quired for each approach (see Table 6). This pro-vides an approximate measure of computational cost. FULL requires a single call per dataset. SCALE uses 688–1,044 calls depending on the dataset. APO requires 113,200 calls uniformly, over two orders of magnitude more than SCALE and five orders more than FULL, yet does not con-sistently outperform either alternative (Table 4). The cost-benefit profile of iterative optimization is thus far from guaranteed. Beyond cost, accessibil-ity also matters: minimal prompt adjustments are within reach of any social scientist, whereas APO and SCALE demand non-trivial engineering skills. 

6 Minimal Validation Protocol 

To mitigate failure modes from Sections 4 and 5, we recommend the following minimal, low-cost validation protocol, intended as a practical screen-ing procedure rather than a statistically exhaustive evaluation: 

Minimal Validation Protocol Tail Risk: Report macro-F1 , worst-label-F1 ,per label scores and label supports alongside micro-F1/accuracy; include macro-Precision to detect overcoding. 

Meaning Adherence: Test Label-Only , Generic Label , and Swapped Label to detect reliance on label-name semantics. 

Stability: Repeat inference ≥ 3 as a practical minimum for detecting substantial instability; report the number of runs and the resulting vari-ability, and use more runs when variance is high or the API is stochastic/opaque. 

Sensitivity: Apply semantics-preserving for-matting or label-order perturbations to reveal prompt-specific effects. 

Transfer: If possible, evaluate on an additional dataset from the same domain to detect shifts under domain or style changes. 

Optimization Safeguards: When refining code-books, include worst-label-F1 to protect minor-ity labels, verify constructs with experts, and report computational cost. 

7 Conclusion 

LLMs enable large-scale content analysis in CSS but remain fragile and context-dependent. Our ex-periments demonstrate that high aggregate scores routinely coexist with severe label-level disparities, reliance on label-name semantics over codebook definitions, instability across runs, sensitivity to superficial formatting choices, and fragile domain transfer. When LLMs are additionally used to opti-mize codebooks, these problems do not diminish, they compound: optimization amplifies minority-label bias, produces dataset-contingent effects, and can silently alter the theoretical constructs a code-book is designed to measure. A recurring theme across all failure modes is that standard metrics are insufficient to detect them. To address this, we proposed a minimal validation protocol (Sec-tion 6) that operationalizes our findings into con-crete checks, intended to be lightweight enough for routine adoption in CSS research. 

Limitations 

Our analysis is limited to a small set of datasets, codebooks, and model configurations. While these reflect common CSS research settings, results may not generalize across domains, languages, or an-notation frameworks. Nonetheless, our evaluation framework is applicable to any dataset, codebook, or LLM, and we hope future work will extend it to larger, multilingual, and domain-specific contexts. Finally, the human evaluation of optimized code-books relies on only two experts, which may limit intersubjective validity. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work studies the reliability of LLM-based text annotation and highlights potential risks when such annotations are used in downstream social science analyses. By identifying failure modes in infer-ence and codebook optimization, our goal is to promote more transparent and responsible use of LLMs as measurement tools in research workflows. All datasets used in this study are either publicly available or released by the authors with appropri-ate documentation. We do not introduce new per-sonal or sensitive data, and our experiments focus on methodological evaluation rather than produc-ing judgments about individuals or groups. LLMs were used to assist with grammar, spelling, and refinement of the manuscript text. All technical content, analyses, and conclusions were developed and verified by the authors.

B.1 Original Dataset B.1.1 Annotation Scheme 

The annotation scheme is grounded in an abstract model of value creation systems, which distin-guishes eight interdependent categories that char-acterise the essential elements of value creation processes. Each category is defined relationally— that is, through its connections to other elements in the model. The categories and their definitions are as follows: • Actor (0): an individual or a collective that contributes a resource at its disposal to a pro-cess of creating an artefact. • Resource (1): a capability of an actor that is used in the process of creating an artefact. • Process (2): an activity for the creation of an artefact to which actors contribute their resources. • Pattern (3): an organising principle through which a process integrates the resources to create an artefact. • Artefact (4): a good that is created by inte-grating resources according to a pattern and that serves a demand. • Demand (5): an articulated or anticipated need that an artefact serves. • Revenue (6): a return an actor receives for contributing to the process of creating an arte-fact. • Framework (7): a context factor that influ-ences how value creation takes place. Annotators were instructed to label noun phrases in English-language abstracts of scientific articles about value creation. Labels capture both positive and negative instances of each category (e.g., finan-cial losses are labelled R EVENUE ; material costs are labelled R ESOURCE ). 

B.1.2 Annotation Process 

The annotation procedure was developed iteratively across two phases: a pilot study and a main annota-tion campaign. 

Pilot study. An internal pilot was conducted in June–July 2023, in which 8 annotators labelled 20 abstracts in parallel using the D OCCANO anno-tation tool. 5 The pilot yielded a Fleiss’ κ of 0.31, indicating fair for multi-label span annotation, but insufficient agreement. A qualitative analysis of 75 annotator comments and a co-occurrence analy-sis of category overlaps revealed three main sources of disagreement: (i) ambiguous category bound-aries, particularly between P ATTERN and P ROCESS ,REVENUE and D EMAND , and A CTOR and F RAME -

> WORK

; (ii) missing formal coding rules regarding grammatical forms, compound phrases, and ab-breviations; and (iii) insufficient attention to the value-creation context when annotating. 

Guideline revision. Based on the pilot findings, the annotation guidelines were substantially re-vised. Key improvements included: sharpened category definitions supplemented with illustrative positive and negative examples drawn from the pi-lot data; the introduction of a step-by-step annota-tion procedure recommending that annotators first identify A CTOR , P ROCESS , and A RTEFACT before turning to the remaining categories; explicit cod-ing rules restricting annotations to complete noun phrases, prohibiting the splitting of compound ex-pressions, and requiring annotators to consider the relationships between labelled entities as specified by the value creation model. A one-page refer-ence sheet summarising the definitions, explana-tory notes, and examples was provided as a quick-reference document. 

Annotator training. Before the main campaign, annotators participated in a training session con-sisting of a presentation of the revised guidelines followed by a joint practice labelling exercise. An interactive training quiz was also distributed, which walked annotators through a sample text and tested their understanding of noun phrase boundaries, context-dependent category assignment, and the distinction between entities and concepts.

D Statistical Significance Testing 

In the following, we present the procedure for as-sessing (i) run-to-run stability and (ii) instance-level uncertainty when comparing each prompt pro-totype against the baseline. 

D.1 Setup 

For each dataset, we compare each prompt proto-type against a fixed baseline prompt. We perform

E Encoder Baseline: Fine-Tuned RoBERTa 

We additionally evaluate a fine-tuned RoBERTa (Liu et al., 2019) baseline to contextualize the observed failure modes against a conven-tional supervised encoder model. We fine-tune 

roberta-large 6 as a multi-label, or multi-class for CCC, sequence classification model using the training split of each dataset and select the best checkpoint on the validation split. Inputs are trun-cated to a maximum length of 512 tokens. We train for 3 epochs with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −5, batch size 8. Table 7 reports the performance of the RoBERTa baseline across the three datasets. The results show that RoBERTa exhibits a pattern similar to the one observed for LLMs: relatively high aggregate scores, especially Micro-F1, can coexist with sub-stantially lower Macro-F1 and a Worst-Label-F1 of 0.0. What shows that even a supervised encoder baseline can achieve moderate Micro-F1 while en-tirely failing on minority or difficult labels. How-ever, the underlying causes of these failures differ. In LLM-based coding pipelines, failures may arise from prompt sensitivity, run instability, domain sen-sitivity, or semantic drift in label interpretation. In contrast, RoBERTa’s collapse on individual labels is more likely driven by label imbalance and sparse supervision, which are common in content analysis datasets.
