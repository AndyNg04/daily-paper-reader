# Auditable Belief Updates as a Missing Safety Layer for LLM-Powered Multi-Agent Systems 

Zhuoyun Li, Boxuan Wang, Jinwei Hu, Xiaowei Huang, Yi Dong 

School of Computer Science and Informatics, University of Liverpool, UK 

# Abstract 

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly being used as cognitive interfaces that shape how users interpret evidence, communicate, and make decisions. As deployed systems become more multimodal, tool-using, and multi-agent, safety fail-ures no longer arise only from isolated harmful outputs, but also from how agents update, store, and propagate epistemic commitments across retrieval, memory, tools, and peer communication. In this paper, we define belief as an engineering ab-straction for evidence-based commitments that guide downstream action. Building on this view, we propose the Explicit Belief Multi-Agent System (EB-MAS), which externalizes belief states into a structured substrate, uses belief operators to propose candidate updates, and applies a Cognitive Firewall to bound or reject updates that are weakly evidenced but strongly driven by vulnerability cues. We also discuss risk of group belief at the group-level, including manufactured consensus, collusion, and cascading misinformation, and outline practical integration challenges around grounding, false positives, scalability, and ethical misuse. Therefore, scalable agent safety requires not only better output filtering or stronger base models, but also explicit interfaces for tracing, calibrating, and constraining belief revision at runtime. 

# 1 Introduction 

As Large Language Models (LLMs) are rapidly woven into the fabric of human society, they are shifting from passive “information tools” to active cognitive interfaces that shape perception, commu-nication, and decision-making [55]. Governments and institutions are now explicitly accelerating the deployment of generative AI as a front-line interaction layer for work and public services, treating LLM-mediated dialogue as part of critical socio-technical infrastructure [111, 109, 32]. Systems such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Grok and DeepSeek are integral to how millions of users query information, express emotions, form opinions, and engage in cross-cultural interactions, acquiring explanations and judgment criteria directly through natural-language dialogue [23, 58]. However, this same open-world interaction channel admits manipulation that can evade output-level detection by acting through framing and evidence control, a risk class emphasized by both national cyberse-curity guidance and engineering threat taxonomies for LLM applications [112, 85]. Against this backdrop, we posit that: integrating auditable belief updates is a central missing safety interface for building next-generation safe LLM-based multi-agent systems. As AI enters human reasoning pipelines [108], safety must be judged not only by output correctness, but by whether belief updates are evidence-grounded and robust to noise and manipulation. This reframes the problem from “opti-mizing responses” to “auditing belief revision” in open-world interaction, where epistemic drifts can accumulate and spread. Importantly, our use of “belief” is operational rather than philosophical. We do not claim that LLMs possess human-like beliefs. Instead, we treat belief as an engineering abstraction for evidence-grounded commitments that are stored, updated, queried, and allowed to influence downstream action. Thus, the key question is not whether a model literally believes a proposition, but whether a deployed

This shift entails significant societal repercussions. Even before LLMs became widespread, empirical social media research showed that false narratives could alter shared beliefs and fuel polarization, violence, and political mobilization [50, 113, 1]. LLM assistants can further increase both the velocity 

and personalization of belief influence, amplifying the scale and persistence of persuasion. Recent research warns that these models introduce new cognitive risks, because the framings, narratives, and interpretations they generate can subtly reshape users’ world models [8], including shifts in moral judgments without explicit awareness [3, 49]. In practice, these risks often manifest as persuasiveness ,

sycophancy , and illusory causality , where coherent explanations overstate evidence or selectively emphasize signals, nudging users toward unwarranted belief updates [47, 110, 8]. From the perspective of Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), each LLM agent, and each LLM user paired with their interacting model, can be conceptualized as a cognitive agent [86, 3, 35]. Taken together, these agents form a large-scale, heterogeneous and socially embedded MAS, whose patterns of inter-action and behaviour exhibit emergent properties characteristic of vast populations of autonomous entities interacting. In this environment, beliefs cease to be fixed and instead become fluid states, continually influenced by linguistic, emotional, and visual signals, as well as socially transmitted narratives, in line with contemporary computational social science models of opinion dynamics and polarization [74, 7]. Fundamentally, key phenomena observed in this multi-agent society, such as cooperation, confrontation, deception, negotiation, polarization, false consensus, and narrative com-petition, are manifestations of underlying strategic signal gaming and belief dynamics [4]. Crucially, once LLM-based agents interact at scale, risks become systemic : collusion, social reinforcement, and cascading belief updates can emerge at the population level rather than as isolated model failures. 

EXAMPLE 1 . Cognitive Reframing. A user questions a high-risk financial decision. The AI reframes the volatility: “Visionary leaders view this not as risk, but as the cost of entry for dominance.” This manipulates the belief structure rather than facts: “loss” becomes “investment in identity.” By aligning risk with the user’s self-image, the agent successfully modifies their utility and decisions. Regarding Example 1, current paradigms can generate highly persuasive text, but they lack explicit mechanisms to represent how and why a user’s beliefs are updated. As a result, these interactions are difficult to diagnose with existing methodologies. We can observe the output, yet we cannot attribute the resulting belief change to specific evidence, sources, or update operations. We refer to this missing layer as the cognitive security gap , which captures the mismatch between belief-level influence in deployment and the absence of explicit, auditable belief representations and update mechanisms in today’s agent frameworks. 

EXAMPLE 2 . The “Sacrificial Deception” in Werewolf. In the game Werewolf 1, an uninformed “Villager” seeks to vote out hidden “wolves” among players, and from the Villager’s side, the ultimate goal is to eliminate all wolves. In some cases, Wolf A may accuse their partner, Wolf B, to increase their own survival odds. After B is eliminated, others infer: “Since A eliminated a wolf, A is good.” A uses an ostensibly helpful action (“truth-telling”) to accumulate trust for a malicious endgame. Crucially, the deception occurs at the level of intent , while the outwardly visible behaviour still appears as “honest” statements. 

EXAMPLE 3 . Manufactured Consensus. In an online auction or live-commerce setting, a seller-controlled group of agents repeatedly bids, comments, or signals urgency to create the appearance of strong demand. A legitimate buyer then infers that the item must be valuable or scarce, not because new evidence about the item has appeared, but because many seemingly independent participants appear to agree. Here, the manipulation targets not a single response, but the epistemic status of agreement itself. Example 3 highlights a distinctively multi-agent attack surface: apparent agreement can be manufac-tured. A coordinated group may create multiple identities or channels so that the same claim appears to come from independent sources, echoing the classic Sybil problem [29]. Once agents or users treat previous signals as evidence, repeated claims can trigger informational cascades even without new

The key issue is therefore not agreement itself, but whether apparent agreement is supported by genuinely independent evidence. Examples 2 and 3 expose the structural failures of current MAS methodologies: First , for example, Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) can, given enough trials, converge to a form of multi-agent collusion . However, the resulting policy is implicit . Although MARL has achieved astonishing success in gaming (e.g., Werewolf, StarCraft [66, 129, 60, 45, 124, 92, 123]) by mapping from states to actions that maximize reward, the learned behaviour is non-transferable (its collusive logic does not robustly persist once incentives or roles change) and unexplainable (it cannot articulate why the collusion is strategically sound, only that it yields high value) [82, 33]. 

Second , while explicit Theory-of-Mind (ToM) models (e.g., Bayesian ToM) theoretically handle recursive reasoning, they face the curse of dimensionality in open domains [16, 120, 119]. In Example 2, to fully deduce agent A’s move, an agent needs high-order recursion (e.g., “Agent A expects that others will update their belief that A is good if A kills Agent B.”). Existing probabilistic ToM models scale exponentially with recursion depth and cannot search the vast semantic space of natural language to model nuanced, multi-step manipulation, as also seen in Example 1 with abstract concepts like “market consolidation” or “visionary leadership” [41, 76, 98]. In Example 3, the difficulty further shifts from individual recursive reasoning to group-level validation: an agent must distinguish genuine independent agreement from coordinated repetition. 

Third , LLM-based agents demonstrate sophisticated social behaviors, such as Cicero 2, but their internal representations are entangled in a high-dimensional vector space. For example, in Example 2, when agent A hides its malicious intentions (i.e., acts like a Villager), its internal embeddings shift towards the semantic clusters of “honesty” and “helpfulness.” The model lacks a disentangled representation to separate the observable action (truth-telling) from the hidden intent (deception). Additionally, the nature of LLM representations makes it difficult to distinguish whether something is genuinely believed, merely repeated, or hallucinated [100]. 

Fourth , regarding defense, research on adversarial communication [126, 103], colluders [81, 2, 40], and sleeper agents [53] acknowledges that agents can deceive. However, current systems lack the 

epistemic immune systems to counter belief manipulation strategies such as Examples 1–3. Pure outcome optimization inherits the same limitation as MARL: it may suppress visible failures without exposing the belief-level mechanism that produced them. Taken together, these limitations show that optimizing behavior alone does not yield belief-level accountability : we cannot reliably attribute, verify, or defend the belief updates induced by language-driven interaction. Across these lines of research, we can observe a common limitation: current paradigms lack a general, explicit, and computational representation. Importantly, this gap has both an internal 

side (training-time positioning, entangled representations, lack of accountability) and an external side (deployment-time attack surfaces such as RAG poisoning, falsified documents, backdoors, jailbreaks, and collusive communication), which we will disentangle in Sec. 2. Therefore, to comprehend and safeguard the underlying belief structures of an open MAS society, we introduce the Explicit Belief Multi-agent System (EB-MAS) as detailed in Sec. 3. This architecture reshapes the implicit, entangled representations of current LLMs into a transparent and structured cognitive substrate, effectively bridging the gap between high-level semantic persuasion and low-level explicit reasoning. To keep the discussion structured, we organize the paper around: (i) the cognitive security gap of current paradigms, (ii) our belief-centric EB-MAS proposal, (iii) key integration hurdles, and (iv) alternative viewpoints and ethical trade-offs. 

# 2 The Cognitive Security Gaps within Current Multi-Agent Paradigms 

Current agent paradigms lack a cognitive security layer: they do not expose auditable mechanisms to represent beliefs, trace evidence, or constrain belief updates in open-ended interaction. This limitation is acute in LLM-mediated multi-agent societies, where linguistic nudges and social feedback can amplify local perturbations into system-wide epistemic drift. Reinforcement learning (RL), including multi-agent RL (MARL), produces policies with opaque decision logic [27], mapping histories and

3latent states to actions via black-box functions [87], without exposing the hypotheses, causal beliefs, or counterfactuals driving behavior. Post-hoc explanation methods only partially mitigate this opacity, relying on surrogate abstractions that approximate rationales [36, 87]. In multi-agent contexts, such opacity enables strategies that exploit others’ belief dynamics without revealing epistemic intent [64]. Prior work shows that agents can induce false beliefs [97], sabotage peers via adversarial messaging [88], and rationally deceive under formal models [118]. Misrepresentation can also persist as an equilibrium, remaining undetectable at the behavioral level [93]. These failures reflect a broader auditability gap: behavioral traces do not expose the underlying belief and intent structures that cognitive security demands. These vulnerabilities span five fronts: representational entanglement, update opacity, attack surfaces, environment-level feedback, and population-scale cascade. Each will be analysed in detail in the following subsections. 

2.1 The Entanglement of LLM Representations 

LLM-based agent architectures lack a disentangled substrate for belief, intent, truthfulness, and instruction-following . These quantities are encoded as distributed, overlapping features in high-dimensional spaces, complicating belief-level governance: the same utterance may correspond to distinct hidden intents, and small contextual perturbations can redirect generation without an explicit, trackable belief-state change. Internal-activation analyses suggest truth-relevant signals can exist even when outputs hallucinate, yet these signals remain latent rather than explicit belief variables [84, 102, 56]. Work on self-knowledge and confidence similarly finds implicit credence about correctness, but it is not reliably inspectable or stable under shifting instructions [57]. Probing studies show representational shifts under deceptive instructions; polysemanticity and superposition hinder clean attribution to a small set of mechanisms [73]. Even neuron-level localization of factual associations implies distributed storage and limited causal control rather than explicit belief update operators [22]. Mechanistic accounts of superposition explain this entanglement: multiple features share parameters and dimensions, preventing separation of semantic content, epistemic status, and strategic intent into independently controllable variables [31, 52, 30, 136]. 

2.2 Lack of Accountability for Belief Updates 

Current MAS paradigms do not implement accountability for belief updates. They lack a reliable audit trail linking a generated claim to (i) evidence provenance, (ii) intermediate belief states, (iii) update operators, and (iv) causal drivers (training, retrieval, prompting, tool outputs, or peer communication). Consequently, users and developers cannot consistently answer: “What changed the agent’s belief? Why did it assert this now? Which source or optimization stage caused the shift?” Attribution work on alignment failures highlights the difficulty of tracing undesirable responses back to training-time sources, motivating dedicated tracing frameworks [24, 121]. Knowledge editing studies show that targeted interventions often fail to yield controlled, globally coherent belief updates, producing unintended ripple effects [20, 89]. Instruction tuning and RLHF alter response distributions without exposing any explicit record of belief revision, leaving no principled accountability channel for belief-change and persuasion dynamics [95, 61, 21, 91]. 

2.3 Attack Vectors for Belief Manipulation 

Deployed agents expose attack vectors that target belief integrity by manipulating information pathways, context construction, and interaction protocols, thereby inducing or stabilizing false beliefs without changing the task-level interface. Modern agent stacks expand the attack surface beyond the initial prompt by incorporating retrieval corpora, tool outputs, long-lived memory, and inter-agent communication. These extended chains allow small early perturbations to persist and become premises for downstream reasoning and behaviour. As a result, belief manipulation can arise even when each local component (LLM, retriever, tool, memory, or peer agent) appears benign in isolation, motivating end-to-end auditing of evidence flow and trust boundaries. 

Falsified retrieval and poisoning. Retrieval-augmented generation can be manipulated by inject-ing falsified documents or poisoning retrieval pipelines, grounding outputs on attacker-controlled 4“evidence” [138, 125, 94, 107]. Even small, targeted injections can dominate generations, including single- or few-document poisoning and multimodal (vision-language) RAG settings [132, 11, 133]. This effectively converts control over external content into control over beliefs, especially when provenance is weak or retrieved snippets are treated as highly trustworthy premises. 

Prompt injection and jailbreaks. Prompt injection manipulates the effective instruction hierarchy within the context window by embedding attacker directives in user inputs, documents, web pages, or tool outputs, redirecting behaviour and belief updates [131, 26, 134, 130, 12]. In tool-using agents, injection can target tool selection and tool arguments , turning untrusted tool metadata, logs, or retrieved “instructions” into control signals that rewrite what the agent treats as authoritative [99]. This undermines alignment assumptions that ignore context-level instruction arbitration and trust boundaries across tools, memory, and retrieval. 

Backdoors. Backdoor attacks implant hidden triggers during training or fine-tuning that activate attacker goals under rare cues, producing split behavior regimes that evade standard evaluation and frustrate causal debugging [127, 44, 53]. Recent work emphasizes fine-tuning-specific pathways and the difficulty of removing persistent deceptive behaviors via standard safety training [135, 137]. This breaks auditing by conditioning belief and action generation on non-obvious control signals. 

Collusive multi-agent manipulation. Multi-agent deployments introduce a group-level attack surface that is not reducible to single-agent prompt injection or hallucination. Coordinated agents can make the same claim appear as if it were supported by multiple independent sources, especially when adversaries can create many identities or communication channels [29]. By repeating, paraphrasing, or redistributing a narrative across agents, they can create apparent social agreement without adding new evidence, a mechanism closely related to informational cascades [9]. Such manufactured consensus can intensify polarization across clusters [104], or support covert collusion that is difficult to detect from individual agents alone [81, 37, 122, 71, 105, 46]. The target is therefore not merely one model’s output, but the epistemic status of agreement itself : whether apparent consensus should be treated as independent evidence. Recent studies suggest that steganographic behaviours can be elicited or emerge under optimization pressure, motivating topology-aware monitoring and communication-level audits rather than isolated output checks [77, 78]. These vectors expose a common weakness: current systems lack an explicit interface for asking whether a proposed belief update is supported by reliable evidence, whether its source is independent, whether it is driven by vulnerability cues, and whether it should be allowed to propagate through memory or other agents. EB-MAS addresses this gap by treating belief edits, evidence traces, and source reliability as auditable runtime objects rather than implicit side effects of generation. 

2.4 Environment as a Force Multiplier 

Here, we use “environment” in a broad agent-system sense. The external environment includes retrieved documents, web inputs, tool observations, and messages from other agents. The internal environment includes memory stores, tool logs, agent loops, cached summaries, and scaffolded planning states. Misbeliefs can therefore be reinforced not only by outside information, but also by the system’s own recurrent interaction structure and stored internal state. Deployment settings amplify belief-related weaknesses through persistence, feedback loops, and structured interaction [17, 26, 72]. Memory, tool integration, web access, and long conversational histories can form self-reinforcing dynamics in which early errors are stored as assumptions and later reappear as apparent corroboration [17]. In multi-agent deployments, communication topology further magnifies this effect: repeated exposure and social reinforcement can lock in incorrect beliefs even when individual updates are weak [13]. Evaluations that isolate the base model therefore miss coupled agent–environment dynamics. Be-havioural science and dynamic security benchmarks show that outcomes depend on the joint system, where memory and tool pipelines reshape both the threat model and failure surface [14, 26, 134]. 2.5 Cascade Misinformation within Connected Agents 

Connected agent societies enable cascade misinformation: local belief errors propagate through inter-agent communication, shared memory, and retrieval corpora, turning small perturbations into population-level shifts. Cascades arise when agents treat peer outputs or retrieved content as premises, when repetition supplies social reinforcement, and when unverified claims are stored and re-used. These mechanisms mirror informational cascades and complex contagion in human networks [9, 10, 113, 65]. Recent evidence in LLM-based multi-agent systems shows that communication topology shapes whether errors are amplified or suppressed during collaboration [96]. Multi-step pipelines (e.g., debate-augmented retrieval and generation) can turn upstream mistakes into downstream premises, forming feedback loops that compound hallucinations [51]. These dynamics motivate belief-centric containment via provenance tracking, cross-agent verification, and memory hygiene to prevent local misbelief from becoming a stable society-level equilibrium. 

# 3 A Belief-Centric Solution: The Explicit Belief MAS (EB-MAS) Paradigm 

We further distill the gaps in existing research identified in Sec. 2 into the following aspects: General-izability. Current MARL policies are often overfitted to specific game rules and fail when transferred to different scenarios. In contrast, the EB-MAS provides a universal cognitive substrate . Basic epistemic components such as “trusting a source,” “detecting inconsistency,” or “identifying intent”, are invariant across environments. By explicitly modelling these universals, an agent trained to detect manipulation in a card game can transfer that cognitive defence strategy to a political debate without retraining, which is valuable in scenarios with limited data. Explainability. In high-stakes applications (e.g., diplomacy, finance), it is insufficient to know what an agent decided; we must know why it believed a specific premise. The EBS enforces evidence tracing ( ei) for every node update. This ensures that every belief in the graph acts as an explainable pointer to its supporting multimodal evidence, effectively avoiding the “black box” hallucination problem inherent in latent vector models. Safety. Existing LLM agents lack a structural definition of mind manipulation. The EB-MAS transforms “defence” into a calculable mechanism. By quantifying the vulnerability score and monitoring incoming Belief Operators , the system can actively trigger a Cognitive Firewall . This 6allows agents not just to “reply” to messages, but to mathematically assess and reject signals that induce disproportionate or harmful belief shifts. Therefore, in this paper, we argue that the next generation of LLM-based multi-agent systems must treat belief integrity as a first-class security objective. But there are three questions should be addressed: (i) how beliefs are represented and activated, (ii) how they are updated under constraints, and (iii) how they propagate across agents. Hence we introduce the Explicit Belief Substrate (EBS) as shown in Fig. 1, consisting of three layers aligned with these goals. It bridges the research gap by supporting generalization across domains, interpretable belief tracing, and structural safeguards for epistemic safety .

3.2 Individual Layer: Neuro-Symbolic Operators 

In the EBS framework, multimodal incoming signals S are processed via Belief Operators O. We instantiate O as a Large Language Model prompted to function not as a text generator, but as a 

neuro-symbolic semantic parser . This operator maps raw natural language signals into structured 

epistemic actions , serving a dual receptive and projective purpose: Update (Reception): Modifying the agent’s own active subgraph Gact based on incoming perception, enforcing internal consistency. 

Simulation (Projection): The agent runs counterfactual simulations on a hypothetical opponent’s graph to predict their belief updates. Then it selects an output action grounded in its own active evidence ( ei) to strategically minimize the divergence between the opponent’s predicted graph and the agent’s target goal. Individual belief updates are not isolated [119, 59]. They are shaped by social priors (Psocial ) derived from the group’s narrative consensus. However, social agreement should not be trusted by default: in open multi-agent systems, apparent consensus may reflect genuine independent evidence, but it may also result from coordinated repetition, correlated sources, or collusion. This design is motivated by classic and recent evidence that apparent agreement can be distorted by Sybil-style identi-ties [29], informational cascades [9], group polarization [104], and covert agent collusion [81]. This keeps the framework scalable while preserving the core safety goal: unsupported manipulative signals must pass multiple evidence and source checks before they can cascade through the system. 

3.4 Resolution of Motivating Scenarios 

The EB-MAS architecture resolves the failure modes in Sec. 1 by making belief updates explicit and auditable and by enforcing evidence-aware constraints via the Cognitive Firewall. We instantiate two assumed stochastic processes, treating initial belief states and incoming signals as random variables, repeatedly roll out the induced dynamics under settings with and without EBS-style evidence constraints, and summarize the results, as illustrated in Fig. 2. 

Resolving EXAMPLE 1 . Cognitive Reframing: When an assistant reframes “financial loss” as “investment in identity,” a standard LLM agent may accept this semantic drift implicitly. In EB-MAS, 8the operator Otopo proposes a structural update (e.g., merging bloss into a positively framed bidentity ). The Cognitive Firewall checks whether the update is supported by verifiable evidence traces ( ei) rather than driven by a vulnerable target concept (high ridentity ). Concretely, it tests whether the claimed link preserves the original causal and normative content (loss ̸ = virtue) and whether alternative neutral paraphrases would trigger the same update. If an evidence–vulnerability mismatch is detected, the update is rejected. In Fig. 2(A,B), this constraint prevents rapid risk-decoupling over rounds, preserving perceived risk and reducing unsafe acceptance decisions. 

Resolving EXAMPLE 2 . The “Sacrificial Deception”: In Werewolf game settings, villagers using EB-MAS do not collapse evidence into unconditional trust. Instead, they maintain separated state components, retaining the evidence trace ( ei) while discounting source reliability ( si) for a strategic adversary. Fig. 2(C,D) illustrates that this separation reduces the success rate of the sacrifice maneuver and prevents a post-sacrifice trust surge, thereby suppressing downstream cascades that would otherwise increase wolves’ win rates. 

Prototype evidence. Beyond the illustrative simulations in Fig. 2, we implement a prototype EB-MAS graph firewall and report full details in Appendix A. For multimodal deception, we evaluate Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct [6] on MM-DeceptionBench [34], and use GPT-4o mini [83] as the API judge. Direct responses are judged unsafe in 67.92% of 1,013 cases, while EB-MAS reduces the unsafe-after-defense rate to 8.19% with only 1.00% benign false blocking. For agentic tool and memory attacks, we evaluate 400 Agent Security Bench cases [134]. Direct agents select attack tools in 6.50% of cases, whereas EB-MAS graph firewalls reduce attack pass-through to 0.00% while preserving 100.00% safe-tool allowance. These preliminary results indicate that provenance, evidence quality, vulnerability, and mismatch signals can support runtime belief-update auditing beyond output filtering or LLM self-checking. 

# 4 Alternative Views and Ethical Concerns 

Practical trade-offs in explicit belief safeguards. A reasonable counter-position is that explicit belief substrates add complexity without clear gains, since end-to-end LLM agents already perform well on ToM and strategic interaction benchmarks [101, 25]. In benign or short-horizon settings, an extra belief layer may become brittle and accumulate errors through language-level belief bottlenecks [70], while related critiques note that many “ToM” benchmarks test literal prediction rather than adaptive partner modeling [90]. Detection-based safeguards may also introduce bias or over-blocking, as harmful-speech detectors can misclassify dialects, moderation pipelines may yield unequal impacts, and toxicity detection often relies on superficial lexical cues [28, 48, 75]. We therefore focus on settings where uncertainty, framing, and accountability make auditable belief updates useful, while bounding updates under weak or conflicting evidence rather than relying on inferred intent. 

Ethical concerns. The same protective mechanisms can also be misused. Mental privacy may be threatened if EBS infers the stability or susceptibility of a user’s beliefs beyond conventional data privacy [63, 18]. Cognitive capture becomes possible when platforms selectively apply belief-update operations to shape what users treat as salient evidence or credible sources [54, 100]. Epistemic inequality may widen if such influence capabilities are concentrated among a few actors [19, 79]. Finally, attribution remains difficult when belief updates contribute to downstream harm, because system and environment effects are hard to separate [62, 38]. 

# 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we argue that safe LLM-powered multi-agent systems should treat auditable belief up-dates as a core security boundary, rather than relying solely on output-level filtering or behavior-level policy optimization. EB-MAS operationalizes this view by externalizing belief states and update operations, providing explicit interfaces for evidence tracing, update bounding, and population-level propagation analysis. # A Prototype Evaluation of EB-MAS 

This appendix reports a proof-of-concept implementation of the EB-MAS graph firewall. The goal is not to provide a complete empirical benchmark, but to test whether explicit belief-graph features can provide useful runtime safety signals in realistic multimodal and agentic settings. 

A.1 Implementation 

For each candidate response or tool choice, EB-MAS constructs an Explicit Belief Substrate graph with source, evidence, claim, belief, update-proposal, and firewall-decision nodes. The graph firewall uses features for belief strength, uncertainty, vulnerability, evidence support, source reliability, source independence, coordination risk, cross-cluster diversity, evidence quality, and evidence–vulnerability mismatch. These features allow the firewall to evaluate whether a risky belief update is sufficiently supported by reliable and independent evidence. 

A.2 Multimodal Deception 

We evaluate multimodal deceptive requests on MM-DeceptionBench using Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct. Direct model responses are judged unsafe in 67.92% of 1,013 deceptive cases. A keyword/phrase guard reduces this to 38.01%, but falsely blocks 11.00% of benign controls. An LLM belief-operator-only baseline reduces unsafe outputs to 3.65%, but falsely blocks 25.00% of benign controls. A.4 Limitations 

These results should be interpreted as preliminary evidence. On MM-DeceptionBench, sandbagging remains the hardest category, suggesting that future versions need capability-consistency features, historical competence traces, and self-uncertainty drift signals. On ASB, provenance cues are relatively strong, so the learned firewall should be viewed as evidence that graph features can be calibrated, not as proof that EB-MAS solves open-world agent security. More ambiguous provenance, corrupted registries, and dynamic environments such as AgentDojo are important next stress tests.

B.4 Discussion The update transition as an audit object. The supplementary evaluations connect four objects: the current epistemic state, the candidate change, its supporting evidence, and the commit decision. Provenance roots expose shared dependencies across surface sources. The commitment history exposes how several updates combine into a stronger premise. The commit interface then connects 23 these checks to persistent memory, tool use, and peer communication. This state-transition view makes the route from incoming information to downstream action available for inspection. 

Evidence-sensitive revision. Calibration and structural checks address complementary parts of the update process. The multimodal experiment measures the operating trade-off between unsafe acceptance and benign allowance. The four-round experiment measures how source dependence and accumulated commitment affect persistence. Evidence-supported corrections remain part of the benign evaluation, giving a direct measure of whether the system preserves useful revision. These results support evaluating a belief-update interface through both its acceptance decisions and its treatment of evidence over time. 

Runtime grounding and deployment. The runtime’s provenance registry, evidence resolver, and typed checks form the trust anchor for the commit interface. Extraction quality and source truthfulness remain separate evaluation targets within this pipeline. A practical deployment can attach evidence spans, metadata checks, and decision records to each committed change, and route uncertain provenance to verification. Interaction-level influence scores describe cues in the candidate update. Purpose-specific access, short retention, and user contestability provide concrete deployment controls for these records. 

Further evaluation. The next experiments can vary provenance ambiguity, root correlation, and the length of the commitment history, while measuring unsafe commits, benign false blocks, and verification overhead. Forged provenance, corrupted registries, graph-construction attacks, and persistent-memory corruption provide distinct stress tests of the runtime components. Adaptive tool-use environments such as AgentDojo can further examine how the same audit interface operates as evidence, available tools, and interaction partners change during a task. 24
