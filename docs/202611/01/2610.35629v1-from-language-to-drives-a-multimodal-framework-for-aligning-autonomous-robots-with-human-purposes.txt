# From Language to Drives: A Multimodal Framework for Aligning Autonomous Robots with Human Purposes 

Diana Cerdas-Vargas, Alejandro Romero and Richard J. Duro 

Abstract — This work explores a multimodal approach to the alignment of the behavior of open-ended learning autonomous robots with human purposes through the use of large language models (LLMs) as cultural enablers. While autonomous robots can generate their own skills and goals through interaction with the environment, they often lack mechanisms to ensure that these remain relevant and aligned to those of human users. The proposed approach makes use of the general and cultural knowledge contained in LLMs and VLMs to establish a multimodal interactive alignment process in which human purposes, expressed in natural language, are transformed into motivational components of a robot architecture in the form of robot missions and drives that guide the behavior of the robot. The system is structured into three layers for seman-tic alignment, mission generation, and drive modeling. This design enables a bidirectional interaction loop: human inputs define high-level purposes, robots expand them into executable missions making use of multimodal information relayed by LLMs and VLMs, and feedback maintains alignment with human intent. Experiments in simulation and with a physical robot demonstrate that the process produces valid missions and behaviors, supporting effective human–agent alignment in interactive scenarios with autonomous robots. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

A central objective of cognitive and developmental robotics is to construct artificial agents that acquire increas-ingly sophisticated competencies through prolonged interac-tion with their environment. Inspired by human cognitive de-velopment, developmental robotics emphasizes intrinsically motivated exploration, incremental skill acquisition, and the progressive organization of behavior [1], [2]. In parallel, the paradigm of Open-Ended Learning (OEL) formalizes the capacity of autonomous systems to continuously generate new goals and expand their behavioral repertoire beyond predefined tasks or datasets [3]. Together, these perspectives frame autonomy not as the execution of fixed policies, but as an ongoing developmental process. Within cognitive architectures, such developmental open-endedness is commonly achieved through motivation-based control systems [4]–[6]. We refer to these domain-independent motivations as robot purposes .Robot purposes include designer-embedded needs , such as homeostatic requirements and intrinsic motivations that drive exploration and competence acquisition [7], [8], as well as externally derived purposes, or missions , which reflect what human users expect the robot to accomplish during runtime. Both needs and missions are operationalized through drives – quantitative variables encoding the urgency of satisfying a given purpose [9]. By acting to regulate its drives, the robot autonomously discovers goals and learns skills that are grounded in the robot’s sensorimotor system. Alignment is achieved when the resulting behavior fulfills human expectations [10]. However, integrating human purposes into a develop-mental, open-ended system presents a significant challenge. Human intentions are expressed through natural language – ambiguous, context-dependent, and culturally grounded [11] – whereas motivational architectures require structured, formally defined representations [12]. This discrepancy cre-ates a semantic gap that may lead to misaligned internal purposes, undermining trust and safety [10]. Explainability [13] and grounding strategies [14] address aspects of this issue, but they do not fully solve the problem of embedding human purposes into the motivational dynamics that drive developmental learning. Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) provide powerful tools for interpreting linguistic instructions and relating them to perceptual context. Nevertheless, employing such models as end-to-end robot controllers raises concerns regarding explainability, controllability, safety, and the absence of explicit motivational structure [15]. From a developmental perspective, delegating control to monolithic policies risks bypassing the very mechanisms – intrinsic motivation, goal discovery, and incremental learning – that underpin au-tonomous cognitive growth. We therefore adopt a complementary view: LLMs and VLMs are integrated not as end-to-end control policies, but as high-level semantic and cultural knowledge resources that provide structured priors to a motivation-based cognitive architecture. These multimodal models support the inter-pretation and clarification of human purposes, constraining the space of plausible missions. The discovery of specific goals and skills, however, remains grounded in the robot’s developmental learning processes and regulated by explicit drives. In this way, large models inform but do not replace the arXiv:2610.35629v1 [cs.RO] 26 Oct 2026 motivational dynamics that sustain autonomous development. To operationalize this perspective, we propose a multi-modal alignment framework composed of three layers: (1) 

semantic alignment , which interprets and refines human pur-poses through interactive dialogue; (2) mission generation ,which translates aligned purposes into structured, domain-consistent missions; and (3) drive modeling , which trans-forms missions into quantitative drive functions embedded in the motivational system. An explicit alignment need ,implemented as an alignment drive, motivates the robot to seek clarification until a coherent internal representation of the intended purpose is achieved. The contributions of this work are threefold: (i) a cog-nitively and developmentally grounded framework for em-bedding human purposes into a motivational architecture supporting open-ended learning; (ii) the integration of spe-cialized LLM- and VLM-based modules as semantic priors within this architecture; and (iii) experimental validation in simulation and on a physical robotic platform, demon-strating effective human–robot alignment while preserving autonomous developmental processes. By combining multimodal semantic mediation with ex-plicit motivational regulation, this work contributes to the design of developmental autonomous systems that can sus-tain long-term growth while remaining aligned with human intentions. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Instruction Following and Foundation Models in Robotics 

Bridging the semantic gap between high-level human purposes and executable robot behavior has become a central research topic in embodied AI. Recent approaches increas-ingly leverage large-scale foundation models to interpret user instructions and generate robot actions. For instance, SayCan [16] combines a Large Language Model (LLM) with a predefined library of robot skills. The LLM ranks candidate skills according to semantic relevance, while a value function evaluates feasibility in the current state. This modular strat-egy enables execution of long-horizon instructions, provided the required primitive skills are available. However, the system remains constrained by a fixed behavioral repertoire and does not generate new goals or expand its skill space. Other approaches, such as RT-2 [17] move toward tighter integration by training a unified vision-language-action model on Internet-scale and robotic data. The model directly maps multimodal inputs to low-level actions, enabling im-pressive zero-shot generalization. Nevertheless, this end-to-end formulation lacks explicit motivational or goal repre-sentations. The resulting policies are difficult to interpret, regulate, or adapt structurally, raising concerns regarding transparency, controllability, and long-term alignment in open-ended environments. From another point of view, there are systems that ad-dress pragmatic intention understanding. One such case is [11], which classifies user instructions according to their explicitness and leverages contextual cues to infer appro-priate actions. Another is the work presented in [18] that consideres introducing an estimation of uncertainty in user commands and tries to increase certainty through dialogue. While improving robustness in human–robot interaction, such approaches typically operate within predefined task templates and do not support autonomous goal discovery. Finally, shared autonomy methods [19] adapt robot poli-cies to user preferences through interactive feedback. Al-though effective for collaboration, they primarily refine ex-isting behaviors rather than reorganizing the agent’s motiva-tional structure or enabling the emergence of new purposes. 

B. Developmental Robotics and Open-Ended Learning 

Developmental robotics investigates how autonomous agents can acquire progressively complex competencies through processes inspired by human cognitive development [1], [2]. Rather than relying on externally specified task objectives, developmental systems are driven by intrinsic motivations such as curiosity, competence improvement, or learning progress. These mechanisms support exploratory behaviors, goal babbling, and the autonomous construction of hierarchical skill repertoires over extended interaction with the environment. Motivation-based cognitive architectures operationalize these principles through internal drives that regulate behavior and trigger self-generated goals [4]–[6]. Within the broader Open-Ended Learning (OEL) paradigm [3], agents contin-ually expand their behavioral and representational spaces, discovering novel goals and refining predictive models through sensorimotor experience. Crucially, learning remains grounded: goals emerge from embodied interaction rather than from predefined symbolic plans. Despite these advances, most developmental approaches assume that high-level purposes are either hard-coded by the designer or provided in structured form. While they excel at autonomous goal discovery and skill acquisition [4], [5], [12], they do not directly address how abstract, linguistically expressed human purposes can be integrated into the agent’s motivational system without disrupting its developmental dynamics. As a result, the problem of semantically aligning human intentions with intrinsically motivated, open-ended learning processes remains largely unresolved. 

C. Alignment in Embodied Agents 

Beyond robotics, the AI alignment literature highlights the difficulty of ensuring that artificial agents pursue objec-tives consistent with human intentions [10]. Many alignment strategies for large models rely on techniques such as rein-forcement learning from human feedback [20] or instruction tuning [21]. Although effective at shaping output behavior, these methods do not necessarily yield explicit, inspectable representations of purpose within the agent’s control archi-tecture. In embodied, open-ended systems, alignment re-quires mechanisms that integrate human purposes into the internal motivational dynamics governing goal generation and learning. Taken together, existing research either (i) leverages large multimodal models for instruction following, often through fixed skills or end-to-end policies, or (ii) develops develop-mental architectures capable of autonomous goal discovery without addressing semantic alignment with human pur-poses. What remains lacking is an integrated framework in which multimodal models provide semantic and cultural priors while an explicit motivational architecture preserves grounded developmental learning, and alignment. Our work addresses this gap by embedding LLMs and VLMs as knowledge resources within a motivation-based cognitive architecture that integrates information from differ-ent sensing modalities, enabling the transformation of human purposes into aligned drives that guide autonomous open-ended development. III. P ROPOSED APPROACH 

Translating human purposes expressed in natural language into motivational representations such as missions and drives is not a trivial task. Humans rely on cultural and contextual knowledge to interpret intentions and desires, whereas com-putational systems lack this implicit background. To bridge this semantic gap, we use LLMs as cultural knowledge bases related to different sensing modalities that, through an alignment process, are able to clarify human purposes. Fig. 1 illustrates the proposed framework. At the input of the framework there are several elements that allow the user to add multimodal inputs such as an image, language and audio. Next, at its core, the Alignment Engine extracts and interprets the human purpose from multimodal infor-mation (ususally speech and vision). In cases of ambiguity, it engages in iterative dialogue with the user to refine the interpretation, seeking confirmation before proceeding. Once the purpose is clarified, it is passed to the Translation En-gine , which decomposes it into missions and corresponding drive functions. These are explicitly instantiated within the motivational system of the cognitive architecture, guiding the robot’s autonomous behavior. The system is designed for non-expert users. Interaction is therefore concentrated at the specification stage: the user expresses the desired objective in natural language, while the architecture autonomously generates the underlying missions and drives. This allows intuitive purpose specification with-out requiring knowledge of the robot’s motivational structure. The function and configuration of each of the three in-stances of the LLMs used are explained in detail below. Additionally, the prompts used in each of the LLM instances, as well as the source code and the results from the design tests are all available in a public repository 1.

A. Multimodal Input 

This module manages multimodal inputs to improve the user experience by providing a more intuitive interaction interface. Visual data is processed through a Vision Language Model (VLM), specifically we used GPT-4o by OpenAI. B. Alignment Engine 

This first LLM uses an instance of the phi4:14b model by Ollama. It receives the natural language purpose provided by the user and/or a visual description of the environment and reformulates it into a clear, unambiguous specification. When necessary, it engages in a brief interactive dialogue with the user to resolve ambiguities or request missing details. 

1) Prompt: The prompt used to guide this LLM instance was carefully structured to include all relevant contextual and operational information. It consists of six main components: (1) Context, which introduces the LLM’s task and defines key concepts such as needs, missions and drives; (2) Robot description and general description of its environment; (3) Robot perceptions, specifying available sensory inputs along with their value ranges; (4) Input description, explaining the nature and format of the user-provided purpose; (5) Task constraints, which define what the LLM must do and explicitly limit its scope; and (6) Interaction rules, which describe the expected structure and format of the model’s responses, ensuring consistency and compatibility with downstream components. 

2) Input and Output Configuration: The input to this module is a purpose expressed in natural language by the user, along with the image description from the Multimodal Input, when used. The user is required to answer questions and provide additional details to the LLM when needed. To determine when a purpose was fully aligned we looked at how humans clarify intentions during conversation. Often, they ask: “Is this what you meant?” or “Is this what you want?” . Based on this behavior, the user is who decides when the purpose is correctly understood. From a human-robot interaction perspective, this means the human user has full control over when to move to the next stage. The output is a final aligned and refined purpose state-ment, written as a clear paragraph. If, based on the robot’s capabilities and perceptions, the model determines that the purpose is not achievable, it returns a standard message: “It is impossible to achieve your purpose with this robot”. C. Mission Generator 

Once the user purpose has been aligned, a second LLM instance, also using the phi4:14b model, is responsible for decomposing it into a structured set of missions. These missions represent concrete end-states that the robot must achieve in order to fulfill the user’s intention. Each mission also includes an associated weight that indicates its relative importance within the overall task context, reflecting how critical it is for achieving the human’s intended purpose for the robot. D. Drive Generator 

A third LLM instance uses the GPT-4.1 model from OpenAI, and is responsible for modeling a mathematical drive function for each mission. A drive function is a con-tinuous, monotonic mathematical expression that quantifies the urgency to satisfy a given mission based on the robot’s current state. This function must return zero only when the mission has been fulfilled, and increase smoothly as the robot deviates from the desired goal-state. The output of this component integrates directly into the robot’s motivational system. 

1) Prompt: The prompt provided to this LLM was de-signed to ensure internal consistency with the elements of the cognitive architecture, and alignment with the robot’s perceptual space. It includes: (1) Context definitions for missions, drive functions, and drive values; (2) Robot de-scription; (3) Robot perceptions; (4) Format specification of the missions generated in the previous stage; and (5) Structured instructions for generating a drive function for each mission. 

2) Input and Output Configuration: The input to this component consists of two elements. First, the fully refined purpose provided by the Alignment Engine, expressed in text form. Second, the list of missions generated by the previous LLM instance. The output is a list of mathematical drive functions, one for each mission. Each entry follows the structure: Mission: [tag, weight] and Drive: valid Python expression . If the LLM detects that the purpose cannot be fulfilled with the robot’s current capabilities, it returns the message: “It is impossible to achieve your purpose with this robot” .IV. E XPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 

To evaluate the proposed framework, we conducted two experiments. The first experiment evaluates whether the framework can align with and correctly interpret human pur-poses, while the second assesses the generation of missions and drives and their application to robotic tasks. 

A. Dataset 

To evaluate whether the proposed framework can align with and interpret human purposes expressed with different levels of clarity, we defined three robot-executable purposes with increasing complexity. These purposes were: arranging apples by color (simple), classifying cylinders by color into baskets (intermediate), and stacking cylinders at the center of the table from largest to smallest (complex). To simulate how a non-expert user might express these purposes in natural language, we created 15 different am-biguous user inputs for each of the three purposes. Table I presents example ambiguous inputs for each purpose. In addition, three different images illustrating the intended outcome were used for each human purpose. During the experiments, the images associated with each purpose were alternated across trials so that the VLM was exposed to different visual scenarios. The complete dataset, including the 45 expressions and 9 images used in the experiments, is available in a public repository 2.

C. Mission and Drive Generation Test 

This test evaluates the Translation Engine’s capacity to transform refined human purposes into structured motiva-tional representations through two interconnected stages: (1) decomposing purposes into missions, and (2) gener-ating mathematical drive functions for each mission. To isolate these stages from spurious effects introduced by the Alignment Engine, no feedback from the mission or drive evaluation was used to further disambiguate the purpose during these tests. The inputs to the mission generator were the aligned purposes obtained from the experiment described in Section IV-B. The resulting list of missions was then used as input to the drive generation stage, allowing this second step to be evaluated independently from unrelated input errors. To assess the validity of the missions and drives, an expert manually classified each output as valid or invalid. To this end, several factors were considered. First, we assessed whether the complete set of missions would enable the robot to achieve the intended human purpose and whether the associated weight distribution was appropriate. Additionally, each generated drive function was individually examined to ensure both mathematical correctness and proper imple-mentation in Python. It was also verified that each function reached zero, or decreased, only under the appropriate task conditions.
