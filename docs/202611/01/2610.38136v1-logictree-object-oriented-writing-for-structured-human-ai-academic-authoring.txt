## Abstract 

Academic writing is non-linear, iterative, and structure-driven, requiring authors to develop fragmented ideas into arguments with local coherence and global consistency. However, current AI-assisted tools primarily exchange linear prompts and free-form text, forcing authors to reconstruct generated logic and repeatedly verify its alignment with their structural intentions. We propose 

object-oriented writing (OOW), an OOD-inspired representation and interaction paradigm in which persistent writing objects serve as shared, manipulable referents for authors and AI. We instantiate OOW in LogicTree, an Overleaf-integrated proto-type that combines a hierarchical object representation with a structure-grounded writing assistance module for author-initiated generation and issue-grounded revision and a structural logical review module for inspecting local coherence and global consis-tency. A within-subjects study (N=12) provides initial evidence that, in controlled academic writing tasks, LogicTree helps users structure ideas, reduces workload, and improves measured logical quality compared with a conversational baseline.

## 1 Introduction 

Academic writing is a fundamental yet cognitively intensive ac-tivity for researchers across disciplines [13, 33]. Authors do not simply compose text from start to finish. They progressively de-velop fragmented ideas, refine outlines, reorganize arguments, and maintain logical coherence across multiple revisions. This process demands rigorous reasoning, clear argument, and consistent evi-dential support. It remains one of the most time-consuming and error-prone stages of academic work [10, 32]. Large language models (LLMs) have been widely adopted to assist academic writing. They excel at text generation, grammar correction, and stylistic polishing, lowering the barrier to produc-ing fluent text [41, 71]. However, when applied to long-form and logic-intensive academic writing, existing AI-assisted tools exhibit fundamental limitations. They typically treat writing as a linear

process of generating or revising text in a conversational man-ner [39, 68]. As a result, users receive dense blocks of generated text and must work backward to infer, reconstruct, and verify the underlying logic. In contrast, academic writing is inherently non-linear and structure-centered. Authors start from fragmented ideas, iteratively build hierarchical arguments, and continuously adjust structure while filling in content. This natural workflow clashes with the linear output of current AI-assisted tools, causing a grad-ual misalignment between what authors intend to argue and what the generated text actually conveys [6, 46]. This mismatch creates two core difficulties. On one hand, users struggle to understand and verify AI outputs because argument structure and logical de-pendencies are embedded within linear text, requiring manual ex-traction and reconstruction. On the other hand, conveying writ-ing intentions is equally challenging, as authors think in terms of structural elements such as section purposes and argument hierar-chies, yet have to translate these into linear prompts or local edits, constantly checking whether the generated text aligns with their goals. Prior work has pursued two main directions to address these dif-ficulties. One direction uses structured representations such as out-lines [12, 48] or node-graph interfaces [30, 60] to organize content in a non-linear format, aiming to reduce the burden of understand-ing linear text. However, these systems treat structure primarily as a navigation tool, not as a manipulable network of logical relation-ships. Another direction focuses on helping users articulate their intentions more clearly through conversational prompts [40, 61] or goal articulation. Nevertheless, these tools still operate within a linear prompt-to-text framework, offering no support for track-ing or revising structural intentions across the long writing pro-cess. More recently, some work has explored introducing a shared intermediate representation between humans and AI to improve collaboration and alignment [66, 67, 69], suggesting the problem lies in the lack of an operable representation layer. However, exist-ing approaches are designed for one-shot or short-cycle tasks, and do not support the long-term evolution, structural reorganization, and cross-section logic maintenance required in academic writing. To better understand how these limitations manifest in real aca-demic writing workflows, we conducted a formative study with six doctoral students and researchers (Sec. 3). Participants described a tightly coupled, highly iterative workflow of drafting, reviewing, and revising. They reported that current AI tools are useful for grammar correction and wording refinement, but struggle to sup-port the progressive organization of fragmented ideas into struc-tured arguments ( C1 ) and the inspection of logical issues, such as local coherence and global consistency ( C2 ). These findings con-firmed the requirement for a framework that externalizes logical structure, makes it inspectable, and enables controllable AI assis-tance. Based on these insights, we propose object-oriented writing 

(OOW; Fig. 2), an OOD-inspired representation and interaction paradigm in which identifiable academic-writing units persist as shared, manipulable objects for authors and AI. Each writing object maintains explicit state, supports scoped operations, and participates in structural or rhetorical relations with other objects. LogicTree represents sections and paragraphs as container objects that organize scope and context, while leaf-level propositional objects additionally carry argument roles and par-ticipate in logical relations. It couples two complementary compu-tational modules: a structure-grounded writing assistance module that supports author-initiated generation and issue-grounded revision, and a structural logical review module that inspects linear cohesion, argument coherence, and global consistency. • We introduce object-oriented writing , an OOD-inspired represen-tation and interaction paradigm that uses persistent writing ob-jects as shared referents between authors and AI. 

• We present LogicTree, an Overleaf-integrated instantiation of OOW that combines a hierarchical object representation with structure-grounded writing assistance and structural logical re-view modules while preserving user control. 

• We provide initial empirical evidence from a within-subjects user study (N=12) that compares the integrated LogicTree workflow with a conversational baseline in controlled academic writing tasks.

## 2.1 AI-Assisted Academic Writing 

Academic writing demands rigorous logical reasoning, well-structured argument, and clear evidential support, placing high cognitive and professional demands on authors [13, 62]. With the development of large language models (LLMs), AI-assisted tools are increasingly used for academic writing [11]. They excel at text generation, language polishing, and surface-level grammar checking, lowering the barrier to fluent and well-formed text [41, 71]. However, in long-form academic writing, these tools are constrained by known LLM limitations such as hallucination [29] and the “lost in the middle” phenomenon [42]. Moreover, HCI researchers have noted that these systems often overlook the recursive nature of scholarly thought [60], hindering user intent alignment [5, 29]. These limitations motivate further research into AI-assisted academic writing. Current AI-assisted writing tools treat writing as a linear process of text generation and local revision. Systems such as CoAuthor [39] and Wordcraft [68] provide sentence or paragraph level continuation, while commercial tools like Grammarly [15] and Writefull [65] focus on localized grammar and style correction. This sentence-level granularity is insufficient for managing logical dependencies across a manuscript. More recent research attempts to raise the abstraction level by operating on user intentions. For example, Rambler [40] helps users articulate writing goals through conversational prompts, and Sensecape [61] supports hierarchical organization of information needs. However, both still return a single piece of text as output, without modeling cross-section relationships or supporting interactive manipulation of the under-lying logic. While works like Graphologue [30] and Luminate [60] introduce structured abstractions such as node-graph represen-tations, they treat the manuscript as a static collection of blocks rather than a dynamic, editable argument network. More recent AI agents, such as the AI Scientist [43] and Scideator [50], attempt novelty validation and even automated writing, but they require manual verification of intent alignment and offer limited sup-port for deep logical revision [54]. Across these systems, explicit structure, scoped AI operations, and logic-aware review are often supported separately. This leaves limited support for continuously maintaining an author- and AI-shared representation in which argument units, their relations, and the operations applied to them remain jointly inspectable throughout drafting and revision. Our work addresses this gap through object-oriented writing , an OOD-inspired representation and interaction paradigm instan-tiated in LogicTree. LogicTree combines a hierarchical object representation with structure-grounded writing assistance and structural logical review modules, allowing authors to manipulate writing objects and inspect theory-guided feedback within the same iterative workflow. 

## 2.2 Structured Content Manipulation 

Structured content manipulation transforms unstructured infor-mation into modular units with defined attributes, relationships, and operations [18]. This paradigm is established in software engineering through object-oriented programming [7] (e.g., C++ [59], Java [14]) and in data science environments (e.g., Jupyter notebooks [38]), where text, code, and data are organized as composable units. This paradigm has also influenced writing tools. Outlines and block-based editors shift writing from continuous linear progress to organizable units, enabling basic structural operations such as moving and deleting content [12]. Systems like Notion [48] extend this idea by supporting hierarchical organiza-tion and structural visualization, representing early attempts to modularize writing. For academic writing, logical quality can be examined from both micro and macro perspectives: local coherence within specific con-texts and global consistency across the entire document. Linguistic theory provides established frameworks for analyzing these two dimensions. For local coherence at the level of neighboring text segments, cohesion theory [19] explains how local semantic con-tinuity is achieved through fluent expression. At the level of argu-ment structure, rhetorical structure theory (RST) [44] models text as a hierarchy of discourse relations, distinguishing between core claims and supporting information. The Toulmin model of argu-mentation [63] complements RST by defining the components of valid arguments: claim, grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier, and rebuttal. A claim is only valid when supported by grounds and li-censed by a warrant. However, existing applications of these the-ories remain limited. VISAR [70] applies the Toulmin model to an-alyze argumentative essays, but focuses on short texts and one-time segmentation, emphasizing visualization rather than iterative verification. Other systems adopt structurally similar modular ap-proaches without explicit linguistic grounding. Script&Shift [58] and IntentFlow [35] organize content around author intent, while Qin et al. [49] use node-graph interfaces for interactive content or-ganization. These systems demonstrate the practical value of treat-ing writing as structured manipulation of modular units, but they lack the analytical rigor that linguistic frameworks provide for as-sessing logical quality. The core challenge across both categories is that structure is treated as a static organizational scaffold rather than an active, computable property. Addressing this limitation, an object-oriented approach, as explored in LMCanvas [34], offers a promising direction by representing text segments as manipu-lable objects. Our work extends this direction by applying these linguistic theories to a computable review framework that both represents structure and detects logical issues, supporting iterative refinement. 

## 3 Formative Study 

We conducted a formative study (N=6) to investigate the chal-lenges researchers face during current iterative academic writing and to identify design requirements for academic writing support. 

## 3.1 Setup 

Recruitment & Screening. We recruited participants through uni-versity forums and academic social networks. Candidates were re-quired to complete a questionnaire regarding their academic back-ground and experience with AI-assisted writing. We specifically se-lected researchers who had experience using LLMs (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude) for deep, multi-round revisions of academic manuscripts (e.g., conference papers, journal articles, or theses). 

Participants. We recruited 6 participants (P1–P6) from three universities, including 2 females and 4 males, aged 24 to 30 years. 

> 3Zhu et al.

All participants were graduate students or professional researchers in their respective domains, including Computer Science (P1–P2), Mathematics and Statistics (P3), Economics (P4), Computational Media and Arts (P5), and Medicine (P6). Participants reported high levels of expertise in both LLM usage (M = 4.33, SD = 1.03) and it-erative document editing tools like Overleaf or Word (M = 4.5, SD = 0.84) on a 5-point Likert scale. This collective profile ensured that the participants possessed the necessary domain expertise to provide nuanced insights into the complex workflows of academic writing. 

Interview. We conducted semi-structured interviews and case demonstrations lasting about 45 minutes. The study received approval from the responsible institutional ethics committee be-fore data collection. All participants provided written informed consent for participation, audio recording, transcription, and the research use of de-identified study data. Participants described their academic writing workflow through concrete real-world cases, followed by questions on their use of AI-assisted tools and challenges encountered. Each participant received approximately US$7 in compensation. 

Analysis. Two researchers independently performed open cod-ing on the interview transcripts and participant case descriptions. They then compared their codes, discussed disagreements, and iteratively grouped recurring patterns into higher-level themes through inductive thematic analysis. Theme prevalence is reported below as the number of participants who endorsed a theme, rather than the frequency of coded excerpts. The resulting themes iden-tify challenges in aligning researchers’ high-level intentions with academic writing and directly inform the design requirements. 

## 3.2 Findings 

Participants described academic writing as an iterative process in-volving three tightly coupled activities: drafting, reviewing, and revising. They recognized that AI-assisted tools perform well in grammar correction, wording refinement, and stylistic polishing, but their support remains limited for broader tasks such as itera-tive organization of fragmented ideas, logic-intensive reviewing, and maintenance of cross-section consistency. Based on the inter-view results, we summarize the practices of participants at each stage and the key challenges they encountered as follows. 

C1. Mismatch between non-linear, iterative, structured drafting and linearized, text-centered AI support. During early drafting, most participants (5/6) started with fragmented ideas like rough outlines rather than sequential text, then iter-atively organized and refined them into coherent content. P3 stated, “ I usually start by listing some rough main points, which are often still fragmented at first. I then develop the content while continuously adjusting the organization and logic (of those initial points). ”All participants (6/6) described using LLMs to explore possible logic and organization from fragmented ideas and expand partial content. However, such support was delivered mainly as fully ver-balized text rather than explicit logical structure. Thus, participants had to reread lengthy outputs to reconstruct and verify the gener-ated logic, judge whether it offered a useful direction for further development, or check whether it followed their intended writing reasoning. P2 noted, “I often spend more time reading and under-standing what the AI generated than actually developing my own ideas.” Participants also noted that LLMs often produced logical jumps or hallucinations when asked to generate long, logically connected text from rough ideas. Consequently, most participants (4/6) rarely relied on AI-assisted tools for organizing complete writ-ing logic. As P3 summarized: “My writing process is about building a structure piece by piece, but the AI gives me a block of text that I then have to break down. It feels like we are working in opposite directions.” 

C2: Limited support for logic-intensive reviewing and revising across local coherence and global consistency. 

After completing an initial draft, participants entered a tightly coupled review-revision process, needing to assess whether ar-guments were clearly conveyed, whether paragraphs supported the narrative, and whether revisions required updates elsewhere. Reviewing was often conducted through repeated self-reading (5/6) and feedback from collaborators (6/6). As P3 noted, “I usually pay particular attention to whether the overall writing framework really communicates what I want to say, and whether the sentences in each paragraph actually convey that idea.” Revising required authors not only to refine or restructure the problematic part of the manuscript, but also to reread large portions of the manuscript, or even the entire paper, to locate and update related statements across the document. Participants reported that current AI-assisted tools could sup-port this process mainly by inspecting local expressions (5/6), such as suggesting rewording, and by rewriting problematic passages (4/6) at the sentence or paragraph level. P5 added, “I sometimes ask it to rewrite a paragraph that feels awkward, and it often re-turns something that reads more smoothly.” However, such support was limited for deeper logic-intensive review and revision. Some participants (3/6) noted that AI revisions could appear fluent with-out guaranteeing reasoning correctness. As P4 explained, “AI can rewrite a paragraph to make it sound perfect, but it might change the meaning or introduce claims that I never intended. I then have to go back and check everything carefully.” Participants also observed that when revisions involved logical inconsistencies across multi-ple paragraphs, LLMs often performed poorly on lengthy content (5/6), could be distracted by irrelevant context (4/6), failed to iden-tify the exact issue source (3/6), and could introduce new errors (5/6). This made it difficult to reliably review and revise both local coherence and global consistency with current AI support. 

## 3.3 Design Requirements 

Together, C1 and C2 reveal a representational gap in current AI-assisted academic writing. Authors develop and evaluate an evolv-ing logical structure, whereas interaction with AI is mediated pri-marily through linear prompts and generated prose. We therefore derive one foundational requirement for a shared representation and four operational requirements for drafting, reviewing, and re-vising through that representation. 

DR1. Provide a shared, persistent, and structured interme-diate representation for author-AI interaction in academic writing. The tool should externalize the evolving structure and logic of a manuscript in a form that authors and AI can jointly

reference throughout drafting, reviewing, and revision ( C1 , C2 ). This representation should persist across interactions so that au-thors need not repeatedly translate structural intentions into linear prompts or reconstruct the structure of AI output from prose. 

DR2. Provide structured and progressive support for draft-ing from fragmented ideas. The tool should accommodate au-thors’ non-linear, iterative, and outline-centric drafting practices (C1 ). It should support progressively externalizing, organizing, ex-panding, and revising fragmented ideas before and alongside their realization as full prose. 

DR3. Make local coherence and global consistency ex-plicit, inspectable, and diagnosable. The tool should help authors inspect whether arguments are clearly conveyed, whether local content supports the larger narrative, and whether a revision requires updates elsewhere in the manuscript ( C2 ). It should con-nect potential issues to the affected manuscript parts and relations so that authors can identify where an issue occurs, what type it is, and which parts are implicated. 

DR4. Provide explainable and actionable support for re-solving issues in local coherence and global consistency. Be-cause fluent AI output may still alter intended reasoning, the tool should explain why an issue was raised and support informed re-vision rather than presenting a correction as authoritative ( C2 ). 

DR5. Provide controllable, structure-grounded AI as-sistance throughout the writing workflow. The tool should ground assistance in author-recognizable writing structures, make the intended scope and proposed changes visible, and preserve au-thors’ final judgment over whether those changes are incorporated (C1 , C2 ). 

## 4 Object-Oriented Writing 

Motivated by the representational gap identified in our formative study and formalized in DR1 , we propose object-oriented writing 

(OOW), an OOD-inspired representation and interaction para-digm for AI-assisted academic writing (Fig. 2). OOW introduces a shared intermediate layer through which authors and AI can refer to and operate on the evolving structure of a manuscript. Rather than requiring authors to communicate structural intentions only through linear prompts or infer the organization of AI output from prose, OOW represents a draft as a collection of identifiable and manipulable writing objects. OOW draws inspiration from object-oriented design (OOD), which represents a complex system as identifiable objects with their own state and available operations that interact through explicit relations [7]. Following this representational logic, a writ-ing object is a persistent and addressable unit of an academic manuscript. Its state records what the object currently expresses and the communicative intention or logical annotations associ-ated with it. Its operations are scoped actions through which the object can be created, developed, revised, moved, inspected, or reviewed. Its relations connect it to other writing objects through structural, ordering, rhetorical, or argumentative dependencies. Its persistent identity allows the same unit to remain a stable referent as its state and relations change across interactions. These properties make writing objects shared referents for author-AI interaction. Authors can externalize and refine partial intentions by manipulating objects and their relations, while AI assistance can ground generation, review, and revision in the same identifiable units. Writing therefore becomes an iterative process of constructing, inspecting, and manipulating an evolving collection of related objects rather than communicating with AI solely through exchanges of linear text. Here, “object-oriented” denotes an object-centered represen-tational commitment inspired by OOD, not the adoption of programming-language mechanisms such as classes, inheritance, or polymorphism. OOW does not prescribe a particular hierarchy, object granularity, relation vocabulary, interface, or AI implemen-tation. Different systems may instantiate the paradigm through different kinds of writing objects and operations, and AI agents are not required for an implementation to follow OOW. In the next section, we present LogicTree as one concrete instantiation that uses a hierarchical object tree to support academic drafting and logical review. 

## 5 LogicTree 

We instantiate OOW in LogicTree, an Overleaf-integrated aca-demic writing prototype that operationalizes writing objects as a shared intermediate representation between authors and AI. This section first provides an overview of the system, then describes its interaction views and computational modules, and concludes with a usage scenario. 

## 5.1 Overview 

LogicTree coordinates the conventional Overleaf document editor (Fig. 3 1) with two interaction views for object-oriented authoring: an object tree (Fig. 3 2) and an error list (Fig. 3 3). The object tree is the primary workspace for constructing and manipulating the intermediate writing representation, while the error list presents potential logical issues and links them to the implicated objects and relations. Together, these interfaces let authors move iteratively be-tween developing a manuscript structure and inspecting its logical quality. LogicTree realizes each writing object as a node with a per-sistent identifier ( Object Tree in Sec. 5.2.1), textual content, an ordered parent-child position, and a visible change state. A node may also store an argumentative role ( Argument Coherence in Sec. 5.3.2), object-level conversation history, explicit relations to other nodes, and annotations produced by logical review. Higher-level container objects organize scopes such as sections and paragraphs (Fig. 3 4), while leaf-level propositional objects ex-press finer-grained argument units such as Claims, Evidence, and Analysis (Fig. 3 5). This distinction is a design choice of LogicTree rather than a requirement of the broader OOW paradigm. The object tree and error list form the system’s interaction views (Sec. 5.2). A structure-grounded writing assistance module and a structural logical review module operate over the same object rep-resentation (Sec. 5.3). Fig. 4 summarizes the inputs, computations, outputs, and author decisions that connect these views and mod-ules. This shared representation supports persistent author-AI ref-erence ( DR1 ), progressive drafting ( DR2 ), inspectable and explain-able review ( DR3 , DR4 ), and scoped AI assistance ( DR5 ).

## 5.2 Interaction Views 

LogicTree provides two linked interaction views over the shared writing-object representation. The object tree supports structured authoring and inspection of object-level changes, while the error list organizes logic-review results and provides entry points for revision. Together, the two views connect manuscript construc-tion, review, and revision without requiring authors to translate between separate representations. 

5.2.1 Object Tree. Authors begin with a root object that records the manuscript’s main idea (Fig. 3 4). They add container objects for

manuscript units such as sections and paragraphs and place propo-sitional objects beneath them to express directly writable Claims, Evidence, and Analysis. The resulting hierarchy lets authors move from an initial idea to progressively more specific content while keeping unfinished intentions visible ( DR2 ). Controls attached to each object support direct content editing, child creation, subtree deletion, writing assistance, and expansion or collapse of the vis-ible hierarchy (Fig. 3 6). Direct editing changes the selected ob-ject’s content, whereas author-initiated generation proposes new descendants beneath it. Each object retains its identifier after con-tent edits, which allows later assistance and review results to refer to the same writing unit ( DR1 ). LogicTree can project the current hierarchy into the Overleaf source editor as nested outline comments (Fig. 3 1). The indentation of these comments mirrors the parent and child structure so that authors can inspect the structured draft beside the object tree. Au-thors continue to edit writing objects through LogicTree because arbitrary prose edits in Overleaf are not automatically parsed back into the tree. The object tree is also the entry point for the author-initiated generation workflow through its object controls (Fig. 3 6). Select-ing an object determines the insertion point and opens the conver-sation retained for that object (Fig. 3 7). The author states how the selected object should be developed, while the current tree supplies the surrounding manuscript context. LogicTree returns the gener-ated descendants as a blue proposed subtree beneath the selected object (Fig. 3 8). The proposal remains separate from accepted ob-jects until the author accepts or rejects it through the proposal controls (Fig. 3 9). Accepted objects enter the established hierar-chy and trigger structural logical review, while rejected objects do not affect the tree or later review results ( DR5 ). Logic-review annotations are visualized on the same tree. Lin-ear cohesion warnings and errors appear as yellow or red connec-tions between the neighboring objects involved in the transition (Fig. 3 10). Argument coherence issues appear as severity markers and argumentative role badges on the implicated propositional ob-jects (Fig. 3 11). Selecting a marker highlights the objects connected by the relevant argument relations and opens the corresponding explanation in the error list (Fig. 3 12). Global consistency issues use the error list to link an affected Claim to the other Claim with which it may conflict. 

5.2.2 Error List. The error list organizes potential issues in linear cohesion, argument coherence, and global consistency (Fig. 3 3). Each entry shows its issue category and severity in the collapsed state. Expanding an entry reveals the implicated object or pair and a concise explanation of the detected transition, support gap, or inconsistency (Fig. 3 12). The related-object locator expands the paths to the implicated objects in the object tree so that the author can inspect the issue in its structural context (Fig. 3 13). Conversely, selecting a tree marker opens the corresponding error entry. This bidirectional link connects each diagnosis to the structural context from which it was produced ( DR3 , DR4 ). Authors can respond to an issue by revising the implicated objects manually or selecting the issue-grounded revision control (Fig. 3 14). For supported linear cohesion and argument coher-ence issues, the system passes the implicated objects, their local structural context, and the diagnostic explanation to the structure-grounded writing assistance module. The control returns a con-textualized revision and maps it to additions, modifications, or removals shown as pending change states in the object tree. The author can accept, ignore, or further revise the proposed change while inspecting it together with the original issue. 

## 5.3 Computational Modules 

As shown in Fig. 4, LogicTree organizes its operation into three workflows that reuse two computational modules. Each module combines an LLM-based agent with deterministic procedures for context construction, output validation, and object-state man-agement. The structure-grounded writing agent and logic-review agent perform model-based generation or diagnosis, while their enclosing modules coordinate the complete system workflows. The structure-grounded writing assistance module supports both author-initiated generation and issue-grounded revision. Author-initiated generation transforms an author instruction and the selected object context into a proposed subtree. Issue-grounded revision transforms a selected review issue and its implicated ob-jects into proposed editing actions. The structural logical review module forms the third workflow and analyzes accepted or di-rectly updated objects for local coherence and global consistency. Each workflow follows the same four stages of input, computa-tion, output, and author decision. The modules return object-level proposals and annotations that remain inspectable before authors accept changes or initiate further revision. 

5.3.1 Structure-Grounded Writing Assistance. LogicTree supports two structure-grounded writing workflows, namely author-initiated generation and issue-grounded revision. 

Author-initiated generation. Author-initiated generation begins when an author wants to expand a selected object with additional manuscript content. The author invokes writing assistance on that object and describes the content to develop. LogicTree uses the se-lected object as the insertion scope and returns a proposed subtree beneath it. The selected object identifier fixes the insertion position. Logic-Tree combines the author’s instruction and the conversation stored for that object with the identifiers, textual content, and hierarchi-cal relations between parent and child objects in the current tree. This input lets the structure-grounded writing agent interpret the local request in relation to the surrounding manuscript structure. The generation prompt asks the writing agent to begin with im-mediate children of the selected object, develop them into progres-sively more specific descendants, and end in propositional objects containing directly writable key sentences. It constrains the gen-erated subtree to at most three levels beneath the selected object with multiple siblings at each developed level. The writing agent returns a JSON object containing a short re-sponse and a recursively nested list of proposed child objects. Log-icTree parses this output and accepts only recursively nested child records with nonempty textual content. The system then assigns each proposed object a persistent identifier and an added change state. A separate classification call assigns provisional argumenta-tive roles to generated leaf objects by considering their common 

> 7Zhu et al.

parent and sibling content together. The resulting subtree appears in blue beneath the selected object so that the author can accept or reject it before it becomes part of the established tree. Acceptance changes the proposed objects to the normal state and triggers relation construction and structural logical review. This sequence keeps the selected object as the scope defined by the author while using the remaining tree only as context ( DR5 ). It also prevents rejected generations from affecting later review results. 

Issue-grounded revision. Assistance initiated from an issue be-gins with a specific warning or error rather than an open author instruction. For supported linear cohesion and argument coher-ence issues, LogicTree provides the structure-grounded writing as-sistance module with the implicated object identifiers and content, their parent and sibling context, the severity, and the diagnostic ex-planation. Within the module, the writing agent first produces an actionable repair suggestion and then converts it into one or more structured add, modify, or remove actions. For example, when a Claim has Evidence but no Analysis, the writing agent can pro-pose an Analysis object that explains why the Evidence supports the Claim. LogicTree maps every action to explicit object identi-fiers and displays its change state in the object tree for author in-spection before incorporation. 

5.3.2 Structural Logical Review. Drawing on theories of textual co-herence and discourse relations [22, 44], the structural logical re-view module examines accepted generated objects and directly up-dated leaf objects through local coherence and global consistency (Fig. 5). The updated objects form the affected set that determines which checks are recomputed. Local review inspects transitions and argument structures in the affected structural context. Global review compares each affected Claim with Claim objects elsewhere in the current tree. Every review routine returns object identifiers, a severity label, and a concise explanation. LogicTree validates the returned identifiers and the eligible comparison scope before stor-ing warning or error annotations on the corresponding objects or relations. An object or relation without such an annotation re-ceived no reported issue under the applied check. 

Linear Cohesion. Following cohesion theory [19], linear co-hesion evaluates whether adjacent manuscript units form acomprehensible semantic progression. LogicTree constructs can-didate pairs from adjacent sibling subtrees and from the transition between adjacent parent subtrees within the modified parent. The logic review prompt receives the identifiers, content, and descen-dant hierarchy of this scope and asks the logic-review agent to return only insufficiently coherent candidate pairs. A transition is a warning when the two units retain a plausible topical or argumentative connection but that connection is implicit, abrupt, or insufficiently developed. A transition is an error when the second unit does not provide a plausible continuation of the first or introduces content unrelated to the surrounding topic. For example, moving from evidence about demand for programming skills to an isolated statement about remote work productivity is classified as an error because the latter does not continue the former topic. For every returned pair, LogicTree verifies that both identifiers exist, that the pair is adjacent, and that at least one side contains an affected object. A validated warning or error is rendered as a colored connection between the two objects, and the same issue appears in the error list with its explanation. 

Argument Coherence. LogicTree operationalizes argument co-herence through discourse relations [44] and six roles adapted from the Toulmin model [63]. A Claim is the debatable assertion that the author asks the reader to accept. Evidence consists of independently verifiable facts, observations, data, or examples. Analysis states the reasoning that explains how or why Evidence supports a Claim. Backing supplies an authoritative source, theory, or principle for that reasoning. A Limitation restricts the scope or strength of a Claim, while a Rebuttal identifies an exception or competing position. The module groups leaf objects by their direct parent and asks the logic-review agent to assign one role to each object using the parent topic and sibling content as context. The agent then pro-poses semantically justified relations that conform to the five role relation patterns in Tab. 1. Evidence supports a Claim, Analysis li-censes a Claim, and Backing supports Analysis. Rebuttal and Limi-tation objects connect to a Claim through the stored attack relation, where Rebuttal opposes the Claim and Limitation constrains its ap-plicability. The first three relation types are constructed among leaf siblings under the same parent. Rebuttal and Limitation relations may connect to an affected Claim within the same section. Logic-Tree accepts a proposed relation only when its endpoints are leaf objects, its direction matches their roles, and its structural scope satisfies these constraints. The system applies deterministic criteria after role and relation construction. It reports an error when a Claim has no incoming support from Evidence, when a Claim has no incoming license from Analysis, or when a propositional object other than a Claim has no relation to another object. It reports a warning when an Analysis has no incoming support from Backing or when a lin-guistically strong Claim has no Limitation. Strong Claims are iden-tified through categorical or necessity expressions such as must ,

all , necessary , and essential . For example, the Claim “Programming literacy is essential for all students” receives an error if it has Evi-dence but no Analysis and receives a warning if it has no Limita-tion. Objects with detected issues receive severity markers and role badges in the object tree, while selecting a marker highlights the related objects and opens the explanation in the error list. These checks operationalize the presence and arrangement of argumen-tative support rather than the truth of the Claim.

Global Consistency. The Toulmin model treats a Claim as the proposition advanced for acceptance and relates other argumen-tative roles to that proposition [63]. Global consistency therefore evaluates whether an affected Claim can coexist with Claims elsewhere in the manuscript. LogicTree limits this comparison to Claims because they express the manuscript’s substantive posi-tions, while the relations of Evidence, Analysis, Backing, Rebuttal, and Limitation to Claims are assessed during argument coherence review. LogicTree forms the comparison pool from all leaf ob-jects currently classified as Claims and pairs each affected Claim with every other Claim in that pool. LogicTree represents these candidate Claim pairs in a semantic relationship graph and links each affected Claim to that graph for evaluation. The logic-review agent receives the identifiers and content of these Claims and returns only conflicting or potentially conflicting pairs. A direct contradiction is recorded as an error. A likely tension, scope mismatch, or overgeneralization is recorded as a warning. For example, an unrestricted Claim that programming is necessary for every student may receive a warning when another Claim states that advanced programming is unnecessary for students in some disciplines. LogicTree verifies that the first identifier belongs to the affected Claim set, that both endpoints are Claim leaves, and that the two identifiers differ before storing the issue and explanation. The graph organizes candidate comparisons and does not impose a formal logical relation between the Claims. The module uses the logic-review agent for diagnosis rather than formal logical inference. 

## 5.4 Usage Scenario 

Frank, a doctoral student in education, opens LogicTree with the main idea that programming literacy is increasingly important in modern education. He edits the root object and adds section-level container objects to establish an initial manuscript structure (Fig. 6 A and B). Because his Introduction remains incomplete, he invokes writing assistance on that object and describes the ideas he wants to develop. LogicTree presents proposed paragraph and proposi-tional objects beneath the selected object, and Frank inspects them before acceptance (Fig. 6 C). After the accepted objects are reviewed, Frank notices a red linear cohesion edge connecting a statement about programming skills in the job market with an unrelated statement about remote-work productivity (Fig. 6 D). He selects the edge to read the expla-nation in the error list and requests repair assistance. The structure-grounded writing assistance module proposes removing the off-topic object, which Frank reviews before accepting. Frank then inspects an argument marker showing that a Claim about the broader value of programming literacy has Evidence but lacks Analysis (Fig. 6 E). The linked error entry explains the missing connection, and the structure-grounded writing assis-tance module proposes an Analysis object that Frank accepts after inspection. Later, Frank strengthens a Claim about students outside techni-cal fields. The consistency review flags tensions between this re-vision and Claims elsewhere in the manuscript (Fig. 6 F). Frank examines the implicated objects and revises the statement him-self. Through repeated cycles of construction, inspection, and re-vision, he uses the same object representation to coordinate his in-tentions with AI assistance while retaining final control over the manuscript (Fig. 6 G).

## 6.1 Experiment Settings 

We conducted a within-subjects study with 12 participants to com-pare the complete LogicTree workflow against a conversational baseline. Participants completed two academic writing tasks, af-ter which we analyzed their performance and subjective feedback. Our use of OpenAI Codex for prototype implementation and post-study data aggregation and visualization is detailed in the Genera-tive AI Use Disclosure at the end of the paper. 

Participants. We recruited 12 participants (P1–P12; 8 males, 4 females) through social media and word‑of‑mouth. They were all graduate students and researchers with diverse backgrounds, such as computer science, medicine, optical engineering, and marketing. According to their self‑ratings on a 7‑point Likert scale (1: lowest extent, 7: greatest extent), participants were experienced with aca-demic paper writing (M = 5.42, SD = 1.16) and had prior experience using LLMs to assist with academic writing (M = 5.58, SD = 1.00). All participants had authored at least one peer‑reviewed English paper and were familiar with Overleaf, ensuring they met the pro-ficiency requirements for the academic writing tasks. 

Baseline and Apparatus. We adopted a within-subjects de-sign with two conditions. In the LogicTree condition, participants used our prototype integrated into Overleaf. In the Baseline con-dition, participants used a system functionally equivalent to con-versational AI tools like ChatGPT for academic writing. The Base-line and LogicTree conditions used the same foundation model (i.e., qwen3-next-80b-a3b-instruct), and the baseline preserved conver-sation context and could access the same task materials. The com-parison therefore evaluates LogicTree as an integrated workflow rather than isolating the causal contribution of each feature. 

Tasks. To reduce the influence of domain‑specific knowledge and ensure controllable experimental conditions, the evaluation comprised two academic writing tasks. For each task, partici-pants received an unordered information package containing ten snippets drawn from top‑tier academic journals, government documents and authoritative media. Task A focused on the relia-bility and interpretability of medical AI, and Task B on the ethical dilemmas of autonomous driving. Participants were instructed to write an academic discussion section of 500–800 words, use only the provided snippets for empirical content, and aim for the highest possible logical quality. They were not required to use every snippet.

The evaluation received approval from the responsible institu-tional ethics committee before data collection. We employed a counterbalanced within-subjects design, ran-domly assigning participants to one of four sequences balancing tool (LogicTree or Baseline) and task (A or B) order. Prior to the experimental procedures, participants took part in a 10‑minute training session that introduced the two systems and included a brief practice task. 6.3.1 Accuracy of the logical review. We evaluated the reliability of the structural logical review module by first establishing a con-sensus human reference and then comparing the module’s outputs with that reference. The results are shown in Tab. 2. The annotation set covered the 12 texts produced in the Logic-Tree condition and used a task-specific unit for each review compo-nent. It comprised 239 propositional objects for role classification, 205 candidate object pairs for relation construction, 228 boundaries between adjacent objects or subtrees for linear cohesion, and 170 Claim pairs for global consistency. Two researchers independently labeled these units without inspecting the system outputs. They then resolved disagreements through discussion to form the con-sensus reference used for system evaluation. Based on their inde-pendent annotations before disagreement resolution, we assessed inter-annotator agreement using three complementary measures: percent agreement, Cohen’s 𝜅 , and Gwet’s AC1. Percent agreement measures the proportion of instances for which the two annotators assigned the same label. Cohen’s 𝜅 further accounts for agreement that could occur by chance given the annotators’ label distribu-tions. However, 𝜅 can be sensitive to highly imbalanced label dis-tributions, as in the linear cohesion and global consistency annota-tion sets where “No Issue” was the majority label. We therefore ad-ditionally report Gwet’s AC1, a chance-corrected agreement mea-sure that is more robust to such label imbalance. For both Cohen’s 𝜅 

and Gwet’s AC1, values closer to 1 indicate stronger agreement. As shown in Tab. 2, percent agreement ranged from 85.37% to 97.06%, and AC1 ranged from 0.836 to 0.969 across the four tasks. Linear cohesion had a lower Cohen’s 𝜅 of 0.530 but retained 88.16% agree-ment and an AC1 of 0.865, a pattern consistent with the prevalence of “No Issue” labels. We ran the frozen structural logical review module three times with the same model, prompts, and parameters and evaluated every prediction against the consensus human annotation using anonymous object or pair identifiers. We evaluated performance using accuracy and F1 score and report the mean and standard deviation across runs. Accuracy measures the proportion of pre-dictions that exactly match the consensus human reference. F1 jointly considers precision and recall and is therefore more in-formative when the labels are imbalanced. For role classification, which contains multiple argumentative roles, we report macro-F1 by computing the F1 score for each role separately and then aver-aging across roles, so that each role contributes equally regardless of its frequency. For relation construction, linear cohesion, and global consistency, we report F1 for detecting the presence of a re-lation or issue, treating “No Relation” or “No Issue” as the negative class. Pooling the 603 relation and issue decisions from relation construction, linear cohesion, and global consistency yielded an accuracy of 85.07% with an SD of 0.50 percentage points (Tab. 2). Role classification reached 92.05% accuracy and 85.90% macro-F1, showing that the module usually recovered the argumentative role assigned in the human reference. Exact relation construction was the most demanding component and reached 77.56% accuracy because both relation type and direction had to match. Global consistency reached 93.53% accuracy but 56.78% issue F1 because only 11 of 170 Claim pairs contained a Warning or Error. This result indicates that the module usually recognized “No Issue” pairs but had greater difficulty identifying the less frequent con-sistency issues. Overall, the remaining errors were concentrated in exact relation matching and rare consistency issues rather than distributed uniformly across the review pipeline.

## 7 Discussion 

We reflect on the theoretical foundations of the object-oriented writ-ing paradigm, its implications for human-AI interaction with in-spectable logic, and the limitations that point to future work. 

## 7.1 Object-Oriented Writing as a Design Paradigm 

A central tension in the object-oriented writing paradigm lies be-tween providing structural scaffolding for logical reasoning and preserving flexibility. The paradigm draws inspiration from object-oriented design, which represents systems as identifiable objects with state, available operations, and explicit relations [7]. OOW borrows this object-centered representational logic without requir-ing programming-language mechanisms such as classes or inheri-tance. OOW adopts an external representation approach [37, 71], making argument structure manipulable rather than merely read-able [53]. In LogicTree, the outline hierarchy serves as what dis-tributed cognition theorists term a cognitive artifact [26, 28, 51], re-shaping how writers reason about logical structure. While partici-pants valued LogicTree’s structured workflow, they noted the hier-archical form could feel rigid and force early commitment to orga-nizational decisions [55]. This limitation concerns LogicTree’s hier-archical instantiation rather than a requirement that every OOW system use a tree. More broadly, it reflects the fundamental rep-resentational tradeoff between providing structure that scaffolds reasoning and preserving flexibility for exploratory thinking [36]. The distinction between container and propositional objects partly relieves this tension by letting authors work at multiple granular-ities, but it does not remove the need to move between structured and linear representations. Beyond academic writing, this object-oriented framing suggests a possible design direction for human-AI interaction [27, 57]. For HCI, the approach contributes to feedforward mechanisms [56] by making system reasoning inspectable and modifiable before output generation, directly addressing the gulfs of execution and evalua-tion [47]. In data analysis, users could manipulate analytical steps as objects [23, 31]. In creative design, decisions could be encap-sulated as objects with traceable dependencies [16]. In collabora-tive work, shared artifacts could be decomposed into modular ob-jects with clear ownership [24]. Across these domains, the com-mon thread is shifting from interacting with opaque outputs to manipulating the underlying logic that generates them. This vi-sion aligns with emerging HCI research on transparent AI [1, 17] and suggests a future where human-AI collaboration centers on shared manipulable representations of reasoning, making coordi-nation more controllable and aligned with human intent [25]. At the same time, the paradigm assumes that useful aspects of writing can be represented as identifiable objects, an abstraction that may feel unnatural for exploratory or narrative forms like creative non-fiction. LogicTree’s logical review framework, built on the Toulmin model and cohesion theory, is tailored to argumentative writing and may not transfer directly to genres prioritizing temporal flow or aesthetic expression. ## 7.2 HAI with Inspectable Logic 

Making logical criteria machine-readable introduces a new form of human-AI interaction where formal criteria become inspectable and negotiable rather than binding. The structural logical review framework in LogicTree treats logical quality as a set of LLM-checkable criteria rather than a formally decidable property, drawing inspiration from software engineering formal methods [3, 9] and a series of linguistic theories [19, 22, 44, 63]. By decom-posing logical quality into linear cohesion, argument coherence, and global consistency, the system transforms abstract norms into machine readable criteria, enabling automated detection that helps users locate issues without manually reading entire drafts, while the resulting flags remain suggestions rather than guarantees of logical correctness. However, this computational approach has limitations. P2 and P12 encountered false positive warnings, re-flecting the gap between formal logic and natural argumentation, which echoes critiques of early automated reasoning systems [64]. More fundamentally, there is an inherent tension between making logic computable and respecting the interpretive flexibility of aca-demic writing, where disciplinary conventions and authorial voice resist full formalization [4, 45]. P6 also noted that the system’s strict detection standard flagged issues that experienced writers considered acceptable, adding overhead to the editing process. For human-AI interaction, this points toward designing sys-tems where explicit review criteria serve as a shared referent rather than a binding constraint, enabling contestable AI [2]. Concrete directions include adaptive logic models that learn from user corrections to reduce false positives, and adjustable detection thresholds users can calibrate to disciplinary context. Interfaces can also support conversational contestation where users explain why a flagged passage is acceptable, refining the system’s un-derstanding of domain norms. These practices shift AI from an authority to a negotiable partner while respecting the interpretive flexibility of academic writing. 

## 7.3 Limitations and Future Work 

Despite the promising results, our approach has several limitations that point to future research directions. 

System scalability and interaction fluidity. The object tree view, while effective for structuring logical arguments, faces scala-bility challenges as manuscripts grow in depth and breadth, poten-tially overwhelming screen real estate and reducing navigability. Additionally, LogicTree currently processes agent requests sequen-tially, forcing users to wait for one request to finish before editing another independent subtree. Future work could address scalabil-ity through collapsible cluster nodes that abstract away subtrees, and improve fluidity with parallelized AI processing for indepen-dent branches as well as asynchronous background review. 

Experimental limitations. Our within-subjects study with 12 participants provides initial evidence but limits generalizability. The 20-minute tasks may not fully capture authentic writing behaviors that typically unfold over days or weeks, and auto-mated evaluation by LLM judges, while consistent, reflects score stability rather than independently established rating validity. Moreover, comparing the complete LogicTree workflow with a conversational baseline does not isolate the independent effects of the object representation, the writing assistance module, and the structural logical review module; the feature ratings describe perceived usefulness and do not replace an ablation. Future work should conduct longitudinal field studies with diverse participants and compare structured baselines and component ablations. 

## 8 Conclusion 

We introduced object-oriented writing , an OOD-inspired represen-tation and interaction paradigm in which persistent writing ob-jects serve as shared, manipulable referents for authors and AI. We instantiated this paradigm in LogicTree, an Overleaf-integrated prototype that distinguishes container and propositional objects and operates structure-grounded writing assistance and struc-tural logical review modules over the same object representation. Together, these modules support an iterative authoring loop in which users can inspect the scope of proposed edits, review potential issues in local coherence and global consistency, and retain final control over changes. A within-subjects study (N=12) provides initial evidence that the integrated LogicTree workflow
