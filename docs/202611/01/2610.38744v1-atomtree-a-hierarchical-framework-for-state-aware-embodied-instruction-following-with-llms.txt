# AtomTree: A Hierarchical Framework for State-Aware Embodied Instruction Following with LLMs

Abstract — Embodied instruction following in interactive en-vironments requires agents to interpret natural language com-mands and complete long-horizon tasks. Existing planners either rely on large labeled trajectories, or use few-shot and zero-shot LLM prompting that produces a whole plan in a single serial reasoning process. These approaches depend on linguistic common sense, commit to a plan from the initial instruction, and only update after failures, which weakens robustness and state awareness. We present AtomTree, a hier-archical framework for state-aware task planning with LLMs. AtomTree incrementally builds a tree of subgoals and actions, where the LLM decides at each step, from the current symbolic state, whether to further decompose or to output a grounded low-level action. A lightweight memory module summarizes past outcomes for reuse in later decisions. When a low-level action stalls, AtomTree triggers hierarchical backtracking replanning to return to the parent node and regenerate the subtree plan from the latest state, preventing continued drift along an incorrect trajectory. In zero-shot evaluation on the ALFRED benchmark, AtomTree outperforms prior LLM-based planners. Project page: https://zhaihaotian.github. io/atomtree-website/ .

I. INTRODUCTION 

“Planning is the art and practice of thinking before acting.” — Patrik Haslum 

Building embodied agents that can execute diverse house-hold tasks from natural language instructions and rapidly acquire new skills is a core goal in robotics and embodied AI[1], [2], [3]. Embodied Instruction Following (EIF) re-quires an agent to jointly perform visual perception, subgoal planning, navigation, and object interaction in interactive 3D environments to complete long-horizon tasks such as “clean a mug and place it into the coffee machine.”[1] In bench-marks such as ALFRED[1], this requires not only language understanding but also continuous instruction decomposition and execution under partial observability. Therefore, how to achieve EIF that is both generalizable and capable of long-horizon planning under limited supervision remains an open problem[4]. A straightforward approach is to train policy networks with large-scale pairs of natural-language instructions and expert action sequences[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. However, both instruction annotation and expert demonstration collection are expensive and time-consuming, and collecting sufficient data in complex environments is often impractical. To re-duce annotation cost, several recent studies manually design action sequences for specific task types, but such predefined

LLM-Planner[12] uses few-shot in-context learning to generate high-level subgoal sequences, which are then executed by low-level controllers. FLARE[4] further introduces visual in-formation during planning. Socratic Planner[13] decomposes instructions via self-questioning and performs Socratic-style replanning with visual feedback after failures. Despite these advances, most LLM-based methods remain unstable on complex long-horizon tasks. On ALFRED Valid Seen, we perform an error analysis of state-of-the-art few-shot planning methods[4] and categorize failures into four groups in Fig. 1: planning errors, navigation failures, percep-tion failures, and interaction failures . When task horizon is measured by ground-truth PDDL plan length, planning errors account for approximately 38 .9% when the length is no greater than 7, close to interaction failures at 35 .2% . When the length exceeds 7, planning errors increase to 68 .7% and become the dominant failure mode. We further group tasks by the PDDL length threshold k. As k increases from 5 to 

15 , the proportion of planning errors among all failures rises arXiv:2610.38744v1 [cs.RO] 28 Oct 2026 monotonically from around 50% to nearly 100% , showing a strong linear relationship with task length (Pearson r =0.8892 , p ≈ 2.1 × 10 −5). This finding indicates that, in long-horizon embodied instruction following, the primary bottleneck has shifted from low-level navigation, perception, and interaction to high-level task planning itself. The key limitation of existing LLM-based planners on long-horizon tasks is that decision making is still centered on a single unidirectional sequential plan[1], [12], [10], [11], [13]. After receiving an instruction, the model typ-ically generates a full high-level plan in one pass based mainly on linguistic commonsense, and replanning is trig-gered passively only after repeated subgoal failures, rather than through closed-loop decisions at every step conditioned on the current environment state. Consider the ALFRED instruction “Put a washed potato slice on the white table.” The environment ontology does not contain a category named “white table,” and only exposes a limited set of semantic classes such as “Dining Table.” The agent must infer during execution that “white table” is aligned with the observed dining table instance, revealing the gap between human language and environment ontology. By analogy to travel planning, humans rarely commit all details at the outset. Instead, they first decide high-level steps such as “go to Paris” and “book flights and hotels,” and then refine details based on actual conditions. Motivated by this observation, we model EIF as a state-conditioned sequential decision process[14] in which, at each decision node, the agent determines whether to further de-compose the current objective or directly execute an atomic action based on the current state and subgoal, resulting in an AtomTree composed of decomposition and execution nodes. This state-driven hierarchical reasoning can mitigate error accumulation caused by one-pass sequential planning in long-horizon settings. Building on this formulation, we propose AtomTree, which explicitly organizes planning as a tree of decompo-sition nodes and execution nodes. At each node, the policy conditions only on the current environment state, the current instruction segment, and internal memory to decide whether to decompose into finer subgoals or output an atomic action. For example, for the instruction “slice a tomato with a knife,” if the agent is already holding a knife in front of the tomato, AtomTree directly selects the slice action. From an initial state, the system instead decomposes the objective top-down into subgoals such as “find a knife,” “pick up the knife,” and “move to the tomato,” followed by progressive refinement and execution. To realize true state awareness, we maintain memory in each node state to summarize historical observations, enabling consistent context for downstream decisions. When a low-level action stalls for an extended period, we do not perform only local replanning for that action. Instead, we trigger a Hierarchical Backtracking Replanning mechanism that returns to the corresponding parent node and regenerates the subtree plan conditioned on the latest environment state. This enables structured top-down adjustment after local failures, rather than accumulating deviation along an incorrect sequential trajectory. We conduct a systematic evaluation of A TOM TREE on the ALFRED benchmark. The results show that, under a fully zero-shot setting, A TOM TREE surpasses state-of-the-art few-shot LLM-based planners on multiple primary metrics, and even achieves better performance than some few-shot and supervised methods, with especially strong gains on long-horizon tasks and complex instructions. Based on these results, our main contributions are as follows:  

> •

We propose A TOM TREE , a hierarchical tree-structured planning framework for EIF, which explicitly mod-els “decompose / execute” decisions as a state-aware Markov process.  

> •

We design a memory mechanism to enable the planning process to remain continuously aligned with the current environment state during execution, rather than relying on a one-shot sequential plan.  

> •

We validate the effectiveness of the framework through zero-shot experiments on ALFRED. Compared with representative few-shot, zero-shot, and supervised base-lines, ATOM TREE achieves stronger overall perfor-mance. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Embodied Instruction Following 

Embodied Instruction Following (EIF) asks embodied agents to understand and execute natural language instruc-tions in 3D environments, typically requiring long-horizon navigation and object interaction[1], [15]. Prior end-to-end supervised methods directly map visual observations and instructions to fine-grained actions, but rely heavily on large annotated trajectories or task-specific supervision and thus generalize poorly to new environments[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [16]. More recent work uses hierarchical or modular plan-ning and treats LLMs as high-level planners that generate subgoals or action sequences, then verify and replan with environment feedback or external modules[17], [4], [12], [13], [11], [18]; however, these approaches still depend on few-shot prompting and mostly perform one-shot, sequential planning with limited use of evolving state and execution history. In contrast, our method abandons a fixed two-layer decomposition and performs dynamic, divide-and-conquer planning: at each step, the model decides based on the current state whether to further decompose subgoals or emit executable actions, enabling more state-aware and adaptive long-horizon planning. 

B. LLM for Embodied Planning 

With the rapid development of large foundation models (LLMs and VLMs), they have been widely applied to rea-soning, planning, and manipulation in robotic systems[19], [20], [21], [22]. At the task-planning level, a series of works show that decompose-then-solve strategies and structured prompting methods, such as Chain-of-Thought, can signifi-cantly improve LLM performance on complex compositional Fig. 2: Overview of our hierarchical planning framework for embodied instruction following. Given a task instruction and the current visual state, our method recursively decides whether to decompose the current goal into finer-grained subgoals or execute it directly. This process incrementally builds an explicit hierarchical goal tree, shown in the upper-right panel, where higher-level goals are progressively refined into executable actions. In contrast, we compare against two representative paradigms: (1) a fully supervised end-to-end EIF model trained on the full dataset, and (2) an LLM-based method using retrieved few-shot examples. While these baselines may produce incorrect action sequences, our method maintains a structured tree-based plan and performs step-by-step decomposition conditioned on the current state and memory, enabling more accurate long-horizon reasoning and execution. tasks[23], [24]. These results provide a methodological foun-dation for hierarchical planning in embodied settings. In embodied planning, many methods follow the paradigm of using an LLM as a high-level planner. Represen-tative approaches include SayCan[3], which combines an LLM with skill affordance value functions, and In-ner Monologue–style methods that iteratively revise plans from environment feedback[25]. Other works, such as ProgPrompt[26], AutoTAMP[27], and LLM-State[28], en-hance executability and reliability via programmatic prompt-ing, intermediate logical representations, and explicit state tracking. More recent systems for long-horizon tasks[29], [30], [31] adopt bi-stage or hierarchical structures that partially decouple task-level planning from execution-level decision making. However, their planning processes typically still rely on generating relatively long plans in a single shot or on predefined hierarchies, which offers limited support for EIF scenarios like ALFRED that involve long action sequences and highly dynamic states. We also note the concurrent work ReAcTree [32], which is closely related to ours; however, AtomTree is a zero-shot framework that relies only on lightweight symbolic memory, whereas ReAcTree uses explicit control-flow nodes and retrieval-based episodic memory. III. P RELIMINARIES 

We study embodied instruction following (EIF) on the standard ALFRED benchmark [1], which is built on top of the AI2-THOR simulator [33]. Despite recent progress, the overall task success rate of state-of-the-art methods[4] on ALFRED remains below 50%, and our analysis further shows that long-horizon tasks are still the primary bottleneck. In an EIF task, the agent must execute a sequence of actions in the environment to satisfy a language-specified goal. Formally, an EIF task can be represented as a tuple 

(I, E, G ), where I is the instruction, E is the environment, and G is the goal test corresponding to the instruction. In ALFRED, the instruction typically consists of a high-level goal IH and, optionally, a list of step-by-step instructions 

IL. In this work, we deliberately discard the step-by-step instruction list IL and only condition on the high-level goal, following the zero-shot setting in [13] rather than the step-wise language–supervised zero-shot formulation adopted by FlowPlan [17], which better matches real-world human in-structions that are often underspecified and rarely spell out every intermediate step. Following prior work, we adopt a hierarchical planning formulation, but with a key difference: we augment the high-level action space with an explicit Decompose action. At each LLM decision step, the planner conditions on the current state and the current subgoal, and decides whether (1) to further decompose the current objective into finer-grained subgoals, or (2) to directly output an executable high-level atomic action. Concretely, our high-level action space consists of one decomposition action ( Decompose ), two navigation-related actions ( Navigation , Find ), and ten interaction actions (PickupObject , PutObject ,

OpenObject , CloseObject , ToggleOnObject ,

ToggleOffObject , SliceObject , HeatObject ,

CleanObject , CoolObject ), which is consistent with prior ALFRED-based hierarchical methods [10], [4], [16]. The low-level controller follows the standard ALFRED pipeline and maps each high-level action or subgoal to executable primitive actions through a set of deterministic components, including a map-based navigation module and rule-based interaction executors [8], [10], [16], [4]. In par-ticular, the navigation module performs obstacle-free path planning with deterministic planners such as A* and Fast Marching Method (FMM) [34], computing a shortest path or cost-to-go field toward the target and converting the planned route into a sequence of navigation primitives for closed-loop execution. Consequently, once the high-level plan Lh

is determined, low-level execution no longer depends on the original instruction I, i.e., P (Ll | I, L h, E ) = P (Ll |

Lh, E ) [12]. The low-level controller does not require any additional language-annotated training data. IV. M ETHOD 

We present A TOM TREE , a state-aware hierarchical plan-ning framework for embodied instruction following in Fig 2. Building on the formulation in Sec. III, ATOM TREE 

maintains an evolving subgoal tree and interleaves tree ex-pansion with closed-loop execution. Starting from the high-level instruction, the planner iteratively focuses on one active subgoal node, queries an LLM once to obtain a high-level decision conditioned on the current state and memory, and then either expands the subgoal into an ordered list of finer subgoals or executes a single atomic action via the low-level controller.

B. State-Aware Node Memory 

ATOM TREE maintains a lightweight symbolic memory that provides long-horizon context under partial observabil-ity. At step t, the memory is 

mt = ( St, H t), (6) where St is the set of seen object names accumulated so far, and Ht is a high-level action history. Each record in 

Ht stores an atomic action invocation and its final outcome, e.g., Find(Apple): 0 and Navigation(Table): 1 .In implementation, both St and Ht are serialized as short textual lists and included in all subsequent LLM calls. This memory also bridges the gap between descriptive referring expressions in language and the closed vocabu-lary of executable entities under partial observability. For example, consider the instruction “Put a washed potato slice on the white table.” In ALFRED, the agent can only ground actions on object categories that actually exist in the current scene (e.g., DiningTable , SideTable ,

CoffeeTable ), rather than free-form descriptions such as “white table.” At the beginning of an episode, the agent has limited observations and cannot identify which concrete table instance the description refers to. Thus, the planner may initially produce object-centric subgoals that are executable without resolving this reference, e.g., “slice a potato,” “wash the potato slice,” and “place the washed potato slice on the table.” As execution proceeds, the seen-object set St is updated (e.g., discovering that the only table in the scene is DiningTable ). When revisiting the placement subgoal , the planner can then ground the previously ambiguous reference using St and generate an executable action se-quence, e.g., Navigation(DiningTable) followed by 

PutObject(PotatoSliced, DiningTable) .The same memory interface is used consistently for decision making, decomposition outputs, and backtracking replanning, allowing the policy to exploit discovered objects and prior successes/failures via serialized (St, H t) without passing the full raw interaction trace to the LLM. 

C. Hierarchical Backtracking Replanning 

Execution in realistic household environments can fail for many reasons, including interaction errors, target ob-jects being occluded inside containers, or earlier high-level assumptions becoming invalid as new observations arrive. ATOM TREE addresses these issues with hierarchical back-tracking replanning at the subgoal-tree level. For an execution attempt at node v, the low-level controller returns an outcome rv ∈ { 0, 1} together with an execution status. When an action fails ( rv = 0 ) or the controller stalls under its budget[4], [12], the planner backtracks to the parent node u = parent (v) and revisits the higher-level subgoal g(u)

under the updated state and memory: 

yu ∼ π(y | st′ , g (u), m t′ ). (7) The new decision yu may again return Decompose with a revised ordered subgoal list, or return an atomic action for immediate execution. The planner then replaces the previously attempted child subtree under u with the newly produced expansion (if any) and resumes execution. Find(Apple) in order to localize the target but fails ( rv =0). Meanwhile, the accumulated memory records container candidates observed during execution, such as Cabinet

After backtracking to the parent node, the subsequent decision conditioned on the updated state and memory can revise the subtree to include container-oriented search steps, e.g., OpenObject(Cabinet) , and then Find(Apple) again. Once the target is discovered, the planner resumes the original navigation objective. By re-structuring the subtree based on newly discovered evidence, ATOM TREE produces a plan that is better aligned with the current environment state and avoids repeatedly retrying an infeasible action sequence. V. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Benchmark and Splits 

We evaluate A TOM TREE on the standard ALFRED bench-mark [1], an embodied instruction following testbed in inter-active 3D household environments built on AI2-THOR [33]. We follow the official ALFRED evaluation protocol and re-port results on both validation and test splits, each consisting of seen and unseen environments. Specifically, the validation set contains Valid Seen (820 episodes) and Valid Unseen (821 episodes), while the test set contains Test Seen (1,533 episodes) and Test Unseen (1,529 episodes). “Seen” denotes scenes that appear in the ALFRED training set, whereas “Unseen” denotes held-out novel scenes for evaluation. 

B. Models and Inference Setup 

We use DeepSeek-V3 as the base language model in all experiments. We set the sampling temperature to 1 and cap the maximum number of generated tokens per LLM call to 2,024. Our pipeline operates in a zero-shot manner and does not perform any training on ALFRED trajectories. Although our prompts include a small number of exemplars to constrain the output format, we do not leverage any language-annotated supervision or labeled data for learning. 

C. Metrics 

We report the standard ALFRED metrics Success Rate 

(SR) and Goal-Conditioned Success (GC) [1]. SR measures the percentage of episodes in which the agent completes the full task successfully. GC measures the fraction of goal conditions satisfied at the end of an episode, capturing partial progress when a task is not fully completed.

E. Ablation Study 

We conduct ablations on the ALFRED validation splits and report SR on Valid Seen and Valid Unseen. We start from a ReAct[36]-style reactive baseline that re-queries the LLM after each executed atomic action, without hierarchi-cal planning, memory, or backtracking. Since this baseline already performs step-by-step closed-loop decision making, its performance remains close to prior zero-shot sequential planners such as S OCRATIC -P LANNER .We then progressively add three components: (i) hierar-chical planning with an explicit Decompose action and a subgoal tree, (ii) state&history memory mt = ( St, H t),and (iii) hierarchical backtracking replanning. As shown in Table II, each component consistently improves SR on both validation splits. Hierarchical planning provides the first clear gain by allowing the model to decide whether to further decompose the current objective or directly execute an action. The largest improvement comes from memory, which prevents the planner from forgetting completed actions and falling into repetitive predictions, especially for composite subgoals such as cleaning. Backtracking replanning brings a smaller additional gain by revising the subtree after failures under the updated state and memory. Fig. 4: Qualitative comparison with and without memory. F. Qualitative Analysis 

Fig. 4 illustrates why memory is important. Consider the instruction of putting a washed lettuce into the refrigerator. A natural decomposition is to first clean the lettuce and then place the cleaned lettuce into the fridge. After the cleaning subgoal has already been completed, the planner should move on to the placement subgoal. However, without memory, the planner cannot reliably tell whether the lettuce has already been washed. As a result, when it receives the next subgoal phrased as “put the washed lettuce into the refrigerator,” it may decompose it again into cleaning-related steps followed by placement, leading to redundant subgoals or loops. In contrast, A TOM TREE records completed actions in memory and correctly proceeds to the next subgoal, namely navigating to the fridge. Fig. 5 compares A TOM TREE with a few-shot planner. In this example, after placing the first spray bottle, the few-shot method predicts a cabinet-related action even though the current scene does not require opening a cabinet. This suggests that retrieved exemplars can bias the planner toward irrelevant patterns. In contrast, A TOM TREE continues with the correct next action by conditioning on the current state and execution history. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented A TOM TREE , a state-aware hierarchical plan-ning framework for embodied instruction following. Instead of relying on one-shot sequential planning, A TOM TREE in-crementally builds and executes a subgoal tree, using explicit decomposition decisions, lightweight state&history memory, and hierarchical backtracking replanning. Experiments on ALFRED show that A TOM TREE substantially outperforms prior zero-shot and few-shot LLM-based planners, and is competitive with several supervised methods, especially on long-horizon and unseen tasks.
