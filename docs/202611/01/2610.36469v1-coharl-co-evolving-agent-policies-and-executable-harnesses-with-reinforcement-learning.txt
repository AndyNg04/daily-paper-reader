Abstract 

Large language model (LLM) agents interact with their environments through executable harnesses that govern tool use and feedback. Optimizing a harness for a fixed policy overlooks how its behavior changes during learning, while training a policy under a fixed harness leaves the interface that generates its training experi-ence unchanged. This motivates adapting the harness throughout policy training. We introduce CoHARL, a population-based reinforcement learning framework that co-evolves a shared policy and a population of executable harnesses. The policy learns from rollouts across the population, and a proposer uses its execution traces to revise harness code during training. CoHARL achieves a 27.6% mean resolved rate on SWE-rebench, improving over harness-only search by 6.4 percent-age points. Experiments on SWE-bench Verified and Terminal-Bench 2.0 show gains in software engineering and terminal-based tasks. Under matched training budgets, CoHARL also outperforms searching for a harness once before RL and alternating policy updates with revisions to a single active harness. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

LLM agents solve software-engineering tasks by interacting with repositories, tools, and execution environments (Weng, 2026; Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Karten et al., 2026). Their behavior depends on both model weights and the harness: the program that handles prompts, action parsing, tool responses, and episode control. The same program controls inference-time execution and the collection of training trajectories. Agent failures can originate in either the execution code or the policy. If an action parser rejects a valid tool call, or a tool-output handler discards feedback needed for the next decision, updating model weights does not repair that code. Policy-only reinforcement learning (RL) (Wei et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025) may teach the agent to work around the defect, but leaves its cause in the harness unchanged. Conversely, even with correct tool execution, a policy may choose an uninformative command or misinterpret a test failure. Harness-only search (Lee et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a; Lin et al., 2026) can add guidance and safeguards, but does not update the model’s parameters to learn from these mistakes. The two forms of adaptation are also linked during training. Repairing a tool handler changes the feedback available to the policy and the trajectories from which it learns. As the policy improves, its execution traces can reveal further harness weaknesses that were not apparent under the initial model. Searching once before RL cannot respond to those later failures; keeping the policy frozen cannot train it on the experience collected through the revised interface. Recent methods update both model weights and harness or training code (Chen et al., 2026a; Luo et al., 2026). WHALE alternates harness search under frozen model weights with rejection-sampling fine-tuning under a selected harness (Kim et al., 2026). Its policy updates therefore use one selected execution interface at a time. In CoHARL, multiple harnesses remain active during training: a scheduler allocates rollouts across the evolving population, and these rollouts jointly update one shared policy through reinforcement learning. This allows new candidates to contribute training experience alongside existing versions, rather than replacing the interface for all subsequent rollouts. We evaluate the benefit of this population-based design against single-harness alternating updates under the same training budget. In CoHARL, a proposer model reads the policy’s successful and failed trajectories and revises harness code. Accepted candidates join the population, from which a scheduler selects versions for training rollouts. The proposer also receives a Pareto archive of historical accuracy–cost trade-offs. Figure 2 shows how search and rollout collection alternate with policy updates. We evaluate CoHARL on software-engineering and terminal-based tasks. On SWE-rebench, it achieves a 27.6% mean resolved rate, compared with 21.2% for harness-only search and 20.3% for policy-only training. CoHARL also outperforms both baselines on SWE-bench Verified without further adaptation. A separately trained Terminal-Bench 2.0 agent improves on harness-only search. Matched-budget controls favor continued population-based training over one-time search and single-harness alternation; ablations examine execution feedback and historical context. Our contributions are threefold: (i) a population-based formulation of policy–harness co-evolution, in which diverse execution interfaces supply experience to one shared policy; (ii) a search-and-training procedure that uses current-policy traces and historical accuracy–cost context to propose programs, then schedules accepted versions for future rollouts; and (iii) evaluation across three benchmarks, with matched-budget controls for one-time search, single-harness alternation, and search context.

Reinforcement Learning for LLM Agents. CoHARL instead uses a separate proposer to evolve agent-side programs while training one shared policy across the scheduled population. Unlike HarnessBandit’s scheduling over a given harness set (Wei et al., 2026a), our search also changes the programs available during training. Appendix F details these distinctions.

Two hard constraints apply: the proposer cannot modify the training framework, and it cannot change the action-format family used by the policy. Candidate checks are domain-specific: SWE uses a structural contract check followed by pool trials, while TB-2 additionally uses smoke testing and candidate validation before pool trials. Accepted versions are staged and admitted only at step boundaries. Trial rollouts provide initial usage statistics and update their ranking. Appendix C.1 details these paths. Search and policy optimization operate on different objects: the proposer edits execution code from trajectory feedback, while RL updates the shared weights from population rollouts. An accepted edit adds an alternative interface for subsequent collection; it does not replace every active harness. Its usefulness is then assessed through pool trials under the training policy.

The LCB term lowers the score of under-sampled members; protection from premature eviction instead comes from forced trials and a minimum-use rule. This is a heuristic score rather than a calibrated statistical confidence interval. It governs harness selection, eviction, and parent choice, but is not added to the training advantage in Eq. (3). The scheduler thus changes which experience is collected without redefining the reward used by the policy update. Scheduling combines performance-based allocation with candidate trials. The scheduler allocates N slots, not necessarily N distinct versions, so training can start from the seed alone. Newly admitted candidates receive forced trials and remain protected until the minimum-use requirement is met. Their rollout outcomes update the ranking used for later allocation and eviction. Parent selection draws from the active pool using these performance scores; retaining a version in the archive alone does not make it eligible as a parent. 

Historical accuracy–cost archive. The archive retains non-dominated recorded accuracy–cost pairs: a version dominates another when its accuracy is no lower and its mean generated-token cost no higher, with at least one strict inequality. The pool and archive serve different decisions. The 5pool determines which programs collect training experience; the archive supplies the proposer with previously observed accuracy–cost trade-offs, including records of versions evicted from the pool. Parent traces describe how the current policy uses one program, whereas archive records provide context across earlier evaluations. These records retain their historical scores rather than estimating every version’s performance under the current policy. Identical rewards within a task–harness group give zero advantages in Eq. (3). For K ≥ 2 condition-ally independent binary outcomes with success probability p, the probability of a non-uniform group is 

ρK (p) = 1 − pK − (1 − p)K . (6) For rare success, ρK (p) ≈ Kp : improving the interface can make reward contrasts more frequent on difficult tasks. The relation reverses above p = 1 /2, so higher success does not always yield more contrast. This provides an intuition for coupling harness adaptation with group-relative learning, not a convergence guarantee. Appendix A gives the derivation; SWE’s shaped filtering scores are treated separately in Appendix D.1. 3.4 TASK REWARDS AND EVALUATOR INTEGRITY 

Joint adaptation creates opportunities for reward exploitation through both policy behavior and harness code. For SWE evaluation, success is strictly binary, r=1 iff all FAIL _TO _PASS tests pass and all PASS _TO _PASS tests continue to pass, with forced submission at episode end and no LLM judge. SWE training combines test correctness with submission-gated process rewards and applies a soft length penalty before the policy update. Group filtering uses the separate test_score field, whose meaning depends on the harness: it contains only test pass fractions in the seed harness, but can include process rewards in evolved versions. Thus, evaluation uses strict resolution, policy advantages use the transformed training reward, and retention diagnostics use the filtering score. A retained SWE group need not contain a fully resolved episode. Appendix C.1 gives the reward and score mapping. TB-2 instead uses binary training rewards, without soft length penalties or per-harness reward normalization. Harness edits could expose hidden tests to the policy. The seed harness disables hidden-test injection, and an audit gate rejects detected attempts to reintroduce access. Code inspection found no hidden-test access in five admitted harnesses; a separate probe rejected six test-access proposals. Appendix B describes the audit scope and limitations. 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Our experiments address three questions: 1) Does co-evolution improve task performance over harness-only search and policy-only RL, and do the gains extend beyond SWE-rebench? 2) What do online search, the harness population, and proposer context contribute under matched training budgets? 3) How do training rewards and harness utilization evolve during learning? 4.1 SETUP 

Benchmarks and metrics. Our primary experiments use SWE-rebench (Badertdinov et al., 2025), with tasks dated before 2026 for training and 110 tasks dated after March 2026 for held-out evaluation. We report mean resolved rates over three attempts per task (avg@3; temperature 0.7). To assess cross-dataset generalization, we evaluate the SWE-rebench-trained agents on the 500-task SWE-bench Verified benchmark (Jimenez et al., 2024; Chowdhury et al., 2024). We also train a separate agent on Terminal-Bench 2.0 (TB-2) (Merrill et al., 2026), using Harbor (Harbor Framework Team, 2026) for evaluation and Terminus-2 as the baseline harness. SWE tasks are scored by strict resolution; TB-2 tasks use their benchmark-provided evaluators. 

Model and training. We train Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (Qwen Team, 2026) directly with RL using rank-32 LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) based on SkyRL (Griggs et al., 2025). SWE-rebench performance. CoHARL achieves a 27.6% resolved rate, outperforming Meta-Harness by 6.4 percentage points and Plain GRPO by 7.3 points (Table 1). Both baselines improve on the initial agent, with Meta-Harness achieving a higher resolved rate than Plain GRPO without changing the policy weights. Improving the execution interface can therefore be effective even without policy training, but neither interface optimization nor policy-only RL matches joint adaptation. In CoHARL, harness revisions also change the experience available for subsequent policy updates. Its advantage over both baselines is consistent with treating the harness as part of the learning process rather than only an inference-time wrapper. 4.3 ANALYSIS 

Ablation study. We ablate harness evolution and proposer context, and compare alternative training strategies under matched 30-step training budgets (Table 4). Fixing the seed harness (A1) reduces the resolved rate from 25.5% to 20.0%, showing the benefit of adapting the interface during policy training. Removing archive context (A2) or execution traces (A3) also lowers performance, even though both variants retain online search. These inputs serve different purposes: the archive records previously observed accuracy–cost trade-offs, whereas traces show how the current policy uses the parent harness. The two ablations indicate that both sources of context help the proposer produce useful revisions. Search-then-RL (A4) improves on fixed-harness RL but remains 2.7 percentage points below the full method. Its harness is selected for the initial policy and remains fixed throughout RL, even as the policy’s capabilities and execution behavior change. We presented CoHARL, which jointly trains a shared policy and evolves a population of executable harnesses. Experiments on SWE-rebench, SWE-bench Verified, and Terminal-Bench 2.0 demonstrate the benefits of adapting both components. Matched-budget ablations favor continued population-based evolution over searching once before RL or repeatedly revising a single harness. Together, these results show that executable interfaces are an important target for agent learning alongside policy weights: policy behavior informs harness revisions, and revised harnesses shape subsequent training experience. Training-batch preparation. Batch preparation applies overlength filtering before the group-variance test. If fewer than two groups pass the variance test, the protective fallback bypasses that 17 test, not the preceding length filter. The training adapter then removes groups with fewer than two surviving sequences and aligns the remaining groups to a common size. A batch with insufficient usable samples produces no policy update. The diagnostic counting point precedes this adapter-level alignment (Appendix D.1). 

Candidate checks and policy monitoring. In the main SWE recipe, candidates enter population trials after contract checks, and ranking is updated using actual SWE training rollouts. A1 disables harness evolution; the matched comparison above uses common candidate-check and acceptance rules for the full method and A5. TB-2 uses contract checks, a one-task, one-episode smoke test, and candidate validation on 20 tasks with one attempt per task at temperature zero, before population trials. Candidate validation is distinct from training-time policy monitoring: SWE monitoring uses 32 tasks under a fixed baseline harness, while TB-2 monitoring uses 20 tasks. The ref_check 

monitoring routine runs every 10 training steps; it does not imply an active reference-policy penalty. 

Evaluation scope. The SWE main comparison evaluates 110 tasks with three attempts per task and reports strict resolved avg@3; A1–A5 ablations use 110 tasks with one attempt per task. SWE-bench Verified evaluates transfer without further adaptation on 500 tasks with one attempt per task. TB-2 uses a separately trained terminal agent. These benchmark evaluations are distinct from candidate screening and training-time policy monitoring. For our TB-2 evaluations, temperature is 0.6 and each task’s binary outcomes are averaged over the episodes actually returned; a task with no returned episode contributes zero. The reported score is the arithmetic mean of these task-level rates, rather than success in at least one attempt. For a three-attempt evaluation, this equals fixed-denominator avg@3 only when all three episodes return for every task; missing episodes on a partially completed task do not enter that task’s denominator. Four historical TB-2 evaluations each scheduled 89 tasks with three attempts per task (267 episodes) at temperature 0.6; their returned episode counts were 267, 255, 261, and 265, respectively. The quoted Flat GRPO result instead follows Shepherd’s original 89-task avg@5 protocol. 

Scheduling and selection interfaces. The historical scheduler settings use proposal intervals of four steps in phase A and eight in phase B, shortened to two steps in either phase when the pool contains fewer than four members. The supplementary A4/A5 comparisons follow the schedules specified above. A proposal is eligible at positive scheduler steps divisible by that interval, subject to the proposal budget. Saturation does not trigger proposals. Forced-trial priority and minimum-use protection precede ordinary eviction eligibility; archive dominance alone does not evict a training-pool member. Policy-checkpoint selection and evaluation-harness selection are performed separately. For the main SWE experiment, we use the policy checkpoint at training step 150, paired with the highest-ranked harness in the final training pool.

## E LIMITATIONS 

We evaluate one policy-model setting using single training runs, without confidence intervals or a complete comparison of total compute. The reward-contrast analysis does not prove faster con-vergence, and final task counts cannot establish that gradient instability caused the single-harness control’s lower performance. Repeated validation can overfit harness selection; policy updates can also make archive scores stale. Fixing the action-format family restricts which edits can be explored. We do not separately measure the effects of proposer quality or policy gains under a common harness. Pretraining contamination remains possible in the cross-dataset evaluation. The terminal results use a separately trained agent, not zero-shot transfer from SWE. 20 F COMPARISON WITH CLOSELY RELATED FRAMEWORKS
