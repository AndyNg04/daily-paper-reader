## Abstract 

Autonomous agents are increasingly deployed across sequences of related tasks, yet they often fail to retain and improve their knowledge over time. Existing approaches convert interaction trajectories into memories or reusable skills, but experience is not automatically reliable knowledge. Automatically collected skills and memories can propagate errors, mislead, and corrupt useful knowledge. To address this gap, we introduce Knowledge Evolution from Expertise and Lessons (KEEL), an agent-agnostic framework that uses verifier evidence to transform successful behavior into reusable skills and failures into corrective lessons. As new evidence accumulates, individual skill claims can be supported, contradicted, replaced, or retired. On SkillFlow benchmark, KEEL improves task success by 4.2 to 21.7 percentage points over the no-knowledge baseline, compared with gains of 2.4 to 9.6 points from SkillFlow’s default method. Ablations show that skills and lessons are complementary. These results show that reliable continual agent learning depends not simply on accumulating experience, but on continually validating and evolving the knowledge derived from it. ## 1 Introduction 

Large language model agents increasingly perform complex tasks across diverse domains [1, 2]. Yet most agents are commonly developed and evaluated in static episodic settings due to the challenges of enacting realistic multi-turn/multi-session evaluations [3]. As a result, when applied in a lifelong learning scenario, where an agent is repeatedly applied for different tasks in a given project or group of projects, agents commonly fail to persist knowledge between tasks [4, 5]. A growing body of work enables agents to improve from experience without updating the parameters of their underlying language models. These include methods for distilling interactions into reusable natural-language text [6, 7], creating executable and reusable skill libraries [8–10], and combining the two approaches to maintain complementary representation of individual experiences and higher-level skills [4, 11–14]. Recent methods further improve skill reliability through additional validation executions [15], or combine repeated skill evaluation with policy optimization [16, 17]. While these studies demonstrate that persisted knowledge can support improvement across interac-tions, they suffer from a critical limitation: experience is not automatically reliable knowledge .In particular, a successful task outcome does not guarantee that the underlying trajectory contains reliable behavior [18]. An interaction trajectory may interleave useful behavior with ineffective or harmful actions, and may satisfy some verifier checks while violating others. A single task-level

Persistent memory can propagate errors [19], induce negative transfer or compromise retention [20], and corrupt useful knowledge even when the underlying experiences are correct [21]. Auto-generated skills can introduce implementation faults or override user instructions [22], and brittle assumptions can make them unsuitable for future tasks [23]. Together, these findings identify a key challenge: agents must incorporate new knowledge without corrupting their existing knowledge base . This involves deter-mining which evidence warrants promoting behavior into persisted knowledge, which observations should be retained as lessons, and which individual knowledge claims should be supported, revised, or retired as new evidence accumulates. To address these challenges, we propose Knowledge Evolution from Expertise and Lessons (KEEL), a non-parametric agent continual-learning framework for maintaining procedural and corrective knowledge grounded in verifiable task experience. KEEL consolidates sequential interaction out-comes into two complementary forms of knowledge: expertise and lessons . Expertise captures successful behavior as reusable procedural skills, while lessons record mistakes, violated constraints, and potential recovery strategies. Beyond this skill/lesson decomposition, KEEL introduces fine-grained, evidence-grounded knowledge management that connects verifier outcomes to specific trajectory behaviors and skill claims. Rather than treating skills as indivisible knowledge units, KEEL maintains an explicit claim-level lifecycle that supports evidence-based promotion, contradiction, replacement, and retirement while preserving provenance. We evaluate KEEL using Terminus-2 on SkillFlow [5], a benchmark containing 166 tasks organized into 20 ordered workflow families. Within each family, tasks are presented sequentially in increasing order of difficulty, and the agent carries knowledge acquired from earlier tasks into later ones. This setting evaluates whether an agent can effectively learn from experience and transfer that knowledge over time. Across all three complete model comparisons, KEEL improves pass rates over No Skills by 4.2 to 21.7 percentage points, compared with improvements of 2.4 to 9.6 percentage points achieved by SkillFlow’s default method over the same baseline. With Claude Opus 4.6, KEEL increases the pass rate from 44.0% with No Skills to 65.7%, compared with 53.6% for SkillFlow’s default skill-evolution method. Ablations show that skills and lessons provide complementary benefits. We further analyze the associated inference cost and the effect of the underlying model and reasoning configuration. Experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of KEEL’s evidence-grounded knowledge-management framework for continual agent learning. Our contributions are threefold: • We introduce an evidence-grounded framework for continual agent learning that extracts reusable skills and lessons from agent experience while keeping model parameters fixed. • We develop a claim-level lifecycle that uses verifier and trajectory evidence to promote, contradict, replace, and retire knowledge while preserving its provenance. • We evaluate KEEL across multiple models and reasoning configurations, finding consistent improvements over baselines. We also analyze inference costs and the contributions of individual components.

2.4 Skill-Claim Lifecycle 

A claim about a computational procedure enters as draft after partial success or without direct evidence of the procedure; otherwise, it enters as provisional . All other accepted claims enter as 

provisional . Draft claims await further support, while provisional claims are visible to the Actor. Confirmed claims have been validated through later use. Contradicted and retired claims are hidden but retained for provenance. As shown in Figure 3, a draft claim becomes provisional when it receives independent support from another task’s execution. A provisional claim becomes confirmed when its later use is supported by relevant passed checks. Direct medium- or high-confidence counterevidence moves a provisional or confirmed claim to contradicted , while not_evaluable feedback causes no transition. When an accepted candidate refreshes an existing skill, returned claims preserve their history, while omitted claims are retired. Contradicted and retired identifiers cannot be reused. A corrected claim must use a new identifier and enters as draft or provisional according to its evidence. Before each task, the Actor receives only provisional and confirmed claims not suppressed by the selected lesson. 

## 3 Experimental Results 

3.1 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate KEEL on the SkillFlow benchmark [5], using Terminus-2 [24] (the default implemen-tation included with the SkillFlow codebase [25]) as the execution agent. SkillFlow contains 166 tasks organized into 20 workflow families. Each family comprises an ordered sequence of related, increasingly difficult tasks within the same repository. Tasks are executed in the benchmark-provided order, with one rollout per task. For all evaluated methods, knowledge is curated independently for each family and is available only to subsequent tasks in that family. The benchmark spans diverse domains, including data science, finance, and life sciences. We compare three conditions: No Skills , which retains no cross-task knowledge; the skill-curation mechanism released in SkillFlow [5], as a baseline; and KEEL . For the direct three-way comparison, we evaluate GPT-5.4, GPT-5.6 Sol, and Claude Opus 4.6. We additionally evaluate Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Haiku 4.5 to study the effect of model capability. We use high reasoning effort for GPT-5.6 Sol, medium for GPT-5.4, Claude Opus 4.6, and Claude Sonnet 4.6, and disable extended thinking for Claude Haiku 4.5.

KEEL’s performance gains come with additional inference cost. Figure 4a shows that KEEL achieves the highest pass rate for each model, but requires additional inference to construct, validate, and apply its richer knowledge state. For example, on Opus 4.6, KEEL improves pass rate by about 50% (relative) over No Skills while increasing estimated cost per instance from $0.19 to $0.80. The additional cost stems primarily from increased token usage, which is inherent to KEEL’s current design, and secondarily from reduced cache efficiency, which could likely be improved beyond our prototype implementation. KEEL therefore provides a mechanism for trading additional inference-time computation for substantially stronger continual-learning performance.

3.4 Model and Reasoning Configuration KEEL’s benefits depend on the capability of the underlying model. From Table 2, we observe that KEEL improves Opus 4.6 from 44.0% to 65.7% and Sonnet 4.6 from 44.0% to 61.4%, but decreases Haiku 4.5 from 37.3% to 28.9%. This suggests that KEEL’s richer knowledge can provide substantial leverage to sufficiently capable models, while weaker models may be less able to reliably interpret and apply the additional context. This dependence may arise during knowledge construction as well as execution. KEEL’s Analyzer relates check-level verifier feedback to evidence in the execution trajectory before skills and lessons are constructed or revised. Because its outputs directly inform these updates, reliable Analyzer output is a prerequisite for reliable knowledge evolution. KEEL may therefore benefit from using a sufficiently capable LLM or a stronger reasoning configuration for evidence analysis. 

3.5 Ablation Skills and lessons provide complementary benefits. Full KEEL achieves the highest pass rate at 65.7%. Table 3 shows that removing skills reduces it to 63.9%, while removing lessons reduces it to 59.0%, indicating that both components contribute. The larger decrease without lessons suggests that failure-derived guidance provides the greater marginal benefit in this Opus 4.6 experiment. KEEL-SkillsOnly outperforms baseline SkillFlow skill curation by 5.4 points, suggesting that KEEL’s evidence-grounded skill-management pipeline is beneficial. 

## 4 Conclusion 

We introduced KEEL, a continual-learning framework that treats external agent knowledge as an evolving state rather than an append-only memory. KEEL grounds verifier outcomes in execution evidence, converts supported behavior into reusable skills, and records failures as diagnostic or corrective lessons. Individual knowledge claims are subsequently confirmed, contradicted, or retired as evidence accumulates. We evaluated KEEL on the SkillFlow benchmark, which contains 166 tasks organized into 20 ordered workflow families. KEEL outperforms both the No Skills baseline and the original skill-curation method proposed in the SkillFlow paper across all complete model comparisons. Further analysis shows that skills and lessons provide complementary benefits, and that KEEL’s advantage emerges after a single task is completed. The limitation is that knowledge quality depends on reliable evidence attribution by the Analyzer.
