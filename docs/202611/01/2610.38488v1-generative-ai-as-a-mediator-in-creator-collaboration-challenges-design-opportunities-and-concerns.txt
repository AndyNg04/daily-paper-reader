Generative AI as a Mediator in Creator Collaboration:
Challenges, Design Opportunities and Concerns
HAJUN KIM∗, Independent Researcher, Republic of Korea
JINI KIM∗, Carnegie Mellon University, USA
YUNJAE JOSEPHINE CHOI, KAIST, Republic of Korea
The development of GenAI tools for collaboration among creators has recently attracted significant attention.
Despite their growing use, prior studies have yet to fully explore the broader challenges of creative collaboration
or the role of GenAI in supporting creators from diverse fields. To address this gap, we conducted a multi-
method study with narrative writers (N = 10) and visual designers (N = 10) in the context of game development,
where diverse collaboration challenges and asymmetries are especially salient. Our findings suggest that future
GenAI tools have the potential to bridge the knowledge gap and workflow gaps, reduce the psychological
burden of interacting with creators of different background, and improve work efficiency. At the same time,
we highlight concerns that GenAI may reduce communication among creators, heighten reputational risks,
and reshape interpersonal dynamics and collaborative processes. We also identify design opportunities to
ensure that GenAI tools genuinely enhance collaboration without undermining the creativity and values
inherent to human work.

INTRODUCTION
Generative AI (GenAI) has begun to integrate into the workflows of individual content creators,
innovating individual creative processes from ideation to the automation of repetitive tasks [1, 19,
71, 78, 98]. Moving beyond these individual-centric use cases, the focus of the HCI community has
recently expanded toward integrating GenAI into multi-party collaborative processes [41, 109].
Early studies have concentrated on exploring new collaborative dynamics by leveraging GenAI
as a teammate, such as by coordinating its intervention timing or control [47, 100]. However, the
discussion needs to deepen from treating GenAI merely as a team member to address the core
challenge of creative collaboration long emphasized by CSCW researchers: building Common
Ground [16, 21, 26, 56, 104]—that is, the body of mutual knowledge and beliefs that collaborators
should continuously construct and update through an effortful process of communication.
Building common ground becomes particularly challenging when heterogeneous creators con-
vene [79]. Due to differences in their professional domains [22, 80], perspectives, and goals [8],
these creators possess epistemic asymmetry, which induces continuous friction in the process of
building shared understanding [24, 27, 82]. Furthermore, structural conditions including remote,

mitigated these issues by utilizing boundary objects, such as managing joint documents through

work sequences, decision-making authority, and role division, and more [44, 88, 92]. However,
these approaches have limitations, as they impose significant time, cognitive, and social costs on

In this context, GenAI presents significant potential to mitigate these challenges. Through
translation between different modalities, such as text and image, GenAI can support participants
in reducing friction in meaning interpretation and approaching mutual understanding while
maintaining their preferred creative languages [53, 66]. Moreover, unlike traditional boundary
objects which tend to remain as static outputs that are costly to update [59], GenAI holds the
potential to function as a fluid medium that is continuously modified and concretized across
repeated interactions, as context accumulates over time [100]. This can alleviate the formalization
costs borne by humans and accelerate the process of meaning-making through interaction [39].
Furthermore, GenAI provides an impersonal mediation process that complements face-to-face
interactions prone to emotional exhaustion or hierarchical pressure, thereby efficiently managing
the social costs of grounding [15]. Despite this potential, empirical understanding of how GenAI
can mediate collaborations among heterogeneous creators remains limited.
To examine the mediating potential of GenAI, this research selected the early stage of game
development as the study context, where the difficulty of creative collaboration is maximized. This
phase serves as a high-stakes testbed where the friction in building common ground between
creators with distinct epistemic systems is most clearly revealed. In the early game development
phase, narrative writers share a vision using narrative documents containing text and visual
references as boundary objects, and visual designers translate these into visual language while
repeatedly tuning for intentional alignment. This workflow implies high collaboration burdens in
two aspects. First, epistemic asymmetry becomes pronounced as text-centric thinking and image-
centric thinking intersect [107], generating substantial cognitive costs in interpreting boundary

Moreover, when GenAI is integrated into collaborations between heterogeneous creators and
begins to take on the role of facilitating their work, it becomes necessary to explore a new set
of concerns [102]. Existing research has primarily focused on existential concerns in the one-
to-one creator-GenAI relationship—such as creator replacement [75], threats to creative identity
[10, 51], and loss of autonomy [28, 63]. Other studies have highlighted technical concerns with
GenAI systems themselves, such as their unpredictable and biased outputs [69, 75, 81], potential
for stereotypes, and plagiarism [54]. However, when GenAI operates between human collaborators,
the focus of concern shifts to a relational and procedural dimension: how GenAI influences the
relationships and processes among collaborators, and what concerns creators themselves raise
about GenAI in this context. These new potential concerns remain underexplored.
Therefore, we pose the following research questions:
• RQ 1. What collaboration challenges do heterogeneous creators face, and what design oppor-
tunities do they envision for GenAI to better support their collaboration?
• RQ 2. When GenAI is integrated into their collaborative workflows, what potential concerns
do creators perceive?
To answer these questions, we conducted a multi-method study with narrative writers (N=10)
and visual designers (N=10), including preliminary interviews and participatory design studies.
Specifically, through co-design using designed stimuli—such as GenAI feature cards and creative
prompting sessions—we helped participants concretely imagine and discuss the unfamiliar role of
GenAI as a collaborative facilitator and mediator.
We aim to contribute to CSCW and HCI through an empirical study on the role of GenAI in
facilitating and mediating the collaborative processes of multiple creators. Our primary contribu-
tions are as follows: (1) We closely examine the collaboration between narrative writers and visual
designers in the early game development stage to identify the fundamental challenges arising in
heterogeneous creative teamwork across different modalities; (2) We propose design opportuni-
ties and guidelines for GenAI collaboration tools that facilitate and mediate creator relationships
and mutual understanding throughout the entire collaborative process; (3) We identify potential
concerns and key considerations that emerge when GenAI is integrated into collaborative work-
flows, offering practical insights for future tool design; and (4) We introduce an effective co-design
methodology and creative-promting session that incorporates domain experts’ insights. Overall,
these contributions collectively inform the design of GenAI tools, particularly in muti-creator
collaboration contexts.

RELATED WORK
2.1
Supporting End-to-End Creative Collaboration with GenAI
Recent research on GenAI-based creativity support has increasingly expanded its focus from
assisting individual creators to supporting collaborative processes. While early studies focused on
aiding individual creative processes [6, 30, 46, 48, 60, 83, 94] and designing workflow-optimized
interactions [1, 13, 54, 78, 97, 98, 109], recent work, leveraging GenAI’s ability to streamline decision-
making and consensus-building [42, 96], has seen emerging attempts to utilize it as a team member
or a facilitator of collaboration [32, 93]. For instance, Houde empirically analyzed how the timing,
method, and distribution of control for a GenAI teammate’s interventions affect a group’s idea

of individual interests, reactions, and participation, GenAI can identify common ground among
participants and provide tailored materials to facilitate smoother collaboration and conversational
flow [100].
Despite this progress, how GenAI can mediate collaboration between creators with heterogeneous
backgrounds remains a challenging task [79]. Participants with differing professional domains
[22, 80], goal and perspectives [8] incur continuous communication costs to build common ground
[24, 27, 82], a challenge that is further intensified under structural conditions such as remote,
asynchronous environments or team hierarchies [36, 37, 55, 85]. Traditionally, the HCI and CSCW
community has reduced collaborative friction by utilizing boundary objects, shared artifacts that
bridge the epistemic gap between distinct creative domains, allowing collaborators to translate
abstract concepts into a unified vision [33, 61, 89], or through coordination mechanisms that
systematically manage the procedural flow and complex interdependencies among participants
[44, 88, 92].
Recent researchers are revisiting these classical CSCW methodologies by leveraging GenAI’s
capabilities in multimodal interpretation [53, 66], context augmentation [100], and consensus
building [42, 96]. First, GenAI is being utilized as a tool for generating boundary objects that assist
in knowledge sharing by resolving asymmetries in expressive competence. For example, Ko et
al. converted ambiguous ideas into concrete visual boundary objects by visualizing writers’ text
drafts for designers [53], while Ling et al. supported game designers with limited visualization
skills in manifesting their ideas as illustrations [66]. However, these approaches were limited to
unidirectional information transfer by focusing on augmenting individual expressive capabilities,
and failed to sufficiently address the bidirectional consensus process of interacting around boundary
objects to build common ground. Second, there are attempts to utilize GenAI as a coordination
mechanism or real-time mediator to reduce friction in the collaborative process. Jose et al. demon-
strated that the process of a facilitator or creator developing GenAI-generated ‘imperfects’ acts as a
dynamic boundary object that promotes communication [39]. Meanwhile, Claggett et al. proposed a
Social Coordination model where an LLM identifies the state of social alignment during conflicting
opinions and suggests appropriate phrasing [15].
However, these prior studies are limited by their focus on fragmentary phases of collabora-
tion—primarily synchronous ideation sessions or real-time conversations. Yet, complex projects
in real-world creative industries are predominantly conducted in remote and asynchronous man-
ners due to spatio-temporal constraints [52, 77]. Participants rarely meet in real-time, instead
communicating intermittently by creating and delivering outputs in their own timeframes. In
such asynchronous environments, where immediate feedback or social attunement is difficult, an
approach that diachronically manages the shifting asymmetries across the entire process—from
preparation to interaction to utilization—is essential. For instance, information asymmetry may
serve as the primary challenge during the preparation phase, whereas power asymmetry might
hinder communication during the interaction phase. Yet, a systematic exploration of these dy-
namic asymmetries and an integrated approach to support the full collaborative lifecycle are still
underexplored.
Therefore, this study aims to fill this gap by examining the collaboration process between
narrative writers and visual designers in the early stages of game development. In this phase, there
exists a fundamental asymmetry of conflicting languages and goals [66], with writers prioritizing
narrative consistency while designers prioritize visual coherence [107]. This setting provides
an ideal research environment where spatio-temporal constraints, information imbalances, and
organizational power asymmetries complexly intersect. The tight deadlines characteristic of the
game industry serve as factors that further exacerbate these conflicts [3, 9]. We explore how GenAI

2.2
Perception and Concerns About Collaborating with GenAI in Creative Process
The discourse surrounding GenAI in creative fields has been shaped by a fundamental tension
between its potential as a tool and the threat it poses to human creators [23, 51]. Creators’ anxieties
about GenAI have given rise to various concerns that act as a key barrier to technology adoption
[75], and the HCI community has focused on identifying the substance of these concerns.
The concerns identified in prior research can be broadly divided into two categories. The first
involves threats to the creator’s agency and professional identity. This includes the fear of creative
roles being replaced [75], the loss of autonomy in the creative process [28, 63], and worries about the
loss of a ‘human touch’ and the dilution of unique identity [10, 51]. The second category comprises
risks stemming from the opacity and unreliability of the AI system itself. The unpredictable and
biased outputs of AI [69, 75, 81], the reproduction of stereotypes and potential for plagiarism [54],
and its inability to understand social nuances [29] are key technical and ethical barriers that make
it difficult for creators to accept AI as a trusted partner.
While prior work has extensively identified the existential and technical concerns arising from
the personal creator-AI dyad, providing crucial insights for designing GenAI systems for individual
creators, the emerging context where GenAI’s role expands to that of a facilitator and mediator
for collaboration between heterogeneous experts remains largely underexplored. This shift in role
may require our inquiry to expand, allowing us to revisit established concerns in this new context
and to discover new ones that were previously unaddressed [7, 20, 50]. Specifically, how creators
perceive this new role—and what relational and procedural concerns they experience within their
collaborative practices—remains a significant gap in the literature. This study, therefore, aims to
fill this gap by empirically investigating the specific nature of concerns that arise when GenAI
functions as a facilitator and mediator in a heterogeneous creative environment.
2.3
Co-design Method for User-Centered GenAI
The Co-design method is one of the most effective approaches for designing digital technologies
from the end-user’s perspective [43, 51, 87, 108]. By involving users from the initial design stages,
this approach helps in revealing hidden problems and discovering user-centered solutions. Solu-
tions designed based on users’ domain insights help users more easily accept and adapt to new
technologies [86, 91].
With the integration of AI into systems, the co-design method has faced new challenges, such as
AI’s unpredictability [64, 105] and the difficulty of aligning AI technology with human needs [76].
In response, the HCI community has focused on helping users better understand AI’s capabilities
and ideate ways to integrate it into their workflows [18, 35, 49, 57, 72]. Recently, one study built
upon this research to design co-design sessions that prompted domain experts to devise AI-driven
ideas for solving problems in their creative processes using stimuli [51]. This study utilized four
types of stimulus materials to help participants successfully generate appropriate AI ideas.
Inspired by this research, we structured our co-design activities, but our methodology diverges
in two significant ways. First, many widely used stimuli in previous work were developed with
predictive or analytical AI in mind [4, 34, 106, 110], and are thus limited in addressing the unique
expressive and interactive modalities of today’s generative AI. Second, as previously discussed,
existing approaches primarily focus on AI that assists individual task performance, meaning there
is scarcely an established process for co-designing a generative AI that interacts and mediates
within a collaborative context.

This study devised and executed a participatory design process to guide participants in under-

We provided ‘GenAI Capability Cards,’ a set of stimuli updated for the generative paradigm. We
also designed ‘Creative Prompting Sessions’ to allow participants to explore the potential of GenAI
across their entire workflow by experiencing interaction points with state-of-the-art GenAI tools.
The stimulus materials we proposed were as follows: (i) GenAI Capability Cards: Cards to help
participants easily understand GenAI features, (ii) Interventions for GenAI Concept Formation,
(iii) Creative Prompting Sessions: Sessions designed for participants to experience and understand
interaction points by using the latest GenAI tools in a complete workflow, and (iv) A template
for organizing AI feature and task ideas: A series of questions to help creators articulate both
the positive effects and the concerns associated with their ideas [67]. Through this structured co-
design process, we not only enabled participants to propose user-centered ideas but also effectively
captured the nuanced concerns and collaboration-specific design requirements that arise when
GenAI intervenes as a mediator in creative collaboration.

METHOD
3.1
Positionality Statement
Our author team consists of researchers with academic training and research experience in design,
human–AI interaction, and responsible AI, as well as diverse expertise in AI design and development
practices. Two authors have conducted extensive research on human–AI interaction design and
responsible AI, drawing on practical experience with user concerns and experiences around the
creative use of AI. Another author has research experience in building and designing AI systems as
creativity-support tools. These backgrounds and experiences inform our positionality and shape
the subjectivities inherent in our research questions, study design, participant recruitment, and
data analysis.
3.2
Overview of the Multi-Method Study
We first conducted preliminary interviews with four narrative writers and four visual designers to
understand collaboration in early game development. These sessions revealed differences in their
creative processes and provided guidance for structuring our study. Building on these insights, we
designed a creative-prompting session that integrated GenAI into creators’ workflows. We then
ran a participatory design study with 14 creators to examine major collaboration barriers and AI’s
potential roles. The findings highlight how GenAI can support creativity and efficiency, enhance
communication across roles, and surface concerns around its use in creative workflows.
3.3
Participants
We aimed to recruit creators who have substantial experience in narrative production or concept art
in the game industry based on the following criteria: (i) individuals aged 19 or older, (ii) narrative
writers or visual designers with at least one year of experience in the game industry, (iii) individuals
who have participated in at least one game development project, and (iv) for narrative writers,
at least three years of writing experience, and for visual designers, at least three years of design
experience.
The study recruited participants based in South Korea. We employed a purposive sampling
strategy supplemented with snowball sampling. Purposive sampling was necessary because our
study required participants with professional experience in narrative writing or visual design
for games, ensuring that participants could meaningfully reflect on collaboration practices and
evaluate the role of GenAI in their workflows. Snowball sampling was additionally used to access

Preliminary Interviews
We conducted preliminary interviews with four narrative writers and four visual designers. These
interviews were conducted as individual, one-on-one sessions. The purpose of these interviews was
to gain a deep understanding of the individual workflows and collaborative processes of writers and
designers, with the goal of designing a participatory design study process that reflects their creative

workflows. The interviews lasted one hour, and the questions were focused on their personal and
collaborative production processes and their experiences with GenAI tools. All interviews were
recorded via Zoom and transcribed using Clova Note6 and DaGlo, with cited quotes translated into
English.
Using qualitative thematic analysis [11], three researchers independently coded the transcripts,
resolving discrepancies through team discussions for consistency. The codes were then iteratively
organized into themes that informed the production process and insights for participatory design
study. These interviews provided critical insights into creators’ production processes (Section 4.1)
and guided the structure and planning of the participatory design study to align with participants’
preferences and work styles. Key insights for shaping the participatory design study are summarized
as follows:
First, we found that creators with limited experience of using AI often struggled to envision
how AI could be integrated into their collaborative processes. To address this, we incorporated a
‘creative-prompting’ activity into the participatory design study, allowing participants to explore
the latest GenAI tools within the context of their own work.
To further support participants with limited experience using GenAI, we developed tutorial
materials after the preliminary interviews, demonstrating step by step how to integrate ChatGPT-4o
and MidJourney Alpha into their workflows. These tutorials were specifically tailored based on
the insights gained from the preliminary interviews, ensuring that participants could easily follow
along and replicate the steps. After the interview, the tutorials were provided in various formats,
including text, comic, and video to accommodate participants’ preferred learning styles and to offer
flexibility in their engagement. Consistently, participants found the video format to be the most
accessible and effective, aligning with research suggesting that video-based learning materials are
more effective than text-based materials for information delivery and comprehension [65]. Based
on these findings, we decided to offer instructional tutorials on ChatGPT-4o, MidJourney Alpha,
and prompt creation in video format.
Furthermore, the study revealed that writers, especially those with prior experience using
MidJourney, generally prefer text-based communication over GUI-based tools. Based on this insight,
we chose to focus solely on ChatGPT for writers in the main study.
Importantly, the preliminary interviews also revealed that early-stage collaboration between
narrative writers and visual designers is often remote and asynchronous rather than conducted
through real-time, co-located collaboration. Participants described working independently on
their respective artifacts (e.g., narrative documents or concept images) and exchanging these
outputs through remote channels such as shared documents, messaging tools, or email, rather
than engaging in continuous joint co-creation sessions. Several participants highlighted challenges
arising from this collaboration structure, including temporal delays, limited opportunities for
real-time clarification, and difficulties in conveying creative intent across roles.
Based on these insights, we deliberately designed the creative-prompting sessions as individual
sessions to reflect creators’ existing collaborative practices and to examine how GenAI could
mediate collaboration across separated roles and asynchronous workflows, rather than assuming
synchronous, paired co-creation that does not reflect participants’ everyday work contexts.

writers and visual designers, stemming from the unique characteristics of game production and
their differing areas of expertise. Based on the insights gathered from these interviews, we then

the activities, and the procedures. To build rapport and mutual understanding, participants were

Phase 2: Completing Work Through AI Tool Prompting (40 min): Based on the preliminary
interview, we designed a ‘creative-prompting’ session to help participants imagine how AI could
be integrated into their workflow (See Figure 2, bottom). Before the session began, researchers

practice for using GenAI systems. Participants then created their own prompts, with researchers
actively encouraging questions and open communication. These efforts were intended to minimize
confusion during the prompt creation phase.
During the session, first, the narrative writer closely collaborated with ChatGPT-4o to create
a narrative, writing a game narrative document for the character and generating a simple main
character image. Then, to simulate the process in which an designer would ask questions via email
or in meetings about the game narrative document, the writer shared the conversation log with
ChatGPT-4o to the designer. The designer could then ask follow-up questions to ChatGPT-4o within
the conversation log shared by the writer to resolve any uncertainties. Afterward, the designer used
MidJourney alpha to visually develop the main character based on their understanding. This process
provided participants with practical experience of how AI could be applied not only in individual
work but also in collaborative settings. Unlike previous studies [90] that utilized participatory
prompting—a method in which researchers craft prompts by interacting with AI, present the outputs
to users for feedback, and iteratively refine the prompts based on that feedback—our study takes
a novel approach. We empower creators to independently craft their own prompts and directly
integrate them into their creative workflows. Example prompts created by participants are provided
in Appendix A.
Phase 3: Evaluating Challenges and Sharing Experiences (10 min): Two researchers partic-
ipated in the activities—one as the facilitator and the other as the note-taker, whose role was to
document the challenges discussed by participants. Based on the challenges identified during Phases
1 and 2 within each session, we constructed a survey that included an inclusive list of collaboration
challenges raised in that session. Accordingly, the survey content varied across sessions rather than
relying on a fixed or predefined challenge set. Through a self-evaluation questionnaire, participants
selected the most important 4-5 challenges they personally faced. From these participant-selected
challenges, the researchers then chose two key challenges to frame collaborative reflection activities,
which were presented as missions. Participants shared their experiences and reflections on why
these challenges were significant, generating insights and inspiration for subsequent analysis.
Phase 4: Designing Solutions to Address the Challenges (40 min): Participants were provided
with GenAI capability cards, an introduction to the latest GenAI tools, and an idea summary
template to assist them in devising solutions (See Appendix B). The GenAI capability cards, inspired
by prior research [106], were designed to help participants understand GenAI technology and
support brainstorming. These cards were created based on existing studies [70, 95, 103], making it
easier for participants with limited GenAI background knowledge to imagine its functions through
everyday language and visual materials. During the experiment, the GenAI cards were presented in
the form of PowerPoint slides. Each function and example of GenAI was explained individually, and
participants were encouraged to either build on the cards provided or freely ideate about additional
GenAI capabilities not included in the cards. Participants used these slides as a reference while
brainstorming ideas.
Additionally, examples of the latest GenAI tools were introduced to encourage participants to
explore new possibilities, even with GenAI features they had not previously experienced. Finally,
participants utilized the Insight-Action-Outcome idea summary template to reflect on the insights
gained from challenging situations, define the actions AI could take to address these challenges, and
organize specific solutions in a structured manner. They were also asked to consider and discuss
potential side effects of their proposed solutions.

3.6
Data Analysis
We recorded our participatory design study sessions via Zoom and transcribed the audio using
Clova Note6 and DaGlo. All cited quotes were translated into English. We analyzed the data using a
reflexive thematic analysis approach [11]. In the initial open coding stage, three researchers carefully
read the transcripts, took notes, and coded the data. Each researcher independently generated
initial codes from participants’ accounts. This process resulted in 538 codes. Each transcript was
reviewed by at least two researchers, and the team held regular meetings to compare insights and
discuss emerging ideas.
Through an iterative process, we clustered codes into higher-level themes related to collaboration
challenges, clarifying the difficulties and needs in collaborative work. We also analyzed design
solutions, categorizing them into high-level themes based on their purposes and the specific
challenges they addressed. For instance, one initial code noted: “D4 suggested that a conversational
AI that understands and retrieves information from documents uploaded by writers would be
highly helpful, reducing the need to ask writers directly and easing psychological burdens.” Such
codes were grouped into broader, overarching themes—for example, “Facilitating Information
Accessibility and Mediation through GenAI.” We further identified recurring patterns reflecting
concerns about integrating GenAI into collaborative work.
The research team held regular collaborative sessions whenever new codes emerged, focusing
on discussing their meaning, scope, and relationships to other codes. When particular excerpts
allowed for multiple possible interpretations, the researchers returned to the original transcripts
to re-examine how participants framed their experiences in context. Interpretive ambiguities or
disagreements were addressed through these ongoing discussions by refining code definitions or
adjusting boundaries between codes. The rationale for these analytic decisions was documented in
analytic memos, through which themes were iteratively refined and interpretive differences were
reconciled. Following the principles of reflexive thematic analysis, we did not calculate inter-rater
reliability, as collaborative interpretation and repeated team discussions were central to generating
codes and themes [11, 74].
Finally, this process generated 65 first-level themes, which we clustered into 22 second-level
themes and then synthesized into 3 third-level themes. These three overarching themes capture: (i)
challenges in creative work, (ii) corresponding design solutions, and (iii) the creators’ perceptions
and concerns of using GenAI in creative collaboration. We present these themes in detail below
and summarize them in Table 2.

FINDINGS
We divided the findings into three main sections: (1) the creative process and collaboration between
narrative writers and visual designers, (2) the challenges within these collaborative processes and
design suggestions for GenAI tools to address them, and (3) creators’ concerns about GenAI’s
intervention in creative work and collaboration. We present the challenges and design suggestions
(Section 4.2) in alignment with the production process so that the themes follow the actual sequence
of creators’ workflows. We then explain broader concerns about integrating GenAI into creative
collaboration across all stages of the workflow (Section 5).
4.1

To create GenAI tools that support the creative production and collaborative production processes
between narrative writers and visual designers, it is essential to first explore the production process

in the early stages of game development. Through our analysis of the preliminary interview, we
organized the early stages into three key levels:
4.1.1
Narrative Construction Phase. In the initial phase, the narrative writer generates foundational
story ideas and preliminary settings (e.g., game universe, characters, and backgrounds) aligned
with the game’s genre and core concepts. The writer refines the narrative direction and outlines
key elements, laying the groundwork to enable clear communication and effective collaboration
with visual designers in subsequent stages.
4.1.2
Game Narrative Document Communication Phase. In this phase, the narrative writer translates
the developed story and its key elements into a detailed Game Narrative Document. This document
includes concise descriptions of abstract visual elements and is supplemented with relevant reference
images sourced online. Its purpose is to ensure the visual designer clearly understands the intended
narrative, mood, characters, and visual style, minimizing the risk of misinterpretation and guiding
visualization.
4.1.3
Visual Concept Art Creation Phase. In the final collaborative stage, the visual designer creates
visual concept drafts based on the Game Narrative Document. The designer clarifies any ambiguous
aspects through direct communication with the narrative writer, while independently gathering
background knowledge and visual references to maintain historical and thematic accuracy. Typically,
the designer prepares several rough drafts, iteratively refining them through feedback from the
narrative writer. Once a final draft is selected, the designer meticulously details the artwork by
defining composition, outlines, colors, lighting, shading, and intricate visual textures.
4.2
Challenges and Design Opportunities in Creative Workflow and Collaboration
In this section, we outline the challenges narrative writers and visual designers encounter during
each stage of their production process. For each stage, we provide tailored design suggestions
for future GenAI collaborative tools to address these challenges. Our following insights not only
present these challenges but also demonstrate how each design opportunity directly addresses
specific challenges and collaboration asymmetries. This mapping between challenges and design
opportunities is illustrated in detail in Figure 3.
4.2.1
Challenges in Narrative Construction Phase.
Challenges in Ideation and Cognitive Load during Narrative Development. In the prepara-
tion phase for collaborative ideation, writers face intensive ideation demands as they attempt to
align story concepts with gameplay mechanics and player experience. While many ideas emerge
from abstract and personal sources of inspiration, these ideas often remain unstable and insuffi-
ciently grounded in shared genre conventions, target audiences, or gameplay constraints. As a
result, early ideation frequently fails to produce concrete, shareable reference points that collabora-
tors can easily align around. Moreover, writers must repeatedly generate, discard, and revise ideas
while also bearing the burden of externally structuring and documenting them for collaboration.
Spontaneous ideas are often lost or delayed before they can be formalized and shared, increasing
cognitive load and creating friction at the very entry point of collaboration.
Narrative Information Loss Undermining Collaborative Communication. Information
asymmetry often originates from narrative writers’ difficulty in consistently remembering and
managing extensive narrative details accumulated over time. As a result, narrative documents may
contain incomplete or inaccurate information from the outset, leaving designers to work with
gaps or inconsistencies. Such narrative information loss significantly undermines consistency and
creates confusion in later collaboration stages. W5 explained, "There are so many different settings,
and it’s easy to forget or struggle to remember all the details. So, I have to keep separate records in

Design Suggestions.
• Enhancing Collaborative Ideation through Genre-Aligned GenAI Insights. Participants
noted that collaborative ideation often breaks down when early ideas are not clearly aligned
with the game’s genre, target audience, and gameplay mechanics. To address this, they
envisioned GenAI as a mediator that could provide genre- and gameplay-aligned ideas,
helping narrative writers and visual designers align around a shared starting point. For
example, W4 stated, “It would be great to have an AI that can develop detailed ideas based on
the unique story rules (plots) and character attributes specific to genres such as fantasy, sci-fi,
or martial arts.” Similarly, W3 discussed first-person shooter (FPS) games, suggesting that
GenAI could propose combat scenarios set in environments with natural cover, such as ruins
or dense forests, to support strategic gameplay, while deliberately avoiding locations with
limited tactical depth, such as open plains.
• Structuring and Sharing Real-time Ideas through Conversational GenAI Assistance.
Participants described a breakdown at the entry point of collaboration, where spontaneous
ideas are often lost or delayed because they must be individually structured and documented
before being shared. To intervene in this breakdown, they envisioned conversational GenAI
that transforms fleeting ideas into minimally structured drafts that can be immediately shared

4.2.3
Challenges in Narrative Document Communication and Collaborative Interaction.
Knowledge Transfer Gaps between Writers and Designers. In the game narrative document
creation and communication phase, there is the asymmetry of knowledge and expertise between
narrative writers and visual designers. Therefore, the game narrative document should be crafted
to help visual designers understand the core elements of the narrative from multiple perspectives,
enabling them to better interpret and visualize the narrative. D6 noted, “Not only the visual elements
but also comprehensive information such as personality and backstory must be provided to create a
lifelike character design.” However, narrative writers, being less familiar with design, often fail to
convey the necessary details for visualization effectively. As a result, as seen in D4’s case, missing
information in the game narrative document is often filled in by the visual designer’s imagination.
This leads to situations where, as W3 expressed dissatisfaction, “The drafts that designers bring
always differ from the narrative writer’s original intention.”
To address this issue, narrative writers often search for reference images and attach them to the
game narrative document. However, finding images that clearly convey their intentions is nearly
impossible, often requiring compromises. D2, D3, and D6 argued that the reference images provided
by narrative writers are often insufficient to fully capture the tone and atmosphere of the overall
concept. W5 expressed frustration, saying, “If I could sketch, I’d be able to communicate my intent
more effectively, but lacking that ability, I always feel limited in conveying my ideas through stock
images.”
Communication Avoidance Stemming from Relationship and Work Style Differences.
Collaboration was frequently affected by asymmetries in relationships and work styles, including
hierarchical dynamics and differences in individual ways of working, which complicated commu-
nication across roles. Participants W6 and W7 pointed out that conflicts among team leaders or
hierarchical requests for revisions cause issues. Delays in communication due to strained team rela-
tionships can stall work progress, while top-down directives from decision-makers unfamiliar with
the relevant domain often blur work contexts, resulting in outputs that practitioners find difficult

Design Suggestions.
• Enhancing Visual Communication through Automated Narrative-to-Image Genera-
tion. Participants envisioned GenAI collaboration tools that could support narrative writers
in creating narrative documents by easily generating reference images from text-based ma-
terials, enabling more effective communication with designers. For instance, W1 expressed
a desire for GenAI to automatically identify sections of the game narrative document that
require visualization and generate relevant images. W3 added, “When searching for images or
doing research, I often use Pinterest, but it’s difficult to find something that exactly matches the
image in my mind. If GenAI could generate visuals based on the narrative document, I think
it could produce results closer to what I’ve imagined. This would make communication with
designers much smoother.” In response, W2 and W6 suggested a system that combines the
latest image generation tools with interactive agents. This system would enable GenAI to
engage in real-time conversations based on the game narrative document, identifying sec-
tions where additional information is needed for visualization and generating corresponding
images. It would also allow writers, even those unfamiliar with production tools, to refine
these GenAI-generated visuals based on their textual input.
• Mediating Knowledge Gaps and Enabling Information Access through GenAI. Partic-
ipants described GenAI as a mediator that could support collaborative communication by
reducing knowledge gaps and communication delays. In particular, visual designers wanted
GenAI to directly access narrative materials and provide task-relevant information in real
time. D4 emphasized, “Maintaining the continuity of creative work is crucial. If writers could
upload their documents to AI, a conversational interface enabling easy information retrieval
through text-based questions would be incredibly helpful. This way, we wouldn’t need to wait to
ask writers directly, reducing the psychological burden. If AI could take on that mediating role,
it would be extremely valuable.”

4.2.5
Challenges in Visual Concept Art Creation Phase.
The Dilemma of Worldbuilding Accu-
racy: The Endless Learning Journey of Visual Designers. Previous studies suggest that visual
designers often spend considerable time searching for terms used by writers [17]. Our study extends
this finding by showing that designers go beyond simple term searches, investing substantial effort
in understanding narrative contexts and historical details to accurately represent the world. This
highlights an asymmetry of knowledge and expertise: while narrative writers construct worlds
rich in historical and cultural references, visual designers must undertake additional learning to
visualize them faithfully. D2 noted, “I have learned about fantasy universes by watching YouTube
videos and movies.” Similarly, D6 described needing to research not only how medieval armor
was worn but also the intent behind its design. Despite these efforts, designers still worry about
maintaining historical accuracy, which they view as essential for producing high-quality games
and establishing a solid foundation for gameplay.
Drafting Under Pressure: Balancing Creativity, Writer’s Intent, and the Pitfall of Generic
Designs. In the visual concept art creation and communication phase, visual designers’ creativity
is often constrained by competing demands: they must faithfully reflect the writer’s intent and
maintain historical accuracy, while also producing compelling and original visuals that define the
game’s identity. This tension reflects an asymmetry of goals, as writers prioritize precise realization
of narrative intent, whereas designers emphasize creativity and aesthetic appeal. Under tight
deadlines and limited access to inspiring references, designers are often forced to compromise by
producing safe and generic drafts rather than exploring more innovative options. Visual designers
typically present multiple drafts to align on creative intent; as D3 noted, “The more drafts we
prepare, the higher the chance one will resonate with the writers.” However, W5 and W7 emphasized
that establishing a clear direction early in the drafting process often leads to better outcomes.

constrain the quality of concept art, particularly in character design, which plays a central role in
a game’s aesthetics and commercial success. Given the substantial time required for visual work,
designers often have little choice but to compromise quality or forego iterative revisions. Narrative

provide feedback on initial concepts, designers could quickly implement rough adjustments,
after which GenAI could immediately produce high-quality finalizations, thus minimizing
repeated rounds of feedback.

CREATORS’ CONCERNS ABOUT COLLABORATION WITH AI
Creators are not always enthusiastic about integrating GenAI to their creative process. From hearing
their perspectives, we learned that there are concerns about collaborating with GenAI. Creators do
not necessarily view GenAI positively, and there are several psychological barriers that need to be
addressed before they can truly recognize AI as a collaborator.
Concerns about Appearing Less Competent: Our study explored how creators perceive AI use
within collaborations among fellow creators, including their preferences regarding transparency
of AI involvement. Participants expressed particular concern that revealing their real-time col-
laboration process with AI could negatively impact their professional image. Consequently, they
preferred sharing only the final outputs rather than the entire collaborative process. Participant
W6 explained, “When sharing work with the design team, I usually only show the final results, not
the process. There is still prejudice against using AI, and I don’t see much benefit in exposing the
trial-and-error involved in creating a story. It feels more appropriate to summarize or organize the
AI conversations before sharing.” Similarly, participant D5 added, “Art is a process of continuously
developing one’s individuality and style over time. However, the perception that results can be produced
simply by clicking a button remains negatively viewed by other artists and creators.” Additionally,
participants expressed concerns that errors or mistakes caused by AI would ultimately reflect on
them, potentially damaging their professional reputation. Even if AI generates or significantly con-
tributes to the creative work, creators felt they would still be held responsible for any inaccuracies.
This situation places significant pressure on creators, as it could lead to negative evaluations of
their professional competence.
Concerns about Eroding Collaborative Trust among Creators: Participants emphasized
that the accuracy and reliability of information and visual materials provided by AI are critical
issues in collaborative creative work. They noted that when AI provides inaccurate or contextually
incorrect data related to story settings or character details, it can lead to confusion and trust issues
among creators. If creators uncritically accept AI-generated materials, incorrect information may
become embedded in the final outputs, potentially causing conflicts over accountability or reducing
overall trust in the AI itself. Participant W2 explained, “When AI provides incorrect outcomes, the
responsibility ultimately falls on me. Therefore, rather than blindly trusting AI outputs, I always need
to double-check them." Similarly, D4 expressed, “Honestly, incorrect information from AI can cause
significant confusion. It can even steer the entire team in the wrong direction, so I always remain
cautious when using AI-provided materials." Consequently, establishing a rapid verification process
for AI-generated content, along with transparent systems that clearly reveal the sources of AI
materials, is essential. These measures can enhance trust among creators, improve collaborative
efficiency, and ultimately contribute to the quality of creative outcomes.
Concerns about the Burden of Adapting to GenAI in Creative Collaboration: Creators
feel pressured to adapt to AI in the creative process to avoid obsolescence. D6 noted that AI
adoption adds the burden of learning new skills alongside creating art. This pressure is intensified
by reduced job opportunities, forcing creators to take on multiple roles. W2 highlighted that
prompt-writing skills in AI collaborations are overshadowing traditional skills like sketching. D4
and W4 emphasized that proficiency with AI tools significantly impacts the quality and diversity
of results, making mastery of these tools an increasingly heavy burden for creators.

replace their creative work, resulting in a sense of loss. Additionally, AI is seen as a barrier for
beginners, with D6 warning that it could eliminate opportunities for novice designers to start their

DISCUSSION
6.1
Strategies for Positioning GenAI as a Trusted Creative Collaborator
This section addresses ways to improve collaboration between creators using GenAI tools and help
creators perceive GenAI as a better partner. While previous studies emphasize the need to assist
creators in adopting GenAI positively, there is a lack of empirical research on specific concerns and
how to address them. Our study identifies the factors that hinder creators from accepting GenAI as
a collaborator and suggests ways to resolve these issues so that GenAI is perceived as a tool that
complements the creativity and capabilities of creators.
Personalized GenAI Companions for Protecting Creative Agency in Collaboration. Creators
expressed a desire to use personalized GenAI “companions” to protect their creative work and ensure
that their expertise is respected during collaboration with GenAI. Such systems are particularly
valuable when working with sensitive information or undisclosed projects, as they allow creators
to selectively share ideas and work outputs. For example, GenAI could be configured to share only
specific portions of work between collaborators. In a writer–designer collaboration, the designer
might refine a character’s visual design with GenAI, informed by discussions with the writer, while
choosing to share only selected design drafts and withholding early sketches or experimental
iterations. Beyond information protection, these practices reflect a broader desire to preserve
creative agency and emergent creativity in GenAI-mediated collaboration. We therefore caution
against overly intrusive GenAI intervention in collaborative creative processes. Prior work has
shown that ambiguity and partial understanding, rather than complete shared understanding, can
foster new interpretations and emergent outcomes. This suggests that excessively high levels of
coordination may paradoxically limit opportunities for unexpected ideas or novel interpretations
[39, 58]. Accordingly, future research should explore how GenAI can support shared understanding
without undermining the emergent processes central to creative collaboration, and how to strike
an appropriate balance between coordination and creative openness.
Designing GenAI as a Resilient Facilitator in Creative Collaboration. Prior research
[5, 12, 99, 101] has primarily emphasized the importance of persona customization in interac-
tions with GenAI within individual work contexts. In contrast, our study foregrounds the distinct
persona characteristics required in collaborative work settings. Our findings show that, in col-
laborative contexts, participants envisioned GenAI as a facilitator that supports communication
and mutual understanding among team members. This collaborative persona was expected to
provide clear and precise information, reduce misunderstandings, and enable smooth interaction.
Participants particularly emphasized the importance of a GenAI persona that is formal yet friendly,
maintaining neutrality while also demonstrating empathetic and attentive behavior. Furthermore,

participants expressed concerns that inaccurate GenAI outputs, when shared among collabora-

Promoting Communication and Collaboration with GenAI Tools. To prevent GenAI tools
from causing a reduction in communication among creators, GenAI should be utilized in ways
that supplement and encourage human collaboration and communication. Even when AI handles
automated tasks, it is crucial for team members to jointly review and discuss GenAI-generated
outcomes, thereby enhancing trust and interaction. Recent studies [2, 40, 45, 73] emphasize that
AI-generated ideas should pass through a human judgment ‘filter’ during the creative process,
highlighting the importance of human trust and control, particularly when AI serves collaborative
roles. While these studies primarily focused on individual interactions between humans and GenAI,
our research uniquely emphasizes that maintaining human agency is even more critical within
collaborative contexts involving multiple creators. We highlight that collective human judgment,
communication, and decision-making become especially significant when GenAI tools mediate
interactions among creators. For example, when Creator A sends a message to Creator B through
GenAI, the system could actively promote additional communication by prompting, “GenAI might
make mistakes. Would you like to directly confirm accuracy with Creator B?" In this way, GenAI
can serve not merely as a tool in the creative process but also as a facilitator that actively enhances
human collaboration and communication.
Supporting Creators’ Emotional Well-being and Collaborative Harmony. In collaborative
relationships, creators perceived AI not merely as a task assistant but rather as an emotional
companion that supports their emotional stability and psychological well-being. For instance,
AI could function as an emotional curator, sensing creators’ psychological states (e.g., stress or
reduced confidence) and assisting them in maintaining an optimal creative mindset. GenAI could
recognize moments of stress and help creators regain emotional balance by reminding them of
past achievements or highlighting positive feedback they’ve previously received. Additionally,
during collaborative processes, when uncomfortable emotions or tensions arise between creators,
GenAI could act as a neutral and amicable mediator, helping resolve misunderstandings, facilitating
smoother communication, and alleviating interpersonal conflicts.
6.2
Design Implications for GenAI: Enhancing Collaboration in Creative Teams
In a prior study, AI was perceived as a central component of a collaborative ecosystem where users,
technologies, and organizational structures interact [62]. Our study finds that GenAI is increasingly
positioned not only as a tool for producing creative outputs, but also as a mediator that enhances
interaction between creators.
Through our study, we identified three key functions that GenAI could perform to enable more
effective collaboration between creators from different domains: (1) supporting creative workflows
to enhance communication opportunities, (2) augmenting missing or unclear information in real
time, and (3) providing psychological stability by anticipating and resolving potential conflicts.
First, participants expected GenAI to flexibly switch between an assistant role for individual
creators and a mediator role for inter-creator collaboration. We envision a system in which GenAI

operates across two interconnected spaces: a private space for individual ideation and a shared
space for collaboration. In the private space, creators work one-on-one with GenAI while the
system accumulates contextual data to understand creators’ intentions. In the shared space, this
context enables collaboration—for example, allowing a visual designer to query a narrative writer’s
creative intent. GenAI mediates semantic gaps by translating individualized creative intent into
shared understanding.
Second, GenAI can detect and clarify missing or ambiguous information that commonly arises
between creators from different disciplines. Interviews revealed that even when documents are
shared, background details or visual cues are often underspecified, leading to confusion or misin-
terpretation. In such cases, GenAI could proactively prompt clarification, for example: “This scene
lacks specific visual details. Would you like to consider referencing 19th-century French urban
architecture?” By identifying and resolving underspecified content, GenAI functions as an active
information facilitator that supports accurate interpretation and reduces misunderstanding.
Third, GenAI can help maintain psychological safety and mitigate conflict by identifying early
signs of tension during collaboration. Participants described challenges stemming from hierarchical
directives, hesitation to challenge others’ expertise, or mismatched communication styles. As a
neutral communication buffer, GenAI can prompt creators to provide additional context, invite
feedback, or notify collaborators of impactful changes (e.g., “This request may conflict with the
current visual direction. Would you like to provide additional context or invite feedback?”). In
doing so, GenAI helps alleviate interpersonal strain and fosters a more emotionally supportive
collaborative environment.
Based on this insight, we propose a user interface concept we term “Dual-Space GenAI.” In this
model, the GenAI platform features two distinct yet connected modes: a Personal Studio and a
Shared Workshop.
Personal Studio is a private, sandboxed environment where creators interact one-on-one with
GenAI. A key feature is the Contextual Memory toggle, which, when enabled, allows GenAI to
record creators’ decision-making processes, discarded ideas, and stylistic preferences as structured
metadata. For example, if a writer explores multiple character backstories before selecting one,
the GenAI logs this process. Prior to sharing, GenAI can also proactively identify potentially
ambiguous expressions and suggest refinements into clearer, collaboration-friendly language to
reduce misunderstanding. Shared Workshop is the collaborative space. When a creator shares an
artifact (e.g., a story draft) from the Studio to the Workshop, they are prompted with Granular
Context-Sharing Controls, enabling them to share not only the final artifact but also selected
elements of the associated Contextual Memory. A collaborator can then query the GenAI about
prior creative considerations (e.g., alternative backstories), and the system can respond by explaining
the creator’s reasoning. This approach moves beyond simple file sharing toward the mediated
transfer of creative intent, directly addressing the semantic gaps identified in our findings.
To realize the Dual-Space GenAI concept, future research must address challenges on two fronts:
core system implementation and effective creator use. On the system side, this includes structuring
nuanced, unstructured creative-process data into a queryable knowledge representation, paired with
robust protections for privacy and intellectual property. On the interaction side, it requires designing
intuitive interfaces that provide granular control over sharing sensitive contextual information,
while developing non-intrusive AI mediation models that enhance collaboration without disrupting
creative flow.
Our study derives these insights from the game development domain by examining collabo-
ration between heterogeneous creators with distinct roles, namely designers and writers. While
grounded in this specific context, our findings are not domain-bound; rather, they offer broader
implications for the CSCW community concerned with AI-mediated collaboration. In particular,

6.3
Takeaways from a Participatory Design Study Using Creative Prompting
In this study, we employed the Participatory Design approach, widely recognized in the HCI
community [14, 51, 108], to collaboratively design GenAI concepts supporting effective collaboration
among creators with different expertise. Here, we highlight key insights from our participatory
design study to inform future research and practice on GenAI-supported collaborations.
Through initial observations and interviews with GenAI prompting, participants concretely
understood how GenAI could impact their collaborative work. We found preliminary interviews or
literature reviews essential for participants to accurately contextualize their collaborative challenges,
enhancing their engagement during subsequent exploration activities.
Structured missions, guided by insights from preliminary interviews, allowed participants un-
familiar with GenAI to directly envision practical enhancements to their collaborative processes.
Hands-on experiences through simple prompting activities and immediate feedback from GenAI sig-
nificantly deepened their understanding, inspiring innovative ideas previously considered abstract
or unattainable.
Effective participant engagement with GenAI required thorough preparation and proactive
facilitator support. Although pre-session educational materials (e.g., instructional videos) were
provided, facilitators’ active real-time assistance, such as answering questions, guiding demonstra-
tions, and encouraging screen-sharing, was indispensable for building participants’ confidence and
proficiency within limited session times.
Additionally, providing diverse and stimulating materials enriched participants’ creative ideation.
We developed generative AI function cards, practical intervention examples, and concise idea
summary sheets. Function cards specifically enabled participants unfamiliar with GenAI to easily
conceptualize features using everyday language and visuals. Introducing advanced GenAI capabili-
ties beyond the tools directly used by participants (e.g., ChatGPT, Midjourney) further expanded
their creative explorations, prompting broader and more innovative ideas.
Finally, clearly structured guidelines helped participants balance the strengths and potential
concerns of GenAI-based solutions. Encouraging comprehensive reflection on both benefits and
ethical or practical limitations facilitated realistic and practical outcomes. Creating an empathetic
environment where participants openly discussed real-world collaboration challenges was also
essential in identifying meaningful opportunities for GenAI support.
Collectively, these methodological and practical insights confirmed the effectiveness of co-design
sessions for domain experts to generate realistic and practical GenAI solutions. These findings
can broadly inform Participatory Design (PD) processes involving diverse creator groups, such as
engineers, designers, and artists, fostering deeper understanding of their collaborative challenges
and potential technology-supported solutions. Ultimately, collaboration tools bridging creators and
GenAI can significantly enhance creative effectiveness across diverse creative domains.

LIMITATIONS
We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, as the research focused on game writers
and designers in Korea, the findings may not fully represent the broader population or creators
from other regions, whose characteristics and practices might differ. Additionally, the participants

primarily consisted of designers and writers aged 20 to 50, with no representation from creators
over 60. This demographic limitation may have influenced the results, particularly as the materials
provided could present challenges for those with limited technological literacy. Future research
will aim to address these limitations by including creators from diverse cultural backgrounds and
age groups, as well as those with minimal prior experience with AI tools, to develop more inclusive
and comprehensive solutions.
Second, the generative AI capability cards and examples of interventions presented to the
participants in this study may have been limited to what currently exists. While the study provided
guidance to encourage more creative thinking beyond the AI capability cards and interventions,
we also used ChatGPT-4 and MidJourney in the participants’ prompt-writing process. Although
these tools represent some of the best technology we are aware of, AI is continuously evolving,
and new technologies are being introduced. Therefore, ongoing research into AI stimuli that adapt
to these changes will be necessary.
Third, this study focused on single sessions with individual participants within a short timeframe,
allowing for an in-depth exploration of their experiences and insights. However, the breadth of
insights that could be gained from iterative idea development over an extended timescale, involving
multiple sessions and diverse stakeholders, may have been limited. Drawing on previous studies
that observed creator-AI interactions over longer periods [68], future research plans to expand the
scope by incorporating multi-session co-design approaches with a variety of stakeholders, aiming
to derive richer and more comprehensive insights.
Fourth, this study focused on the remote collaboration process between two creators, as pre-study
findings indicated that this approach was more commonly used and uncovered more challenges.
While the study provided valuable insights into how GenAI can support this process, it may not
have fully addressed the collaborative dynamics and challenges of creators working with AI in a
shared workspace. Future research is needed to explore emerging trends and tackle the unique
challenges of shared work environments.
Lastly, we found that some challenges, such as deep organizational issues or interpersonal
conflicts, may be difficult to resolve solely through GenAI and may require human-centered
interventions. However, this study focused on exploring the possibilities and opportunities of GenAI,
with an emphasis on identifying how GenAI can be applied to enhance collaboration. Through
this approach, we uncovered a wide range of promising opportunities for GenAI to facilitate
future collaborative processes. That said, it is important to remain cautious of AI universalism,
recognizing that not all problems can be solved by GenAI alone. For challenges that require
non-GenAI approaches, we plan to address them in future research endeavors.

CONCLUSION
Our study demonstrates that Generative AI (GenAI) tools have great potential not only to sup-
port individual creative work but also to enhance collaboration between creators. We conducted
preliminary interviews with game narrative writers and visual designers to understand their
collaboration processes, and then applied a multi-method approach combining semi-structured
interviews and a participatory design study. Through the semi-structured interviews, we identified
multi-dimensional challenges and asymmetries that span the entire creation workflow, encompass-
ing from preparation phases to interactive co-creation processes of working together. To address
these, we facilitated the participatory design study. During these sessions, we employed creative
prompting and various stimuli based on the work processes of narrative writers and visual de-
signers to derive design solutions tailored to their needs. Through the design sessions, we found
that GenAI tools can help creators manage their stress with emotional connection and organize
ideas through a vocal assistant, anytime and anywhere. Additionally, we found that GenAI has the

In contrast, our research also revealed concerns about the use of AI in collaboration. Beyond
practical worries such as privacy and reduced communication, participants raised relational and
procedural concerns about how GenAI might reshape interpersonal dynamics and decision-making

conclusion, while GenAI tools hold significant potential to enhance collaboration between creators,
careful design is necessary to realize this potential. In summary, this study contributes to a deeper

design opportunities for GenAI to function as a facilitator and mediator that supports—rather than
replaces, human creativity and values. At the same time, our findings highlight critical concerns
that must be addressed in future system design, offering balanced guidance for developing GenAI
