# Beyond Federated Learning: Decentralized Agentic Systems for Self-Evolving Clinical Intelligence 

## Haoyuan Li, Mathias Funk and Aaqib Saeed 

Eindhoven University of Technology, Eindhoven, The Netherlands. *Corresponding author(s). E-mail(s): h.y.li@tue.nl; Contributing authors: M.Funk@tue.nl; a.saeed@tue.nl; 

Abstract 

Clinical expertise is inherently distributed: it resides not in any single dataset or institution but in the accumulated reasoning experiences of thousands of clinicians navigating diverse patient populations, resource constraints, and evolv-ing treatment protocols. Yet current approaches to scaling medical AI, from centralized large language models to federated learning, remain anchored to a data-centric paradigm that treats knowledge as static parameters to be aggregated. In this Perspective, we argue for a fundamental shift from data aggre-gation to experience aggregation . We propose Decentralized Agentic Systems (DAS), a framework in which distributed AI agents operating across healthcare institutions synthesize collective clinical intelligence by sharing abstracted rea-soning trajectories rather than raw data or model parameters. Unlike federated learning, which averages numerical gradients across homogeneous model archi-tectures, DAS exchanges structured records of clinical reasoning, specifically the sequence of observations, hypotheses, tool invocations, and outcome feedback that constitute diagnostic experience. We examine the architectural principles, deployment considerations, governance requirements, and critical open challenges of this framework, laying the groundwork for a collaborative medical AI ecosys-tem where distributed expertise autonomously adapts to the complexities of longitudinal patient care. 

Keywords: Agentic AI, Decentralized Learning, Digital Healthcare 

1arXiv:2610.35453v1 [cs.AI] 26 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Medicine is, at its core, a distributed enterprise. The expertise required to manage a complex patient rarely resides in a single clinician, department, or institution. A rural primary care physician develops pattern recognition for atypical presentations under resource constraints that an academic specialist never encounters; a community cardi-ologist accumulates experience with medication titration across diverse socioeconomic contexts; a home health monitoring system learns to distinguish clinically signifi-cant decompensation from benign fluctuations in daily vital signs. This distributed expertise, forged through thousands of diagnostic encounters across heterogeneous clinical environments, represents the most valuable and least utilized knowledge asset in healthcare. The rapid development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has contributed to growing interest in agentic Artificial Intelligence (AI), which seeks to extend AI sys-tems beyond static information retrieval toward perception, reasoning, and action in dynamic environments. Prior work has reported promising results in complex domains such as software engineering, mathematical theorem proving, and scientific hypothesis generation [1–4]. In healthcare, where clinical reasoning demands synthesizing hetero-geneous evidence under uncertainty, agentic AI promises to augment human expertise by orchestrating knowledge-intensive diagnostic and therapeutic workflows. Early applications of agentic AI in medicine established foundational approaches to algorithmic clinical reasoning. Systems such as those in [5–8] employ LLMs as centralized orchestrators, using modular decomposition to partition clinical queries into sub-tasks spanning evidence retrieval, differential diagnosis, and treatment rec-ommendation. While effective for localized decision support, these systems operate as isolated instances constrained by static reasoning templates and single-institution deployment. Recent work has advanced toward hierarchical multi-agent systems enabling dynamic collaboration for symptom assessment, diagnostic interpretation, and treatment recommendation, simulating multi-turn clinical consultations [9–13]. However, a fundamental architectural tension pervades current systems: clinical expertise is inherently distributed across institutions, specialties, and care settings, yet agentic AI remains stubbornly centralized. This tension is deeper than the well-known challenges of data fragmentation and privacy regulation, though it encompasses them. We identify three dimensions of this mismatch, each reflecting not merely a data problem but an expertise problem. 

Expertise fragmentation problem. 

The conventional framing of healthcare data silos emphasizes regulatory barriers to data sharing. Though real, this framing obscures a more fundamental issue: even removing regulatory barriers would not pool clinical expertise . The diagnostic insight a rural emergency physician develops managing acute coronary syndrome without catheterization access is not encoded in the EHR; it resides in the physician’s reason-ing process and outcomes observed. A centralized system trained on pooled records acquires statistical patterns but not the situated reasoning constituting clinical mas-tery. The challenge is therefore not merely to share data across institutions but to share the experience of navigating clinical complexity. 2Scaling limits of static knowledge. 

The advancement of LLMs has been predicated on access to ever-larger corpora of human-generated text, yet recent analyses suggest this paradigm is approaching asymptotic limits as high-quality medical data becomes increasingly scarce [14, 15]. In healthcare, where rare conditions, evolving treatment protocols, and longitudinal patient trajectories demand continuous learning, reliance on static datasets creates systems that are perpetually outdated. More critically, static training data captures 

declarative medical knowledge such as facts, guidelines, and associations, but not the 

procedural knowledge of how to apply it under the uncertainty and time pressure of clinical practice. 

Sovereignty-utility tension. 

Autonomous agents handling Protected Health Information (PHI) must comply with HIPAA, IVDR, and GDPR [16–18], yet compliance is only one dimension of a broader sovereignty challenge. Healthcare institutions increasingly assert governance over not only their data but their institutional knowledge (i.e., clinical protocols, reasoning con-ventions, and accumulated expertise) as competitive and intellectual assets. Current architectures offer institutions a binary choice: contribute data to centralized systems or remain isolated. What is needed is an architecture that enables institutions to con-tribute to and benefit from collective intelligence while retaining sovereignty over their clinical assets , sharing insights without surrendering the experiences from which those insights derive. These challenges are compounded by the computational realities of deployment. State-of-the-art agents rely on multi-model toolchains: EchoAgent [19] orchestrates domain-specific vision models for echocardiogram interpretation, while Echo-CoPilot [20] leverages EchoPrime [21] and PanEcho [22] for multi-view cardiac analysis. Cen-tralizing such systems incurs substantial inference costs [23], while naive distribution faces severe infrastructure heterogeneity. Such converging pressures cannot be addressed by existing distributed learn-ing paradigms. Federated Learning (FL), the prevailing privacy-preserving approach, aggregates model parameters through gradient averaging, operating at the wrong level of abstraction for clinical expertise. Gradients encode statistical correlations rather than reasoning strategies, require architectural homogeneity, and provide no mechanism for transferring the procedural knowledge characterizing clinical mastery. In this Perspective, we propose Decentralized Agentic Systems (DAS) as an alter-native paradigm: a framework wherein distributed agents operating across healthcare institutions synthesize collective clinical intelligence through the sharing of abstracted reasoning trajectories, while concurrently self-evolving through the reciprocal incor-poration of network-wide experiential knowledge into local reasoning capabilities. The transition toward decentralized agentic architectures represents a necessary evolu-tion aligned with fundamental shifts in how AI acquires knowledge. LLMs’ progress has been predicated on scaling human-curated datasets, a paradigm approaching asymp-totic limits as high-quality clinical data grows scarce [14, 15]. In healthcare, this constraint is particularly acute: medical knowledge is continuously generated through diagnostic encounters, treatment adaptations, and clinical judgments across thou-sands of institutions, accessible only in part to any centralized system. We argue that the next frontier lies in enabling AI agents to learn directly from distributed clinical experiences, transforming ephemeral diagnostic interactions into durable, shareable intelligence. Experience differs fundamentally from data. Data represents static records, lab-oratory values, imaging findings, documented diagnoses. Experience, by contrast, encompasses the dynamic process through which an agent perceives clinical signals, reasons about their significance under uncertainty, selects among possible interven-tions, and observes outcomes [24]. Consider the difference between a dataset of electrocardiograms labeled with diagnoses and the experience of an agent that detects a subtle ST-elevation pattern, retrieves relevant patient history, considers confound-ing factors, and refines its interpretation based on downstream clinical confirmation. The former provides declarative knowledge, facts about the world; the latter generates procedural knowledge, that is, how to navigate clinical reasoning itself. Each institutional agent continuously generates experiential feedback through clinical interactions. A cardiology-focused agent may develop nuanced expertise in distinguishing Takotsubo cardiomyopathy from acute coronary syndrome in post-menopausal women; a primary care agent may learn to recognize atypical thyroid dysfunction in elderly populations. Such experiences represent the contextual, pro-cedural knowledge unavailable in static datasets. The challenge lies in synthesizing this distributed intelligence into collective clinical wisdom without violating data sovereignty constraints. To address this, we propose DAS as a framework for agent-centric insight aggrega-tion. Unlike FL [25], which aggregates numerical model parameters through gradient averaging, DAS operates at a higher abstraction level: the sharing of semantic tra-jectories . We define a semantic trajectory as a structured record comprising an agent’s reasoning path: observations, hypotheses, tool invocations, and inferential steps, together with environmental feedback that validated or refuted its conclusions. Exchanging these traces rather than raw data or model weights enables knowledge transfer that is both privacy-conscious and semantically meaningful, forming the sub-strate through which individual agentic nodes continuously self-evolve: each agent refines its local reasoning capabilities by assimilating experiential knowledge distilled from across the distributed network.

Relationship to adjacent paradigms. 

DAS relates to, but differs from, several active research directions. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [31] enhances LLM reasoning by retrieving relevant documents; DAS can be viewed as a federated extension of RAG where the retrieval corpus consists of validated reasoning trajectories from distributed agents. Chain-of-thought distillation [32, 33] transfers reasoning via training on reasoning traces; DAS extends this from a teacher-student relationship to a network of peer agents shar-ing experiential knowledge. Experience replay in multi-agent reinforcement learning [34] enables learning from stored interactions; DAS adapts this to healthcare where experiences comprise clinical reasoning under real-world constraints. The aggregation of validated semantic trajectories enables what we term collective self-evolution : the emergence of shared diagnostic intelligence exceeding any individ-ual agent’s capabilities. This evolution occurs not through centralized retraining on pooled data but through continuous refinement of reasoning strategies based on cross-institutional experience. When an agent successfully navigates a novel presentation to correct a diagnosis, that trajectory becomes available network-wide, enriching the collective repertoire of clinical problem-solving once abstracted and validated. 

# 3 Architectural Foundations of Decentralized Agentic Systems 

We now formalize the DAS architecture. The central challenge is not merely distribut-ing computation but enabling meaningful knowledge synthesis across agents operating in heterogeneous clinical contexts: different patient populations, institutional pro-tocols, specialty focuses, and infrastructure capabilities. As illustrated in Figure 1, DAS addresses this through three architectural layers: distributed agentic nodes that generate clinical experiences locally, a semantic abstraction layer that transforms het-erogeneous experiences into transferable representations, and a collective intelligence layer that aggregates and redistributes insights across the agent network.

# 4 Governance, Regulation, and Deployment Realities 

The technical architecture of DAS is necessary but insufficient for real-world adoption. Healthcare AI systems operate within complex governance ecosystems involving insti-tutional policies, regulatory frameworks, professional liability structures, and patient trust relationships. We examine the non-technical dimensions that will determine whether decentralized agentic systems can transition from architectural concept to deployed clinical infrastructure. 

## 4.1 Regulatory Considerations 

Agentic AI systems in healthcare face an evolving regulatory landscape. In the United States, the FDA’s framework for Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) was designed for static algorithms with well-defined inputs and outputs, a paradigm poorly suited to agentic systems that reason dynamically and adapt over time [39]. Key open 11 questions include whether each institutional agent requires independent regulatory clearance or whether DAS can be evaluated as a system; how continuous learning from trajectory updates interacts with the FDA’s predetermined change control plan requirements; and what validation standards apply to collectively derived reasoning patterns emerging from network-level aggregation rather than explicit design. In the EU, the AI Act classifies most clinical decision-support systems as high-risk, requiring transparency, human oversight, and robustness testing. DAS creates novel compliance challenges: when reasoning reflects contributions from agents across mul-tiple member states, which jurisdiction governs? These questions must be confronted proactively with regulatory bodies.

Beyond formal regulation, DAS requires governance structures addressing participa-tion, liability, and reciprocity across three dimensions. 

Trajectory curation and quality control. 

Who decides which reasoning trajectories enter the collective knowledge base? Uncurated aggregation risks knowledge degradation (see Section 5.2), while overly restrictive curation may stifle the diversity that makes collective intelligence valuable. We envision hybrid models combining automated quality metrics (outcome validation, internal consistency, novelty assessment) with periodic expert review boards analogous to clinical peer review committees. 

Liability allocation. 

When a recommendation informed by cross-institutional trajectories leads to an adverse outcome, liability is nontrivial. Current frameworks struggle with distributed AI even in non-agentic contexts [40]. DAS liability models might draw on multi-site clinical guideline precedent, where contributing institutions share collective respon-sibility for quality while individual providers retain responsibility for application to specific patients. 

Participation incentives and fairness. 

Institutions must perceive sufficient benefit to justify participation costs, including computational resources, clinical time for quality validation, and governance compli-ance overhead. Free-rider problems, where institutions consume collective knowledge without contributing proportionally, can erode trust. Contribution-weighted access to collective intelligence and transparent accounting of trajectory utilization may help align incentives. Smaller or less-resourced institutions should not be systematically dis-advantaged; enabling rural and community hospitals to benefit from academic medical center expertise is a primary motivation for DAS. 

## 4.3 Integration with Clinical Infrastructure 

Practical deployment requires integration with EHR platforms. Interoperability stan-dards such as HL7 FHIR provide structured interfaces for clinical observations and decision support. However, institutional variation in EHR configuration, documenta-tion practices, and workflow integration means the operational context surrounding each agent differs substantially, affecting experience quality and applicability of received knowledge. We advocate beginning with standardized tasks: clinical risk scoring, medica-tion interaction checking, or diagnostic workup recommendations, before extending to complex reasoning, enabling iterative validation of trajectory sharing while building institutional trust through demonstrated value. 13 5 Technical Challenges and Open Research Directions 

The architecture of Section 3 provides foundational mechanisms for decentralized agen-tic collaboration, yet several substantial technical challenges remain unresolved, which we examine as follows. 

## 5.1 Experience Heterogeneity across Clinical Contexts 

Decentralized healthcare systems must reconcile experiences generated across radically different clinical environments. A diagnostic agent in a tertiary academic medical cen-ter encounters different patient populations, diagnostic modalities, and institutional protocols than one in a rural primary care clinic. This heterogeneity manifests across three dimensions. 

Statistical heterogeneity arises from differences in patient demographics and disease prevalence. An agent serving a pediatric oncology unit accumulates experiences with childhood malignancies of limited relevance to one in a geriatric care facility, and direct aggregation across such disparate populations risks distributional biases that degrade diagnostic accuracy. 

Semantic heterogeneity emerges from differences in clinical vocabulary, documen-tation practices, and reasoning conventions. The same finding may be described differently by cardiologists and internists; the same conclusion may be reached through different inferential pathways depending on institutional culture. Standardization efforts (as in Equation 5) must navigate the tension between abstraction sufficient for cross-context transfer and preservation of clinically meaningful nuance. 

Operational heterogeneity reflects differences in available tools, computational resources, and workflow integration. An agent with access to advanced imaging analy-sis generates fundamentally different trajectories than one limited to laboratory values and clinical notes. The Semantic Abstraction Layer addresses heterogeneity through trajectory stan-dardization and utility-weighted aggregation. However, determining abstraction levels that enable transfer while preserving clinical validity remains empirically open, and the contribution weights wk must reflect source-to-target context relevance, requiring either explicit clinical metadata or learned representations of transferability. 

## 5.2 Hallucination Propagation and Byzantine Resilience 

Large language models exhibit well-documented tendencies toward hallucination [41– 43]. In agentic systems, this vulnerability extends to entire reasoning trajectories: an agent may construct internally coherent diagnostic pathways based on fabricated associations or nonexistent evidence, and when such trajectories enter the sharing mechanism they risk corrupting collective knowledge network-wide. This represents a novel form of the classical Byzantine failure problem [44, 45]. Agentic hallucination differs critically: hallucinated trajectories may be superficially plausible, evading detection mechanisms designed for obviously erroneous outputs; hallucination propensity may correlate across agents sharing similar architectures, 14 violating independence assumptions underlying Byzantine protocols; and clinical con-sequences, including incorrect diagnoses and inappropriate treatments, may be severe and difficult to reverse. The utility threshold in experience distillation (Equation 4) offers partial miti-gation but presupposes reliable utility assessment when ground truth outcomes may be delayed. More robust approaches might incorporate multi-agent verification or external validation against established clinical knowledge bases. The governance struc-tures in Section 4, particularly expert trajectory curation, provide a complementary non-technical defense. The optimal balance between automated and human-mediated quality control remains context-dependent, with high-stakes domains such as oncology warranting more stringent validation. 

## 5.3 Semantic Privacy Leakage from Abstracted Trajectories 

A foundational premise of decentralized learning is that sharing derived representa-tions preserves privacy. However, gradient inversion attacks have demonstrated that model updates can leak substantial information about training data [46, 47], enabling reconstruction of sensitive clinical images from federated exchanges [48, 49]. This vulnerability extends to trajectory sharing. A reasoning path comprising specific tool invocations, diagnostic queries, and decision sequences constitutes a behavioral fingerprint: even without explicit patient identifiers, trajectories query-ing rare disease databases or following condition-specific diagnostic pathways may reveal diagnoses, genetic predispositions, or lifestyle factors patients expect to remain confidential. The standardization function provides partial protection, but the tension between utility and privacy persists: sufficiently abstracted trajectories may lose the clinical specificity that makes them valuable. Developing formal privacy guarantees, poten-tially drawing on differential privacy frameworks adapted to structured reasoning representations, is an important open challenge. The appropriate tradeoff likely varies across contexts, suggesting the need for configurable mechanisms rather than uniform solutions. 

## 5.4 Coordination Complexity in Asynchronous Multi-Agent Systems 

DAS supports asynchronous operation, allowing agents to contribute trajectories and receive updates without synchronized participation. This flexibility is essential across institutions with different schedules and connectivity constraints, but introduces challenges for coherent collective intelligence. 

Temporal inconsistency arises when agents operate on different knowledge versions. Recently updated agents generate experiences reflecting current collective understand-ing, while those with stale knowledge produce trajectories based on outdated patterns, requiring careful versioning and potentially discounting stale inputs. 15 Feedback delays complicate credit assignment. Clinical outcomes validating or refuting diagnostic reasoning may emerge weeks or months after the initial interac-tion, by which point collective knowledge may have evolved substantially, obscuring attribution of learning signal to specific contributions. 

Coordination overhead scales with network size. As participating agents grow, mechanisms for trajectory aggregation and knowledge distribution must remain computationally tractable, and ensuring asynchronous updates do not introduce incon-sistencies affecting patient care requires careful protocol design and potentially formal verification. 

## 5.5 Evaluating Collective Intelligence 

A challenge crosscutting all others is the absence of established evaluation method-ologies. Conventional metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity apply to individual agents but do not capture the value added by trajectory sharing. We identify three dimensions future work must operationalize. 

Marginal improvement from collective knowledge. 

Does an agent with access to collective trajectories outperform the same agent in isolation? This requires controlled comparisons measuring diagnostic accuracy and reasoning quality: does the agent consider a broader differential and reach correct diagnoses more efficiently? 

Cross-context transfer effectiveness. 

Do trajectories from one context (e.g., academic cardiology) improve reasoning in another (e.g., rural primary care)? Transfer effectiveness likely varies across domains, and characterizing this landscape is essential for deployment decisions. 

Longitudinal learning dynamics. 

Does collective intelligence improve as the trajectory repository grows, or do satu-ration and diminishing returns emerge? Longitudinal evaluation requires sustained deployment in realistic clinical environments, a significant but essential validation step. We recommend that early DAS implementations incorporate prospective evalua-tion protocols from the outset, including pre-registered hypotheses and clearly defined metrics for success and failure. 

# Closing Remarks 

The future of healthcare AI lies not in monolithic systems that encode medical knowl-edge centrally, but in networks of specialized agents learning continuously from clinical experience and sharing insights in privacy-preserving ways. We have proposed Decen-tralized Agentic Systems as an architectural foundation for this future, one that respects the distributed nature of clinical expertise, the sovereignty of institutional knowledge, and the safety imperatives of patient care. The challenges are substantial, and we have deliberately identified open questions alongside our proposals. The con-verging pressures of data scarcity, institutional fragmentation, and the maturation of 16 agentic AI capabilities make this a timely moment to chart a path toward collabora-tive clinical intelligence. The architecture of that collaboration matters, and we believe the principles articulated here: experience over data, reasoning over parameters, and governance alongside technology, point toward a productive direction.
