# EnvLunch : The Environment as a Free Lunch for Learning to Interact 

Wei Liu ♠ B, Min Gao ♢, Yali Du ♠♣ , Yulan He ♠♣ B

> ♠

King’s College London ♢Chongqing University ♣The Alan Turing Institute 

Abstract. For agentic data analysis, language-model agents often require a large number of anno-tated tasks to learn how to interact effectively with executable environments (i.e., databases). While high-quality annotated tasks require heavy manual effort, an executable environment already pro-vides rich interaction structure and verifiable outcomes. In this sense, the environment itself offers a free lunch, an additional set of verifiable tasks that require no human annotation. We propose 

EnvLunch , a warm-up stage that turns this environmental signal into agent training without human-written or model-generated task descriptions, before downstream agentic reinforcement learning. 

EnvLunch samples valid action trajectories, executes them to obtain final observations, and trains an agent to recover a functionally equivalent trajectory given only its final observation. This label-free warm-up enables the model to outperform the baseline by over 9% on average across all down-stream training budgets, and with few labels, the baseline needs 8 to 32 times as many annotated examples to match the accuracy that the EnvLunch model reaches at its smallest training budget. Letting the environment alone define the task yields a larger gain than having the model write its own tasks over the same environments, and this warm-up also improves fine-tuning on benchmarks beyond data analysis. EnvLunch therefore saves annotated tasks and provides a better starting point for fine-tuning agentic models when such tasks are limited.

1 INTRODUCTION 

Language model agents can interact with databases to perform long-horizon and complex data querying, analysis, and report generation (Zhang et al., 2024; Grijalba et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; 

Code and data: https://github.com/thinkwee/EnvLunch 

1arXiv:2610.33817v1 [cs.AI] 24 Oct 2026 Preprint Lei et al., 2025), a setting we refer to as agentic data analysis . Training agents to acquire such capa-bilities currently relies heavily on either large-scale collections of human-annotated verifiable tasks for reinforcement learning (Bai et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2025; Xi et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a), or long-horizon trajectories distilled from much larger models (Sun et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025; Tongyi DeepResearch Team, 2025). Both approaches require substantial human effort and careful data curation (Huang et al., 2025). Yet agentic training has an abundant source of supervision that is often underused, which is the 

executable environment itself (Yu et al., 2018; Li et al., 2023; Lei et al., 2025). In data analysis, databases and tables expose rich state and action spaces, while execution provides an automatic signal for the outcome of an action sequence (Tran et al., 2009; 2014; Wang et al., 2017; Silver & Sutton, 2025; Yao, 2025; Xi et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026b; Meta PyTorch and Hugging Face, 2026). Existing methods partly reduce annotation costs by synthesising tasks with LLMs or human-defined templates, but still require these environments to be converted into explicit questions or task statements (Sun et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025; Stojanovski et al., 2025). We ask whether this step is necessary at all: can an agent learn to interact effectively by learning directly from information inherent in the environment? Any executable trajectory in an environment could expose the interaction process, providing signals for interaction behaviours such as recovering from failures or exploring the environment over long-horizon interactions. Such trajectories form an additional set of verifiable tasks without human annotation, which we call the free lunch of the environment. Therefore, we propose EnvLunch , an environment-driven warm-up stage that runs before down-stream agentic reinforcement learning. EnvLunch randomly composes valid actions into an exe-cutable trajectory, executes it to obtain a final observation, and trains the agent to recover a trajectory that could have produced that observation. Intuitively, this is like placing a target at a random loca-tion in a maze and training the agent to navigate to it: the target need not correspond to a meaningful downstream question, as long as reaching it requires effective interaction with the environment. To prevent the agent from simply copying the target observation, we create multiple perturbed copies of the original environment and execute the recovered trajectory in each, and agreement across these counterfactual environments determines the reward. EnvLunch requires no human or model anno-tation, is fully verifiable, and is lightweight, taking 20 minutes on CPU to construct 1600 warm-up tasks compared with more than 4 hours for model-based task synthesis. We validate EnvLunch on Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B across six agentic data-analysis tasks. Av-eraged across different training budgets, EnvLunch improves accuracy by 9.6% at 4B and 9.3% at 9B, with the largest gains when labels are scarce, where the baseline requires 8–32 × as many la-belled examples to match the accuracy reached by the EnvLunch model at its smallest training bud-get. Environment-driven warm-up also yields larger gains than reinforcement learning on tasks that the model synthesises over the same environments. Across seven additional agentic tasks spanning text-to-SQL, question answering, software use, and embodied interaction, EnvLunch also improves downstream performance, despite using warm-up environments disjoint from the evaluation tasks. Finally, in a constructed environment where uncertainty can be computed exactly, EnvLunch pro-duces the largest reduction in uncertainty about the environment, providing evidence that learning to interact contributes to the downstream gains. Our contributions are threefold. First, we show that an executable environment alone can define verifiable agentic tasks without any annotation. Second, we propose EnvLunch , a warm-up stage for agents to learn from the environment without human annotation. Third, across two model scales and thirteen benchmarks, we show that EnvLunch substantially reduces the annotated examples needed for downstream agentic reinforcement learning, which suggests that executable environments con-tain useful training signals that can be extracted without first specifying the meaningful tasks. En-vLunch provides a preliminary step towards agentic training that learns directly from environments. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Learning from executable environments. Rewarding a model by executing its output originated in semantic parsing and program synthesis (Zhong et al., 2017; Le et al., 2022; Gehring et al., 2025) and now extends to agents operating over web pages, devices, and more (Qi et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025; Qian et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a). Executable environments thus act as verifiers that require neither rubrics nor annotation, which has 2Preprint motivated a shift from learning on text towards learning from experience (Silver & Sutton, 2025; Yao, 2025), RL infrastructure for existing environments (Xi et al., 2025; 2026; Liu et al., 2026b; Meta PyTorch and Hugging Face, 2026), and pipelines that synthesise new ones (Song et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Dong et al., 2026; Tu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a; Gandhi et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025), including hand-crafted, model-generated or mined from the web (Zeng et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026; Fang et al., 2026; Cai et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026c; Verma et al., 2025). EnvLunch builds on this view and carries it one step further: an environment that already supplies the verdict can also supply the task. Any environment these pipelines produce can therefore feed EnvLunch directly. A growing body of work lets the environment shape the tasks as well. Agents explore an environment and turn the resulting trajectory into an instruction (Sun et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025; Murty et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026b; Mai et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026), a proposer and a solver improve through self-play (Zhao et al., 2025a; Zhou et al., 2025; Zhai et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026), task generators are paired with verifiers (Stojanovski et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b; Chen et al., 2025b; Sullivan et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Jain et al., 2025), and agents learn from the states their own actions reach along expert demonstrations (Zhang et al., 2026b). Together these works show that interaction with an environment is a rich source of training signal when little is annotated. EnvLunch follows this direction to the point where the environment defines the task and judges it, so no question needs to be written, and even a trajectory with no semantic meaning trains the multi-turn interaction behaviours that downstream tasks require. 

Recovering a query from its output. Recovering a query from its output is a classic problem in databases (Tran et al., 2009; 2014; Zhang et al., 2013; Tan et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2017; Take-nouchi et al., 2021; Orvalho et al., 2020; Pradhan & Haritsa, 2026), which establishes that an out-put constrains the program that produced it. This research identifies instance-equivalent programs, which agree on one database but compute different functions, and separates them with equivalence provers (He et al., 2024), language model judges (Zhao et al., 2025b), or additional databases de-signed to distinguish candidate programs (Chandra et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2025a; Klopfenstein et al., 2026). Execution-based text-to-SQL training meets the same issue as false positives of single-instance matching (Ma et al., 2025; Pourreza et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026), and synthetic-query training sidesteps it by providing the query explicitly (Liu et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025). EnvLunch turns this recovery problem into a training task for agents. Any trajectory that reproduces the target counts as a solution, which makes a task with no statement trainable, and the perturbed copies of Section 3 rule out degenerate solutions such as copying the output. Hindsight relabelling in goal-conditioned reinforcement learning shows that reached outcomes make effective goals (Andrychowicz et al., 2017; Ghosh et al., 2021). EnvLunch 

brings this principle to language-model agents and uses the outcomes of trajectories the agent never sees as the tasks themselves to bring the pressure of environment interaction. 

3 METHOD 

The free lunch in EnvLunch is the additional set of verifiable tasks that the environment provides without human annotation. Any executable trajectory can define a task, and its execution result is the answer, so neither a question nor a manually annotated label is needed. In this sense, the envi-ronment itself provides the task where agentic reinforcement learning can be performed. For agentic data analysis, the database serves as the environment, while database queries and computation state-ments constitute the executable action space. By constructing an action pool and performing online trajectory synthesis and filtering, and training the agent through counterfactual multi-environment verification (Figure 2), EnvLunch provides a fully automated warm-up for agentic capabilities. 

Action Pool Construction. We define the action space associated with each environment as A,e.g., select , where , and group by in data analysis. These operators form a generic interface that applies to any relational database without per-environment design. They are further organised into different levels according to the number of operators composed. On the SQL side, the pool has fifteen operators: ten aggregate at increasing levels of composition, from a grouped aggregate up to a three-table foreign-key chain or a common table expression compared against its own average, and five do not aggregate, such as a projection under a filter or a top-k ranking with a tie-break. Online Trajectory Synthesis and Filtering. Each time a new action is appended to the con-structed trajectory, we execute the partial trajectory in the environment to obtain oℓ, and rebuild the candidate pool A(oℓ) for the next step, keeping an action only if its operands exist in the current observation and it can still change it. Its parameters are read off that observation, so a threshold is an actual quantile of the column and a category filter names a value the column holds. For ex-ample, once the current execution has left only a handful of rows, a top-k ranking action no longer changes the observation and is therefore removed from the pool. Such synthetic trajectories need 4Preprint not resemble useful user tasks, for example, “Pair every postseason batting record of a player with his every postseason fielding record, then group those pairs by the fielding error count and report how many pairs fall in each group.” Such tasks may have little practical meaning, but they still require the agent to interact with the environment and reason over intermediate observations to re-cover an executable trajectory. In addition to dynamically adjusting the candidate action pool, we also check whether the trajectory produces different results when executed under counterfactual per-turbations of the environment. We retain a trajectory only if it executes and yields a non-degenerate result in every perturbed copy, and its result changes in at least one copy, so that an answer which merely copies the target observation cannot obtain the full reward. This rules out the reward hacking illustrated in Figure 6(a). 

Counterfactual Multi-Environment Verification. After obtaining a synthetic trajectory and its final execution result, we use standard agentic RL to train the agent to recover a functionally equiv-alent trajectory. The agent is provided with the final execution result, the corresponding database instance, SQL and Python tools, and a minimal ReAct harness (Yao et al., 2023). We do not reward exact recovery of the original trajectory, because dif-ferent trajectories can represent the same logic. Instead, we compare the execution results of the two trajectories. However, this setup is vulnerable to a simple form of reward hacking, where the agent could simply print the target observation result rather than reconstructing the trajectory. 4 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We evaluate EnvLunch on Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026). For each backbone, 

EnvLunch uses the same pool of environments and warms up only once for every downstream bench-mark. For each benchmark, we fine-tune both the released backbone checkpoint, which we call the baseline , and the corresponding EnvLunch -warmed checkpoint, which we call the EnvLunch 

model , using GRPO under the benchmark’s native multi-turn interaction protocol. We leave its action space, turn limit, trajectory token budget, and official scorer unchanged. Each downstream fine-tuning setting is repeated with three independent random seeds. We plot downstream accuracy against the number of annotated training examples, evaluating the checkpoints at which the number of training examples seen is a power of two, to evaluate whether EnvLunch can improve downstream label efficiency. EnvLunch uses two environment corpora. The first is a relational corpus built on 

112 databases from the training splits of Spider (Yu et al., 2018), BIRD (Li et al., 2023) and Archer (Zheng et al., 2024a) ( 63 from Spider, including the 7 Archer databases, and 49 from BIRD), on which the agent interacts through SQL. The second is a dataframe corpus containing 398 tables from DataMind (Qiao et al., 2026), BIRD and DABench (Hu et al., 2024a) ( 351 reinforcement-learning training tables from DataMind, 32 BIRD training databases read as dataframes, and 15 

DABench tables), on which the agent interacts through Python. All warm-up tasks are built on these two corpora. The environments are not constructed for any particular downstream benchmark, and no evaluation environment from any benchmark is included in the warm-up data: the builder ex-cludes by name the development databases of Spider and BIRD and every table in the DABench evaluation manifests. Since EnvLunch needs no annotation, it can also run on the environments of a target benchmark, but we keep the two disjoint so that the downstream gains measure what transfers across environments rather than familiarity with the evaluation ones. LLMs following the warm-up stage using EnvLunch are then further fine-tuned and evaluated on six downstream data-analysis benchmarks. Four present tables either alone or interleaved with text (ConvFinQA, Chen et al., 2022; TAT-QA, Zhu et al., 2021; MultiHiertt, Zhao et al., 2022; TableBench, Wu et al., 2025). The remaining two, DSEval (Zhang et al., 2024) and DataBench (Grijalba et al., 2024), require interacting with dataframes through code. Each keeps its own official test set and metric, which is proportional match except for the official DataBench accuracy. The held-out sizes and turn limits are 1486 and 16 for ConvFinQA, 494 and 12 for TAT-QA, 300 and 32 

for MultiHiertt, 118 and 16 for TableBench, 87 and 20 for DSEval, and 351 and 16 for DataBench. The fine-tuning pool of a benchmark is drawn from its own training split and subsampled to at most 

2048 examples, and training budgets range over 16 –1024 examples ( 16 –512 for TableBench and 

8–64 for DSEval). For benchmarks without a usable training split (DSEval, and Spider2-lite and SpreadsheetBench in Section 5.2), we split the data at the level of the underlying source (Spider2-lite by database) with disjoint identifiers, which leaves pools of 99 , 74 and 493 examples. We follow the official settings as closely as possible and add no extra harness design or result ensembling for chasing SOTA, since the quantity of interest is downstream label efficiency. The warm-up runs 100 

steps of 16 tasks each, which takes about 4 hours for the 9B model and 3 hours for the 4B model. The warm-up needs no annotated tasks, runs once for each backbone and serves all downstream benchmarks. We also conduct experiments to rule out the possibility that the observed effect simply results from additional training on synthetic data (Section 6). 

Training details. We train with slime (Zhu et al., 2025), which runs Megatron-LM (Shoeybi et al., 2019) for optimisation and SGLang (Zheng et al., 2024b) for rollout generation. Both the warm-up and downstream fine-tuning use GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) with 8 rollouts per task, full-parameter updates and Adam at a learning rate of 1 × 10 −6 with β = (0 .9, 0.98) and weight decay 0.01 , a clipped surrogate with ϵ = 0 .2 and ϵhigh = 0 .28 , truncated importance sampling clipped to [0 .5, 1.5] ,a low-variance KL loss against the initial policy with coefficient 0.01 , and an entropy coefficient of 0.001 . Rollouts are sampled at temperature 1.0 with at most 8192 response tokens per action turn. The warm-up draws its 1600 tasks at random in a single pass and takes 4 optimiser updates of batch size 32 per step. Downstream fine-tuning takes 8 labelled examples and one optimiser update of batch size 64 per step, for nmax /8 steps, where nmax is the largest training budget of the benchmark. Evaluation metrics. Following standard practice in self-supervised pre-training, we evaluate the warm-up stage by measuring the labelled downstream data required to reach a given performance level (H´ enaff et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020a;b). We define downstream label efficiency as the number n of annotated examples required to reach a given accuracy. For each label-efficiency curve, we report two statistics. The curve-level gain is the mean accuracy difference between EnvLunch and the baseline across all training-data budgets and serves as our primary comparison. The matching budget is the smallest baseline budget needed to match or exceed EnvLunch ’s accuracy, expressed as a multiple of the corresponding EnvLunch budget. It summarises the low-budget regime; “never (> k ×)” indicates that the baseline does not reach the target within the evaluated range. 

5 RESULTS 

5.1 AGENTIC DATA ANALYSIS 

Figure 1 gives an overview of the downstream fine-tuning curves, and Table 1 reports the detailed curve-level gain and matching budget. Each curve averages three independent runs, with shaded bands showing their minimum and maximum across runs. Across two models and six benchmarks, 

EnvLunch enables agentic LLMs to reach or even surpass baseline performance with substantially fewer annotated training examples. The advantage is most pronounced when labels are scarce. Notably, the warm-up tasks differ in form from the downstream ones. The warm-up trains the model to reconstruct synthetic trajectories, whereas the downstream tasks span data science coding, report analysis, table question answering, and other related data analysis tasks. Nevertheless, the performance gap generally persists as fine-tuning proceeds. On TableBench at 9B, although the 

EnvLunch model starts below the baseline, it overtakes the baseline after only 16 examples. In terms of label efficiency, on every curve, the baseline requires more annotated examples to match the accuracy achieved by the EnvLunch model at its smallest training budget. Specifically, the baseline requires between 8× and 32 × as many examples when it reaches the target, with a median of 16 ×;on the two DSEval curves, it does not reach the target within the evaluated range. Recent RL studies show that repeatedly training on a single example can keep improving agentic LLMs (Wang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026), as a well-designed example leads the model through different intermediate states across iterations (Fu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a). We test this extreme low-label regime and hold one annotated example fixed for both models and train on it for up to 32 

steps. An example qualifies only if both models show a non-zero spread of rewards within its group, so that both are learning from it, and among the qualifying examples we take those at the 25 th, 

50 th and 75 th percentile of the per-group accuracy of the baseline. Figure 3 reports the accuracy difference after each step, averaged over the six benchmarks. We extend the EnvLunch model to seven downstream benchmarks beyond agentic data analysis in-cluding text-to-SQL (BIRD, Li et al., 2023; Spider, Yu et al., 2018; Spider2-lite, Lei et al., 2025), question answering (HotpotQA, Yang et al., 2018), embodied simulation (ALFWorld, Shridhar et al., 2021), application use (AppWorld, Trivedi et al., 2024), and spreadsheet editing (SpreadsheetBench, Ma et al., 2024). On BIRD, Spider and the local SQLite subset of Spider2-lite, each turn either runs a read-only SQL query, whose rows or error message is returned, or submits a final query, scored by execution accuracy. HotpotQA uses the distractor setting and proportional match. ALFWorld takes text commands, returns the game feedback with the list of admissible commands and is scored by success rate. In AppWorld the agent writes Python that calls the application APIs and ends the episode by calling the task-completion API, scored by task goal completion. In SpreadsheetBench the agent writes openpyxl code that produces an output workbook, may inspect a recalculated preview of it before submitting, and is scored by execution accuracy. The held-out sizes and turn limits are 1534 and 32 for BIRD, 1034 and 32 for Spider, 61 and 8 for Spider2-lite, 400 questions sampled from the validation split and 32 for HotpotQA, the 134 unseen games and 30 for ALF-World, 57 and 30 for AppWorld, and 394 and 6 for SpreadsheetBench. 16 –2048 examples for BIRD and Spider, 8–64 for Spider2-lite and AppWorld, 8–256 for Spread-sheetBench, and 16 –1024 for the rest. For these tasks, the warm-up differs entirely from the downstream tasks in task formulation and domain, yet EnvLunch still exhibits a degree of generalisation, improving downstream label efficiency. Although the free lunch claimed by EnvLunch is in the form of ad-ditional data rather than reduced compute, across the data analysis and additional benchmarks, the extra fine-tuning required for the baseline to match EnvLunch at its smallest budget takes longer than the warm-up stage: summed over the thirteen benchmarks, it takes about 7 hours at 9B and 4 hours at 4B on the same GPU node (a lower bound where the baseline never reaches the target), against a one-off warm-up of about 4 and 3 hours.

What, then, does the model actually learn during this RL process? We consider four intuitive possibilities. First, the model is unlikely to benefit simply by memorising the warm-up environments, since none of them appears in any evaluation set (Section 4), and benchmarks whose domains do not overlap with them, such as ALFWorld and AppWorld, still benefit from EnvLunch . Two other possibilities are that the model learns the schema of the warm-up environments, or that it imitates some key actions appearing in successful trajectories. We test these hypotheses by replacing the RL-based warm-up with contin-ued pre-training on environment text and rejection sampling fine-tuning on successful trajectories, respectively. For the former, a template sampler writes, for each table of the dataframe corpus, doc-uments giving the file name, the columns and their types, a templated pandas expression and its execution result ( 34 ,267 documents, 6.60 M tokens), and we train on them for one epoch of full-parameter next-token prediction. For the latter, we sample up to 8 attempts from the backbone on each of the 1600 EnvLunch tasks under the same limits and scoring, keep the attempts with full reward, and fine-tune on them for one epoch with the loss on model tokens only. Neither of these two warm-up approaches yields a gain: averaged over the six data-analysis benchmarks, their curve-level gains over the baseline are −3.5 and −5.7 at 4B and −2.4 and −4.7 at 9B, respectively. A fourth possibility is that any reinforcement learning on these environments brings the gain, what-ever the tasks are. We test it by keeping the EnvLunch stage unchanged and replacing only its task pool with tasks that the LLM synthesises itself over the same databases and tables, following three lines of prior work. In Self-Challenging (Zhou et al., 2025), the model inspects an environment (field descriptions, table schemas or creation statements, and sample data) and writes a question to-gether with a program that answers it as verification, and tasks whose program fails are dropped. In Explore-First (Sun et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025), the model first interacts with the environment freely and then rewrites its trajectory into a question and an answer. In Self-Play (Zhao et al., 2025a; Huang et al., 2026), the model proposes a question and solves it eight times, taking the majority answer as the label, and tasks are dropped when all eight attempts agree or fewer than three agree. In all three, a backbone of the same scale generates the same number of tasks over the same environments, and the harness, tools, interaction limits and all other settings are shared with EnvLunch . At both scales, all three fall short of EnvLunch on the downstream curves: their curve-level gains are +5 .2, +5 .3

and +6 .4 at 4B and +1 .6, −0.3 and −4.0 at 9B, against +9 .6 and +9 .3 for EnvLunch . In each of them the model labels its own tasks, and nothing checks that the label answers the question it wrote, which likely makes the training signal less reliable.

Uncertainty Reduction in a Controlled Environment. As shown by the trajectory analysis above, after warm-up, the model appears to learn how to interact with the environment to reduce uncertainty and narrow the search space for subsequent steps. However, real-world benchmark en-vironments are large and complex, making it difficult to cleanly and accurately measure how well a model understands the environment. Following prior work that measures information seeking by how strongly an agent narrows hypotheses about a hidden state (Grand et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024b; Golde et al., 2025), we construct a controlled environment with exactly measurable uncertainty. The model queries a constructed sales table whose three columns each admit one of four encodings, yielding 64 possible environments. All environments share the same prompt and final answer and differ only in their hidden encodings, which can be distinguished only through queries. Concretely, the model is asked for the total amount of the completed orders of one product in one target town, given the column documentation and the first rows of a table covering 20 towns. The records of the target town write the town, status and product columns in one of four ways each (e.g., the town as 

Marlborough , MLB , Mrlbr or Mboro ). The documentation warns that some records use legacy codes without naming them, a common data-quality problem (Rahm & Do, 2000; Kim et al., 2003), and the model never works in the version that matches the documentation, so it must inspect the data to compute the answer. Following possible-world semantics for incomplete databases (Imielinski & Witold Lipski, 1984), we execute each model action across all 64 hidden tables and discard those whose output differs from the model’s observation. The remaining tables are consistent with the model’s observations, so we define uncertainty as log 2 of their number, decreasing from 6 to 0 bits as the encodings are identified. Six domains contribute 25 tasks each. The models are evaluated right after their warm-up, without downstream fine-tuning, in the DataBench harness with at most 

24 turns; each task is run once with greedy decoding and twice with sampling (temperature 0.7,10 Preprint top-p 0.8), and the 95% intervals come from bootstrap resampling over tasks. Figure 5 shows that 

EnvLunch reduces uncertainty more effectively than the baseline and the continued pre-training, rejection sampling fine-tuning and synthesised-task variants at both scales. This difference is not explained by taking more actions: at 4B, EnvLunch and rejection sampling fine-tuning take a similar number of actions but achieve substantially different uncertainty reduction. The warm-up there-fore shapes the model to select actions that reduce uncertainty about the environment, providing a behavioural mechanism for the downstream gains in Section 5. 

7 CONCLUSION 

We propose EnvLunch , a warm-up stage that trains agentic LLMs to reconstruct randomly synthe-sised trajectories from the environment alone before downstream agentic reinforcement learning. 

EnvLunch improves accuracy by over 9% on average across six agentic data analysis benchmarks at both model scales. When labels are scarce, the baseline requires 8–32 × as many annotated examples to reach comparable performance, and the gains transfer to almost all of the seven additional bench-marks. Our study focuses on relational databases and dataframes with verifiable outcomes at the 4Band 9B scales, and extending the warm-up to other executable environments, open-ended tasks and larger models is left for future work. More broadly, these results show that an environment’s existing information and executable dynamics can provide useful supervision for learning agent behaviour before any downstream question is written.
