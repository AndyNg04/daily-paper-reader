# ABSTRACT 

Recommender systems face a tension between broadening exposure to diverse viewpoints and avoiding interac-tions that reinforce extreme positions. This tension is especially consequential in already polarized populations, where limiting disagreement may reduce backlash while preserving divisions between opposing groups. Here we investigate whether recommendation strategies can reverse polarization rather than merely slow its development. Building on the Attraction–Repulsion Model of opinion dynamics, we introduce two complementary design principles: Tolerance-aware recommendation, which seeks to learn each individual’s tolerance for disagreement and reduces exposure to viewpoints beyond that tolerance, and adaptive diversification, which broadens exposure for agents with more extreme opinions while retaining stronger personalization for moderates. Agent-based experiments show that highly personalized recommendations intensify polarization under polarized initial condi-tions. Tolerance awareness alone primarily slows polarization, whereas combining it with adaptive diversification can reduce polarization below its initial level and, under suitable conditions, restore consensus. Additional experiments incorporating persistent extreme influencers and a spiral-of-silence mechanism show that the com-bined strategy can mitigate polarization under social pressure and selective participation. To examine whether these findings extend beyond predefined opinion-update rules, we repeat the experiments on a simulated social platform populated by 100 LLM-driven agents prompted to move toward opinions similar to their own and away from those that differ substantially. Across three runs, the combined strategy is again the only recommendation policy tested that reverses polarization in every run. These findings distinguish the prevention of polarization from its reversal and identify tolerance-aware, adaptive exposure as a promising recommendation design princi-ple, providing a mechanism-based foundation for reconnecting divided populations while accounting for users’ responses to disagreement.

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Extreme ideological polarization can undermine democratic compromise by making mutual understanding across ideological groups increasingly difficult [49]. Algorithmic recommender systems, which tailor content and social connections to users’ preferences, have therefore attracted considerable attention as potential drivers of polarization through selective exposure and ideologically segregated information environments [11, 13, 18]. Yet efforts to counteract polarization often turn to diversity-enhancing algorithms that simply increase exposure to divergent content, overlooking a crucial psychological constraint: when individuals encounter views that fall beyond their tolerance threshold, the result may be not accommodation but repulsion, driving them toward more extreme positions [2, 5, 31, 39]. This repulsion-driven dynamic is precisely captured by the Attraction–Repulsion Model (ARM) introduced by Axelrod et al. [4]. The ARM is a minimal agent-based model with two simple rules. The first rule governs the interaction: The probability that two agents interact decays exponentially with their opinion distance, scaled by an exposure parameter that determines how quickly the probability declines as distance increases. Once an interaction occurs, its effect depends on a common tolerance threshold. If their distance falls within this threshold, the active agent moves toward the passive agent (attraction); if their distance exceeds it, the active agent moves away (repulsion). Using this model, prior work showed that under normally distributed initial opinions, ultra-personalized recommendations (low exposure parameter) can mitigate polarization by limiting repulsive cross-cutting interactions across a range of tolerance levels [4]. However, these results were obtained under a unimodal, symmetric initial distribution. Whether the same mitigating effect holds when the population is already polarized — and whether recommender strategies could be redesigned to reverse, not merely slow, polarization — remains unknown. arXiv:2610.40289v1 [cs.SI] 29 Oct 2026 To address these gaps, we extend the ARM in three directions. First, we introduce tolerance-aware recommendation into the interaction rule: the probability of interaction depends not only on the distance and exposure parameter, but also on the tolerance threshold. When the distance between two agents falls within the tolerance threshold, their interaction probability follows the standard exposure-based rule; when it exceeds the tolerance, a penalty factor reduces that probability. This mechanism aims to reduce repulsive interactions without eliminating all cross-cutting exposure. Second, we propose adaptive diversification: the degree of personalization is made a function of an agent’s distance from the reference position. Agents near the reference position maintain high personalization, while those at the extremes receive more diverse recommendations. Third, we incorporate the spiral of silence and extreme influencers, allowing us to examine whether our proposed strategies remain effective under social pressure — conditions known to intensify polarization [25, 29, 38]. Using agent-based simulations, our extended ARM shows that recommendation strategies combining tolerance-awareness with adaptive diversification can reverse an ongoing polarizing trend, even when the population is already polarized. In contrast, personalized recommendations alone — based solely on opinion distance and exposure parameter — fail under these conditions. This mitigating effect remains robust even when the spiral of silence and extreme influencers amplify polarization. Like other agent-based models, these results depend on behavioral rules specified by the modeler. We therefore also test them on a simulated platform whose 100 users are agents driven by a large language model (LLM) [20]. We prompt the LLM agents with the attraction–repulsion premise, motivated by psychological evidence that people evaluate belief-consistent information more favorably and may respond to conflicting information by reinforcing their existing positions [30, 51]. Specifying this premise keeps the behavioral assumptions consistent across all LLM experiments while allowing the LLM to generate individual responses without a fixed numerical update rule. Under these conditions, ultra-personalized and tolerance-aware feeds leave the two camps divided, while the combined design is the only policy tested that reverses polarization in all three runs. Excessive diversification widens the divide again, and under social pressure the combined design still lowers polarization, though less than in the rule-based model. These findings suggest that when societies are already divided, reducing exposure based solely on similarity is insufficient; incorporating tolerance into the interaction rule offers a promising mechanism for reversing polarization. While our simplified model does not capture the full complexity of real-world systems, it provides insights into how exposure can be calibrated to both individual tolerance thresholds and the current distribution of opinions. 

1.1 Related Work 

Research on recommendation and polarization emphasizes the interaction between exposure and behavioral responses to disagree-ment. Axelrod et al. [4] distinguish encounter probabilities from opinion updates in the Attraction–Repulsion Model, showing how tolerance and exposure jointly influence polarization. Santos et al. [46] similarly demonstrate that the consequences of link recommendation depend on network structure and individuals’ responses to outgroup contact. These models caution against treating greater exposure diversity as inherently beneficial. Empirically, Bail et al. [5] find that Republican participants exposed to opposing political views became more conservative, illustrating that cross-cutting exposure can produce backlash in some settings. Together, these studies motivate recommendation policies that account for responses to disagreement alongside opinion similarity. Prior research also establishes that interventions can reduce existing polarization. Currin et al. [15] introduce random dynamical nudges based on sampled population opinions and demonstrate depolarization of established echo chambers in two opinion-dynamics models. Piccardi et al. [44] provide complementary field evidence that reranking exposure to posts expressing antidemocratic attitudes and partisan animosity can alter affective polarization. Their outcome concerns hostility toward political opponents, whereas our model examines dispersion in opinion positions. Our study contributes to this literature by examining tolerance-aware interaction selection together with opinion-dependent exposure within the ARM. We investigate when their combination moves beyond slowing polarization to reversing an existing division, including settings where low tolerance or excessive diversification undermines the benefit. LLM-based social simulation offers an additional approach to examining recommendation policies. Törnberg et al. [52] compare alternative news-feed algorithms and find that a bridging feed promotes more constructive conversation across political divides. Donkers and Ziegler [17] investigate human responses to polarized environments populated by LLM agents. However, Chuang et al. [12] identify an inherent tendency toward consensus in LLM opinion simulations, while Ju et al. [27] demonstrate sensitivity to minor prompt variations. These findings motivate explicit behavioral checks and comparison with reference dynamics. We use LLM agents to examine whether the qualitative policy comparison persists when opinion updates are generated under a shared, explicitly prompted attraction–repulsion premise.

# 3 RESULTS 

Four main findings emerge from our simulation experiments: (i) the failure of ultra-personalized recommendations under polarized initial conditions; (ii) the slowing effect of tolerance-aware filtering; (iii) the reversal achieved by adaptive diversification; and (iv) the robustness of the combined framework against spiral-of-silence dynamics. 

3.1 Limit of Personalization 

We begin by assessing baseline performance under ultra-personalized recommendations (exposure parameter E = 0 .520 ). Previous work shows that under a unimodal Gaussian ideological distribution, highly personalized recommendations ( E = 0 .520 ) can slow polarization by reducing repulsive cross-cutting interactions [4, 10, 16, 41, 48]. Whether this protective effect persists when the population is already polarized at initialization remains unclear. Our analysis therefore centers on polarized initial 6configurations—where opinion variance is already elevated (bimodal distribution, mean initial variance approximately 0.073) and opposing camps are entrenched (see Fig. 2b for an example). Under these conditions, ultra-personalized recommendations ( E = 0 .520 ) fail to reverse polarization and may instead reinforce existing boundaries. As shown in Fig. 4a, for low-to-intermediate tolerance ( T ≤ 0.45 ), polarization increases over time. Although repulsive interactions occur infrequently under low exposure, the large initial distances between opposing clusters mean that when repulsion does occur, the positional shift is substantial, driving agents toward the extremes. Attractive interactions, by contrast, occur primarily within each cluster and tend to maintain the initial polarized configuration. Personalization-based mitigation thus exhibits a fundamental limitation: its efficacy depends critically on the initial ideological distribution. 

3.2 Tolerance-Aware Recommendation Slows Polarization 

Can incorporating tolerance into interaction selection overcome the limitations of personalization alone? Figure 3a shows that introducing tolerance awareness substantially slows the growth of polarization from a unimodal initial distribution. Even a modest level of tolerance awareness markedly suppresses the increase in opinion variance relative to the baseline without tolerance awareness, leading to consistently lower polarization throughout the simulation. Evaluating the mechanism under an initially polarized population with highly personalized exposure reveals a similar benefit. As shown in Fig. 4b, tolerance-aware recommendation consistently reduces polarization compared with the corresponding baseline (Fig. 4a) across a broad range of tolerance thresholds. Because this improvement is observed for both initially homogeneous and already polarized populations, this relative benefit is robust across the tested initial configurations. Despite these improvements, mean trajectories remain polarized at the simulated horizon. Tolerance-aware recommendation slows the development of polarization and lowers its overall level, but it does not consistently restore polarized populations to a low-polarization state across seeds. Motivating the next stage of our design, this limitation points to the need for the adaptive diversification mechanism introduced in the following section. 

3.3 Adaptive Diversification Reverses Polarization 

While tolerance-aware recommendation substantially slows polarization, it remains insufficient to consistently restore already polarized populations across seeds; we therefore ask whether adaptive diversification can reverse polarization once fragmented opinion distributions have formed. Figure 4c demonstrates that adaptive diversification fundamentally changes the long-term dynamics. From initial configura-tions matched within each seed, the combined mechanism reduces final mean polarization below its initial level for T ≥ 0.25 .Consensus occurs in 5/5 runs at T = 0 .35 , 0.45 , versus 3/5 at T = 0 .25 . For T = 0 .05 and 0.15, however, the mean variance rises from approximately 0.073 to about 0.195 and 0.102, respectively. These are respectively above and below the tolerance-aware-only means (about 0.164 and 0.140). Unlike tolerance-aware recommendation alone, which generally lowers the level of polarization, adaptive diversification enables polarized populations to recover from fragmented states under suitable conditions. To clarify this transition, Fig. 3b traces the opinion dynamics across different diversification strengths. Reversal occurs at small D (0.05 and 0.15 in Fig. 3b); for D from 0.25 to 0.55 the mean variance settles between approximately 0.064 and 0.103, and for D = 0 .85 and 0.95 the population polarizes, with final mean variance approximately 0.247–0.248. Broader exposure from a larger D therefore does not necessarily yield lower polarization. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that adaptive diversification not only mitigates polarization but can also reverse established polarization, with consistent consensus across seeds under suitable tolerance levels. 

3.4 Robustness to the Spiral-of-Silence Mechanism 

To test whether the proposed framework remains effective when opinion dynamics are further influenced by social pressure, all simulations in this section start from the Gaussian initial distribution and, unless otherwise specified, include two extreme influencers located at the opinion boundaries (0 and 1). Each influencer broadcasts with probability Pinf = 0 .15 , and the spiral-of-silence parameter is set to S = 0 .01 , corresponding to a strong social-pressure condition. Adding extreme influencers accelerates polarization (Fig. 5b), and the spiral of silence amplifies it further for some T (Fig. 5c). Deploying the proposed framework under the same social-pressure setting substantially counteracts this amplification. As shown in Fig. 5d, combining tolerance-aware recommendation with adaptive diversification reduces mean polarization across the tested tolerance levels relative to the corresponding baseline with the spiral-of-silence mechanism. # 4 TESTING THE MECHANISM WITH LLM-P OWERED AGENTS 

We examine whether the recommendation effects in Section 3 persist when LLM-generated opinion changes replace the ARM update rule. The agents are explicitly told that nearby views can persuade them and distant views can push them away (P1); the LLM determines the direction and magnitude of each response. Feed selection uses known agent positions and a single estimated population threshold, adapting the ARM’s shared-tolerance and known-state assumptions (P2 and P3). This comparison tests the policy mechanisms under a shared behavioral premise, extending work on LLM-based feed and opinion simulations [12, 52]. 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

The platform contains 100 persona-based agents discussing a fictional state’s retirement contribution rate, ranging from 4% to 24% of wages. Rates are mapped to [0 , 1] by x = ( p − 4) /20 . Agents post their views and read up to three sampled posts per round, with display probabilities determined by the recommendation policy. A 20-round pilot estimates a common attraction–repulsion threshold, ˆT = 0 .175 , used in all runs. Preliminary trials revealed a directional bias: agents moved farther toward nearby posts favoring higher contribution rates than toward equally distant posts favoring lower rates. This asymmetry could produce upward drift even under balanced exposure. We used randomized mirrored reading to control this asymmetry; the procedure and eligibility restrictions are described in Appendix B.1. Implementation, calibration, prompts, and the workflow are in Appendix B. Study 1 compares six feeds from a polarized initialization over 80 rounds: random (A1), ultra-personalized (A2), tolerance-aware (A3), adaptive diversification alone (A4), the combined design (A5), and tolerance-aware random (A6). Study 2 varies diversification strength over 160 rounds. Study 3 examines influencers and silence from a unimodal initialization over 30 rounds, using three opinion-climate cues. Each arm has three LLM runs with different seeds and 10 rule-based reference runs using the same feed rules and initialization distributions, with independently drawn starts. We report opinion variance and define reversal as a reduction of at least 0.05 in the gap between the two initial camps’ mean opinions. The comparisons test persistence under personalization (C1), mitigation with tolerance filtering (C2), reversal with the combined design (C3), failure of exposure alone (C4), and mitigation under social pressure (C5). 

4.2 Results and Scope 

In Study 1, personalized and tolerance-aware feeds barely changed the camp gap ( −0.013 and −0.019 on average over three runs), although tolerance awareness kept final variance slightly lower in every run (0.061 versus 0.064). Only the combined design met the reversal criterion in all three runs, reducing the gap by 0.113 on average (0.070–0.166) and ending at variance 0.041, compared with 0.105 under adaptive diversification alone (Fig. 6a). Random exposure widened the gap in every run ( +0 .249 ). As in Section 3, tolerance awareness alone did not reliably reverse polarization, whereas combining it with adaptive diversification did. The parameter sweep broadly follows Fig. 3b (Fig. 6b). At D = 0 .15 and 0.35, the gap reductions persisted through 160 rounds in all three runs ( −0.127 and −0.103 on average), and excessive diversification ( D = 0 .95 ) widened the gap in every run (+0 .091 ). At D = 0 .05 , an early narrowing partly faded ( −0.040 on average; one run stayed reversed), whereas the rule-based reference reversed in 9 of 10 seeds. Under influencers and silence, the combined design lowered final variance under all three cues in every run, by 0.008–0.020 on average, versus 0.029–0.057 in the reference (Fig. 6c). These scenarios test mitigation from a unimodal population; adding influencers did not increase variance over the 30-round horizon. 8The results support a conditional policy comparison: the combined design retains its advantage with LLM-generated updates, while the response to weak diversification and the size of the social-pressure benefit differ from the rule-based model. # 5 DISCUSSION 

5.1 Mechanism-Level Insights 

This study highlights a central tension in recommender-system design: personalization can limit destabilizing disagreement while also restricting opportunities for already divided groups to move closer. Algorithms that maximize engagement through preference alignment—such as those deployed by YouTube, TikTok, X and other content platforms—are increasingly implicated in the amplification of homophily and the erosion of cross-cutting exposure [14, 26, 47, 50]. While personalization satisfies immediate cognitive comfort and increases retention, it inadvertently fosters epistemic fragmentation [14, 19]. Our results suggest that the solution is not to reject personalization but to modulate it through mechanisms responsive to user-specific thresholds and system-level ideological dynamics. Central to our argument is the recognition that ideological tolerance is a latent but critical user attribute that governs the downstream impact of exposure diversity. Existing recommender systems tend to model users as monolithic in their receptivity to divergent views [28]. However, both psychological theory and our simulations suggest that this is a flawed assumption: in low-tolerance environments, even modest increases in ideological diversity can trigger repulsion, identity threat, or accelerated radicalization [3, 6, 40, 43]. Our proposed tolerance-aware mechanism addresses this by adjusting exposure probabilities based on ideological distance relative to tolerance thresholds. This approach reduces the likelihood of repulsive encounters while preserving in-threshold heterogeneity—creating informational environments conducive to bounded pluralism rather than polarized bifurcation. Yet, we find that mitigating polarization is not equivalent to reversing it. In systems already characterized by entrenched fragmentation, tolerance-aware filtering merely slows polarization’s progression; it does not alter its directionality [6]. To address this, we introduce adaptive diversification, a structurally asymmetric mechanism that increases recommendation diversity as a function of ideological extremity. This strategy broadens exposure for agents farther from the reference position while retaining stronger personalization near it. Our simulations reveal a nonlinear relationship between diversification and polarization: appropriately calibrated diversification can promote convergence or reduce opinion dispersion, whereas excessive diversification can intensify polarization. This underscores a central finding: polarization dynamics are sensitive to second-order design parameters, not merely the presence or absence of diversity. These mechanisms also highlight the necessity of designing for heterogeneity, not just averages. Modern platforms of-ten rely on aggregate-level performance metrics (e.g., click-through rate, dwell time), obscuring the asymmetric effects that algorithmic exposures have on marginal versus moderate users. By simulating agents with distinct ideological positions and tolerance thresholds, our framework recovers a more ecologically valid understanding of population dynamics. In particular, the disproportionate influence of peripheral agents—who, despite being few in number, shape system-level extremity through high-amplitude repulsion—is an emergent property missed by homogeneous models. We further enrich our model by formalizing the spiral of silence [37], a theory long associated with mass communication but rarely implemented in algorithmic systems modeling. Our approach captures how perceived mainstream positions—whether formed through influencers, media signals, or feedback loops—can suppress dissent and reshape participation landscapes. This pattern has been especially evident during election periods in polarized media environments, where expressive suppression intensifies social conflict [21]. The silencing mechanism attenuates interaction probabilities based not only on ideological distance but also on positional salience. Marginalized views are not only algorithmically downranked but also self-censored, reinforcing the visibility of dominant positions and narrowing the expressive bandwidth of public discourse [22, 33]. Such silencing is shaped not only by immediate social cues, but also by shared representational fields that cognitively anchor what is perceived as mainstream. Importantly, our results show that even under such adversarial social conditions, the proposed tolerance-aware and adaptive diversification framework remains robust. When coupled with spiral-of-silence effects and extreme influencer presence, the joint mechanism stabilizes ideological trajectories and curtails the upward spiral of polarization. Each mechanism addresses a distinct structural vulnerability: tolerance-aware filtering stabilizes short-range interactions; adaptive diversification reintegrates ideological outliers; spiral-of-silence modeling accounts for expressive asymmetry. 

5.2 Modeling Limitations and Future Work 

Despite these strengths, the framework has several limitations that open avenues for future work. First, we assume that the initially polarized population still includes individuals with intermediate opinions, who may provide bridges between the two camps. Whether the proposed mechanisms remain effective when such intermediaries are scarce or absent requires further investigation. Second, our implementation assumes static tolerance parameters and symmetric responsiveness, while real-world users evolve over time. Modeling tolerance as an endogenous variable—shaped by prior exposures, emotional valence, or network 9embedding—could yield richer dynamics and allow for the investigation of resilience thresholds or tipping points. Third, our model operates in a unidimensional ideological space, abstracting from issue-specific polarization or cognitive dissonance between overlapping identity axes. Extending the model to multidimensional opinion landscapes [42] would enhance its realism and applicability to real-world sociopolitical systems. Empirical work has shown that exposure to divergent views across multiple identity dimensions can backfire via affective polarization [6], while even homogeneous interactions can produce bipolarization under certain structural conditions [35]. Although the rule-based model assumes fixed tolerance and responsiveness parameters, the LLM experiments relax these behavioral restrictions by introducing agents with diverse backgrounds and levels of issue involvement, whose tolerance and opinion changes are generated in response to their personas, current views, and encountered content. They therefore provide a complementary test beyond the prescribed numerical update rule. Across three runs, they reproduced the central ordering of the policies: only the combined design reversed polarization in every run, although its benefit under social pressure was smaller than in the rule-based model. For future work, independent evaluation across prompts, models, and issues, together with human studies, is needed to establish broader behavioral applicability. A particularly promising direction lies in the incorporation of multiplex mainstream perception. Currently, each agent’s perceived mainstream position is approximated via proximity to a fixed influencer, which may not reflect the fragmented and siloed realities of digital publics [7]. The lack of algorithmic transparency in mainstream modeling [34] further compounds the difficulty of evaluating salience. Realistically, users are embedded in distinct interpretive communities, each with its own semantic and normative scaffolding. Modeling network-localized mainstreams or algorithmically emergent salience layers (e.g., trending topics, comment sentiment distributions) would allow us to assess the resilience of consensus under multi-polar dominance conditions. While validating these mechanisms in real-world platforms remains challenging due to limited API access and user data, prior field experiments on ideological exposure [23] offer promising templates. Hybrid agent-user simulations and natural experiments—such as ranking perturbations—could bridge theory and deployment. We also encourage future research to conduct field deployments and controlled A/B testing of tolerance-aware and diversity-sensitive algorithms across real platform contexts. Hybrid experimental frameworks—such as human-in-the-loop simulations and agent-based system emulators—could bridge the gap between theoretical modeling and real-world behavioral dynamics. Such validation is essential for establishing construct validity, calibrating real-world design thresholds, and informing the normative trajectories of algorithmic governance. 

5.3 Policy Implications 

Our findings motivate empirical testing of exposure policies calibrated to users’ receptivity to disagreement. First, platforms shall incorporate tolerance-aware filtering to prevent counterproductive exposure beyond users’ psychological thresholds [36]. By dynamically adjusting exposure probabilities based on inferred receptivity, platforms can preserve engagement without triggering repulsive polarization. Second, adaptive diversification mechanisms should be targeted toward users at extremes, rather than applied uniformly. Algorithmic diversity must be context-sensitive, not absolute [6, 42]. Centrists benefit from relevance and stability; extremists require calibrated nudges toward epistemic moderation. Third, addressing the spiral of silence requires that platforms go beyond content-based optimization. Visibility algorithms should account for social pressure dynamics—ensuring that dissenting but reasonable voices are not structurally silenced. Recommender systems shall integrate perceived mainstream modeling and adjust amplification accordingly. Collectively, these recommendations suggest that algorithmic neutrality is insufficient. Recommender systems shall be proactively optimized for epistemic resilience and ideological inclusion [9, 45]. Regulatory frameworks and platform guidelines should adopt exposure diversity not as a fixed quota, but as an adaptive, behaviorally grounded design variable. Platforms can model and report on polarization metrics as part of algorithmic impact assessments. Our model provides a theoretically grounded and empirically testable framework for such evaluations.

# B DETAILS OF THE LLM-P OWERED TESTS 

B.1 Agents, Feeds, and Controls 

Agents and issue. One hundred persona-based agents [1], each running on DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (API identifier 

deepseek-flash ) with reasoning disabled and temperature 0.7, populate the simulated platform. Modelled as residents of Nova, a fictional U.S. state, agents have an age, occupation, place of residence and short background, without party identification. Discourse centers on one issue from our planned human experiment: the share of wages that workers must contribute to a public retirement fund, which the legislature can set between 4% and 24%. Mapping a rate p to x = ( p − 4) /20 equates one percentage point to 0.05. Prompts frame the trade-off symmetrically, as larger pensions later at the cost of lower take-home pay now, or the reverse. View-reporting prompts state receptivity to nearby arguments and possible repulsion from distant ones, without specifying a numerical threshold or change size. The exact wording is given in Appendix B.5. 

Feeds. In each round, every agent posts at its current rate; a previous post is reused while that rate is unchanged after rounding to 0.1 percentage point. Three candidate draws per reader each select an available post at random and display it with the recommender’s probability Pij (Section 2), evaluated at the reader’s rate at the start of the round and the post’s rate. Agents read displayed posts sequentially and report a new rate after each. Feed selection, random draws and measurement are handled in code (Fig. 7). The platform regularizes adaptive exposure as Ei = D1/ max( dm,i ,0.02) . For each arm, 10 rule-based reference runs use the same feed rules, initialization distributions and number of rounds, with independently drawn starts and the attraction–repulsion update [4] at R = 0 .25 and T = ˆT .

Response to distance. Before the platform runs, agents read single posts from a bank of 27 civil posts written at 6–22%, checked by a rater from another model family, Gemini 3.8 Flash ( google/gemini-3.8-flash ). Readers held rates on the same grid or 0.5–1.5 points from the post, and with 90 no-post controls this gave 1,224 trials. Of six update rules fitted to the grid trials, a rule with fixed step sizes fit best (Appendix B.4). Agents moved about 0.7 points toward posts within 5 points ( T = 0 .25 ) and about 0.9 points away from more distant posts, in both directions, and kept their rate when shown no post. Close-range trials revealed a directional asymmetry absent from ARM. Among readers at least 2 points from either end of the scale, an agent 0.5 points below a post adopted its rate in 40–56% of trials, depending on its camp, whereas an agent 0.5 points above the post did so in only 12–27%. Over 0.5–1.5 points, these readers moved 0.13 points (SE 0.02) further toward higher than lower posts, despite the disposition’s bidirectional attraction. 

Mirrored reading as a control. To control directional asymmetry, a fair coin decides whether an eligible post is read from the agent’s position xi or the counterfactual position 2yj − xi, at the same distance on the other side of the post. For a mirrored read, code reflects the reported change back onto the agent’s position and clips to [0 , 1] . Posts at the reader’s own rate, or with a mirror 14 position outside the scale (including influencer posts), are read normally. This symmetrizes eligible responses in expectation while retaining LLM-generated changes, so the platform population is a controlled, symmetrized one rather than an unmodified LLM population. 

Common tolerance and compatibility checks. Feeds use one estimated population threshold, not learned individual tolerances. A 20-round pilot of random and ultra-personalized feeds, which do not depend on T , located the mean response’s attraction– repulsion boundary at ˆT = 0 .175 (3.5 points). Main runs were conditional on ten compatibility checks (Appendix B.4): distant repulsion in both directions, approximately balanced nearby attraction, and C1–C4, including adaptive reversal, in platform simulations resampling the measured updates.

B.3 Results by Arm and Additional Details 

Table 1 reports the change in camp gap, the final variance and the number of reversing runs and reference seeds for every arm of Studies 1 and 3. The three runs, with Step 1 and the pilot, required about 792,000 LLM calls at a total cost of about US$52; no call in the three runs failed. In Study 1, agents starting in the outer third moved 2.3 percentage points toward the center on average (1.5–3.0 across runs), and one tolerance-aware run narrowly crossed the reversal threshold ( −0.051 ). Under random exposure, half of displayed posts lay beyond ˆT . The combined feed ended 0.152 below the tolerance-aware random feed on average (0.084–0.196) while displaying fewer posts across the midpoint (0.2% versus 1.2%). The approximately fixed LLM step sizes offer one explanation for differences from distance-scaled ARM responses. Under silence, agents posted in 22–80% of rounds. The combined feed’s variance advantage was 0.008–0.020 on average across cues (0.001–0.033 in single runs), versus 0.029–0.057 in the reference. Personalized-feed variance was also lower on the LLM platform (0.045 versus 0.119 in condition a). Adding influencers did not raise variance over the 30-round horizon in any run. 

B.4 Rule Fits and Pilot Checks 

We fitted six update rules to the 486 trials in which readers held rates on the grid, using five-fold cross-validation grouped by persona identifier. Their cross-validated log-likelihoods were 923.1 for fixed steps, 910.4 for attraction–repulsion with separate attraction and repulsion rates, 895.8 for attraction–repulsion with one rate, 814.1 for linear assimilation, 781.1 for bounded confidence and 750.7 for no change. Table 2 reports ten compatibility checks, all of which passed. Outcome checks use platform simulations resampling measured updates by distance and position, rather than new LLM runs. Because the gates require the expected qualitative policy outcomes, passing them establishes compatibility of the selected response process; it does not independently corroborate C1–C4. The pilot estimates a common ˆT from the sign change in the mean update toward a post, interpolating between distance bins and rounding to 0.025. It does not estimate each persona’s tolerance.
