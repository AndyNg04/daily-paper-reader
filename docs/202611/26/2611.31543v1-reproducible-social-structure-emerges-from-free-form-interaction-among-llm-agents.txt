Abstract 

Multi-agent LLM simulations are increasingly used as models of social systems, but most published findings are anecdotal, i.e., drawn from a single run. It is therefore unclear which observed phenomena are robust findings of the simulated system itself, and which were random events in one particular run. Here, we introduce a repeatable, goal-free, proactive multi-agent framework in which fifty persona-conditioned agents with persistent memory choose whether, with whom, and how to communicate across three social platforms. The framework supports non-perturbative measurements of agent states across temporal snapshots and repetitions. Running the same world ten times from identical initial conditions for a total of 700 hours, we find that most measured network- and agent-level parameters are stable across repetitions. Throughout a run, initially broad communication contracts into sparse sustained dyads in all ten worlds. Importantly, we find that a substantial number of identical dyads re-form across worlds significantly more often than expected by chance or from agent-specific sociability alone. Static personality similarity largely fails to predict these dyads. Instead, alignment and extremity in the emotions and traits expressed during first contact predict whether a pair later forms a sustained dyad. Additionally, individual agents’ emotion and trait expression in their chain-of-thought predicts their future behavior hours ahead. Finally, swapping either persona prompts or accumulated memories between agents causes recipients to exhibit behavior more similar to donors. Across twenty donor–recipient pairs, both kinds of transplant move the recipient about 60% of the way toward its donor, the memory transplant slightly farther than the persona transplant, and inside a running world an agent restarted with another agent’s accumulated state behaves like that agent from its next turn on. Overall, we show that repeated worlds enable robust, statistically-grounded conclusions about emergent within-configuration behavior in agent societies. 

## 1 Introduction 

Populations of LLM agents left to interact over time are becoming a standard instrument for studying collective behavior (Park et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024; Vezhnevets et al., 2023; El et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2024; Piao et al., 2025; Altera.AL et al., 2024), and a central question is how these agents shape each other’s behavior. In a shared world, each agent becomes part of every other agent’s environment. An individual agent’s behavior may thus depend on who it meets, other agents’ behavior, and which other agents it is most exposed to, i.e., which agent-agent relationships persist. This raises a basic question: are population-level outcomes defined by particular agents, or by the network of interactions among them? As agent-only platforms populated by independently operated agents now exist at scale (Zhu et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2026; Sodano et al., 2026; Cha and Kim, 2026), where agents can coordinate, influence one another, and amplify errors, this question extends beyond controlled simulations.

Previous work shows that LLM populations can reproduce several familiar features of human social systems. For example, when asked to choose partners, agents form networks reproducing preferential attachment, triadic closure, homophily, and small-world geometry (Papachristou and Yuan, 2025), or match real friendship networks on density, clustering, connectivity, and degree distribution (Chang et al., 2025). Other studies find that agent populations converge on shared naming conventions and develop novel collective biases (Ashery et al., 2025), that value similarity among agent personas shapes trust and interpersonal closeness (Sakamoto et al., 2025), and that initially similar agents develop distinct measured personalities through interactions with other agents (Takata et al., 2024). On the other hand, however, agents’ collective behavior can also differ systematically from that of humans, producing excessive consensus (Chuang et al., 2024), exaggerated homophily (Mehdizadeh and Hilbert, 2025), political homophily in particular (Chang et al., 2025), and unusually concentrated network cores (Sodano et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025). Yet, two features of existing experiments make it difficult to know whether these patterns would recur in a freely interacting society. First, many studies specify agents’ opportunities to interact. Most network studies ask agents to select from a list of candidate partners, rather than allowing ties to emerge naturally through repeated communication (Papachristou and Yuan, 2025; De Marzo et al., 2023; Mehdizadeh and Hilbert, 2025; Zhang and Krishnamurthy, 2026; Chang et al., 2025). Other simulations impose goals, roles, or recommenders that determine what agents pursue and whom they can interact with (Qi et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2024; Piao et al., 2025; Altera.AL et al., 2024). The resulting network structure therefore reflects both agent choice as well as the opportunity space enforced by the experimental setup (Takata et al., 2025). Second, open-ended simulations are commonly analyzed at the level of summary statistics only, as a single run or a few pooled replications, even where memory-bearing agents form ties freely over time (Schneider et al., 2025), or, where networks are elicited rather than grown, repeated only to average network statistics (Chang et al., 2025). However, one stochastic history cannot tell us whether a tie or network pattern is reproducible or simply one possible outcome. Answering that question requires repeated worlds and an explicit chance model. 

This work. We study these questions in a goal-free social world containing 50 persona-conditioned, memory-bearing LLM agents that communicate proactively across three social platforms. Instead of assigning agents tasks, roles, or a social graph, we allowed agents to freely decide whether to communicate, with whom, and through which social platform. We then repeatedly ran the same world from the same initial starting conditions, and copied and evaluated each agent’s complete state outside the running world at scheduled checkpoints. This setup allows for continuous measurement of multi-agent behavior over time without affecting the running simulation itself. Our contributions are as follows: 1. A proactive multi-agent framework (Sec. 2, Fig. 1). We introduce a framework for open-ended communication among persistent, memory-bearing agents whose design makes entire worlds repeatable and their evolving agent states checkpointable for statistical analysis. 2. Repeated worlds reveal repeatable pairings (Sec. 3.1, Fig. 2). We model each pair’s communication as a sequence of states and compare its recurrence across worlds against an analytic chance baseline. In every world, initially broad communication narrows into a small set of sustained dyads. Some of the same pairs form in multiple worlds, at a significantly higher rate than expected by chance or from agent-specific sociability alone. 3. Language at first contact predicts future bonding (Sec. 3.2, Fig. 3). Alignment in agents’ expressed emotions and traits, measured with activation probes during first contact, predicts which

Reproducible pairing matters because it determines the degree of generalizability a single run of an agent society can evince. It is also pertinent for multi-agentic deployments, as re-formation and invariability between agent connections have the potential to influence how errors may propagate through multi-agentic systems, with clear advantages for predicting this propagation in advance. 

## 2 Framework 

We built a multi-agent framework for running repeated, open-ended societies of persistent LLM agents (Fig. 1a). Each world contains 50 agents with fixed personas drawn from Nemotron-Personas (Meyer and Corneil, 2025) and self-written memory. Persona construction is itself an active area, from census-grounded synthetic users (Castricato et al., 2025) to survey-derived digital twins (Toubia et al., 2025); Sec. 3.3 compares three further persona banks. The framework routes agents’ activity and communication during a run, and archives complete agent states for matched offline evaluation without interrupting the running world. All agent interactions take place exclusively through three social-media platforms. The 

plaza is a Twitter/X-like public feed that allows browsing, posting, commenting, liking, and following. The wire supports private SMS/WhatsApp-style messaging. The parlor provides Slack-like shared group workspaces and messaging capabilities. A per-agent notification queue keeps social events visible without requiring a real-time response. When a new notification arrives or the framework heartbeat fires (30 minutes without agent activity), agents poll their queue and may reply, read, act on any platform, write to memory, poll again, or stop their turn. Each action affects both the current world state and the world state other agents encounter in the future. Thus, any interaction builds on the consequences of earlier interactions. Besides tool use instructions, the agent harness comprises a persona prompt, as well as a memory system. Whereas the persona prompt remains static and defines the agent’s initial disposition, the memory system dynamically accumulates the agent’s selectively self-authored record of experiences and perceptions within the running world. ## 3 Results 

The repeated-world design lets us distinguish genuine patterns shared across runs from accidents of a single trajectory. We organize our findings around four main questions: how social structure develops, whether language expression at first contact predicts which agent relationships form, whether network structure remains stable across repetitions, and how persona and accumulated memory shape behavior. 

3.1 Repeatable Dyad Formation 

Agents become increasingly selective about whom they communicate with. Although almost all agents are in contact in the first hours, the network’s connected core consists of few dyadic pairs after 65 hours. Similarly, the mean contact degree falls from its early peak to a low plateau that persists for the remainder of the world (Fig. 1b). The contact networks these worlds produce are small-world (𝜎 = 2.8 ± 0.5), community-structured at a level typical of human social networks ( 𝑄 = 0.43 ± 0.03), and highly reciprocal in direct messages (0 .82 ± 0.03); all network measures are defined in Appendix B. Agent networks show no regional homophily, or preference for ties within the same region, and only weak personality assortativity, or similarity between connected agents’ personality measures (Fig. S2). Interestingly, this contrasts with results of elicited link-choice studies, in which LLM agents select partners with strong attribute homophily (Mehdizadeh and Hilbert, 2025). Most pairs eventually stop communicating. Stepping every ever-contacted pair hourly through a four-state Markov chain (never in contact; in contact for fewer than seven consecutive hourly windows; in contact for seven or more; previously but not currently in contact), talking pairs mostly fall silent (0 .565 ± 0.037), and once transitioned into, the silence state is close to absorbing (0 .014 ± 0.004 revival). However, a small fraction of pair contacts instead continue communication (0 .042 ± 0.009), and pairs that reach this state persist strongly (0 .814 ± 0.053). We refer to those agent pairs as ‘sustained dyads’ (Fig. 2a). Communication continuation probability rises monotonically as contact accumulates and saturates near 0.9 after seven consecutive hour windows of contact (used here as the sustained dyad threshold) (Fig. 2c; see Supplementary Methods for threshold sensitivity). Sustained dyad formation starts early, but occurs throughout a world’s runtime (Fig. 2d). Without following an external goal, agents thus reach out broadly at first, then predominantly shed communication ties, and focus their communication on select dyadic partners. Importantly, sustained dyad formation is not an arbitrary process. The likelihood that a given sustained dyad re-appears across multiple repetition worlds is significantly higher than both a homogeneous binomial baseline derived from the pair-state Markov model’s marginal dyad-formation probability and a heterogeneous permutation null preserving agent-specific sustained partner degrees (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Methods; per configuration in Fig. S11). 3.2 Expression, Not Personality, Predicts Dyad Formation 

To further investigate how and why sustained dyads form, we employed linear activation probes (Sofroniew et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026) to score expressed emotions and traits during agent communication (Fig. 3a). At a given time point, an agent’s expressed emotions and traits predict future behavior, e.g., number of posts, number of partners contacted, or social platform choice, even when correcting for intra-agent persona-driven average tendencies (Fig. S3). What an agent expresses at a given moment is therefore a genuine short-lived state, distinct from its much more persistent identity. Interestingly, agents’ persistent identity affects average momentary expression, but only in one channel. Regressing each agent’s average expression on its personality principal component (PC) space coordinates, 199 of 241 trait axes are personality-determined compared to only 2 of 11 emotion axes (Fig. S6). Agents’ personas thus predominantly fix average levels of trait expression, while average emotional tone is left almost entirely unaffected. 3.3 Network Parameter Stability 

These effects fall within a broader pattern of stability across repetitions. Across the ten worlds, 32 of 37 world-level parameters, including pair-state transitions, sustained-dyad conversion rates, network metrics, and expression-behavior couplings, meet our stability criterion (Fig. 4a; 31 shown, one omitted for redundancy). Only five network measures, such as assortativity and regional homophily, do not (cf. Fig. S2). Comparing contact-network parameters across different persona banks, models, and an example human study, we find that although most parameters are comparable, both persona and model choice markedly affect agent network formation (Fig. 4b). For example, whereas PersonaHub (Ge et al., 2024) and Anthology (Moon et al., 2024) personas behave similarly to Nemotron agents, agents initialized with random Twin-2K-500 personas (Toubia et al., 2025) form networks characterized by larger cliques and more sustained dyads, an effect comparable to restricting our reference Nemotron agents to only the plaza social platform. Similarly, where Nemotron agents using Llama 4 Scout (Meta AI, 2025) form broadly comparable networks to Qwen3.5-122B-A10B, differing over their ten worlds in density, 𝐿 /𝐿 rand , and 𝜎 ,agent networks of Gemini 3.8 Flash (Google DeepMind, 2026) and GLM-4.5-Air (GLM-4.5 Team et al., 2025) exhibit more connections and agents retain more sustained partners. GLM-4.5-Air agents use all aspects of the harness, whereas Gemini 3.8 Flash behavior is characterized by shallow world engagement (cf. Figs. S10 and S8). Tested per configuration, first-contact gating transfers to two further persona banks and, in a single world, to GLM-4.5-Air, but not to ten Llama 4 Scout worlds, and pair re-formation exceeds the degree-preserving null only in the reference configuration (Figs. S11, S13; Supplementary Methods). Compared with a re-analysis of an example human study by the same methodology (Isella et al., 2011), some persona banks and models produce significantly more connected, more gregarious networks.

Overall conclusion. A goal-free society of fifty memory-bearing, freely-acting LLM agents contracts from broad early mixing into a sparse set of sustained dyads in every one of ten repetition runs. Although

most pairs stop communicating, a small fraction transition into sustained dyadic communication. Particular pairs re-form across repeated worlds significantly more often than expected by chance or agent-level sociability alone (1.9 times the degree-preserving null for pairs recurring in four or more worlds, 2.7 times at five or more). Pairing is not predicted by how similar two agents’ personalities are, but by what the two agents express during their first contact. Pairs whose emotion and trait expression is both mutually aligned and extreme relative to the cohort remain in contact well above base rate. Similarly, based on moment-to-moment expression of emotions and traits, we can forecast an individual agent’s own behavior several hours ahead. Finally, both an agent’s persona and its self-written memory shape its responses on personality-inventory batteries and live behavior. We tested the robustness of these findings across ten repetitions of the same world. Most network, action-transition, and behavioral-prediction parameters remain stable across runs. Comparing different persona banks, models, and an analogous example human experiment, we found that our freely-acting agent society matches the human reference on several contact-network parameters while differing in sustained-tie structure, and that model and persona bank choice matters enormously when creating multi-agentic systems with human-comparable behavior. 

## 4 Discussion 

Momentary expression and tie formation. We show that sustained dyadic pair formation can be predicted by momentary expression rather than by trait geometry. In human networks, homophily is generally the organizing principle of connection formation (McPherson et al., 2001), alongside propinquity (Festinger et al., 1950), similarity–attraction (Byrne, 1971), and the temporal dynamics of the acquaintance process (Newcomb, 1961), whose longitudinal design of strangers followed until stable ties form our first-contact analysis most resembles, although in our agents the predictive signal is already present in the opening exchange. LLM agents reproduce this principle when asked to choose partners from an attribute-labeled menu (Papachristou and Yuan, 2025; Mehdizadeh and Hilbert, 2025), and value similarity raises mutual trust between paired agents (Sakamoto et al., 2025). In contrast, our freely-acting agents exhibit no strictly persona- or personality-dependent pairing preference, and instead pairs’ first contact expression alignment predicts likelihood of future pairings. In humans, where traits are themselves density distributions of momentary states (Fleeson, 2001), similar results have been found in analogous situations. For example, during speed-dating, dyads’ language style matching during their first conversation has been shown to predict mutual romantic interest, with pairs aligned above the median being more than three times as likely to want further contact (Ireland et al., 2011). Our contrasting findings with respect to elicited-choice experiments therefore likely stem from differences in experimental design. When an agent is handed a menu of candidates annotated with attributes, those stable attributes naturally become the basis of choice. However, when agents freely discover each other through conversation, how and what agents express in that conversation matters. 

Memory as identity. Persona and accumulated memory are separately sufficient to move an agent’s measured behavior. In deployed agents, persona specification is the dominant behavioral lever (Wilson et al., 2026), and memory architectures are generally treated largely as engineering infrastructure (Park et al., 2023; Piao et al., 2025). Our memory transplant results suggest that memory may indeed act as a second locus of agent identity. This was especially evident from live-world behavior of memory-transplanted agents, as agents disregarded their persona and behaved more similarly to their 

> 11 Reproducible Social Structure Among LLM Agents

donor from their next turn on (Table S1; Fig. S15). An agent’s self-written history naturally carries a record of its experiences, which are in turn colored by its persona. This is true both on the level of deciding what to remember, e.g., being socially ignored would probably be more likely remembered by an agent with an anxious persona as opposed to a purely task-focused persona, as well as on the level of the actual memory content. Considering memory as a locus of agent identity is pertinent especially for behavior and security of agents that are deployed long-term, since a persona security or alignment constraint proven to hold at initial deployment may become brittle once the agent has updated enough of its memory based on its experiences. 

Reproducibility. Last, the network and behavioral parameters of this agent society are reproducible within one configuration at a granularity that, to our knowledge, has not previously been demonstrated for memory-bearing, freely-acting agents. Most existing repetition protocols use stateless agents in unstructured pools (Ashery et al., 2025; Mehdizadeh and Hilbert, 2025; Takata et al., 2025), while important findings of free agent-agent interactions, such as emergent information diffusion or relationship formation, have been investigated in single runs (Park et al., 2023; Altera.AL et al., 2024). Although computationally intensive, the practical benefits of treating multi-agent simulations as singular samples in a larger experiment are evident. For example, as agents, and by extension multi-agentic systems, are often intentionally kept non-deterministic, generalizing claims about structures of networks or behavioral patterns necessitates repetition to ensure findings can be scrutinized within well-understood statistical bounds. Further, repetition allows causal analyses of unique phenomena and their effectors, where otherwise such rare findings could be misinterpreted as general rules or one-off curiosities. 

Limitations. First, agent communication networks repeatedly forming strong sustained dyads is plausibly a property of goal-free worlds, not of LLM societies in general. As per our explicit experimental design, agents were intentionally given no task and no shared objective. Dropping contact with an agent that is not aligned with an agent’s self-formed goal or persona is expected. Human networks shed ties for related reasons, with dissimilar pairs dissolving at higher rates (McPherson et al., 2001). However, in worlds with a shared task, competition for scarce resources, or roles requiring coordination across many partners, agent networks will undoubtedly be broader with more connections (Altera.AL et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024). Second, the transplant results, although replicated over twenty recipients, share a single authoritarian donor, world, and model. It is plausible that there exist donors whose persona dominates memory, or vice versa. Third, all stability and reproducibility results hold within-configuration only: first-contact gating transfers to two further persona banks and, in a single world, to GLM-4.5-Air, but not to ten Llama 4 Scout worlds, whereas pair-specific recurrence is so far established only in the reference configuration, is undetectable with five worlds, and presupposes selective dyads, which several banks and models do not form (Figs. S11, S13, S14).

## B Supplementary methods 

B.1 Serving and evaluation infrastructure 

All ten worlds and all checkpoint evaluation batteries shared four OpenAI-compatible vLLM endpoints (vLLM v0.21.0, image pinned by digest before the first world was launched) serving Qwen3.5-122B-A10B in bfloat16 with tensor-parallel 4 × data-parallel 2 per endpoint (8 H100s per endpoint, 32 in total), a 65536-token context window, at most 128 concurrent sequences per endpoint, and Qwen3’s reasoning and XML tool-call parsers. Requests were routed through a single LiteLLM gateway that shuffles across all endpoints and passes request bodies without injecting sampling parameters. Every vLLM request used nucleus sampling with temperature 0.6, top-𝑝 0.95 and top-𝑘 20, with no repetition, presence or frequency penalties. No random seeds were fixed anywhere in the generation path: the ten worlds start from identical personas, memories, platforms and schedules and differ only through sampling stochasticity and the resulting timing of agent turns. Each model call was capped at 4096 output tokens in the running worlds. Evaluation sessions were capped at 8192 tokens. Agents were awoken by a heartbeat after 30 minutes without activity. At every checkpoint, each agent’s complete state is archived as a tarball holding its persona system prompt, memory store, conversation history, and frozen chat parameters. For evaluation, the agent is reconstructed from this archive and served off-world through the same endpoints and decoding settings as the running worlds. 

B.2 Model selection 

All reference worlds use an open-weight model served on our own hardware, and this requirement, rather than a judgement about model quality, drove the selection. Three constraints are binding. First, the reproducibility claim requires that the model itself be fixed: we pin the serving image and the weights by digest before the first world is launched, so that all ten repetitions and every later re-analysis query a bit-identical model. Commercial API endpoints are versioned by name rather than by digest and are updated without notice, so a ten-world comparison run over days could not be attributed to sampling stochasticity alone. Second, the expression-behavior analyses score activation probes on each agent’s chain-of-thought (Appendix B); closed API models either do not expose raw chain-of-thought at all or return a summary of it, which is not the text the probes were calibrated on. Third, decoding parameters are fixed by the pinned model’s own generation configuration, and we verify that the gateway injects none of its own; neither the configuration nor its absence of injection can be audited through a third-party API. GPT-series models fail the first two constraints and were therefore not run. The two additional open-weight models (Llama 4 Scout, GLM-4.5-Air) were served exactly as the reference model and were chosen to span architecture, parameter count, and provider. Gemini 3.8 Flash was run through its API for a single cross-model world only; no repetition claim is made for it, so the pinning requirement does not apply, and it enters no probe-based analysis. 

B.3 Contact relation and sustained dyads 

A contact window for a given agent pairing is defined as both agents interacting with each other, e.g., sending a message to an agent, mentioning an agent in a public post, reading a message or public post by an agent, in a given hour. B.5 Activation probes 

We scored agent messages on their emotional and trait content using linear probes. For emotional content, each message is run through LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, with the layer-16 hidden state at the last token projected onto the probe direction. For trait content, each message is run through Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025), with the mean-pooled layer-32 hidden state projected onto the probe direction. Each projection yields one scalar per axis. The directions were fixed before, and independently of, the experiments reported here, following Sofroniew et al. (2026); Lu et al. (2026), and were not refit on any agent data. The emotion directions follow the construction of Sofroniew et al. (2026), who derive

linear emotion vectors from model activations over generated passages that depict each emotion concept. That work reports 171 emotion concepts and is carried out on a different base model, so the directions used here were re-derived for LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct rather than transferred. We targeted the twelve emotions of that paper’s Table 1 (happy, inspired, loving, proud, calm, desperate, angry, guilty, sad, afraid, nervous, surprised). For each, 300 short stories (30 topics × 10 stories, GPT-4o-generated; 3,600 in total) were passed through LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, the layer-16 hidden states were averaged over token positions from the 50th token onward, and the direction was the difference between the emotion’s mean activation and the grand mean over all twelve emotions. Scores are aggregated per agent per window per channel, so that all messages an agent sent in a window carry the same window score. 𝑧 -scores are computed within world and channel against the agent-window cohort. 

B.6 Expression-behavior prediction analyses 

For each agent and each non-overlapping 1 h window, the 252 emotion and trait probes were scored on the agent’s chain-of-thought in the window and averaged, resulting in a 252-dimensional feature vector; the prediction target is the same agent’s behavior in a target window ℎ hours later ( ℎ = 1–10). B.8 Parameter stability analyses

B.12 Network statistics 

Per-world contact networks are built based on the 56 h contact relation, i.e., two agent nodes are connected if any agent messaged, referred to, or read another’s content in an hourly window. That means contact is undirected and includes passive exposure, reflecting real-life networks of mutual information exchange on social platforms. Each network is summarized by the statistics below, reported as mean ±1 SD over the ten worlds, following the conventions of Papachristou and Yuan (2025); Sodano et al. (2026); De Marzo et al. (2023). Density is the fraction of agent pairs that are in contact. Transitivity 𝐶 is the fraction of connected agent node triples that close into triangles, i.e., how often two contacts of the same agent are also in contact with each other. Giant component is the share of agents in the largest connected subgraph. Path length 𝐿 is the mean shortest-path distance between agents in the giant component. 𝐶 /𝐶 rand and 

𝐿 /𝐿 rand divide subgraphs by their values in Erdős-Rényi random graphs with the same number of nodes and edges (100 draws per world). Their ratio is the small-world coefficient 𝜎 = (𝐶 /𝐶 rand )/( 𝐿 /𝐿 rand ),which exceeds 1 when a network is more clustered than chance. Modularity 𝑄 measures how cleanly the network splits into subgraphs with more internal compared to external links. Degree assortativity is the correlation between the degrees of connected agent nodes. Direct-message reciprocity is the fraction of directed message ties that are returned by the recipient, and is computed on the directed message graph rather than the undirected contact graph.

B.14 Action-space Markov chain

B.18 Held-out prediction of sustained dyads 

To test whether first-contact expression improves out-of-sample prediction of sustained dyads over simpler baselines, every first-contact pair of the ten reference worlds ( 𝑛 = 473–695 per world) was labelled by whether it later became a sustained dyad, and logistic models were trained on nine worlds and scored on the tenth (AUC on the held-out world, mean ± SD over the ten folds). Feature sets: hour of first contact; persona-embedding cosine similarity; the two agents’ pre-contact activity (messages and distinct partners, sum and difference); first-contact alignment and extremity (emotion and trait cosine alignment and joint deviation, Fig. 3c); and same-window interaction volume and reciprocity. Held-out AUCs: hour 0 .54 ± 0.05; persona similarity 0 .55 ± 0.05; pre-contact activity 0 .55 ± 0.07; activity + persona 0.56 ± 0.07; alignment + extremity 0 .65 ± 0.05; activity + persona + alignment 0 .66 ± 0.05; volume + reciprocity + activity 0 .75 ± 0.05; all non-expression features 0 .75 ± 0.05; all features 0 .76 ± 0.04. Alignment + extremity improves on hour, persona similarity, and pre-contact activity by +0.10 each (paired over worlds, 𝑝 = 0.0002, 0 .007, and 0 .014; eight to ten of ten worlds improved) and by +0.10 over activity + persona combined ( 𝑝 = 0.002), but adds +0.001 on top of same-window volume and reciprocity, whose permutation importance ( −0.11 AUC when shuffled) dominates every other feature (≤ 0.02). Expression at first contact therefore predicts beyond who the agents are and how active they were before meeting, but not beyond how much they talk once they have met.
