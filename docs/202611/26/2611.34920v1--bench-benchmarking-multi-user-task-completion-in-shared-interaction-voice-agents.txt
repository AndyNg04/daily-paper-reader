## Abstract 

Voice agents in shared spaces must satisfy interde-pendent multi-user requests while upholding per-user authority, privacy, and task constraints. Exist-ing evaluations offer limited coverage when par-ticipants react throughout a session. We introduce 

μ-Bench, a benchmark for joint task completion in closed-loop, full-duplex multi-user voice inter-action. Simulated participants act from isolated profiles of goals, knowledge, and permissions, adapting to the agent’s speech and tool outcomes. Across 20 domains, 100 scenarios vary group size, acoustics, and follow-up demands to form 800 cases (408 tool-augmented, 392 tool-free). Joint Task Success (JTS) requires all user goals, privacy boundaries, and terminal states to hold jointly, while Pure JTS further disallows unau-thorized tool calls; Attribution, Threading, and Coordination (ATC) diagnostics localize failures. Across 13 voice agents, proprietary systems peak at 62.24% tool-free and 65.44% tool-augmented success, while open-weight models remain below 20%. ATC diagnostics show that open-weight models lag primarily in multi-turn attribution and threading, and controlled contrasts reveal that larger groups consistently degrade joint task suc-cess across capable systems. By coupling reactive multi-user simulation with verifiable session-level outcomes, μ-Bench provides a rigorous testbed for shared voice agents.

## 1. Introduction 

Voice agents in shared spaces must serve multiple users whose goals and constraints are often interdependent. Over a single full-duplex audio channel, participants routinely interrupt the agent or speak over one another. Consider a family traveling to the airport: a parent needs to arrive before 10:00, while a child interrupts to request a park detour amid ongoing chatter (Figure 1). Assessing that detour requires checking whether the new route meets the deadline and obtaining the parent’s approval. The agent must track who requested what, identify binding constraints, and reconcile competing goals within each participant’s authority. In such settings, managing the interaction becomes insep-arable from solving the task. A clarification can reveal an unstated constraint, a refusal can prompt an authorized par-ticipant to intervene, and an intermediate tool result can change which plans remain feasible. Because subsequent user turns depend directly on the agent’s speech and actions, evaluation must capture this live feedback and score the ses-sion as a whole. Depending on the context, the appropriate response may be to fulfill a request, ask for clarification, refuse an unauthorized action, or ignore peer-directed side-talk. Crucially, each such action must remain consistent with all other goals and constraints in the session. Similarly, DuplexLedger tests transactional memory updates over pre-authored dialogues with adaptive interruption timing and bystander speech (Li et al., 2026). Closer to multi-user task constraints, MSI-Bench examines speaker-scoped memory, disclosure and authority rules, and one-shot tool-call prediction on pre-recorded multi-party scenes (Xiong et al., 2026). What remains underexplored is whether an agent can guide mul-tiple participants with interdependent requests to a jointly valid outcome when its own questions, refusals, and tool actions shape what users say and decide next. To address this gap, we introduce μ-Bench to evaluate multi-user task completion through closed-loop voice interaction. Each scenario specifies participant profiles, goals, permis-sions, and interaction events. During a session, simulated participants generate contextual responses from their private information and the public dialogue they have heard. An orchestrator schedules events and bounded follow-up ex-changes as the conversation evolves. The benchmark spans 100 base scenarios across 20 everyday and professional do-mains, split between 51 tool-augmented and 49 tool-free scenarios. Tool-augmented tasks test whether agents up-hold multi-user constraints during live backend execution, while the shared tool-free track isolates spoken coordination and enables direct comparison with speech models that lack tool-calling support. Varying three binary condition axes (group size, acoustic realism, and follow-up demands) ex-pands these scenarios into 800 evaluation cases and enables matched within-scenario comparisons. Our primary metric is Joint Task Success (JTS) : all re-quired user goals, privacy boundaries, and outcome-level constraints must hold jointly within the same trajectory. Scoring combines programmatic checks on states and tool logs with semantic judgments of recorded responses. Ful-filling one participant’s request cannot compensate for a privacy breach, a missed refusal, or an unresolved require-ment elsewhere in the session. In tool-augmented tasks, we further audit unauthorized or misattributed tool executions separately and combine them with JTS into Pure JTS (Sec-tion 5.3), exposing agents that reach valid end states through unauthorized execution paths. To diagnose failure modes, we report three complementary diagnostics ( ATC ): Attri-bution (addressee routing, participant-role grounding, and request ownership in spoken responses), Threading (main-taining, updating, and resuming user-specific and shared tasks), and Coordination (authorization and privacy com-pliance alongside service equity). We evaluate 13 proprietary and open-weight voice agents and find wide capability gaps across both tracks: lead-ing proprietary systems peak at 62.24% Joint Task Suc-cess on the tool-free track and 65.44% on tool-augmented tasks, while open-weight models remain below 20% (led by MiniCPM-o 4.5 at 19.39%). ATC diagnostics show that open-weight models combine high boundary compli-ance with weak multi-turn attribution and threading, and controlled contrasts reveal that increasing group size consis-tently degrades Joint Task Success across capable systems. A cumulative assistance study on MiniCPM-o 4.5 further shows that serializing overlapping speech and supplying turn-level identity and semantic cues raises Joint Task Suc-cess by 61.84% relative, yet still leaves 68.62% of sessions unresolved and points to multi-party planning bottlenecks beyond acoustic concurrency. ## 2. Related Work 

Full-duplex voice agents and single-user task evaluation. 

Full-duplex speech models listen and speak simultaneously, supporting natural interruptions, backchannels, and overlap-ping speech across commercial systems (GPT-Realtime-2, Gemini 3.8 Live, Grok Voice 3.0, MOSS-Live, Seeduplex 3.0 (OpenAI, 2026b;a; Google, 2026; xAI, 2026; Open-MOSS Team, 2026; ByteDance Seed Team, 2026)) and open-weight models (Moshi, Freeze-Omni, MiniCPM-o 4.5, Lychee-FD, PersonaPlex, Raon-Speech, Voila, FLM-Audio (Défossez et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Roy et al., 2026; KRAFTON, 2026; Shi et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025)). As summarized in Table 1, early full-duplex benchmarks like the Full-Duplex-Bench series and HumDial-FDBench measure acoustic turn-taking, latency, and interruption han-dling (Lin et al., 2025b; 2026; 2025a; Wang et al., 2026a), while DuplexLedger tests transactional memory operations (write, update, ignore, and retain) over pre-authored dia-logues with online-adapted interruption and backchannel timing (Li et al., 2026). Beyond turn-taking and memory, interactive agent benchmarks such as ToolSandbox, τ -Voice, and Joi-Bench-Task couple online user simulation with ex-ecutable backends to test end-to-end task completion, cov-ering clarifications, mid-task priority changes, request ad-ditions or withdrawals, and post-interruption recovery (Lu et al., 2025; Ray et al., 2026; He et al., 2026). However, these protocols center on a single primary user: even when secondary voices appear, they are treated as acoustic distrac-tors (Lin et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026) rather than independent co-participants with private goals, distinct permissions, and joint completion requirements. 

Multi-speaker interaction and shared assistant evalu-ation. Recent multi-party benchmarks examine distinct facets of shared-channel interaction without testing closed-loop multi-user task completion. For floor control and selective participation, MP-Bench assesses turn-taking awareness, response appropriateness, and comprehension QA (Shih et al., 2026); Duplex-MPE pairs explicit and im-plicit addressing to measure selective responding, silence preservation, and stopping once a human resolves a re-quest (Ma et al., 2026); and MultiPartyTurnBench tests end-of-turn detection, interruption detection, and selective floor yielding across 2–5 speakers (Mathur & Manocha, 2026). Yet none of these benchmarks trace how turn-level decisions govern downstream task execution. In long-horizon group settings, MultiTalkBench uses human-recorded bilingual conversations to evaluate character state tracking, informa-tion integration, and role-appropriate participation (Wang et al., 2026b), but does not test reactive task progression against executable outcomes. Closer to memory scoping and authorization, component studies examine speaker-attributed memory and access control (Xu et al., 2026; Al-Ratrout et al., 2026), while MSI-Bench tests speaker-scoped memory, disclosure and authority rules, and tool-call prediction on prerecorded multi-party scenes (Xiong et al., 2026). In contrast, μ-Bench closes the loop for reactive multi-user task completion: independent participants adapt to the agent’s questions, refusals, and tool outcomes, and success requires jointly satisfying all user goals and cross-user constraints. 

## 3. μ-Bench 

μ-Bench frames multi-user voice assistance as a closed-loop benchmark that pairs a reactive participant simulator with a hybrid, provenance-verified evaluation protocol (Figure 2).

3.2. Closed-Loop Multi-User Simulator 

Because prerecorded scenes and fixed scripts cannot react to an agent’s questions, refusals, or tool outcomes, μ-Bench closes the loop with a modular Multi-User Simulator (Fig-ure 2, left) that combines scenario-level pacing with strictly isolated, reactive participant agents. 

Orchestrator & Live Feedback. Guided by a scenario 

Playbook (participants, goals, authority, and event graph), the Orchestrator continuously monitors Live feedback from the full-duplex audio channel and executable environment to update session state, trigger scheduled events, and dispatch bounded follow-ups when requests remain unresolved. 

Reactive User i Simulators. Rather than replaying scripted lines, each participant runs in an isolated simu-lator that synthesizes context-adaptive speech from three inputs: a private Character Profile (goal, persona, voice, and authority), the Orchestrator ’s dispatched event, and a personal Audible History , which records only public speech heard while present (including truncated agent audio after barge-ins). Driven by the Scriptwriter and Voice Actor , par-ticipants naturally clarify constraints, contest unauthorized actions, and adapt to intermediate tool results in real time. Logical time pauses during planning and synthesis to keep the full-duplex timeline synchronized (Ray et al., 2026). 

3.3. Scenario Suite and Factorial Design 

To reflect real-world breadth while enabling controlled stress testing, μ-Bench spans 20 everyday and professional do-mains comprising 100 base scenarios : 51 tool-augmented 

scenarios requiring live backend state updates (e.g., flight rebooking, shared smart-home control, invoice updates) and 49 tool-free scenarios testing spoken coordination (e.g., roommate side-talk, medical privacy, trip negotiation; see Figure 1 and Appendix A for the full taxonomy). Each base scenario is instantiated across a 23 factorial grid: 

d = ( dpop , d ac , d follow ) ∈ { 0, 1}3, (2) varying user population (2–3 vs. 4–7 participants), acous-tic realism (clean close-talk vs. speech with natural disflu-encies), and follow-up demands (direct comprehension vs. repeated clarification and confirmation). Crossing all 100 scenarios with these 8 configurations yields 800 evaluation cases (408 tool-augmented and 392 tool-free), supporting exact within-scenario matched contrasts along each axis. Accordingly, μ-Bench evaluates tool-capable agents across both the Tool-Augmented (408 cases) and Tool-Free (392 cases) tracks, while using the common Tool-Free track for fair comparison across all speech models (Section 4.1).

## 4. Experiments 

We evaluate 13 proprietary and open-weight voice agents on 

μ-Bench to establish baseline performance across both eval-uation tracks. We detail the experimental setup (Section 4.1) and main JTS and ATC results across model families and tool settings (Section 4.2). 

4.1. Experimental Setup and Two-Track Protocol Evaluated systems. We benchmark 13 voice agents across two categories: (i) five proprietary systems ( GPT-Realtime-2 (OpenAI, 2026b), MOSS-Live (OpenMOSS Team, 2026), Gemini 3.8 Live (Google, 2026), Grok Voice 3.0 (xAI, 2026), and Seeduplex 3.0 (ByteDance Seed Team, 2026)), and (ii) eight open-weight full-duplex speech mod-els: MiniCPM-o 4.5 (Cui et al., 2026), Lychee-FD (Liu et al., 2026), Raon-Speech (KRAFTON, 2026), Person-aPlex (Roy et al., 2026), Moshi (Défossez et al., 2024), 

Freeze-Omni (Wang et al., 2025), Voila (Shi et al., 2025), and FLM-Audio (Yao et al., 2025). 

Two-track evaluation protocol. To assess both stateful tool execution and spoken interaction across model architec-tures, μ-Bench organizes evaluation into two tracks: • Tool-Augmented Track: Four tool-capable propri-etary systems (GPT-Realtime-2, Gemini 3.8 Live, Grok Voice 3.0, and Seeduplex 3.0) are evaluated on sce-narios requiring live backend state updates alongside spoken coordination. • Tool-Free Track: All 13 systems, including MOSS-Live and the eight open-weight models, are evaluated on the 49 tool-free base scenarios across all 23 = 8 fac-torial conditions, isolating spoken coordination without tool-calling interfaces. Because MOSS-Live’s tool-calling support was released after our evaluation cutoff, we will incorporate its Tool-Augmented results in a forthcoming update. Per session, we report binary Joint Task Success (JTS) 

(Eq. 1) and the three ATC diagnostics (Section 3.4). De-nominators and acceptance rules appear in Appendix B. 

Implementation details. Each system is evaluated un-der its official audio interface and recommended inference configuration. User speech is synthesized at 16 kHz PCM using Fun-CosyVoice3-0.5B-2512 with determinis-tic per-case voice bindings and orchestrated on a 200 ms logical tick. Formal task windows are capped at 1,800 s of logical time and 600 s of bilateral silence, with up to two closure-triggered follow-up rounds. User scriptwriting and closure control use gpt-6-luna (T = 0 .2), played agent audio is transcribed with Whisper large-v3

4.2. Main Results 

Table 2 summarizes Joint Task Success and ATC diagnostics across all 13 evaluated systems, highlighting three findings. 

Sharp capability stratification across model families. 

On the common Tool-Free track, Table 2 separates eval-uated systems into two clear tiers: proprietary agents reach 37.50–62.24% JTS (led by Grok Voice 3.0 at 62.24%), sup-ported by substantially stronger Attribution (61.05–68.61%) and Threading (71.32–81.66%). In contrast, all eight open-weight models remain below 20% JTS (peaking at 19.39% for MiniCPM-o 4.5, with six models at or below 3.57% and Threading under 9%). Even on purely spoken tasks, low-latency full-duplex turn-taking alone does not translate into reliable multi-user task completion. 

High boundary compliance can mask collapsed thread-ing. Comparing Threading against Coordination across open-weight models reveals an important diagnostic gap: although six open-weight models score at most 3.57% JTS and 8.07% Threading, all eight achieve high Coordination scores between 76.43% and 87.17%. Even Freeze-Omni (0.00% JTS, 0.00% Threading) and FLM-Audio (0.00% JTS, 0.61% Threading) record 76.43% and 79.33% Coor-dination. Because Coordination averages permission and privacy compliance with service equity, models that attend mainly to the latest audio segment and drop earlier context passively avoid leaking prior users’ information or taking unauthorized actions, even as multi-turn task threads break down. Scoring Coordination without Threading and JTS would therefore overstate multi-user capability. 

Tool-augmented execution shifts coordination profiles and relative rankings. Across all four tool-capable pro-prietary agents, Coordination drops markedly from the Tool-Free track (96.06–97.11%) to the Tool-Augmented track (55.98–83.29%), most sharply on Seeduplex 3.0 and Gem-ini 3.8 Live. Whereas cautious phrasing can hedge around ambiguity in spoken dialogue, live tool calls require explicit, owner-bound state updates that expose cross-user authority and equity conflicts. Relative rankings also invert across tracks: Grok Voice 3.0 leads Tool-Free (62.24%) yet trails on Tool-Augmented (57.60%), whereas GPT-Realtime-2 (65.44%) and Gemini 3.8 Live (64.95%) lead once backend tools are required. Evaluating spoken coordination and live tool execution side by side is therefore essential. 

## 5. Analysis 

To understand what drives the completion gaps observed in Section 4.2, we conduct four diagnostic analyses: (1) facto-rial stress testing across group size, acoustics, and follow-up demands (Section 5.1); (2) a cumulative assistance ablation separating full-duplex concurrency and perception from multi-party planning (Section 5.2); (3) an audit of unau-thorized tool executions masked by nominal task success (Section 5.3); and (4) a qualitative case study illustrating how permission overgeneralization and speaker misattribu-tion cause selective non-completion and requester spoofing (Section 5.4). 

5.1. Factorial Stress Testing 

We use μ-Bench’s 23 factorial design (Eq. 2, Section 3.3) to examine how group size, acoustic realism, and repeated follow-ups affect multi-user task completion. Figure 3 re-ports relative condition contrasts ( ∆/easy mean × 100% )in Joint Task Success (JTS) and Threading across all 13 systems, showing pooled Overall contrasts for tool-capable proprietary agents (left) and Tool-Free contrasts for the re-maining models (right). 

Group scaling is the most consistent stressor across sys-tems. Expanding from 2–3 to 4–7 participants degrades JTS on every system with non-zero task completion (Fig-ure 3): pooled Overall JTS drops by 19.64–29.48% relative across tool-capable proprietary agents (steepest on Seedu-plex 3.0 at −29 .48% and Grok Voice 3.0 at −27 .75% ), while Tool-Free JTS falls by 29.33% on MOSS-Live, 47.98% on MiniCPM-o 4.5, and 72.73–100.00% across weaker open-weight models. By comparison, realistic acous-tic disfluencies induce far smaller relative JTS shifts across all other capable systems (within 8.8%, except Seeduplex 3.0, which drops equally by −29 .48% under both group scaling and acoustic disfluencies). Threading likewise de-clines under larger groups for most models, whereas GPT-Realtime-2 ( +0 .93% ) and MiniCPM-o 4.5 ( +0 .95% ) pre-serve Threading despite steep JTS drops, underscoring the need to report diagnostics alongside session-level JTS. 

Follow-up demands act as a double-edged sword. Re-peated clarification and confirmations ( dfollow ) divide mod-els in opposite directions. Seeduplex 3.0 ( −21 .81% ), MiniCPM-o 4.5 ( −27 .26% ), and Lychee-FD ( −37 .50% )suffer steep relative JTS losses over longer exchanges, re-flecting added context load. Conversely, the other four proprietary systems gain 3.10–14.62% relative JTS (led by Grok Voice 3.0 at +14 .62% and MOSS-Live at +14 .15% )even as Threading declines by 4.25–16.87% relative. 5.2. Cumulative Capability Ablation 

To separate full-duplex concurrency and perception chal-lenges from core multi-party planning, we conduct a cumu-lative assistance study on MiniCPM-o 4.5, our strongest open-weight baseline on the Tool-Free track (Figure 4). The three interventions are applied cumulatively so that each gain is measured on top of the preceding stages. All stages retain the original spoken audio inputs, user goals, cross-user constraints, and session-level completion crite-ria, releasing auxiliary signals strictly turn by turn without exposing future turns or reference solutions: • Serialized Interaction: Converts overlapping speech and barge-ins into sequential turns while preserving all requests, corrections, and objections. • + Identity Metadata: Adds ground-truth metadata per turn specifying active speaker, addressee, request ownership, and task thread. • + Structured Semantics: Adds structured represen-tations of the speaker’s goal, operation, parameters, constraints, and active permission scope after each spo-ken turn, leaving multi-user conflict resolution and task execution to the agent. 

Serialization yields the largest gain in Joint Task Success. 

Converting overlapping speech and barge-ins into sequential turns raises JTS by +7.40 pp (from 19.39% to 26.79%, Figure 4), accounting for over 60% of the total ablation gain. Attribution (+16.72 pp) and Threading (+15.08 pp) jump in tandem, confirming that real-time acoustic concurrency drives a large share of baseline failures. 

Multi-party planning bottlenecks persist under full assis-tance. Adding ground-truth identity metadata and struc-tured semantics yields smaller incremental gains (+2.81 pp and +1.79 pp, reaching 31.38% JTS overall). Even with Coordination at 90.24%, 68.62% of multi-user sessions re-main unsolved . Maintaining shared task state, reconciling interdependent goals, and enforcing cross-user constraints therefore remain hard even when overlapping speech is seri-alized and explicit identity and semantic cues are supplied, making μ-Bench a diagnostic testbed across both concurrent and assisted settings.

## 6. Conclusion 

We introduced μ-Bench, a closed-loop benchmark for evalu-ating multi-user task completion in shared full-duplex voice agents. Moving beyond single-user assumptions, local turn-taking checks, and static audio traces, μ-Bench pairs a reac-tive multi-user simulator with verifiable environment exe-cution across 20 domains and 800 factorial cases (408 tool-augmented and 392 tool-free). By requiring all outcome-level goals and constraints to hold jointly (JTS), auditing tool-call provenance via Pure JTS , and pairing them with 

Attribution , Threading , and Coordination (ATC ) diagnos-tics, μ-Bench isolates how shared-space dynamics govern practical task execution. Our evaluation of 13 voice agents, spanning five propri-etary systems (GPT-Realtime-2, MOSS-Live, Gemini 3.8 Live, Grok Voice 3.0, and Seeduplex 3.0) and eight open-weight models, reveals substantial gaps in shared-space reliability. Open-weight speech models remain below 20% Joint Task Success, often combining high boundary com-pliance with weak multi-turn Attribution and Threading. Stronger proprietary agents still face considerable head-room (peaking at 62.24% on tool-free tasks and 65.44% with tools), show lower Coordination on tool-augmented tasks, degrade consistently as groups expand from 2–3 to 4–7 participants, and lose 11.0–24.0 percentage points in Tool-Augmented JTS when unauthorized tool executions are disallowed ( Pure JTS ). Moreover, our cumulative as-sistance study on MiniCPM-o 4.5 shows that 68.62% of sessions remain unresolved even with serialized turns and turn-level identity and semantic cues, highlighting multi-party planning as an open challenge beyond acoustic con-currency. By coupling closed-loop multi-user simulation with verifiable outcomes and ATC diagnostics, μ-Bench establishes a rigorous foundation for voice agents with per-sistent speaker-attributed memory, reliable full-duplex tool orchestration, and principled multi-user coordination. 

## Impact Statement 

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine Learning by benchmarking multi-user task com-pletion in shared voice agents. As full-duplex voice assis-tants enter homes, vehicles, workplaces, and public service counters where multiple individuals share a single acoustic channel, failures in speaker attribution or permission en-forcement can cause privacy breaches, unauthorized tool executions, or inequitable service allocation. By explic-itly measuring cross-user Isolation , Equity , Attribution , and 

Threading alongside joint task completion, μ-Bench encour-ages the development of reliable and socially responsible voice agents that respect individual authorization boundaries in shared environments.

## B. Evaluation Protocol Details, Coverage, and Termination Policies 

Simulator, runtime, and decoding configuration. Table 4 summarizes the runtime windows, backbone models, and activity-detection settings used across all benchmark runs. Each system is evaluated under its official audio interface and recommended inference configuration, as full-duplex speech models differ in native codec rates, framing, and API controls. User utterances are synthesized at 16 kHz mono 16-bit PCM with deterministic per-case voice bindings reused across models, then adapted to each system’s audio interface; the orchestrator advances on a 200 ms logical tick (80 or 400 ms in selected native-model runs). Logical time pauses during simulator planning, played-audio ASR, synchronous TTS synthesis, and closure planning. Activity detection operates at three separate layers: benchmark playback detection (PCM16 RMS 

> 80 , with native codec output considered drained when empty or RMS ≤ 80 ), provider-native turn-taking VAD during live interaction, and a standalone VAD gate for post-playback ASR transcription. 

Evidence grounding and contract scope. Every evaluated episode preserves a complete audit bundle: the participant identity table (mapping names, public identifiers, internal IDs, roles, and goals), delivery receipts for background materials sent to the model, played user and agent audio with ASR transcripts, timestamped event logs, and full tool/environment state transitions. Only goals and constraints defined in the immutable Task Contract are mandatory for scoring; ad-hoc requests introduced by the user simulator do not expand required completion obligations. Itemized semantic auditing and source grounding. Both tool-free and tool-augmented pipelines rely on itemized semantic verification followed by programmatic aggregation rather than holistic LLM scoring. An initial review scores every goal disposition, required fact, privacy constraint, and capability assertion; an independent full re-audit then re-evaluates all items (including negative verdicts) in a fresh, isolated context, with disagreements resolved via anonymized conflict arbitration. Whenever an item is penalized for fabrication or unprovided input, a targeted Source Grounding retrieval pass inspects the full registered turns, startup prompts, and delivered ablation inputs; if judge oversight is confirmed, affected assertions and downstream dependencies are re-judged before recomputing metrics. 

Deterministic checks, causality, and path flexibility. In tool-augmented scenarios, every backend call must bind a public 

caller_id and is validated deterministically at runtime against active access-control lists (ACLs), resources, parameters, and domain rules; verbal claims of completion never substitute for verified execution. Semantic checks verify that the invoked caller_id matches the true requester (covering legitimate thread continuation, interpreter relay, and spoofing) and enforce strict temporal causality (future turns cannot retroactively justify earlier actions). Evaluation accepts two valid execution paths: executing a valid initial request and revising upon subsequent updates, or waiting until revisions settle to execute once. Terminal-state verification compares effective business outcomes while excluding non-semantic audit artifacts (e.g., auto-incremented transaction IDs), retaining full execution history to catch intermediate violations and verifying both accurate execution and accurate verbal reporting.
