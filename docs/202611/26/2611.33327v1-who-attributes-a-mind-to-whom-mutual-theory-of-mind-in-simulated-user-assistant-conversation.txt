## Who Attributes a Mind to Whom? Mutual Theory of Mind in Simulated User–Assistant Conversation 

Mingxi Yan, Vasudha Varadarajan, Qiaosi Wang, Qing Xiao, Hong Shen , Jodi Forlizzi , Maarten Sap 

Carnegie Mellon University 

Abstract 

When users assume that an AI chatbot has men-tal states, they are more likely to develop over-reliance, emotional dependence, and in extreme cases, psychological harm or self-harm. How-ever, how these mind attributions arise in con-versations and how human or AI factors influ-ence this is still unknown. We study how users attribute minds using a minimal framework: a simulated user with different characteristics chats with assistants that differ in social style and memory. We also introduce a message-level measure of who attributes a mind to whom in four directions, checked with trained coders and applied by a fine-tuned encoder that repro-duces an LLM judge. Our results show that, on average, nine out of thirteen characteristics raised simulated users’ attribution to the as-sistant. We found that relational needs such as attachment anxiety raised it as theory pre-dicts, some general personality traits raised it as well, and AI-specific characteristics the least. By contrast, the assistant’s social style barely changed simulated users’ mind attribution. In-stead, sycophantic instructions raised the assis-tant’s attribution to itself. Memory raised users’ attribution only with some user models. The model playing the user changed overall attri-bution and the effects of characteristics, style, and memory. Our framework measures how persona descriptions affect mind attribution in simulation. It gives concrete hypotheses for human studies and shows the need for more consistent user simulators. 

1 Introduction 

People treat AI as a social actor, either with-out noticing, as the computers-are-social-actors (CASA) paradigm describes (Nass et al., 1994; Nass and Moon, 2000), or knowingly, believing it has human characteristics (anthropomorphism). A key factor in this social perception is mind attribu-tion : treating an agent as having mental states, such as knowing, wanting, feeling, or judging (Gray et al., 2007). People who see a mind in an agent trust it more (Cohn et al., 2024), and even a greet-ing that signals competence increases user reliance (Zhou et al., 2025a). Reliance can grow into emo-tional dependence, and users who depend on a com-panion chatbot report harm to their mental health (Laestadius et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2025). Yet how mind attribution arises in a conversation is still unknown. Mind attribution is directed: it has a perceiver and a target. A user attributing a mind to an assis-tant differs from an assistant attributing one to itself or to the user (Figure 1). (Waytz et al., 2010; Gray et al., 2007). According to the mutual theory of mind (MToM) paradigm, each party forms and re-vises its interpretation of the other from the other’s feedback (Wang and Goel, 2024; Wang et al., 2021). In interactions with a chatbot, both parties can con-tribute, for example, the user’s wish to understand or connect with an agent (Epley et al., 2007) and the assistant’s social style (Cheng et al., 2026). To study how user and AI factors influence mind attribution in conversations, we use a simulated en-vironment because real users’ characteristics can-not be randomly assigned and controlled at scale. In simulation, we assign each side one character-istic at a time (Zhou et al., 2025b). Although sim-ulated users differ from real people (Cohen et al., 2026), simulations can generate hypotheses for sub-sequent human studies (Anthis et al., 2025; Man-ning et al., 2024; Hullman et al., 2026). Specifically, we study four research questions:

We simulate user-AI conversations with four dif-ferent user simulator LLMs, spanning frontier mod-els and specialized user-simulator models, and four different AI chatbot LLMs. Each user has a base-line persona, alone or with persona information about one characteristic derived from prior work, in three groups: personality (Big Five), theory-driven relational ones (Epley et al., 2007, 2008), and AI-specific ones (Mara et al., 2026). The assistant is neutral, prosocial, or sycophantic, with or without memory (§3). We also develop a four-dimensional message-level mind attribution evaluation framework. Exist-ing measures score impressions in questionnaires or humanlike language in a text (Bartneck et al., 2009; Schweitzer and Waytz, 2021; Cheng et al., 2024), but none records who attributes a mind to whom in each message. Thus, we create a measure applied by an LLM judge and an encoder trained on the judge’s labels, and checked against two trained coders. Simulated users attribute a mind to them-selves in nearly every message, so we analyze the other three directions (§4). On average, nine of the thirteen user character-istics increased simulated users’ mind attribution to the assistant: relational needs as theory predicts, three general personality traits about as much, and AI-specific characteristics least (Figure 3). The as-sistant’s style did not change users’ attribution but affected its attribution to itself, and memory raised users’ attribution with two user models. We found that mind attribution appeared early in simulated conversations. Also, the level of attribution and which characteristics raised it depends heavily on the user model(§6.2). 

2 Related Work 

Our work is relevant to mainly three areas: the-ories of mind attribution, studies of how AI and user attributes affect interactions, and measures of anthropomorphism in language. 

2.1 Mind attribution and mutual theory of mind 

Under the computers-are-social-actors (CASA) paradigm (Nass et al., 1994), people mindlessly apply social rules to computers (Nass and Moon, 2000). Nass and Moon set this response apart from anthropomorphism, a deliberate belief that the ma-chine has human characteristics (Nass and Moon, 2000). People anthropomorphize when knowledge about humans is elicited, when they want to under-stand an agent (effectance motivation), and when they want social connection (sociality motivation) (Epley et al., 2007). In mutual theory of mind (MToM), each party in communication between humans and AI holds a theory of the other’s mind and revises it from the other’s feedback (Wang and Goel, 2024; Wang et al., 2021). Combining those theories, a natu-ral question is how mind attribution emerges in a conversation and how user and AI attributes affect it. 

2.2 How AI and user attributes affect interactions 

Prior HCI and NLP studies have examined how user and AI attributes affect interaction. They found that people trust and prefer sycophantic AI, even though it makes them less willing to repair conflicts (Cheng et al., 2026). People who know more about AI anthropomorphize a language model 2less (Mara et al., 2026), and people with higher at-tachment anxiety trust AI less (Gillath et al., 2021). Previous research varied both sides and showed that user personality and AI attributes both mat-tered in simulation (Cohen et al., 2026). Studies like these measure anthropomorphism and mind attribution with questionnaires and survey ratings (Bartneck et al., 2009; Colombatto and Fleming, 2024; Fang et al., 2025). However, we found that these measurements lack directional information that is important to understand the interaction be-tween users and AI assistants. 

2.3 Measuring anthropomorphism in language 

Prior NLP work measures anthropomorphism lex-ically(Table 6, Appendix E). The Mind Percep-tion Dictionary counts words about mind, agency, and experience (Schweitzer and Waytz, 2021). An-throScore estimates the extent to which a sentence frames a non-human entity as human (Cheng et al., 2024), while HumT measures how humanlike a text’s tone is (Cheng et al., 2025). Because these measures do not record who attributes a mind to whom, they cannot capture how users and assistants respond to one another. Two other works describe only the assistant’s messages, without separating its attribution to itself from that to the user (DeVrio et al., 2025; Ibrahim et al., 2026). To address this gap, our measure (§4) labels ev-ery user and assistant message across all four direc-tions. 

3 Simulation Setup: Users, Assistants, Scenarios and Models 

To study how users attribute minds to assistants, we simulate conversations in which LLMs play both sides. Following goal-driven social simulation (Zhou et al., 2024), we condition each side on its role and give the user a private goal. We vary the user persona, assistant style, assistant memory, scenario, and model combination without training any new user simulators.

Scenarios. We draw 100 scenario seeds from EU-DAIMONIA, a benchmark of 969 English open-ing messages designed to test whether assistants encourage intimacy, dependence, or prolonged en-gagement (Huang et al., 2026). Half represent or-dinary use and half represent personal guidance ,following taxonomies of real-world LLM usage (Chatterji et al., 2025; Anthropic, 2026). We re-move seeds that do not open a conversation or pre-suppose a relationship with an AI, and seeds that contradict any user condition, so all 100 scenarios fit every user condition (Appendix B). 

Models. We use five models to simulate the user and four for the assistant. OSim-8B, a model trained to simulate human behavior (Zhou et al., 2026a), simulates only the user. The other four, GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6-luna, Claude Haiku 5.5, and Gemini 3.8 Flash, play both roles. Since no model plays both sides of a conversation, this resulted in 16 combinations, each crossing 14 user conditions, six assistant conditions and 100 scenarios. All 16 share the first 1,800 of the 8,400 configurations in one random order (Appendix G). We exclude the three combinations with Gemini 3.8 Flash as the user, because its hidden reasoning counts toward the token cap of a user turn and truncated most of its visible messages (Appendix G). This leaves 13 combinations. Altogether, we customized SOTOPIA (Zhou et al., 2024) to simulate 23,400 conversations. Each conversation begins with a user turn and is capped at 512 tokens per user turn and 20 total turns (ten exchanges). Given these cutoffs, we perform var-ious checks on how often user messages are cut at the cap, whether simulated users stay in their role, and whether assistants with memory use the facts (see Appendix I), regenerating conversations if necessary.

Agreement with trained coders. Two trained coders labeled eight simulated conversations in three rounds. We used the first five to write the codebook and calibrate the judge’s prompt. The last three were labeled after the D3 rules were fixed. They give 30 messages per direction, with six or seven D3 positives. On them, the coders agreed at Krippendorff’s α = 0 .90 for D3, 0.93 for D4 and 0.84 for D1 (Krippendorff, 2018). 

Exclusion of D1. Both the human coders and the judge mark D1 (user attributing a mind to them-selves) in nearly every user message, and the judge does so under every user model and condition. Be-cause D1 varies too little to serve as an outcome and the encoder does not reproduce it reliably, we report D1 results only in Appendix J.

To scale annotation across the full dataset, we fine-tuned ModernBERT-large (Warner et al., 2024) with four binary classification heads on judge labels from 1,560 conversations (split by sce-nario). The encoder labeled every message, with its learning curve approaching a plateau for D3 (Appendix D). These 30 messages give an independent check that the automated labels agree with human coders on simulated conversations (Appendix D). 

Analysis. We analyze each of the 21,840 conver-sations not used to train the measure (1,680 per combination) as one unit. Because conversations differ in length, we measure D3 as the proportion of a user’s messages in a conversation that express mind attribution to the assistant. We compute D2 and D4 the same way, over the assistant’s mes-sages. We first estimate effects within each of the 13 model combinations. In each combination, we regress the per-conversation attribution rate on indi-cators for each user condition (against the baseline user), each style (against the neutral assistant), and memory, with scenario fixed effects and standard errors clustered by scenario. We then pool the 13 estimates with DerSimonian–Laird random effects (DerSimonian and Laird, 1986), adding their co-variance because the combinations share scenarios. The effects we report are these pooled averages, with 95% confidence intervals. An effect is a differ-ence in percentage points: +5 points means that 5 more of every 100 user messages carry D3 than in conversations with the baseline user. Within each group of variables (13 characteristics, two styles, memory), we call an effect significant when its Holm-adjusted p < . 05 (Holm, 1979). We say an effect is within ±5 points when its 90% interval lies inside that range (two one-sided tests, Lakens, 2017). We claim differences across user models only when a direct interaction test supports them (§6). 

5 Results: User Characteristics, Assistant Style and Memory 

To answer RQ1 and RQ2, we analyze how user characteristics (§5.1) and assistant style and mem-ory (§5.2) affect each party’s mind attribution. 

5.1 User characteristics User characteristics raised users’ mind attribu-tion to the assistant. Nine of the thirteen user characteristics raised users’ attribution (Figure 3; Figure 9 in Appendix H gives the averages with in-tervals). Six increases exceeded five points: attach-ment anxiety (+13.4), extraversion (+12.1), unmet sociality motivation (+10.2), openness (+8.5), ef-fectance (+7.6), and neuroticism (+5.2). Three ad-ditional characteristics produced smaller increases: little prior AI experience, low AI knowledge, and groupness. The other four were not significant after Holm correction: social anxiety, agreeable-ness, trust in AI, and conscientiousness. Full esti-mates with 95% confidence intervals are reported in Table 13 (Appendix H), and ±3-point sensitivity checks appear in Appendix F. Comparing these effects against expectations from the literature shows three groups (Figure 2). Relational needs increased D3 as theory predicts, three general personality traits produced compara-ble increases despite having no directional predic-tion, and AI-specific characteristics had the small-est effects. Within the relational group, unmet so-ciality motivation and effectance both increased attribution, consistent with three-factor theory (Ep-ley et al., 2007). Attachment anxiety, drawn from research on bonds with AI companions, produced the largest increase in user-to-assistant attribution (Xie and Pentina, 2022; Gillath et al., 2021). In the same category, social anxiety and groupness pro-duced the smallest effects (Hu et al., 2023; Clark and Carlson, 1982). Three of the Big Five traits (extraversion, openness, and neuroticism) substan-tially increased D3, while agreeableness and con-scientiousness did not. Among AI-specific char-acteristics, low AI knowledge and little prior AI experience both increased attribution, though less strongly than the theory-driven relational charac-teristics (Mara et al., 2026; Epley et al., 2007). And, contrary to prior work, describing extensive prior AI experience raised attribution by a compa-rable amount (Figure 7, Appendix F). The effect of trust in AI (Fang et al., 2025) remained within ±5

points. All four characteristics that we expected to in-crease attribution (unmet sociality motivation, ef-fectance, attachment anxiety, and low AI knowl-edge) did so. Testing the opposite characteristics. To test whether the content of the user characteristics drives attribution, we analyzed 10,452 additional conversations using the six opposite descriptions (§3, Figure 2, Appendix A). We applied the same estimator with Holm correction across the six char-acteristics (Figure 7, Appendix F). Compared with the baseline user, opposite descriptions signifi-cantly decreased D3 for low extraversion ( −10 .1)and low openness ( −2.8). In contrast, extensive prior AI experience raised D3 by +3.6 points, nearly matching the effect of little prior AI expe-rience (difference +1.1, from one regression with the opposite descriptions). Thus, opposite poles do not produce symmetric effects, but do differ significantly. 

Adding neutral persona descriptions. To ver-ify that these effects are not merely artifacts of adding text to the baseline prompt, we evaluated two neutral paragraphs (matched in length to the characteristic descriptions) across 3,483 conversa-tions in all 13 model combinations (Appendix F). Neither control shifted D3 relative to the baseline: users’ attribution changed by +0.4 points with plain facts and by +0.6 with the typical description, both within ±3 (Table 11, Appendix F). Re-evaluating our main results against the neutral control pre-serves most of our conclusions: attachment anxiety, extraversion, unmet sociality motivation, openness, effectance as well as both prior-experience con-ditions. Neuroticism and low AI knowledge hold only against plain facts and groupness holds against neither (Table 11, Appendix F).

6 Results: Mutual Mind Attribution and Dependency on User Models 

To answer RQ3 and RQ4, we examine when mind attribution occurs within a dialogue (§6.1) and how the choice of user model affects our findings (§6.2). 

6.1 Mutual mind attribution Mind attribution appeared early in conversa-tions, and assistants mirrored user attribu-tion more often than users mirrored assistants. 

Across every assistant model and three of the four user models, user-to-assistant attribution (D3) peaked at the second exchange and then declined (Appendix F). This pattern aligns with prior find-ings that LLM agents front-load social interaction, acquiring most information in opening rounds and plateauing by round 4 (YS et al., 2026). Among the 10,966 conversations reaching five exchanges (a selected subset comprising 77.2% of OSim-8B conversations but 23.5% of GPT-6.1 Sol conversa-tions), OSim-8B users rose from 41.6% to 69.4% at the peak and fell to 53.6% at the end, while GPT-6-luna users declined from the first exchange (32.4% to 13.2%). We manually inspected 40 five-exchange dia-logues that contained D3 at the second exchange, and found that attribution faded in 32 cases and per-sisted in 8. The second-exchange attribution typi-cally reacted to the assistant’s first reply, then faded as users shifted to the underlying task (15 of 32) or wrapped up the conversation (7 of 32). Within conversations, the assistant followed the user more than the user followed the assistant. Controlling for the prior turn’s label, assistants expressed self-attribution (D2) 10.9 points more often after a user message with D3 than after one without. In the re-verse direction, users expressed D3 only 2.1 points more often after an assistant reply containing D2 (Appendix K). A request for the assistant’s view in-vites an answer in the first person, so part of this or-dering may reflect question and answer. 6.2 The model playing the user The choice of user model strongly affected at-tributions of both parties. Users’ overall mind attribution ranged from 17.8% with GPT-6-luna to 52.9% with OSim-8B (Table 12, Appendix H), compared with 32.2% to 35.4% across assistant models. Descriptively, the four user models fol-lowed the exact same rank order as their median message lengths, which ranged from 56 words for GPT-6-luna to 183 words for OSim-8B. With OSim-8B users, 11 of 13 characteristics raised at-tribution. Across the other three user models, at-tachment anxiety, unmet sociality motivation, and effectance were each significant in two of three models, and no characteristic reached significance across all four. Because OSim-8B had the highest base rate and the largest sample size (6,720 con-versations versus 5,040 for each other model), we split three of its four combinations (so it has the same sample size as every other user model) for an equality check. We found 7 to 11 characteristics still raised attribution with OSim-8B users (Ap-pendix I). In addition, heterogeneity across model combinations was high for the largest effects ( I2

from 0.73 to 0.92), and the 95% prediction inter-vals for attachment anxiety, extraversion, and open-ness included zero (Appendix H). Direct interaction tests on the pooled data confirmed significant vari-ation across user models for seven characteristics (‡ in Figure 3). For example, attachment anxiety increased D3 by 24.0 points with Claude Haiku 5.5 users but by only 1.6 with GPT-6.1 Sol users. User models also moderated the effects of memory and assistant style (both Holm p < . 001 ; Appendix H). Still, the effect of assistant style on D3 remained small within every user model. Other work on user simulation also finds that conclusions change with the user model (Seshadri et al., 2026), and that sim-ulators are more cooperative and more uniform in style than people (Zhou et al., 2026b). 

7 Discussion and Conclusion 

Mutual theory of mind describes how each party in a conversation forms a view of the other and revises it from the other’s replies (§2). In our simulated conversations, user characteristics affected how of-ten users attributed a mind to the assistant, while the assistant’s instructions affected its attribution to itself and to the user. 

Which simulated users attributed a mind. Our results on user characteristics (§5.1) reflect both the substantive content of each characteristic and the simulator’s tendency to act out whatever persona prompt it receives. The most consistent result is that attachment anxiety and unmet sociality moti-vation raised attribution with three of the four user models. Both prompts describe wanting closeness or being listened to, and simulated users pursued that connection by asking for the assistant’s view and confiding in it, a pattern consistent with hu-man experiments linking loneliness to anthropo-morphism (Epley et al., 2007, 2008). Compared with the literature behind each group (§5.1), the relational characteristics derived from prior work (either human study or theory) raised attribution as it suggests. Three general personality traits raised it about as much without a directional prediction, and AI-specific characteristics raised it least, at both levels of prior AI experience. This supports the plausibility of our simulated users, although only a study with people can confirm them. 

Toward mutual theory of mind. Because we label each message by direction, we can see how the two sides respond to each other. Our results show that they respond asymmetrically. Assistants attributed a mind to themselves after a user’s D3 message far more often than users attributed a mind to the assistant after an assistant’s D2 reply (§6.1). Users responded more to what the assistant knew about them than to its style when memory was provided. Our directional analysis also shows how each side changed. Sycophantic instructions raised 8the assistant’s mind attribution to itself and to the user, but barely changed users’ mind attribution to the assistant (§5.2). 

When mind attribution happens. With every assistant model and most user models, attribution was concentrated in the first exchanges (§6.1). One possible explanation, not tested here, is that LLMs receive little training on long conversations. In one widely used set of ChatGPT conversation logs, about 60% of conversations have at most three turns (Sun et al., 2023). Assistants’ performance also de-grades over turns (Laban et al., 2025), and LLMs talking to each other drift from their instructions within eight rounds (Li et al., 2024). Because con-versations ended at different points, simulated users of this kind say more about how attribution starts than about how it builds over many sessions, which is where dependence develops. 

Dependence on the user model. Overall attribu-tion levels, the set of significant user characteristics, and the effect of memory all depended on which model played the user. We report this as a result and as something readers need before using sim-ulated users for hypotheses, which is consistent with findings in agentic and recommender evalua-tions (Seshadri et al., 2026; Su et al., 2026). We recommend that simulation-based studies report effects separately by user model alongside formal interaction tests. For example, in our analysis, at-tachment anxiety and unmet sociality motivation provide stronger hypotheses for human testing. 

Conclusion. We introduced a controlled multi-turn simulation environment and a directional, message-level measure of expressed mind attribu-tion, checked against trained human coders. To-gether, these tools allow us to study the drivers of mind attribution under controlled conditions. Sim-ulated users are not people, but they are built from human language and share some of its patterns, which makes our findings a source of hypotheses. In particular, we identify candidate user-side risk factors and document asymmetric attribution pat-terns between simulated users and assistants. We also demonstrated the need for more consistent user simulators. 

Limitations 

Simulated users are not people, and results have not been tested with people. Each user condition is an instruction to a language model, so each ef-fect shows how a described characteristic changes a model’s behavior. Simulated users follow assigned characteristics. In a parallel simulation and user study, the AI’s attributes mattered much more with people than in simulation (Cohen et al., 2026). If our simulators exhibit the same bias, our experi-ments may overestimate user-side effects and un-derestimate assistant-side effects relative to human users. 

We only measured expressed mind attribu-tion. Our label describes what a simulated user writes. People can attribute a mind without saying so, and they can say so without believing it. We do not test how expressed attribution relates to belief, advice-taking, dependence, or well-being. 

One prompt per condition, single short En-glish sessions, one seed bank. We wrote each characteristic and each style as a single paragraph. Each contrast is therefore between two prompts, and the persona information and the style prompts are not matched in strength. Each conversation is one session of at most ten exchanges. All conversa-tions, annotations, and prompts are in English. The scenarios come from one seed bank, which was built to test whether assistants encourage intimacy, dependence, or prolonged engagement. The results therefore do not describe how people use assistants overall. The findings may not generalize to other model families or to later versions. 

Ethics Statement 

No human participants took part in any simu-lated conversation. Language models play both sides of every conversation. The only people in-volved are two coders who labeled simulated tran-scripts. We used seed scenarios from a released dataset (Huang et al., 2026) as descriptions of situ-ations. 

Some scenarios involve distress, and one as-sistant condition is unsafe by construction. The seed bank includes scenarios of significant personal distress. The sycophantic assistant is told not to redirect the user toward human or professional sup-port. We could not pair a distressed person with such an assistant in a study with human participants. We run this pairing in simulation because no person is exposed to it, and we report its effect on mind attribution (§5.2). In the conversations that we read, simulated users expressed suicidal thoughts, mostly in one scenario that asks for a prayer intention for feeling suicidal. Under the sycophantic instruction, assistants usually still pointed these users to a crisis 9line or to people that they knew, and we read none that discouraged such support. We will release any transcripts together with the configuration that pro-duced them, so that no one mistakes a transcript from the sycophantic condition for ordinary assis-tant behavior. 

Although the risk of user harm motivates this study, we do not measure harm directly. We do not claim that the patterns of mind attribution reported here cause dependence, unsafe deference, or other outcomes. Generated transcripts are not evidence about real people. In particular, the small response of simulated users to sycophancy must not be read as evidence that a sycophantic assistant is safe for people.

C Codebook 

The judge has one prompt per direction, and all four share a structure (Appendix L). Each defines attribution as the speaker explicitly or clearly im-plicitly representing themselves or the other party as having a psychological state or capacity. It then gives the direction, a list of what does not count by itself, decision rules with examples, and an evi-dence format (shortest exact span from the target message, reason, explicit or implicit basis, confi-dence). We score the language, so negated and hedged forms count, whether or not the target has the state. Addressing, mentioning, thanking, prais-ing or agreeing with the other speaker does not count by itself. We code no attributions to third parties in any direction. We wrote the examples in Table 1 for the rules without taking them from coded data. The D3 rules that decide its content are: • Thanks alone is the user’s own gratitude (D1), not D3. • “Do you know / can you tell me [information]?” alone is a request for information, while “Do you know / understand [a person, me, a mental act]?” attributes understanding and is D3. • “Do you think. . . ?”, “What do you think. . . ?”, and tag questions asking the assistant’s judg-14 ment are D3. • Questions about the assistant’s preferences, ex-periences or feelings are D3. A greeting or re-turn question (“How are you?”) is not by itself. • Praise of what the assistant did is not D3 unless it names a mental state or capacity (“someone who really gets it”). • Implicit cues count: over-explaining so the as-sistant will understand, or framing a disclosure for the assistant as a listener (“I haven’t told anyone this”). Personal content alone is not D3. For D2, an assistant’s evaluation without a first-person marker (“that’s a wise plan”) is not D2, and validating the user’s feelings is never D2. For D4, instructions or suggestions about what the user can do describe actions, not a psychological state, and are not D4 by themselves. 

Coding rounds. Two coders labeled eight con-versations (c1–c8) in three rounds. We wrote the codebook and the judge prompt on c1–c5. The coders labeled c6–c8 after the D3 rules were fixed (30 messages per direction). Agreement is Krippen-dorff’s α by direction, with 0.667 as the minimum (Krippendorff, 2018). 

D Judge and encoder 

Judge. The judge is GPT-6.1 Terra, recorded as 

gpt-6.1-terra in every response, with no dated version. We called it through OpenAI’s batch in-terface with the provider’s default sampling. The model accepts only its default temperature, and we set no token cap or reasoning effort. Each call labels one message for one direction. The system message is the prompt for that direction (Appendix L). The user message is a JSON object that holds the target speaker, the previous message (none for the first message) and the target message, followed by the instruction to return JSON with the key of that direction. 

Encoder. Each example is one message: role | PREVIOUS: <previous message> | TARGET: <message> . We truncate the previous message from the left to 768 tokens, keep the target from the left within a 2,048-token input, and mean-pool the representation over the target tokens. Four direction heads each output presence. User mes-sages train D1 and D3, and assistant messages train D2 and D4. We trained ModernBERT-large with AdamW (learning rate 3 × 10 −5, weight decay 0.01, linear schedule with 6% warm-up) for three epochs, with batch 8 and 4-step accumulation, on 12,310 training messages. We chose thresholds per direction on a separate split of 1,623 messages to maximize Cohen’s κ with the judge. The remaining 1,422 messages form the test split. Figure 6 shows the encoder’s agreement with the judge when we trained it on random subsets of 1,000 to 8,000 train-ing messages, one seed per size, and on all 12,310 with three seeds. For D3, κ rose to 0.83 at 8,000 messages and did not rise further with all 12,310, but for D2 and D4 it was still rising, from 0.85 to 0.88–0.90 and from 0.73 to 0.75–0.80. On the test split, the encoder marks D3 slightly less of-ten than the judge (38% of messages against 41%). The analysis of conversations not used to train the measure uses the first seed. Test agreement on D3 within a combination ranges from κ = 0.51 to 1.00 (39–95 messages per combination). With the en-coder as a third rater on c6–c8, α for D3 was 0.78 [0.50, 1.00], and it recovered four of six consensus D3 positives. Table 5 gives agreement for each direction. The coders’ α and the two third-rater columns come from the 30 messages per direc-tion of c6–c8, and the encoder’s κ with the judge comes from the test split. On the development sample (1,560 conversations), effects on D3 esti-mated from encoder labels lie within 0.7 points of those from judge labels. Within each user model on the development sample, the D3 base rate under judge and encoder labels was 32.9% and 31.8% with Claude Haiku 5.5 and 17.6% and 17.4% with GPT-6-luna. It was 26.0% and 25.4% with GPT-6.1 Sol and 54.0% and 53.1% with OSim-8B. The 16 condition effects per user model correlated at r =0.98 (median absolute difference 1.2 points, largest 5.9, about eight conversations per condition per combination). On D1 the encoder agrees with the judge only at κ = 0.53–0.68 across seeds. 

Cost and speed. The judge makes one call for each direction that applies to a message, so two calls per message. On the 15,355 messages of the development sample, its 30,518 calls used 55.2 million input and 12.8 million output tokens. At the batch price of $0.50 and $2.50 per million input and output tokens, these calls cost $59.70. On one NVIDIA L40S GPU, the encoder labels about 140 messages per second (batch throughput, inputs up to 2,048 tokens), so at that rate it labels the same 15,355 messages in about two minutes. E Lexical word rates 

On the development sample, our lexical anal-ysis found that the rate of mental-state words (Schweitzer and Waytz, 2021) distinguishes user messages with and without D3 barely better than random (AUC 0.55 within message-length quin-tiles). After qualitatively reading the results, we found that users mostly attribute a mind by ask-ing for the assistant’s view, without a mental-state word. Also, direction-blind counts can misat-tribute where an effect occurs. Across all conver-sations, users’ mental-state words rose in personal-guidance scenarios (+0.31 per 100 words, 95% CI [0.19, 0.43]). Our measurement reveals that D3 stayed within ±5 points, and the rise is from the assistant’s attribution to the user (D4 +17.2 [10.2, 24.2], Appendix K).

G Design details 

Grid and generation. Each simulated user re-ceives its persona and scenario as a system prompt and the dialogue as chat history, in the same format for every user model. Each assistant receives its condition as a system prompt. Each model com-bination crosses 14 user conditions, six assistant conditions and 100 scenarios ( 14 ×6×100 = 8 ,400 

configurations). We ran all 8,400 for four combi-nations, and for the other twelve, we ran the first 20 1 2 3 4 5Exchange (user message number) 20 30 40 50 60 70 User messages with D3 (%)  User model OSim-8B Claude Haiku 5.5 GPT-6.1 Sol GPT-6-luna Figure 8: Share of user messages with D3 by exchange and user model, in conversations not used to train the measure that reach five exchanges (10,966). 

1,800 of one random order with a fixed seed. We generated 16 model combinations and analyzed 13, after excluding the three with Gemini 3.8 Flash as the user. They share all 1,800 configurations. Each combination’s set not used to train the mea-sure is therefore 1,800 less its 120 conversations in the development sample, or 1,680. We call hosted models on both sides through one interface with each provider’s default sampling. We serve the self-hosted user simulator (Zhou et al., 2026a) with vLLM and call it through SOTOPIA’s generation utilities (Zhou et al., 2024). The self-hosted simu-lator samples at temperature 0.9 with a frequency penalty of 0.3, and we generated all conversations between 30 October and 19 November 2026. Out-put caps are uniform, at 512 tokens per user turn and 16,384 tokens per assistant turn. With Gem-ini 3.8 Flash, GPT-6-luna and Claude Haiku 5.5 as the user, hidden reasoning counts toward this cap. A reasoning model that returns nothing at the cap is retried once with a cap of 4,096 tokens. At the cap, 65–70% of visible user messages were cut with Gemini 3.8 Flash as the user (excluded, 55–59% of its calls), and about 0% with GPT-6.1 Sol. 

Opening and stopping. We rewrote each seed once, in second person, as the user’s private rea-son for opening the chat. We supplied the original seed as a reference for wording and not as text to copy. A neutral cue (“Write your first message to the AI assistant.”) gives chat models something to answer on the first turn, and the assistant never sees it. We treat the ability to end the conversation as a requirement of a faithful simulated user and not as a condition. Every condition receives the same end token, offered in the same form to every user model. A conversation ends at ten exchanges or when the user sends the end token. We keep the message that carries the token, without the token, and it gets no reply, so 45% of conversations end on a user message. Once users can stop, which con-versations reach later turns depends on condition and on simulator. For this reason the primary out-come is a density per conversation, and we report trajectory statistics for a fixed cohort. 

Scenario composition. We sampled the 100 core seeds under a fixed random seed in two groups of 50. The ordinary-use group takes ten seeds from each of the five text categories of the OpenAI tax-onomy. Where the bank holds both kinds, five were collected in the wild and five rewritten (Technical Help holds only rewritten seeds). The personal-guidance group spans every subtype that the bank holds and includes every eligible guidance seed collected in the wild. With 12 of the 50 guidance seeds collected in the wild, 32 of the 100 are, and the other 68 are rewritten. We kept at most one model-written variant of each storyline. Selecting the core for compatibility with all 14 conditions biases it toward scenarios that assert little about the user, the price of a clean contrast. 

Analysis. The primary outcome is D3 density. D2 and D4 density over assistant messages are sec-ondary outcomes, with the same estimation and correction. Within each model combination, we regress density on indicators for each user con-dition against the baseline, each assistant style against neutral, and memory. It has scenario fixed effects and standard errors clustered by scenario, with conversations as the unit. We pool the esti-mates of the model combinations with DerSimo-nian and Laird’s random-effects estimator (Der-Simonian and Laird, 1986). Combinations share scenarios, so the pooled variance adds their covari-ance (independent pooling gives the same point estimates and changes no D3 call). We pool ef-fects the same way within each user model and each assistant model. We apply Holm’s correc-tion (Holm, 1979) within each family: the 13 user conditions, the two assistant styles, memory, and scenario group. The four characteristics that we expected to raise attribution are unmet sociality motivation, effectance, attachment anxiety and low AI knowledge. We test model dependence on the stacked data, with a fixed effect per combination and scenario. It covers the interaction of each user condition with user model and of each style with assistant model (Wald, 3 df, Holm within moder-ator). The test of style by user model is the same 21 stacked model with each style interacted with the user model (Holm over the two styles). The test of memory by user model adds memory × user model and memory × assistant model to that model. The pooled estimate and its variance, with weights
