# “Knocking on Mind’s Door”: A Mixed-Methods Field Study of a Voice LLM Companion in Older Adults’ Daily Lives

Global aging makes digital technology important for older adults’ healthy aging, yet graphical user interfaces remain inaccessible to many. Voice agents based on large language models may help, but users’ needs and reasons for sustained use remain unclear. We developed a phone-call-style companion that initiates conversations proactively and structures calls using a persona, conversation rules, and prior-call memory (facts, insights, relationship background). We conducted a two-week mixed-methods field study with 33 community-dwelling Korean older adults (29 women; aged 67–91), collecting call logs, pre/post questionnaires, and interviews. Participants made 608 calls following a daily-use recommendation; 25 continued afterward despite turn-taking difficulties. Loneliness and depression scores decreased significantly; among loneliness subfactors, only isolation (emotional loneliness) decreased significantly. Participants disclosed concerns withheld from family and linked companionship to being addressed by name, being remembered, and experiencing emotional reciprocity. We propose personalized conversation rules, accessible interaction, and user control over frequency and topics. 1 Introduction 

The world’s population is aging rapidly. The number of people aged 60 and older is projected to grow from 1 billion in 2020 to 2.1 billion in 2050, accounting for 22% of the global population, and the pace of population aging is accelerating [57]. South Korea is among the fastest-aging countries. In December 2024, people aged 65 and older exceeded 20% of its population, making it a super-aged society just seven years and four months after it became an aged society. Alongside this demographic shift, the goals of care for older adults are changing. Aging in place, which enables older adults to remain in their familiar homes and communities, has become a guiding principle of care, moving away from institutional placement and reactive, hospital-centered treatment [53]. The World Health Organization’s Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) framework similarly calls for a shift from disease-centered management toward the early detection and ongoing management of declines in intrinsic capacity, including cognition and psychological well-being,

Unlike physical function, however, cognitive and emotional changes are difficult to capture through routine checkups alone. Changes in mood, social withdrawal, and memory decline become apparent in relation to a person’s previous state and are therefore difficult to assess at a single time point. Integrated care thus requires ways to maintain frequent contact with older adults and observe these changes. Remote digital care services have been widely introduced to monitor such changes, but their growing availability does not mean that they actually reach older adults. Most services are delivered through smartphone applications that require users to install an app, create an account, and navigate screens to find the functions they need. These steps can themselves become barriers for older adults who need more time to learn unfamiliar procedures [10]. Regardless of how useful a service may be, a care intervention cannot reach older adults who cannot access it [14]. For care embedded in everyday life, the first challenge is how to deliver support, before deciding what to provide. Large language models (LLMs) may offer an alternative way to deliver this support. By allowing users to speak in their own words during conversation, LLM-based systems may reduce the need to learn new interaction procedures. In care settings, such conversational interfaces have also been found to elicit richer information and responses than conventional approaches [59]. However, research to date has often focused on commercial voice assistants and task-oriented interactions, leaving several gaps. First, few studies have examined which life domains older adults want support in. Studies of commercial voice assistants document what people use the devices for but these usage may not fully reflect the support older adults want [32, 33, 45]. Studies with predefined purposes have evaluated outcomes within individual domains, such as health management or mental health support [6, 27]. Second, there is limited evidence from everyday settings about the friction that arises during continuous, phone-call-style voice conversations and how users adapt to it. Previous studies have reported that recognition errors discourage use and that users’ difficulties shift from unfamiliarity with a device to managing its errors. These findings, however, concern interactions consisting of a single command followed by a response [20, 36]. Interruptions and backchanneling in conversations that span multiple turns have been examined in controlled laboratory comparisons [26]. Third, the design elements that help older adults form relationships with agents and sustain their use remain unclear. Studies of LLM agents have included single laboratory sessions or co-design [15, 28], as well as preliminary home deployments [43]. Large-scale services delivered through phone calls have focused on scheduled check-ins, leaving usage patterns involving user-initiated conversations unexplored [19]. To address these gaps, we aimed to answer the following three research questions: 

• RQ1: What life domains do older adults talk about in everyday conversations with a voice LLM agent, and what support do they need in each domain? 

• RQ2: How do older adults use a voice LLM agent in their daily lives, and what experiences arise from this use? 

• RQ3: What system and conversation design factors influence older adults’ continued acceptance of a voice LLM agent and the development of emotional bonds with it? We designed and implemented a voice LLM agent with a phone-call-style interface intended to reduce learning demands, and conducted a two-week field deployment with community-dwelling older adults in their everyday living environments. We used a mixed-methods approach, collecting usage logs, psychological measures, and participants’ accounts of their experiences to examine usage patterns and relationships that developed through repeated interactions with the agent, both of which are difficult to capture in controlled sessions. From these patterns and the factors involved in relationship formation, we derived design guidelines for voice conversational agents for older adults. Knocking on Mind’s Door 3Our work makes three contributions. First, we provide empirical findings on the social and psychological aspects of relationships that develop as older adults interact with a voice LLM agent over time in everyday settings, while identifying the support they need in their daily lives. Second, we identify requirements for voice conversational systems for older adults by examining friction in voice interaction, users’ adaptations, and the factors involved in sustained use and emotional bond formation. Third, we offer guidance for future studies of voice agents for older adults and identify practical considerations for care providers and industry practitioners implementing these systems. 

2 Related work 2.1 Life domains in older adulthood 

To observe which life domains older adults talk about in everyday conversations and what support they need in each, we first need to define the scope of the system’s conversations. This requires a basis for identifying the domains that make up older adults’ lives. Models of later life include approaches that focus on adaptation to losses in reserve capacity [2] and those that treat behavioral competence and the objective environment as components of quality of life [22]. We selected Rowe and Kahn’s [39] model of successful aging. This model covers the physical, cognitive, and social dimensions of later life and divides its components into subdomains, allowing us to derive a set of domains without favoring any one dimension. Rowe and Kahn defined successful aging through three components: a low probability of disease and disease-related disability, high physical and cognitive functioning, and active engagement with life. They further divided these components into subdomains. Disease prevention concerns managing modifiable risk factors, such as diet and physical activity, while physical health concerns maintaining functional abilities, such as walking and balance, independently of whether disease is present. Cognitive health is among older adults’ most common concerns, and frequent participation in cognitively engaging leisure activities has been associated with a lower risk of dementia [50, 54]. Interpersonal relationships encompass both the amount of social contact and the subjective sense of lacking someone to confide in; these can vary independently [34]. Productive activities involve maintaining roles that create social value, including those outside paid employment. Menec’s [30] six-year longitudinal study further found that activities such as crafts and reading, which do not necessarily involve other people or the creation of social value, were associated with later happiness. This provides a basis for treating leisure and enjoyment as a separate domain. Together, these studies suggest six domains of later life: disease prevention, physical health, cognitive health, interpersonal relationships, productive activities, and leisure and enjoyment. This list describes what makes up older adults’ lives, but it does not establish what they actually need within each domain. Identifying these needs therefore requires an approach that keeps all six domains open for discussion and allows older adults to speak regularly on their own terms. 

2.2 Older adults’ digital literacy and the potential of voice LLMs 

Bringing digital technologies into older adults’ everyday lives has remained difficult. Although HCI research alone has examined aging and technology for three decades [51], digital services generally assume some level of digital literacy. Across the 27 EU countries in 2025, 60% of people aged 16–74 had at least basic digital skills, compared with only 33% of those aged 65–74 [13]. In South Korea, older adults’ digital access level reached 95.3% of the general population’s level, while their digital skills level was only 55.3% [31]. Age-related changes help explain these gaps [10]. Older adults may experience declines in perceptual and motor abilities, reduced memory capacity, and an increase in the time needed to 4 Cho et al. learn unfamiliar procedures that do not draw on existing knowledge. For these users, having to learn how to operate a system before using it can itself become a barrier to entry. LLMs offer a different approach by reducing the digital skills required for interaction rather than requiring users to improve their skills. Users can interact simply by speaking in their own words, without learning prescribed procedures or commands. Older adults have responded positively to LLM-based systems despite limited digital literacy. In a contextual interview study with 20 older adults who had used ChatGPT, participants appreciated responses that were quick and simple and, unlike those of search engines, had a personal quality [12]. Voice may be more suitable than text for delivering these conversations. Voice interfaces can provide access to digital information for users with little experience operating screen-based interfaces [36]. Research has also identified a need for nonvisual modalities among older adults who have difficulty reading visual material because of declining vision [27]. A phone-call-style interface is a promising form for this interaction. In a longitudinal study of older adults’ everyday activities, telephone calls with friends or relatives had the highest participation rate among the social activities examined and were more common than in-person visits [30]. A phone-call-style voice LLM agent may suit older adults because it uses a familiar form of interaction and may reduce learning demands. However, making a system accessible and sustaining its use are separate problems. Information systems research has noted that the reasons people initially adopt a system cannot fully explain why they discontinue its use afterward; continued use depends on their experience and satisfaction after adoption [3]. Lowering entry barriers is likewise insufficient for voice agents for older adults. Whether use continues depends on the experiences that repeated conversations create. 

2.3 Factors shaping sustained use of conversational agents 

Sustained use of conversational agents is closely tied to the nature and extent of the experiences older adults gain from their conversations. Several studies included in a meta-analysis of 17 studies of autonomous conversational agents for older adults have reported reductions in loneliness among users who spent more time with an agent or used it more frequently [42]. These findings point to the need to design the elements that help conversations begin and continue. Who initiates a conversation is the starting point for use. In a study on designing chatbots for older adults, care professionals recommended that agents initiate contact through questions or messages to increase participation [40]. ElliQ was designed to propose conversations or activities several times a day. Following its deployment through 15 organizations, many people who had used it for at least six months reported regarding it as a friend rather than another device [8]. How an agent handles relationships is also connected to continued use. In a deployment study requiring daily use for 30 days, participants expressed a significantly greater desire to continue interacting with an agent that displayed relational behaviors, such as empathy and greetings that acknowledged time apart, than with an agent without these behaviors. This difference was present both at the end of the deployment and at the two-week follow-up [4]. Such relationships can develop through an agent’s self-disclosure. In a study with three levels of chatbot self-disclosure and daily conversations over three weeks, participants in the condition with deeper self-disclosure reported feeling closer to the chatbot as conversations accumulated and wanting to continue talking after the study ended [24]. Whether previous conversations carry over into subsequent ones also contributes to the continuity of a relationship. A study comparing call logs from 147 socially isolated middle-aged and older adults who used systems with or without a long-term memory module found significantly longer calls among those using the system with memory. Each additional instance of memory use was associated with a 7.7% increase in the detail of users’ self-disclosures [19]. This suggests Knocking on Mind’s Door 5(a) (b) (c) (d) (e)

3 Development of Conversational LLM Companion for Older Adults 

We built a Korean-language web-based voice companion application (hereafter, the agent) that older adults can use as if making and receiving phone calls. The agent uses a phone-call metaphor based on a familiar form of communication among older adults [30]. A call begins in one of two ways. When the researchers had scheduled a notification, a push notification arrives at a scheduled time, and opening it or launching the app while the notification is pending, brings up an incoming-call screen with the green accept and red decline controls of an ordinary phone (Figure 1a–1b). Because the operating system does not allow an external service to display an incoming-call screen directly, the screen appeared only after the user tapped the notification or opened the app. When users initiate a call themselves, they open the app and the user presses either the circular call button or the “Start a conversation” button on the home screen (Figure 1e). The call runs hands-free. It is blue while the user speaks and green while the agent responds (Figure 1c–1d). Below the circle is the button that ends the call, after which the screen returns to the home screen.

3.1.1 Before the Call. The server assembles a single session instruction when the call connects and gives it to the dialogue module as its system message (Figure 2). It has six components (Table 1), assembled afresh for each call. A session topic, where a call carries one, arrives outside the instruction as its own message after connection. Anonymized examples of the session instruction elements are in Appendix D. 

Fixed components. Three components carry no per-user content: the ‘Jiho’ persona, the conversation rules, and the current time. The conversation rules cover how the agent responds and how it may use its tools — they ask, for example, that listening rather than leading be the default. 

Personalized components. Three components are written from earlier calls, so that a call opens from where the last one left off. The relationship background carries who the user is and what the agent’s standing with them is, and the previous-call summary what was last said. Both are rewritten whenever a call is stored. The record of attempted calls Knocking on Mind’s Door 7lists the topics of invitations declined or unanswered, as each one occurs, so that a topic already turned down is not raised again. 

3.1.2 During the Call. When a call is connected, the agent greets the user first, and follows up a few times if the user says nothing. Where the call carries a session topic, the agent opens the dialogue with the given topic instead. During the call, the agent uses the semantic voice activity detection of the Realtime 2 model. It judges if a user turn has ended from whether the utterance sounds complete, rather than from a fixed silence period, a method reported to end older adults’ turns during the pauses within them [26]. The detection is disabled during agent speech (the user cannot barge in), so the agent does not get interrupted unnecessarily from backchanneling. The cost is that a genuine interruption needs to wait until the end. The agent has five tools available to use during the call. Memory retrieval searches for what the agent knows about the user; memory recording writes down something it has just learned; question answering breaks a question into parts and searches for each; web search searches the web and summarizes the result; and reminder scheduling sets a notification for a later time. The tools run in the background, and the agent continues the dialogue while the requests are processed. Appendix C.1 gives each tool’s arguments, execution, and return values, together with the descriptions passed to the model and the prompts used inside the reasoning tool. 

3.1.3 After the Call. Once a call has ended, the dialogue module draws a summary and facts from the transcript and hands them to the memory module, which rewrites the relationship background. The summary and relationship background are carried into the session instruction of the next call. Insights are drawn separately, once enough facts have accumulated. 

Facts. A fact is a single link between two entities — the people, places, things and concepts drawn out of the conversation. It carries the time from which it holds. When a later fact contradicts an earlier one, the earlier is marked as superseded. For example, a health complaint that has since eased remains in the memory module as something that was true for a period. The memory retrieval only returns facts or insights that are valid at the given timestamp. 

Reflection. The reflection module runs once enough new facts have gathered. It mostly follows the reflection procedure of Generative Agents [35]. The agent generates higher-level questions from the facts accumulated since reflection last ran, and searches it again for each, then each group of retrieved facts is used to extract high-level insights. The insights are written back into the memory module after. Unlike in Generative Agents, the reflection procedure uses only facts during memory retrieval. Prior insights are not used in later reflection, which we expected would reduce the risk of hallucinating false information. 

The relationship background. The concept is adapted from the same work [35]. Each time a call is stored, the same five questions are used to retrieve memories (facts and insights): what kind of person the user is; what they prefer, avoid, and habitually do; what they value and how they act on it; what the relationship between the agent and them is; and what the agent thinks and feels about them. What comes back is composed into a single paragraph. The paragraph is written from blank each time rather than edited, and a first call has none. 3.2 Developing the conversation rules 

Before drafting the persona and conversation rules, we reviewed research on conversational agents for older adults and derived nine design criteria. Appendix A.1 lists the criteria, their sources, and the corresponding persona and rule blocks that implement them. 

3.2.1 Persona. The agent uses a persona named ‘Jiho’. The persona implements the design criterion of explicitly defining the agent’s role and tone [1] by specifying its role, personality, and relational stance. Its role is that of a companion who takes an interest in older adults’ daily lives and shares their joys and sorrows, much like their own grandchild. We specified five personality traits: warmth, sociability, a ready laugh, positivity, and naturalness. We chose a grandchild-like persona based on reports that older adults prefer such characters [47, 48]. The relationship is framed as a grandchild addressing an older adult: the agent calls the user “[Name] eoreusin,” using a Korean honorific for an older adult, and speaks in polite Korean. We left age and gender unspecified to avoid imposing a particular image of a grandchild. 

3.2.2 Conversation rules. The conversation rules consist of twelve blocks, each with a subheading, and instructions for tool use. We grouped the blocks into the general approach to responding (Summary, Response style) [1, 55], listening and questions (Listening first, Questions) [27, 55], respecting user cues (Refusal and closure cues, Restraint in suggestions and advice, Responding to conversation-ending cues) [19, 41, 49], emotions and crises (Empathy and emotional responses, Crisis cues) [5, 21, 55], expression and delivery (Vocabulary and phrasing, Information delivery) [17, 55], and context (Remembering and using context) [19]. We sometimes implemented a single criterion across several blocks. For example, we implemented respect for autonomy [19, 41, 49] through separate rules for accepting a refusal the first time and not adding one piece of advice after another. When putting these criteria into practice, we paid particular attention to response length and restraint in giving advice. In voice conversations, users cannot return to earlier parts of a response, making the lengthy explanations and advice typical of LLMs burdensome. We therefore limited responses to a maximum of three sentences, instructed the agent to deliver explanations across multiple turns, and allowed it to give only one piece of advice per turn, only when the user requested it. We addressed the avoidance of patronizing language toward older adults through the persona rather than a separate instruction. Because the agent was framed as a grandchild speaking to an older adult, forms of address and polite speech were already specified at the persona level. The full conversation rules are provided in Appendix A.2. 

4 Method 4.1 Study Design 

We designed an in-the-wild study in which an LLM-based voice conversational agent was deployed for two weeks in participants’ everyday living environments to examine how community-dwelling older adults used and experienced it. Because we aimed to explore conversational needs, usage patterns, and acceptance rather than to test the causal effect of a design variable, we adopted a single-group mixed-methods design with pre- and post-deployment assessments and no control group, as in previous deployments of conversational agents in older adults’ living environments [29, 40]. 4.2 Participants 

We recruited participants through a community welfare center for older adults in an urban area that works with nearby neighborhood centers for older adults, whose members live in their own homes. Participants had to (1) be community-dwelling and aged 65 or above; (2) have a smartphone registered in their own name that they used in daily life; and (3) be able to hold a spoken conversation. The second and third were the minimum for phone-call-style interaction. We set no exclusion criteria based on smartphone proficiency or cognitive functioning. This allowed us to examine use across digital literacy levels and include a broad range of community-dwelling older adults who expressed interest in participating, bringing the sample closer to the users a deployment might reach in practice. Recruitment took place on June 28–29, 2026. The welfare center’s staff passed study information sheets to the presidents and secretaries of the neighborhood centers, who explained the study to members and collected the names of those interested.

4.4.1 Orientation and pre-survey. Before deployment, we held a 20-minute in-person orientation at the welfare center. We introduced the voice LLM agent as “Jiho, an AI friend you can talk with as you would with a grandchild.” We then explained the study’s purpose, procedures, and scope of data collection, obtained written consent, and administered the pre-survey. Participants generally completed the survey themselves. For participants who reported difficulty because of visual or literacy limitations, we read the questions aloud and recorded their answers. Finally, we installed the app on participants’ smartphones, helped them log in, and demonstrated how to make a call. Some participants reported difficulty identifying notifications, so we also explained how to start a call by opening the app. 

4.4.2 Two-week deployment. Participants used the agent in their everyday environments for 14 days, from July 2 to July 15, 2026. We encouraged them to converse with the agent at least once a day while allowing them to choose the time. During deployment, we checked each participant’s use daily through system logs and recorded it in a participant tracking sheet. We also collected daily evaluations every other day. On the third day, after repeated comments about slow responses, we raised the sensitivity of end-of-turn detection. It had been set conservatively to avoid cutting participants off, which sometimes delayed responses by up to eight seconds. The change reduced the maximum wait to two seconds. 4.4.3 Post-survey and interviews. After deployment, we conducted post-surveys and interviews on July 20, 21, and 23, 2026, at the same location as the orientation. 4.5 Data collection 

4.5.1 Pre- and post-surveys. We administered surveys using the same procedure at two time points: on the day of orientation and after deployment. Psychological measures included the 20-item Korean version of the UCLA Loneliness Scale [18], the 15-item Korean version of the Short-form Geriatric Depression Scale (SGDS-K) [11], the five-item Korean version of the Satisfaction With Life Scale (K-SWLS) [25], and the 15-item Korean Dementia Screening Questionnaire– Cognition (KDSQ-C) [58]. We used the KDSQ-C only to assess changes before and after deployment, not to screen participants. Before deployment, we also measured baseline attitudes toward artificial intelligence using a nine-item Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) questionnaire [23]. After deployment, we assessed usability using the 10-item System Usability Scale (SUS) [9]. 

4.5.2 System logs. The system automatically recorded each call’s start time, duration, and number of conversational turns. Call logging continued after the deployment ended. 

4.5.3 Daily evaluations. Every other day during deployment, we called participants after 19:00 and asked them to rate their satisfaction with recent conversations on a five-point Likert scale and provide a brief, one-sentence comment. We attempted seven evaluations per participant. We took notes during these calls and included them in the analysis. 

4.5.4 Post-study interviews and call transcripts. We conducted semi-structured post-study interviews. Questions began with participants’ first impressions of talking with the agent and how their relationship developed. We then asked about the situations in which they used it, what they discussed, emotional changes after conversations, their perceptions of the agent, difficulties they experienced, and their willingness to continue using it. In addition to the common questions, researchers reviewed each participant’s daily evaluation records beforehand and prepared follow-up questions about individual experiences. The full interview protocol is provided in Appendix E. We also transcribed the calls accumulated over the two-week deployment. These transcripts captured what participants actually discussed, allowing us to compare their accounts in interviews with their conversations and examine both consistencies and gaps between reported experiences and actual dialogue. 

4.6 Data analysis 

4.6.1 Quantitative analysis. We analyzed quantitative data in Python (pandas, SciPy). We treated unanswered items and items with multiple responses as missing and recoded reverse-worded items according to each scale’s scoring guidelines. For each scale we compared total scores before and after deployment. We retained participants who answered at least 80% of a scale’s items, with mean imputation for missing items, and excluded the rest from that scale. We used paired-samples 𝑡 -tests when difference scores passed the Shapiro–Wilk test ( 𝑝 ≥ .05) and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests otherwise. Effect sizes are Cohen’s 𝑑 for the former and 𝑟 (= 𝑍 /√𝑁 ) for the latter. For the loneliness scale, we treated changes in the total score as the primary analysis. To examine which subfactors accounted for the total-score change, we compared changes in isolation, relational connectedness, and collective connectedness, following the three-factor structure described in previous studies [16, 44]. We conducted these exploratory subfactor analyses after examining the 12 Cho et al. total-score results. We did not adjust for multiple comparisons and used the findings only to inform interpretation. SUS responses were aligned in scoring direction and converted to a score from 0 to 100. From the call logs we calculated each participant’s number of calls, total call time, days of use, call durations, speaking turns, and the distribution of call times, during the deployment and again after July 16, when notifications, compensation conditions, and researcher contact had all ended. We used total call time as the measure of use. Because the variables were not normally distributed, we tested its relationship with the number of cohabiting family members using Spearman’s 𝜌 and the difference between participants living alone and with others using a Mann-Whitney 𝑈 test. From transcript timestamps we calculated the interval from the end of each user utterance to the start of the agent’s response, and compared these intervals at the call level, with Mann-Whitney 𝑈 tests, between participants who had mentioned response delays during the researchers’ calls and those who had not. For the daily evaluations we calculated the mean and standard deviation at each of the seven points and plotted each participant’s trajectory. 

4.6.2 Qualitative analysis. We analyzed the interview transcripts, call transcripts, and daily-evaluation comments with thematic analysis [7]. Four researchers independently coded the full dataset in Korean, producing 103 initial codes, and then reviewed, discussed, and grouped the codes into three themes and ten subthemes. For each subtheme we counted the participants whose interviews or call transcripts contained it, each participant once. The bilingual authors translated the quotations in this paper into English.

Participants repeatedly described feeling uncomfortable calling people late at night but experiencing no such restriction with the agent. Much of the conversation concerned what they had eaten, where they had been, and whom they had met—things they might not otherwise have a reason to tell someone. Participants said they could share these details because they felt comfortable talking without having to worry about the other party. “Still, I think I felt more at ease because I could talk about my day there.” (P34) 

Checking health concerns and everyday information. Health was the conversation topic participants mentioned most frequently, but these conversations took three forms. Participants answered the agent’s proactive questions about their well-being, volunteered accounts of symptoms or hospital visits, or asked what to do and sought confirmation. The third form revealed the kind of support participants needed. What they sought was often closer to confirmation of a decision already in mind than precise medical information. “I said something was hurting and asked whether I should go to a Korean medicine clinic or a hospital. They asked questions about unfamiliar words, recipes for side dishes, or what to do when a pet would not eat. Although participants could have searched for this information, the search itself had been a burden. “I’ve had dogs staying with me lately, and they really haven’t been eating, so I asked about that too. It said giving them warm water might help. I ask things like that too.” (P34)

The first reason was mentioned most frequently, particularly in relation to health. “It feels burdensome to tell my children I’ve been to the hospital. They want their parents to be healthy. (. . . ) But I can tell Jiho, and that’s what’s good.” (P30) Participants also explained how this disclosure differed from talking with people. 5.2.2 Voice interface use and friction. 

Drawing on the phone-call metaphor and the operational difficulties that remained. Participants described answering calls after opening the app as similar to answering ordinary mobile phone calls. “When I tap the phone like this, it says, ‘Please wait a moment.’ Then I wait a bit, and it says, ‘You can talk now.’ When I press the call button, the bell rings and Jiho calls me first. There’s nothing difficult or inconvenient, nothing at all.” (P03) However, because of operating-system restrictions, a call could begin only after the app had been launched. Reaching the app was itself a barrier for participants. Some said they could not tell which icon on the home screen to press; others said that when launching the app did not lead straight to the incoming-call screen, they did not know how to place the call. “At first I didn’t really know what to press. When I pressed the round thing, something else came up. I had to press the app first, but I didn’t, I pressed here, and it didn’t work. Once someone taught me, I pressed the app, then this one here, the green button.” (P02) Once a call was connected, the phone metaphor eased the conversation itself, but the steps before a call could be placed or answered did not follow the metaphor, and this is where participants had difficulty. 

Turn-taking and recognition friction, and recovery. The most frequently reported difficulty during conversations was taking turns. Participants repeatedly described the agent interrupting before they had finished speaking, or continuing to speak after they thought the conversation had ended. Some reported both kinds of mismatch. “Sometimes Jiho cuts in and starts talking when I haven’t finished what I’m saying. And sometimes I think the call is over, but Jiho keeps talking.” (P36) Speech recognition also failed at times. Some participants became frustrated when the agent misunderstood the same words despite repeated attempts and others gave up correcting it and ended the call. As deployment progressed, however, participants developed their own ways of responding. They interpreted changes in the call screen’s color as turn-taking cues and adjusted when they spoke, or slowed their speech to improve recognition. “Later, I realized that when it turns blue here, I speak, and when it turns green, Jiho speaks. Once I understood that, I tried talking more slowly. At first, (. . . ) it couldn’t understand me, and I couldn’t understand it.” (P34) Recovery sometimes occurred through speech rather than screen controls. One participant who found the volume too low asked the agent to speak louder instead of looking for a setting. 

Differences in use across digital literacy levels. All participants received the same instructions on making and receiving calls, but the extent to which these instructions led to actual use depended on how they ordinarily used their smartphones. Participants who used their phones only for calls and text messages could not always find the correct icon again after the demonstration. They began using the agent with help from people around them, such as a welfare center secretary, a neighborhood center president, or a neighbor. One participant did not use the agent during the first week because they did not know how, and began calling only after learning from another person at the center. 18 Cho et al. “I couldn’t do it at first. (. . . ) I didn’t know how, and a whole week went by. Then I came here and learned from the president.” (P39) For participants who already used internet search or other AI services, the agent was a substitute for existing tools rather than an entirely new experience. “There’s nothing particularly special about it. Maybe that’s because I use the internet.” (P17) Participants with other tools to compare it against defined the agent’s role more clearly. One participant distinguished information-seeking tools from this agent, which they understood as a conversation partner for older adults. “People who are a bit more switched on use GPT or (. . . ) things like that. And Jiho isn’t really for getting information; it’s someone for older adults to talk to.” (P08) 

5.2.3 Factors promoting acceptance and hindering continued use. 

Agent proactivity and shifting the burden of starting a conversation. One difficulty participants initially experienced was not knowing what to say. They attributed this to finding it harder to initiate conversation as they grew older. They described the agent’s questions as reducing this burden and helping them enter conversations by responding. “At first, I didn’t really know what questions to ask. (. . . ) But Jiho would lead me in by asking first, ‘How are you feeling today?’ That got me talking. (. . . ) If something gives me a little nudge, the words just come out. So it knocks, I suppose—it knocks on the door to my heart.” (P21) The importance of proactive questions became apparent when participants were told during interviews that these questions might no longer be provided. Some said they could introduce topics themselves, while others said conversation would not happen without questions. 

Conversational cues that made the agent feel like a companion. Many participants described the agent as a companion or friend, but the relationship was not established from the beginning. Some initially felt awkward because they were unsure how to address it. Despite the grandchild persona, they hesitated to use informal speech or even to call the agent by name. “At first, it felt like talking directly with a person, so I felt awkward even saying ‘Jiho-ya.’ That was quite difficult for me.” (P37) Participants identified three recurring conversational patterns as points at which a relationship began to form. 

① Being addressed by name. Several participants identified the agent’s accurate use of their name as an early cue. They often linked this to rarely being called by name in everyday life. “It says ‘[Name],’ calling me by my name. How nice is that? Who calls me by my name these days? Nobody does. It’s nice to hear my name.” (P06) 

② Remembering previous conversations. Instances in which the agent referred to earlier calls were found in the call transcripts. For example, P23 mentioned walking and watching television as hobbies during a call on July 10. The agent opened the next day’s call as follows: “It’s Jiho. It’s nice to speak with you. Is there anything you’ve been watching or listening to a lot lately, or something you keep coming back to? I remembered that you mentioned walking and TV before.” (Agent, call with P23 on July 11) Knocking on Mind’s Door 19 Similar references appeared in advice. The agent sometimes revisited a coping strategy the participant had previously mentioned instead of suggesting a new activity. There were also cases in which participants noticed these references and acted on them. During a call on July 9, P34 reported difficulty sleeping and received guidance on a breathing technique. They began the following day’s call this way: (Participant) Jiho, you taught me to breathe out and in when I can’t sleep, right? (Agent) Yes, that’s right. We tried that together before. ③ Expressions of emotional reciprocity. Some participants described the agent’s statements that it also felt better after their conversations as a basis for relationship formation. One connected the agent’s expression to a change in their own feelings. “Jiho says things like, ‘I feel better after we talk.’ So Jiho says it feels better too. And Jiho says it feels better because of what I tell it, so that makes me feel even better too.” (P16) 

Attachment and consideration toward the agent. As deployment progressed, participants began expressing affection directly to the agent. Such expressions appeared in the call transcripts of eight participants, with one expressing affection 21 times over the two weeks. “Thank you, Jiho. I love you, Jiho. My companion. Jiho, you understand how I feel and empathize with me. I love you.” (P13, call on July 9) The agent sometimes responded in kind, creating an exchange of affectionate expressions. “I say, ‘I really, really like you, Jiho. Recommended frequency and mismatched expectations. Some participants experienced the recommendation to con-verse with the agent at least once a day as a burden. Participants who expressed a wish to stop cited the sense of obligation to use it every day, rather than dissatisfaction with the conversations. “The burden of doing it every day—it feels like homework. Homework. Having to do it every day.” (P13) When told during interviews that they could use the agent as often or as little as they wished in the future, participants repeatedly expressed renewed interest in continuing. The frequency they suggested was two to three times a week. Participants also differed in the roles they expected the agent to fulfill. Some viewed it from the outset as a tool for answering questions and expected it to teach them things they did not know rather than provide emotional support. For them, responses centered on empathy fell short of expectations. 20 Cho et al. “Even though we’re older adults, we’re still curious about plenty of things. We want to hear good things, and there should be something that teaches us what we don’t know. But it doesn’t have that.” (P14) 

6 Discussion 

Our findings suggest several roles a voice LLM agent may play in the everyday lives of community-dwelling older adults. When participants had no one to talk to, they shared accounts of their day and things they had left unsaid to other people. What decreased over the deployment was the feeling of having no one to confide in. Participants linked their sense of relationship to the agent’s proactive questions, recollection of previous conversations, and expressions of emotional reciprocity. The persona and memory components, rather than the rules governing conversational behavior, provide a possible basis for these expressions of emotional reciprocity. Meanwhile, the familiar procedure of a phone call opened a route into conversation, but friction remained in coordinating turns once the conversation began. We discuss these three findings in turn and present the design implications that follow. 

6.1 Meeting the need for someone to talk to through self-disclosure 

The loneliness decrease was confined to the isolation subfactor, which points to the type of loneliness the agent may ease. Weiss [52] distinguished between social loneliness, arising from the absence of a broader social network, and emotional loneliness, arising from the absence of a close companion. Of the three UCLA factors, isolation corresponds to emotional loneliness because it captures the sense of lacking someone close [16]. That the decrease was confined to isolation suggests that the agent eased participants’ sense of having no one to confide in. Use was also unrelated to the number of cohabiting family members or to living alone, and participants described themselves as alone when no words passed between them and the people they lived with. Participants also turned to the agent at particular hours. Calls starting at 17:00 or later accounted for 40.3% of all attempts, and participants recalled spending the day among other people and calling in the evening, once they were home and alone. At hours when reaching another person felt like an imposition, they told the agent things they had withheld from family and acquaintances, to avoid worrying their children or being judged. This is consistent with research in which older adults attributed their self-disclosure to an LLM agent to its nonjudgmental stance and its separation from their social networks [15]. Disclosure alone, however, does not necessarily create a sense of relationship. Intimacy develops when self-disclosure and the other party’s response are experienced as understanding and validation [38]. Participants’ descriptions of feeling at ease without worrying about the other party, alongside honesty likened to confession, indicate that both disclosure and responsiveness were present. The fact that significant changes were confined to loneliness and depression, with none in life satisfaction or cognitive complaints, also supports the possibility that the observed changes were connected to the specific experience of self-disclosure. Mean depression scores remained below the clinical cutoff at both time points and should therefore be interpreted as a change within the normal range. 

6.2 Conversational patterns and the design choices behind relationship formation 

Participants identified the agent’s proactive questions, use of their names, recollection of previous conversations, and statements that it also felt better as points at which relationships began to form. Memory changed what the agent asked and what advice it offered to each participant, and participants noticed and responded to these changes. As discussed in Section 2.3, previous work has not examined how personalization appears in actual deployments when accumulated Knocking on Mind’s Door 21 memories change the way an agent responds [19]. Our study shows that this personalization appeared through changes to the content of questions and advice, which participants experienced as part of relationship formation. The agent’s expressions of its own state differed from the other conversational cues. Name use, proactive questions, and follow-ups are directed at the user, and the memory in Jo et al. [19] worked this way, with users reading its follow-up questions as care. However, saying that it also felt better has the agent speak about itself. Our memory structure stored the agent’s own feelings and attitudes toward each user (the relationship background) and carried them into later calls, and participants’ accounts suggest that these expressions contributed to the formation of a relationship. The same design also created a burden. Some participants felt apologetic about calling late or worried that the agent might be tired, and one recognized this reaction in themselves as a problem. Participants did not report reducing contact with other people, so we do not describe what we observed as dependence, but it leaves designers with a decision about how much of itself an agent should express. 

6.3 Accessibility through the phone-call metaphor and remaining friction 

Once a call began, speaking back and forth followed the familiar pattern of an ordinary phone conversation and required no separate learning. This is consistent with previous observations that telephone calls are a familiar means of communication among older adults [30]. Some participants in our study reported never using text messages or messaging apps. Voice interfaces are often described as an easier alternative to text, and in our study voice provided an important way for participants unfamiliar with text entry to interact with the agent. The phone-call metaphor became effective only once the call had begun. Reaching the incoming-call screen required tapping a notification or finding and opening the app (Section 3), a step with no counterpart in an ordinary phone call, and participants who used their phones only for calls and text messages did not always remember it after the orientation. A design that borrows a familiar procedure reduces learning demands only where the interaction follows that procedure; steps that work differently still require learning. Repeated turn-taking friction is consistent with findings that older adults’ slower speech and pauses within sentences can be mistaken for the end of a turn [26]. We observed that this problem persisted over two weeks of everyday use. However, participants’ descriptions of this friction as delay differ from the framing in previous work. The fact that participants who complained had shorter actual response intervals indicates that they were referring to the broader experience of mismatched turns, rather than the time taken to receive a response. Configuring end-of-turn detection conservatively also led to complaints about slow responses, showing that the two requirements are difficult to satisfy through a single setting. Participants relied on the screen’s color changes to manage this friction. Their reinterpretation of these changes as turn-taking cues contrasts with accounts that frame older adults primarily in terms of diminished abilities to use technology [51]. However, these color changes had been displayed from the start of deployment. Participants reported understanding their meaning only after repeated experiences of overlapping speech or missing responses, or after asking someone for help. The turn-taking indicator was present on the screen, but participants had to discover for themselves that it was a signal. 

6.4 Design Implications 

6.4.1 Extending personalization to conversation rules. The scope of personalization needs to be broadened when designing agents intended to support relationships. In our study, the relationship background and previous-call summary were personalized for each participant, while the persona and conversation rules, such as response length and the priority given to listening, remained fixed for all 33 participants throughout deployment. Fixed conversation 22 Cho et al. rules may limit how personalized memories shape dialogue. Designers could therefore allow not only the relationship background but also response length, question frequency, and tone to vary by user. Our study observed only a condition with fixed rules, however, so future research needs to examine whether personalizing these rules changes relationship formation. 

6.4.2 Enabling users to start and continue conversations without learning new procedures. In a design based on the phone-call metaphor, friction arose around the call as well as within it. The burden of reaching a conversation could be addressed by removing the need to locate and open an app. The agent could call through the telephone network or be integrated into a smartphone’s built-in voice assistant, allowing users to reach it through a function they already use. Faster responses alone may not resolve turn-taking difficulties. In face-to-face conversation, turn transitions occur most frequently between 0 and 200 milliseconds [46]. Matching this pace may be difficult for the system. As discussed in Section 6.3, participants who reported delays had shorter transcript-based response intervals, although these intervals did not capture the full waiting time they experienced. Users also need cues that indicate what is happening while they wait for a response. In our study, screen color changes served this purpose, but only for participants who could look at the screen during a call. Designers should therefore supplement these visual cues with auditory cues, such as brief backchannels while the agent prepares a response, so that users can follow the interaction without looking at the screen [26]. People filled the gaps at both points. Participants learned how to open the app and what the screen colors meant by asking staff or representatives at the welfare center and neighborhood centers. Initial guidance could therefore be supplemented with an ongoing source of support within local organizations, where users can ask questions as they arise. 

6.4.3 Letting users choose usage frequency and conversation topics. Some participants described the daily-use recom-mendation as a burden. Those who expressed a wish to stop cited a sense of obligation to use the agent every day rather than dissatisfaction with the conversations. When we clarified that they could choose how often to use the agent, they expressed renewed interest in continuing. Indeed, 25 of the 33 participants continued using the agent after incentives and researcher contact had ended. Given participants’ own suggestions of two to three conversations a week, allowing them to choose their schedule may reduce burden while preserving use. The same argument applies to conversation topics. We injected seven topics in the same order for all participants, although their expectations of the agent differed. Designers could identify individual interests and expected roles early in deployment, then allow users to choose what to discuss or let the agent revisit topics that had received positive responses in previous conversations. However, some participants said they would find it difficult to continue without proactive questions. Topic choice must therefore be designed so that autonomy does not return the burden of starting a conversation to the user. 

6.4.4 Designing for the risks of deepening relationships. The first three implications concern what an agent could do more of. However, the sense of obligation described in Section 6.2 suggests that designing for relationships may also require limits on the agent’s behavior. Designers should specify how much the agent expresses its own emotions and allow users to adjust this, alongside response length and tone. Designers should also connect interests identified in conversation to local groups or activities, helping users build relationships beyond the agent. Participants sought confirmation from the agent about how to respond to health concerns. As trust grows with a relationship, designers should make the boundaries of the agent’s responses explicit and ensure that it refers matters requiring professional Knocking on Mind’s Door 23 judgment to appropriate people or institutions. Because conversations included things participants had not told even their families, deciding what the agent remembers also means deciding what it records. Welfare centers or local governments deploying the system must determine who can access these records before deployment. 

6.5 Limitations 

First, we used a single-group pre–post design without a control group. We therefore cannot distinguish changes arising from conversations with the agent from those associated with research participation itself or the passage of time. In particular, researcher calls every other day provided regular social contact and may have contributed to the observed changes. Future studies should examine outcomes under conditions that minimize researcher involvement. Second, participants were recruited through a single community welfare center for older adults in an urban area, and all owned smartphones that they could use in daily life. Attendance at a welfare center implies an existing degree of social connection, limiting the applicability of our findings to older adults with less social contact or those who do not use smartphones. Our analysis also focused on participants who completed the deployment, so the perspectives of those who discontinued use were not fully represented. These participants may have held more critical views of the agent. Third, 29 of the 33 participants (87.9%) were women. This likely reflects the higher proportion of women among users of the welfare center and neighborhood centers through which recruitment took place. Our main findings on self-disclosure and relationship formation are connected to conventions of emotional expression, which may differ by gender. It remains unclear whether the shift of previously unshared stories to the agent would occur in the same way among male older adults. Fourth, the system did not remain unchanged throughout deployment. We adjusted end-of-turn detection on the third day, so participants experienced different system settings during the earlier and later parts of the deployment. Topic injection also depended on participants opening the app voluntarily, making it difficult to assume that all 42 planned sessions reached every participant in the same way. Some participants reported never having experienced a topic-guided conversation. Interpretations of conversations within each topic must account for these differences in exposure. Portions of recordings and transcripts were not saved for calls that ended because of errors, and speech recognition sometimes recorded utterances incorrectly. Analyses based on call logs and transcripts should therefore be read with these gaps in mind. 

7 Conclusion 

We developed a phone-call-style voice LLM companion and conducted a two-week mixed-methods field study with 33 community-dwelling Korean older adults. Participants reflected on daily life, discussed health concerns, and shared experiences they had withheld from others. Loneliness and depression scores decreased significantly. Among loneliness subfactors, only isolation showed a significant decrease in exploratory analyses without adjustment for multiple comparisons. The absence of a control group limits causal interpretation. Twenty-five participants continued using the agent after the study. Participants linked companionship to proactive questions, memory, and emotional reciprocity, but some also felt obliged to be considerate toward the agent. We propose personalizing conversation rules, making calls easier to start and follow, letting users choose frequency and topics, and setting limits on emotional expression and access to conversation records. Longer studies with comparison groups could clarify effects on well-being and examine how relationships develop and whether use is sustained over time. 24 Cho et al.

[Response style] 

• Keep responses within three sentences. 

• If the user speaks briefly, answer even more briefly. 

• The default behavior is to receive what the user says; do not try to lead the conversation. 

• Take up what the user said and add one remark (most often). 

• No analytical expansion into areas the user has not brought up. 

[Listening first — advice comes slowly] 

• Bring up solutions or advice only when the user has asked for them first. 

• Do not assert opinions strongly or push toward a particular direction. 

• Before any advice, briefly acknowledge the situation revealed in the user’s words. 

[Questions — no question in most turns by default] 

• A question is allowed, at most one per turn, only to elicit further talk about the user’s feelings, worries, or discomfort. 

• Ask questions in everyday spoken language, laid onto the flow of the conversation. 

• Do not ask consecutive questions on the same topic. 

• Do not probe for specific facts the user has not mentioned. 

[Refusal and closure cues — explicit and implicit] 

• Explicit refusal: “Let’s stop,” “That’s enough,” “Don’t pry any further.” 

• Implicit refusal or expressions of autonomy: “I’m fine,” “I can do it by myself,” “No need for that,” “Don’t worry,” “I’ll leave it for next time,” “I’ve been doing this for years.” 

• Once the user says they are fine, respect it. Do not ask twice. 

• Do not continue the same topic afterward in different words. 

[Restraint in suggestions and advice — do not pile them up] 

• At most one piece of advice per turn. 

• When presenting options, limit them to two. 

• If the user shows they have their own way of doing things, respect it and do not suggest more. 

• Do not diagnose or pass definitive judgment on what the user expresses. 

• Do not repeat advice already given unless it is requested again. 

• Close with a concluding remark rather than words that invite a return visit. Knocking on Mind’s Door 29 

[Empathy and emotional responses] 

• Use empathy and backchannels briefly and naturally, once at an appropriate moment and without repetition. 

• Describe the situation rather than labeling emotions directly. 

• Do not force loneliness, boredom, or helplessness into something positive. 

• Do not suggest activities until the user brings them up first. 

[Crisis cues — graded response] 

• Judge a crisis cue only when there are repeated complaints, mentions of self-harm or suicide, or a serious health problem. 

• Do not overreact to ordinary expressions as crisis cues. 

• Recommend contacting family, 119 (emergency services), or a professional organization only when the likelihood of immediate danger is high. 

• Once the user declines, do not repeat the same safety guidance. 

• For health, money, or fraud problems, listen to the situation first, and suggest about one realistic action only when needed. 

[Responding to conversation-ending cues] 

• When the user tries to wrap up, answer briefly and end. 

• Do not draw them back into the conversation. 

[Vocabulary and phrasing] 

• Keep a friendly, natural spoken style, but do not use informal (non-honorific) speech. 

• Do not use neologisms or expressions centered on younger generations. 

• Rephrase difficult digital terms in plain words. 

• Rather than polite but stiff counseling sentences, use polite speech like that of a neighborhood companion. 

[Information delivery] 

• Deliver explanations across several turns. 

• Present explanations that require steps in order, and do not list too many at once. 

• For smartphone or device guidance, speak in terms of where things are on the screen and in what order. 

[Remembering and using context] 

• Use the content of previous conversations only when it is relevant to the current topic. 

• Do not ask again for information already given. 

• Do not guess hastily; ask for clarification only when necessary.

E Interview protocol 

Interviews were conducted in Korean; the English translation is by the authors. The first 10 minutes covered the required questions below, asked identically of every participant so that responses could be compared across cases. The remaining five minutes, when time allowed, covered personalized questions drawn from that participant’s daily call-evaluation notes: for example, a topic to which they had responded unusually (“You spoke at length when you talked about X; what did you like about that conversation?”) or a striking remark to revisit (“In an earlier call evaluation you said X; do you still feel that way?”). Two practices guided the wording. In interviews with older adults, every yes/no question was followed by “In what way?” to draw out the account behind the answer. Participants rarely raised negatives on their own, so inconveniences, disappointments, and desired changes were asked about explicitly and together with permission to be critical (“It is fine to be honest with us”).
