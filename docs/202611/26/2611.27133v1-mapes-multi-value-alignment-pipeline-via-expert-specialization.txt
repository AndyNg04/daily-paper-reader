# MAPES : Multi-Value Alignment Pipeline via Expert Specialization 

SungHo Kim 1, SangKeun Lee 1,2 

1Department of Artificial Intelligence, Korea University, Seoul, South Korea

Abstract 

Aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences requires simultaneously sat-isfying multiple, often competing values. Exist-ing preference optimization methods collapse these into a single implicit signal, and even multi-objective extensions optimize within a shared parameter space, leaving cross-value conflicts unresolved. We propose M APES ,a multi-value alignment paradigm in which conflict resolution is a first-class concern at every pipeline stage: it first decomposes su-pervision into value-specific experts trained in isolated parameter subspaces, then recom-poses them via preference-aware value merg-ing and a lightweight harmonization stage that aligns the merged model with the expert ensem-ble. Across five preference alignment bench-marks, M APES outperforms strong direct pref-erence optimization (DPO) variants and multi-objective baselines on the benchmark-level av-erage, with no large degradation on GSM8K and GPQA; matched controls isolate the contri-bution of each stage. 1

1 Introduction 

Human values are inherently multi-faceted (Bai et al., 2022b). When we judge whether a response is good , we do not rely on a single criterion; rather, we expect it to follow instructions, be honest, pro-vide genuinely helpful content, and avoid mislead-ing claims (Cui et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). These values are neither independent nor fully compatible; improving one can degrade another, giving rise to cross-value conflicts (Wei et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2024). Achieving faithful alignment in LLMs, therefore, requires improving multiple values without allowing progress on one dimension to undermine another systematically. One line of work incorporates target pref-erence scores into training or generation to empha-size a single value at a time within shared param-eters (Guo et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). Another introduces per-value reward margins into a multi-objective DPO objective to balance competing values (Zhou et al., 2024). How-ever, both optimize a single shared model, treating cross-value trade-offs as inherent rather than ex-plicitly mitigating them. Despite these aligning efforts, the structural limit of training within a single shared parameter space remains difficult to fully overcome (left side of Figure 1). As different values exert opposing opti-mization pressures on the same parameters, gains achieved on one value displace, rather than com-plement, gains on another, a phenomenon long observed for competing objectives in multi-task learning (Sener and Koltun, 2018; Yu et al., 2020) and recently shown to exhibit persistent trade-offs across multiple rewards (Rame et al., 2023), con-flicts that added supervision alone cannot resolve. Mitigating these conflicts suggests a different premise: each value can specialize within its own dedicated parameter subspace, free from compet-ing supervision during training, and only then be carefully composed into a single coherent model. 1arXiv:2611.27133v1 [cs.AI] 20 Nov 2026 Building on this, we propose M APES (Multi-value 

Alignment Pipeline via Expert Specialization), a four-stage pipeline: (i) per-value data routing, (ii) per-value expert training in isolated subspaces, (iii) preference-aware value merging (PAVM), and (iv) post-merge harmonization. The stages target suc-cessive layers of cross-value conflict: data, gradi-ent, parameter, and distributional conflicts, respec-tively. Several components build on established techniques; the contribution lies in their conflict-aware co-design and in PAVM, a new merge rule that weights each expert by the per-coordinate alignment between its delta and its preference-improvement direction. Empirically, across five preference align-ment benchmarks, MAPES attains the highest benchmark-level average for every backbone, al-though individual columns, mostly instruction-following, can regress. On GSM8K and GPQA we observe no large degradation (Ouyang et al., 2022), and harmlessness, a value never used in training, also improves. Matched controls show that ordinary post-merge DPO accounts for most of the recovery after merging, that the ensemble-KL term adds a smaller gain concentrated on honesty, and that the preference direction matters for the merge. Our main contributions are as follows: • We recast multi-value alignment as a conflict-aware pipeline problem, where cross-value con-flicts are mitigated structurally rather than ab-sorbed into a single shared objective. • We propose M APES , a systematic four-stage pipeline that addresses successive layers of con-flict at the data, gradient, parameter, and distribu-tion levels, with PAVM as its core technique. • We empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of MAPES on five preference alignment benchmarks and isolate the contribution of each stage with matched controls. 

2 Related Work 

Multi-value alignment. Existing multi-value alignment methods fall into three groups. (i) Single-model multi-objective methods, such as MODPO (Zhou et al., 2024), extend DPO with auxiliary reward margins to balance multiple ob-jectives. (ii) Score-conditioned methods incor-porate value scores into direct alignment: RA-DPO (Zhang et al., 2025) constructs reward-augmented preference data, while CPO (Guo et al., 2024) conditions on per-value preference scores. RiC (Yang et al., 2024), by contrast, requires sepa-rately trained reward models and thus falls outside our reward-model-free setting. (iii) Weight-space methods, such as Rewarded Soups (Rame et al., 2023), interpolate models trained for individual re-wards to approximate a Pareto front. Unlike these approaches, M APES addresses cross-value conflict across the full pipeline, data, gradient, parameter, and distributional stages, rather than at a single stage. It also requires neither per-value reward models nor frontier sweeping, relying only on stan-dard DPO supervision. 

Model merging. Model merging combines capa-bilities from separately fine-tuned models. Task Arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2023) adds task vec-tors, TIES-Merging (Yadav et al., 2023) mitigates interference through trimming and sign-based se-lection, and DARE (Yu et al., 2024) drops delta parameters before aggregation. Designed for task-specialized models, these methods rely mainly on parameter magnitude and do not identify directions that improve preference alignment. In contrast, PAVM (§3.3) incorporates preference-optimization signals and per-value improvement directions rel-ative to the reference model as alignment-aware merge weights, turning magnitude-based aggrega-tion into preference-aware composition. 

3 Methodology 

We introduce M APES (Multi-value Alignment 

Pipeline via Expert Specialization), a four-stage framework that resolves cross-value con-flict throughout the alignment pipeline (Figure 2): (1) data conflict , score-gap-based assignment routes each sample to its most discriminative value (§3.1); (2) gradient conflict , value-specific LoRA experts are trained using a gap- and exclusivity-weighted DPO loss (§3.2); (3) parameter con-flict , Preference-Aware Value Merging merges the experts via per-value preference improve-ment directions (§3.3); and (4) distributional con-flict , lightweight harmonization aligns the merged adapter with the expert ensemble (§3.4). 4 Experiments 

We evaluate M APES on multi-value preference alignment (§4.2), positive transfer to an unseen value (harmlessness) (§4.3), and alignment-tax ef-fects on general academic reasoning (§4.4). 

4.1 Experimental Settings Baselines. We compare MAPES with strong offline preference optimization baselines: stan-dard DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023); CPO (Guo et al., 2024) (value-specific rating tokens; trained with the CDPO strategy); RA-DPO (Zhang et al., 2025) (reward-augmented preference data); and MODPO (Zhou et al., 2024), a multi-objective extension of DPO. All methods share the same instruction-tuned base models, Qwen2.5-3B/7B (Qwen et al., 2025), Llama-3.2-3B (Meta AI, 2024), and Llama-3.1-8B (Dubey et al., 2024), and a unified reference model; per-method con-figurations are in Appendix C. We additionally report RS, a Rewarded-Soups-style (Rame et al., 2023) baseline that uniformly interpolates our value expert modules with no post-merge training; it therefore uses three quarters of the update bud-get of the other methods (Appendix L reports non-uniform weights). Code: https://anonymous. 4open.science/r/MAPES-F34F/ .

Tasks. We evaluate alignment across multiple human values: instruction-following with Arena-Hard-Auto (Li et al., 2024) and IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023); helpfulness and honesty with HHH-Alignment (Askell et al., 2021) and HH-RLHF (Bai et al., 2022a); truthfulness with TruthfulQA (Lin 5et al., 2022). The harmlessness subsets of HHH-Alignment and HH-RLHF test positive transfer to an unseen value, and GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) (0-shot CoT generation) and GPQA (Rein et al., 2023) (log-likelihood multiple choice) check alignment-tax effects. 

Training details. All models are trained on the binarized UltraFeedback dataset (Cui et al., 2024; Tunstall et al., 2023): after filtering, approximately 36k preference pairs, which M APES partitions by value and balances to approximately 22k samples (Appendix A.2). We fix total update steps to four epochs of standard DPO; M APES allocates three epochs to expert training and one to harmoniza-tion, matching the baselines’ update count since the data are partitioned, not replicated, across ex-perts. All reported numbers are averaged over three random seeds (42/43/44). For every method, in-cluding M APES , the learning rate is selected from

5 Analysis 

5.1 Value Gradient Conflict 

Appendix M reports the pairwise cosine similarity between per-value preference-improvement gradi-ents 𝑝 𝑣 , measured at the base model exactly as in PAVM’s direction estimation (§3.3; Qwen2.5-7B, 𝑁 =128 calibration pairs per value, attention-projection coordinates). The value gradients are moderately aligned, averaging +0.51 across pairs (weakest: helpfulness vs. instruction-following, 

+0.39 ), and at least one of the four gradients dis-agrees in sign with the others on approximately 

71% of the evaluated coordinates. These statistics describe gradient geometry; they are not evidence that improving one value must hurt another, since with all cosines positive a common first-order im-provement direction exists in the measured sub-space. What the sign disagreement does show is that one scalar merge weight per expert is a poor fit at most coordinates, which PAVM’s per-coordinate rule addresses; the behavioral evidence for isola-tion and for the merge rule is §5.2. 

5.2 Ablation Study

Random value assignment in place of score-gap routing degrades the average substantially, and re-placing the value-specific experts with a single ex-pert trained on the same routed data lowers the average from 49.02 to 46.04. We read the latter as evidence for the utility of expert isolation, not as evidence of gradient conflict (§5.1). Among merge operators, Task Arithmetic (Il-harco et al., 2023), TIES-Merging (Yadav et al., 2023), and DARE (Yu et al., 2024) all underper-form PAVM. 6 Conclusion 

We propose M APES , a multi-value alignment pipeline that treats conflict resolution as a first-class concern across all stages of the pipeline, data, gradient, parameter, and distribution, rather than isolating it within a single component. To our knowledge, PAVM is the first method to use per-coordinate alignment with DPO-gradient-based preference-improvement directions as merge weights for value-specialized preference adapters. It is co-designed with a harmonization stage that resolves the residual distributional inconsistencies inherent in parameter-level composition. Empiri-cally, across five preference alignment benchmarks, MAPES attains the highest benchmark-level aver-age for every backbone, with no large degrada-tion on GSM8K and GPQA. Matched controls at-tribute the gains to their sources: uniform composi-tion of the experts is already competitive, ordinary post-merge DPO recovers most of what a static merge loses, the preference direction and its per-coordinate weighting add a further, smaller gain, and the ensemble-KL term contributes mainly to honesty. 

Limitations 

MAPES has three principal limitations. First, the framework relies on preference datasets annotated with fine-grained value-specific scores. While Ul-traFeedback enables training on four core values 8(instruction-following, honesty, helpfulness, truth-fulness), such annotations are expensive to obtain and not universally available. Extending M APES 

to a broader value space, including dimensions such as fairness, harmlessness, or domain-specific safety constraints, would require either richer an-notation pipelines or weaker forms of value su-pervision (e.g., synthetic value labels from strong judges). Our observed positive transfer to harmless-ness (§4.3) is encouraging but is not a substitute for direct supervision over additional values. Second, our experiments cover four instruction-tuned backbones of 3B to 8B parameters from two model families, two sizes per family; M APES is the top method at each measured size (Appendix O), but the aggregate margin over the strongest base-line is smallest on the largest backbone (Llama-3.1-8B), and with two sizes per family we make no claim about the trend beyond the measured range. PAVM’s per-value preference-direction esti-mation uses a fixed, small calibration set ( 𝑁 =128 

per value), so this component does not grow with the training dataset; validating the pipeline on 13B+ and mixture-of-experts models remains fu-ture work. Relatedly, all compared methods live in the reward-model-free offline pairwise setting. PAVM’s direction statistic is not algebraically spe-cific to DPO (at the reference point it is the chosen-vs-rejected log-likelihood-gap gradient up to a pos-itive scalar, a form shared by other pairwise ob-jectives), but we have not established transfer to other alignment objectives empirically. Finally, the training pool shares prompts with TruthfulQA-MC for every compared method (Appendix A.3). Third, our value conditioning is realized through prompt-based injection of scalar scores. While this design is simple and language-model-native, it may be sensitive to prompt formulation and does not guarantee fine-grained inference-time control over value trade-offs. More structured condition-ing mechanisms, such as learned value-control to-kens, compositional weight selection at the merg-ing stage, or routing-style gating informed by user context, could further improve robustness and con-trollability of multi-value alignment systems built atop the M APES paradigm. We view such develop-ments as important future directions, building on the conflict-aware pipeline foundation introduced in this work. 

Ethical Consideration 

MAPES decomposes preference alignment into in-dividual human values, enabling controllable analy-sis and reweighting of alignment objectives. While this design improves transparency, it also intro-duces risks if individual values are overemphasized in isolation, potentially leading to undesirable be-haviors such as confident but inaccurate outputs. Moreover, M APES relies on value-annotated preference data, which may reflect subjective or culturally specific norms embedded in human judg-ments. As a result, value-specific behaviors learned by the model may not generalize uniformly across different social contexts. We partially mitigate these risks through empiri-cal analysis rather than direct deployment claims. Our conflict-aware merging study explicitly demon-strates failure modes of naïve value reweighting, highlighting the importance of balanced integration across values. M APES is intended as a research framework for analyzing multi-value alignment, and practical applications would require additional safeguards and human oversight.

11 A Training Dataset 

A.1 UltraFeedback Dataset 

To perform preference alignment tuning, we used the binarized UltraFeedback dataset. 3 We ap-plied additional filtering to ensure data consistency. Specifically, we removed samples with missing value annotations, as well as those where the cho-sen response had a lower value score than the re-jected response. We also excluded outlier samples with extremely long responses exceeding 6,000 tokens. After preprocessing, we retained 36k high-quality samples out of the original 55k. 

A.2 Data Preprocessing 

All reported results use the score-gap routing of §3.1; no source- or principle-based assignment is used. The procedure, with the counts produced by the released code, is: (1) from the binarized Ultra-Feedback pairs, keep those with complete ratings for the four values, an overall preference in favor of the chosen response, and at most 6,000 tokens in to-tal (43,080 pairs); (2) assign each pair to the value with the largest positive score gap, discarding pairs with no positive gap; ties for the largest gap are broken by a fixed value order (helpfulness, honesty, truthfulness, instruction-following). This retains 37,663 pairs (helpfulness 14,169 / honesty 11,393 / instruction-following 6,352 / truthfulness 5,749; Figure 6, left); (3) hold out 2,000 pairs for valida-tion (random split, seed 42), used for checkpoint and learning-rate selection; (4) downsample every value in the remaining training partition to the size of the smallest one, 5,457 pairs, giving 21,828 train-ing pairs (Figure 6, right). The same 21,828 pairs are used by every method; only M APES partitions them by value. 

A.3 Overlap with TruthfulQA-MC 

UltraFeedback contains prompts drawn from Truth-fulQA. Matching the 817 TruthfulQA-MC valida-tion questions against the prompts of the routed training pool (step 2 above, before balancing), 576 questions appear verbatim (747 within the full 55k binarized set). This affects every compared method identically, since all train on the same pairs, but it means the full TruthfulQA-MC set is not a clean held-out evaluation. On the 241 questions absent

B Prompt Design 

As described in Section 3.2, we train value expert adapters by conditioning them on target values and corresponding evaluation scores. These individual value-specific conditions are provided in the system prompt using structured natural language: 

You are an assistant that generates responses for the instruction while implicitly achieving the following target scores (on a scale of 1-5, where 1 is lowest and 5 is highest): [TARGET] score: [TARGET_SCORE] .This approach is also applied during post-merge harmonization (§3.4). The key difference is that each value-specific adapter is trained only on the subset of data annotated with its corresponding value, whereas the unified adapter during harmo-nization is trained on the full set of individual value-annotated data across all values. During inference, we use a multi-value mixture prompt to reflect all values concurrently. The sys-tem prompt includes all target scores as follows: 

You are an assistant that generates responses for the instruction while implicitly achieving the following target scores (on a scale of 1–5, where 1 is lowest and 5 is highest): Helpfulness score: [HELPFULNESS], Honesty score: [HONESTY], Truthfulness score: [TRUTHFULNESS], Instruction-Following score: [INSTRUCTION_FOLLOWING].

Fact 1 shows that Eq. Fact 2 shows that, rela-tive to uniform aggregation, PAVM only rescales magnitudes in the elected direction, by an amount proportional to the covariance between alignment evidence and delta size, never flips a sign, and never produces a step larger than the experts’ collective step (which makes (A2) automatic). Proposition 2 and its corollary turn this into a conditional dom-inance statement about the merged update itself, replacing the earlier score-level statement. 

Limitations of the bound. Three caveats war-rant explicit mention. First, Assumption (A1) is an idealization: real LLM losses are non-convex and not globally 𝐿 -smooth. We therefore interpret all statements as local properties within a small neighborhood around the base reference 𝜃 0; this assumption is standard in first-order optimization analysis (Foret et al., 2021; Wortsman et al., 2022) and aligns with prior work on linear mode con-nectivity (Frankle et al., 2020; Neyshabur et al., 2020) and parameter-efficient model merging (Il-harco et al., 2023; Rame et al., 2023; Ortiz-Jimenez et al., 2023). N Minimal Recipe 

MAPES reduces to four commands on top of a stan-dard DPO trainer: (1) route each preference pair to the value with the largest positive score gap and balance the partitions (Appendix A.2); (2) train one LoRA adapter per value with DPO on its par-tition (3 epochs); (3) run the merge script, which estimates 𝑝 𝑣 from 𝑁 =128 pairs per value and ap-plies Eq. (3) (about 0.3 GPU-hours); (4) train the merged adapter for one epoch with Eq. (4). No re-ward model or additional data is required, the total cost is 15–19% above standard DPO (Appendix G), the M APES -specific hyperparameters ( 𝛾 , 𝜆 , 𝛼 , 𝑑 ,

𝑁 ) are fixed across backbones, and the output is a single adapter with no inference-time overhead.
