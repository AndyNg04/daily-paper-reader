ABSTRACT 

Characterizing user engagement is essential for develop-ing effective human-computer interaction (HCI) applica-tions, particularly for children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Because home-based HCI naturally involves care-givers as conversational facilitators, analyzing both child and caregiver vocalizations can provide critical insights into char-acterizing interaction dynamics. Using a dataset of home-based socially assistive robot (SAR) interventions, this work demonstrates how child and caregiver vocalization patterns can model engagement in HCI contexts. We employed lin-ear mixed-effects models (LMMs) with child and caregiver acoustic data to identify acoustic features that significantly differentiated engagement from disengagement. The results demonstrated that children and caregivers share three signif-icant acoustic markers of engagement: mean Alpha Ratio (AlphaRatio), mean Harmonics-to-Noise Ratio (HNR), and mean Spectral Slope 500-1500 (SlopeV500-1500). This re-flects cleaner and smoother vocalizations during engagement across both groups. Furthermore, we evaluated a State ×

Group interaction model to compare the magnitudes of stan-dardized state-dependent differences between children and caregivers. The results indicated that children exhibit signif-icantly greater state-related variation than caregivers in three acoustic features: AlphaRatio, HNR, and SlopeV500-1500. 

Index Terms — Speaker Diarization, Autism Spectrum Disorder, Engagement Modeling, Acoustic Analysis, Human-Computer Interaction 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition that influences how individuals communicate, be-have, learn, and relate to others, affecting approximately 1 in 32 children in the United States [1]. Previous research suggests that common human-computer interaction (HCI) technologies, such as screen-based applications, robots, and

virtual reality, have a positive effect on the social and emo-tional development of children with ASD[2]. For HCI in-terventions to be effective, participants must remain actively engaged. Therefore, characterizing engagement provides valuable insights in developing transparent and interpretable HCI interventions. Vocal cues provide a non-invasive method for monitoring engagement in children with ASD. For example, Keerthana et al. [3] developed a speech-only engagement monitoring pipeline to guide personalized learning interventions, while Gupta et al. [4] demonstrated that vocal characteristics reflect engagement levels in children with ASD. Furthermore, in real-world and home-based environments, child engagement rarely occurs in isolation because caregivers actively partici-pate as facilitators. Recognizing this dyadic interaction, prior work has incorporated caregiver audio alongside child vocal-izations to model child engagement [5, 6, 7]. Despite the success of prior work in predicting child engagement using machine learning classifiers, existing ap-proaches predominantly treat audio-based engagement de-tection as a classification task. As a result, limited work has examined which specific acoustic features systematically vary with a child’s engagement state or how these state-dependent acoustic differences compare between children with ASD and their caregivers. Understanding these acoustic differences provides insight into the vocal correlates of child engagement and supports the development of interpretable HCI interventions for children with ASD. To address this gap, we conducted a systematic statisti-cal evaluation of diarized speech segments from children with ASD and their caregivers during an in-home intervention with a Socially Assistive Robot (SAR). Using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) with individual-level random intercepts, we first identified acoustic features associated with a child’s en-gagement state separately for child and caregiver speech. We then constructed a State × Group interaction LMM to test whether the magnitude of state-dependent acoustic differ-ences varied between child and caregiver speech. Together, these analyses characterized how the acoustic features of both children and caregivers varied with respect to a child’s arXiv:2611.26810v1 [cs.RO] 19 Nov 2026 engagement state. 

2. METHODOLOGY 2.1. Data Collection 

We used data collected by our laboratory [5, 8, 6] during a month-long, in-home socially assistive robot (SAR) inter-vention with seven children diagnosed with ASD, ranging in age from three to seven years. During the intervention, participants played math games on a tablet while a squash-and-stretch SAR [9] provided encouragement verbally and through screen-based facial expressions and body movement. An average of three hours of video and audio recordings were collected from each child participant by a camera mounted on top of the tablet that captured the participant and their sur-rounding environment. Caregivers were present in the back-ground for facilitating all session. 

2.2. Annotation 

We used binary engagement annotation labels that are part of the dataset [5]. Each video frame was annotated as the child being either engaged (1) or disengaged (0). The annotation criteria for engagement were listening or responding to the robot’s prompts, interacting with the tablet, or seeking guid-ance from the caregivers in the room. 

2.3. Diarization Model Evaluation 

For speech diarization model evaluation, ground-truth speaker labels were established through manual annotation of 20% of the data by two of the authors. We employed Pyannote [10, 11, 12] for speaker diarization, which achieved a mean diarization error rate (DER) of 29 .9 ± 9.3% evaluated against the ground-truth label. The model was capable of detecting overlapping speech of multiple speakers. 

2.4. Data Preprocessing 

Following speaker diarization, we segmented the recordings into speaker-specific audio clips. We applied the timestamps generated by Pyannote to the original recordings to generate clips for each speaker, excluding segments containing over-lapping speech. Each resulting clip contained speech from a single speaker and was subsequently used for acoustic feature extraction. We then mapped a child’s engagement labels to both child and caregiver speech clips based on the annotations described in Section 2.2. When a speaker-specific clip spanned multi-ple child engagement annotations, we split the clip into dis-tinct sub-clips corresponding to each engagement state to pre-serve the ground-truth labels. 2.5. Feature Selection 

For acoustic features, we used the extended Geneva Minimal-istic Acoustic Parameters Set (eGeMAPS), which contains 88 acoustic features [13]. The eGeMAPS features contain cep-stral parameters that are known to be effective in modeling affective states [13]. To enhance the interpretability of our re-sults, we followed [14] and selected a subset of 22 features related to the central tendencies (e.g., mean) of each acoustic dimension. Engagement had a significant effect on 10 acoustic fea-tures for child diarized audio Table 3), and for caregiver di-arized audio, engagement was significantly associated with five acoustic features (Table 4). For both groups, the signif-icant features were concentrated in energy-related, spectral, and temporal parameters. Three features–HNR, AlphaRatio, and SlopeV500-1500–were found to be significant in both groups. The coefficients showed shared directional alignment between child and caregiver; relative to disengaged states, en-gaged states showed an increase in HNR and a decrease in both AlphaRatio and SlopeV500-1500. The increase in HNR indicates higher harmonics com-pared to inharmonic noise during engaged state compared to disengaged state in both groups, reflecting a cleaner voice during the engaged state. The decrease in AlphaRatio indi-cates that high-frequency energy (1-5 kHz) decreased rela-tive to low-frequency energy (50-1000 Hz). This shows that the overall spectral balance shifted towards lower frequencies (50-1000 Hz), which can reflect a smoother and softer voice compared to a sharp voice associated with higher frequencies. According to [15], SlopeV500-1500 reflects tension, where flatter mean spectral slope indicates a voice with more ten-sion. Our results showed a steeper mean spectral slope in the engaged state, reflecting a less tense and smoother voice in the engaged state compared to the disengaged state. The overall analysis of the Separate Models showed that both child and caregiver produced cleaner, smoother, and higher quality vo-calizations during the engaged state compared to the disen-gaged state.

4. CONCLUSION 

In this work, we examined acoustic differences between en-gagement and disengagement in children with ASD with respect to their caregivers’ acoustic features during an in-home educational SAR intervention. Audio was diarized using Pyannote, and 22 eGeMAPS acoustic features were ex-tracted. Separate Model LMMs that measured the difference between engagement states revealed 10 significant acoustic features associated with child engagement state in child audio and five in caregiver audio. Three features overlapped, indi-cating cleaner and smoother vocalizations during engagement across both groups. Furthermore, the Interaction Model (State 

× Group) LMM showed significantly stronger engagement-related shifts in HNR, AlphaRatio, and SlopeV500–1500 compared to caregivers. No acoustic feature served as an exclusively group-specific marker, and interaction effects occurred only alongside significant within-group state varia-tion. These findings provide interpretable acoustic markers to guide the design of HCI interventions for children with ASD. Acknowledgements
