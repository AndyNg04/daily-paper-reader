## Abstract 

Online A/B testing is the standard approach for evaluating changes to user-facing systems, such as a new recommendation or ranking policy, before deployment. While the difference-in-means estima-tor of a standard A/B test is unbiased, it often suffers from high variance. When the treatment and production policies choose the same actions with similar probabilities, the resulting observations can add noise while providing little information about the differ-ence between the policies. Consequently, A/B tests can require large amounts of traffic and long durations to detect small treat-ment effects. Existing variance-reduction methods mainly improve the estimator after the data-collection scheme has been fixed, and recent overlap-aware designs only optimize a single traffic split between the two policies; thereby, they cannot direct traffic to the actions on which the policies disagree. In this paper, we jointly optimize how actions are sampled and how the resulting observations are used to estimate the treatment effect. We make two technical contributions. First, building on pair-wise off-policy evaluation, we derive the variance-optimal action-level logging policy for estimating the average treatment effect be-tween two policies, which has a closed-form solution for any fixed control variate. Second, we use historical logs to jointly optimize the logging policy and a control variate to further reduce variance. Our experimental results in simulated policy A/B tests show that our designs estimate the average treatment effect more accurately than existing variance reduction methods on every evaluated setting, while generally improving statistical power and reducing how often the worse policy is selected. As a result, our designs enable practi-tioners to reach reliable deployment decisions with considerably less online traffic.

## 1 Introduction 

Online controlled experiments are the standard way to evaluate changes to user-facing systems [16]. Before deploying a new search, recommendation, or ranking system, it is typically compared with the existing production system in an online A/B test. The test estimates how the change affects a business metric, and thereby provides evidence for the decision to deploy it. Many modern A/B tests compare policies: given a context, such as a user and a request, a policy defines a probability distribution over the available actions, e.g., over the items that could be shown

to the user. We call the observed outcome, such as whether the user clicks on the item, the reward. A conventional A/B test first assigns each unit to the treatment or production policy, then sam-ples an action from the assigned policy. However, two policies may recommend many of the same items, sometimes with the same probabilities, and an item that receives the same probability under both policies contributes nothing to the difference between their expected rewards [13]. Nevertheless, both experimental groups may show this item frequently, for instance, when the item is popular, and the difference-in-means estimator includes the resulting reward noise in both group averages. Pairwise off-policy estimation can use the known action probabilities to account for this overlap [13, 21]. 

High variance and sample inefficiency. The difference-in-means estimator of a standard A/B test is unbiased, but its variance determines how much traffic is needed to detect an effect [16]. This problem is especially important for incremental policy updates: the action space can contain tens or hundreds of actions, the two policies can overlap substantially, and the true effect is often small. A high-variance test requires more observations to attain a given power for the same effect size, which can delay the deployment decision [16]. 

Limitations of existing variance reduction. Existing variance-reduction methods for online experiments mainly im-prove the estimator after the experimental design has been fixed. Regression adjustment and CUPED remove predictable variation from the outcome using pre-treatment covariates [1, 4, 20]. Sepa-rately, off-policy evaluation uses logged data to evaluate a target policy from data collected by a different policy, known as the logging policy [2, 12]. More recently, researchers have used policy overlap to optimize traffic allocation between the treatment and production policies [13]. However, adjusting this overall traffic split does not allow them to independently choose how often each action is sampled in a given context. This restriction limits the potential gains in sample efficiency. To the best of our knowledge, no existing work has designed the action-level data collection of a policy A/B test jointly with its estimator. 

Combining off-policy evaluation with A/B testing. We aim to estimate the difference between the expected rewards of a fixed treatment policy and a fixed production policy—the average treat-ment effect (ATE). Instead of first assigning each interaction to one of these policies, we sample its action using a separate log-ging policy, which specifies how often each action is sampled in a given context. We weight each observed reward by the difference between the two policies’ probabilities of choosing its action. These weights also correct for how often the logging policy samples that action, giving an estimate of the ATE [14]. This approach builds on pairwise off-policy evaluation (OPE), specifically Δ-IPS [14]. To

reduce variance further, we use a control variate [2, 8] as a base-line reward prediction. The estimator corrects this prediction using observed outcomes, so that prediction errors do not bias the esti-mated effect. We jointly optimize the logging policy and the control variate to minimize the variance of the ATE estimator. We fit both on historical logs and fix both before collecting any new data. Our key result is a closed-form expression for the variance-optimal logging policy under any fixed control variate. This logging policy samples every action in proportion to the product of (i) the absolute difference between the two policies’ probabilities of that action and (ii) the reward uncertainty that remains after applying the control variate. A balanced A/B test allocates equal traffic to the two policies and samples actions according to the assigned pol-icy. Our optimal logging policy instead favors actions with larger probability differences, especially when their remaining reward uncertainty is high. For each candidate control variate, our closed-form result gives its corresponding optimal sampling policy. We therefore optimize only the control variate, rather than searching separately over both components; this is known as partial mini-mization. We use either a scalar control variate or a model-based control variate whose reward prediction varies with the context and action [2]. We evaluate our designs in simulated online A/B tests that cap-ture the main difficulties of incremental policy updates: stochastic policies over actions that overlap broadly, reward noise that de-pends on the context and the action, and a small treatment effect. We compare them with balanced A/B testing and methods that optimize the traffic split between policies. We measure estimation accuracy, statistical power, and how often the worse policy is se-lected. Across different numbers of actions and amounts of data, our designs estimate the effect more accurately than all baselines and, in most cases, detect it more often and select the worse policy less often. In particular, our model-based control-variate design reaches the mean squared error (MSE) of balanced A/B testing with 39% to 51% of the evaluation traffic, reduces MSE relative to the stronger traffic-split baseline by 26% to 41%, and, with 10,000 evaluation interactions, increases the statistical power of balanced A/B testing by a factor of 1.5 to 2.1. Our main contributions are: 

• A formulation of policy A/B testing as the joint design of an action-level logging policy and a control variate for estimating the ATE using a counterfactual estimator; 

• A variance-optimal contextual logging policy for any fixed control variate in closed form, optimized via partial mini-mization, which allows us to eliminate the logging policy from the joint design problem; 

• Scalar and model-based control-variate designs, together with a uniform safety backoff that retains a small probability of sampling every action, and a two-stage procedure that keeps the estimator unbiased when the historical reward model is misspecified; and 

• Empirical evidence from simulated policy A/B tests that our designs improve estimation accuracy in all evaluated settings, and statistical power and policy selection in most settings, over balanced A/B testing and baselines that opti-mize the overall traffic split between policies. 

## 2 Related Work 

Variance reduction in online experiments. Variance reduc-tion has a long history in online experimentation. CUPED uses pre-experiment covariates to remove predictable variation from the metric [1], and subsequent work uses boosted trees and cross-fitted machine-learning models for the same purpose [4, 20]. These approaches improve the estimator, but keep the randomization scheme fixed. We also use a historical reward model, but we treat the sampling policy as a design choice: the reward predictions help determine which actions to sample in the new experiment. 

Off-policy evaluation and control variates. Inverse propen-sity scoring (IPS) reweights observed rewards to evaluate a policy from logs collected by another policy. It provides unbiased estimates under the full support condition: the logger must have a positive probability of sampling every action needed for evaluation [5– 7, 9, 10, 12]. The doubly robust (DR) estimator combines reward predictions with an IPS correction based on observed outcomes [2]. Optimal OPE theory studies the accuracy of estimators based on reward predictions, importance weighting, or both [24]. More ro-bust doubly robust (MRDR) estimation fits the reward model to minimize the DR estimator’s variance, rather than for outcome pre-diction alone [3]. Optimal baseline corrections give a closed-form constant baseline that minimizes variance for a fixed logging pol-icy [8]. Δ-OPE estimates the treatment effect between two policies from data collected by a single logging policy [14]. Our estimator builds on this two-policy comparison, while MRDR and optimal baseline corrections motivate fitting the control variate to reduce variance. We additionally optimize the logging policy jointly with the control variate. 

Data collection for policy evaluation. Several reinforcement-learning methods design data collection to estimate the value of a single policy; the logger is called a behavior policy in this setting. Hanna et al. [11] derive and search for a behavior policy that mini-mizes the mean squared error of importance-sampling evaluation. Liu and Zhang [19] derive a closed-form variance-optimal behavior policy and approximate it using offline data, while Liu et al. [18] jointly design the behavior policy and a baseline. In contextual ban-dits, Wan et al. [23] study efficient, safe data collection to evaluate and compare policies. Tucker and Joachims [22] design additional logging to augment existing data for sample-efficient evaluation of a contextual-bandit policy. We build on this work to estimate the treatment effect between two stochastic policies, jointly optimizing the logger and a learned control variate and evaluating power and policy selection. 

Adaptive experimental design. Neyman allocation assigns more traffic to treatments with more variable outcomes, with prob-abilities proportional to their outcome standard deviations. Modern adaptive designs extend this idea to treatment effects that vary across contexts [15]. Our result recovers the contextual version of this allocation when the two policies always choose different actions and the control variate equals the true mean reward. For stochastic policies, the allocation must also account for how much their action probabilities differ. 

Policy overlap in A/B testing. Jeunen [13] shows that overlap between the treatment and production policies can reduce the vari-ance of policy A/B tests. The method optimizes one overall traffic

Jeunen [13] also considers model-based control variates through the pairwise MRDR estimator, Δ-MRDR. Other work improves estimation within an A/B test. Sakhi et al. [21] use the policies’ action probabilities, changing only the estimator, not data collection. Konishi et al. In contrast, we also change data collection: for each context, our logger sets individual action probabilities using the differences between the policies and the reward uncertainty remaining after control-variate correction, rather than only an overall traffic split.

We formulate policy A/B testing as pairwise off-policy evalua-tion, introduce a control-variate estimator, and derive its variance-optimal logger. Eliminating the logger by partial minimization yields our scalar and model-based designs.

## 4 Experimental Setup 4.1 Research questions 

RQ1 Does variance-optimal action-level logging improve estima-tion accuracy, statistical power, and policy selection over standard and balanced A/B testing and overlap-aware policy mixtures? 

RQ2 Does the model-based control variate improve over the sim-pler scalar joint method? 

RQ3 Are the conclusions robust to changes in action-space size, the historical log, and the evaluation sample? 

## 4.2 Synthetic environment 

Our synthetic contextual-bandit environment uses Gaussian con-texts ( 𝐷 = 25), overlapping softmax policies, and Bernoulli rewards with context- and action-dependent means. We numerically cali-brate the ATE to approximately 0 .005 for each action-space size, holding the environment fixed across sample sizes. The historical log is collected uniformly over actions. We fit an action-wise ridge reward model and use all historical contexts to design the logger and control variate before collecting fresh evaluation data. All learned contextual designs use a 5% safety backoff. Appendix A.1 gives the exact policy and reward construction, calibration procedure, and optimization settings.

## 4.4 Experiment design 

We fit the reward model and optimize the design using 𝑛 hist his-torical interactions and all their contexts, then estimate the ATE from 𝑛 eval fresh interactions ( 𝑛 in Section 3). The complete grid combines 𝐾 ∈ { 25 , 50 , 75 } actions, 𝑛 hist ∈ { 10 ,000 , 30 ,000 } historical interactions, and 𝑛 eval ∈ { 1,000 , 2,000 , . . . , 10 ,000 } evaluation inter-actions, giving 60 settings. Each of the 500 independent replications per setting has one historical log and one evaluation batch. We freeze the designs fitted on that log before generating evaluation contexts. Methods share these contexts but sample actions from their own loggers. To reduce Monte Carlo noise in comparisons between methods, we use common random numbers: all methods share the same evaluation contexts and the same uniform draws for action and reward sampling, while each samples actions from its own logger. Historical and evaluation samples are generated from independent, fixed random seeds. Standard A/B is coupled with Balanced A/B: given the sampled action 𝑎 , we assign it to the treatment arm with probability 𝜋 𝑡 (𝑎 | 𝑥 )/{ 𝜋 𝑡 (𝑎 | 𝑥 ) + 𝜋 𝑝 (𝑎 | 𝑥 )} .This yields exactly the distribution of a standard randomized A/B test, while sharing contexts, actions, and rewards with Balanced A/B. Standard A/B uses no historical data, so the same evaluation replications are shown in both historical-log rows.

## 5 Results 5.1 Comparison with A/B testing and policy mixtures 

Estimation accuracy. Figure 1 reports the MSE as the evalua-tion sample size increases from 1,000 to 10,000. Standard A/B has the highest MSE across the grid, followed by Balanced A/B, and Overlap-aware A/B and Reward-aware A/B have similar curves. In contrast, Scalar joint and Model-CV have a lower MSE than all four baselines in every panel and at every evaluation size. For unbiased estimators with fixed designs, MSE is inversely proportional to evaluation sample size, so MSE ratios equal the traffic ratios needed for equal accuracy. Averaged geometrically over the ten evaluation sizes, this ratio is 0.49 to 0.53 for Scalar joint and 0.39 to 0.51 for Model-CV relative to Balanced A/B, and 0.73 to 0.77 and 0.59 to 0.74, respectively, relative to the better of the two policy mixtures. Hence, Model-CV reaches the MSE of Balanced A/B with only 39% to 51% of its evaluation traffic. Relative to Standard A/B, the geo-metrically averaged MSE ratio of Model-CV is 0.12 to 0.19, an 81% to 88% reduction. The improvement of Balanced A/B over Standard A/B also shows the benefit of accounting for policy overlap before optimizing the logger. Reward-aware A/B and Scalar joint both fit a scalar control vari-ate on historical reward predictions. Reward-aware A/B selects one mixing weight, whereas Scalar joint chooses an action distribution for every context. The lower MSE of Scalar joint suggests that this additional freedom matters. However, the comparison does not isolate the effect of the logger because each method fits its scalar for its own allocation. 

Statistical power. Figure 2 shows generally increasing power with evaluation sample size: at 10,000 observations, Balanced A/B reaches roughly 0.15 to 0.20 across the six panels, whereas Scalar joint and Model-CV reach 0.21 to 0.38; Model-CV has the highest power in every panel, between 1.5 and 2.1 times that of Balanced A/B. At the same evaluation size, Standard A/B has power between 0.062 and 0.104, so Model-CV has 3.1 to 3.9 times the power. The absolute power remains below 0.40 in the studied range. 

Selecting the better policy. Figure 3 shows that the selection error decreases as more evaluation observations are collected: with 10,000 evaluation observations, Balanced A/B has a wrong-sign rate of approximately 0.15 to 0.23, whereas Scalar joint and Model-CV have rates of 0.08 to 0.14 and 0.05 to 0.13, respectively; that is, Model-CV reduces the wrong-sign rate of Balanced A/B by 42% to 63%. ## 5.2 Scalar versus model-based control variates 

Model-CV improves on Scalar joint in most cases: across the 60 combinations of panel and evaluation size, it has a lower MSE in 58, a higher power in 53, and a lower selection error in 51, and all exceptions in power occur at evaluation sizes of at most 5,000. Since both methods use a contextual logger, this shows that a control variate that depends on the context and the action can help, whilst the scalar method remains competitive. Thus, model-based correction outperforms scalar correction in most settings, particularly at larger evaluation sizes, but not uni-formly; scalar correction remains competitive (RQ2). 

## 5.3 Robustness across settings 

Historical sample size. Increasing the historical sample from 10,000 to 30,000 interactions reduces the MSE of Model-CV in 28 of the 30 combinations of action-space and evaluation size, but has little effect on Scalar joint (13 of 30), whose design depends on the historical data only through a single parameter and, weakly, through the reward model in its logger. 

Number of actions. Performance does not worsen monotoni-cally as 𝐾 increases from 25 to 75; for example, some settings with 

𝐾 = 75 have lower errors than their counterparts with 𝐾 = 50. Since each action count uses a separately generated and calibrated environment, these panels do not isolate the effect of adding actions, but show whether comparisons between methods persist across three different environments. Within the studied range, rankings are broadly similar across action-space, historical-log, and evaluation sizes; additional evalua-tion data yield more consistent benefits than additional historical data (RQ3). 

Evaluation sample size. Scalar joint and Model-CV have a lower MSE than all four baselines at every evaluation size from 1,000 to 10,000. Differences in power and selection error are smallest at the lowest evaluation sizes, where all methods have power close to the 5% level. Thus, the advantage of action-level logging in MSE persists across action-space sizes, historical-log sizes, and evaluation sizes, whereas its advantages in power and selection error are clearest at larger evaluation sizes (RQ3). 

## 6 Discussion 

Interpreting the comparison. Overlap-aware A/B and Reward-aware A/B both choose a single mixing weight between the two policies and perform similarly in our simulations. Scalar joint and Model-CV instead choose an action distribution for every context and obtain a lower MSE. This pattern supports using a broader class of logging policies when the platform permits it. It does not establish that every learned contextual logger outperforms a policy mixture. The result depends on the fitted reward model, the control variate, and the optimization. Corollary 3.3 holds only for a fixed control variate and the true reward moments, before the uniform backoff. 

Scalar versus model-based control variates. Scalar joint of-fers a useful starting point: its control variate has only one param-eter, it performs well in our simulations, and it is inexpensive to optimize once the historical reward model has been fitted. Model-CV allows the correction to vary across contexts and actions, at the cost of additional parameters; its lower MSE and higher power in most settings justify this flexibility, but do not establish uniform superiority. Therefore, the choice between the two methods should depend on the improvement obtained in the application and on the cost of fitting and maintaining the model. 

## 7 Conclusion 

For estimating the average treatment effect between two stochas-tic policies, we have shown that, for a fixed control variate, the variance-minimizing logging policy samples every action in pro-portion to the absolute difference between the two policies’ proba-bilities of that action, weighted by its residual reward uncertainty, which reduces the joint design problem to an optimization over the control variate. Our scalar and model-based designs are learned from historical logs, remain unbiased even when the historical re-ward model is inaccurate, and, in our simulations, improve MSE, power, and policy-selection error over balanced A/B testing and optimized policy mixtures. 

Future directions. Useful extensions include designs for re-peated users and for settings that restrict which actions may be sampled. Updating the logger as new data arrive would require inference that accounts for adaptive data collection. For ranked lists, we would need to specify whether an action is a whole list or an individual item, and how interactions between items affect the reward model.
