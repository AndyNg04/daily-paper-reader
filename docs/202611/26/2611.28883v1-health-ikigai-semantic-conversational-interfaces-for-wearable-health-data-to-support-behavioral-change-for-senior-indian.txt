Abstract
In ubiquitous health contexts, wearable feedback is often encoun-
tered in brief, interrupted moments of everyday life. However,
many wearable and mobile health systems still present personal
health data through numerical dashboards, leaving users and health
coaches to interpret trends, thresholds, and deviations on their own.
This interpretive burden may be especially relevant for adults aged
50–65 in India, where digital literacy, health literacy, language,
and daily routines can shape how health technologies are under-
stood and used. We present Health Ikigai, a design-oriented proof-
of-concept framework that reframes wearable activity feedback
from dashboard-based numerical interpretation to semantic con-
versational feedback. The prototype transforms replayed wearable-
derived activity data into baseline-aware triggers, plain-language
feedback, limited-turn conversational prompts, and coach-facing
review summaries. A preliminary formative evaluation with ten
adults in India suggests that participants perceived the interaction
as understandable, lightweight, supportive, and actionable. We dis-
cuss design implications for low-burden wearable health interfaces
that preserve coach oversight while reducing users’ interpretive
work.

Introduction
Wearable devices increasingly collect continuous health-related sig-
nals such as physical activity, step counts, heart rate, sleep duration,
inactivity, and other behavioral or physiological indicators [8, 21].
However, access to continuous personal health data does not au-
tomatically lead to reflection or behavior change. Prior work in
personal informatics and wearable/mobile based self-tracking has
shown that users must interpret what their data means, decide
whether a pattern is meaningful, and connect that interpretation to
feasible everyday action [5, 11, 13–16]. Wearable health feedback is
therefore not only a sensing problem, but also a sensemaking and
interaction design problem.
Many wearable and mobile health systems still present personal
health data through dashboard-first interfaces that foreground nu-
merical indicators, trend charts, thresholds, and summary views [6,
24]. While such interfaces can support analysis, they often leave
users to determine whether a deviation is important, what may
have caused it, and what action should follow [4, 5, 11]. We use
the term interpretive burden to describe this cognitive work of
transforming wearable metrics into everyday meaning and feasible
action. This burden can increase when users must interpret base-
lines, trends, and deviations during brief, interrupted moments of
daily life rather than sustained analytic review [4, 9]. Prior work
also suggests that users need scaffolding to connect personal health
data to self-management decisions [18].
This challenge is especially relevant for adults aged 50–65 in
India, who may face elevated risks related to diabetes, hypertension,
obesity, and physical inactivity [1, 20]. At the same time, differences
in digital literacy, health literacy, language, and everyday routines
can shape how health technologies are understood and used [10,
22]. For people who check wearable feedback while commuting,
working, caregiving, or resting, dense numerical dashboards may be
less appropriate than simple feedback based on personal baselines
and small feasible actions.
In response, we present Health Ikigai, a design-oriented proof-of-
concept framework that reframes wearable feedback from dashboard-
first numerical interpretation to meaning-first conversational sense-
making. As shown in Figure 1, the system shifts the primary user
experience away from dashboards that require users to interpret

Health Ikigai should be understood as a proof-of-concept rather
than a validated health intervention. The current prototype uses
replayed Apple Health step-count records to demonstrate an in-
teraction pipeline from activity data to trigger detection, semantic
abstraction, limited-turn conversation, and coach-facing review. A
preliminary formative evaluation with ten participants aged 50–65
in India provides early evidence that this interaction style was
perceived as clear, supportive, and actionable. We make three con-
tributions: (1) framing dashboard-first wearable feedback as in-
terpretive burden in preventive activity support; (2) presenting a
layered proof-of-concept system connecting baseline-aware trig-
ger detection, semantic abstraction, limited-turn conversation, and
coach-facing oversight; and (3) reporting preliminary formative
findings on plain-language semantic feedback and bounded action-
oriented conversation.

Design Rationale and System Overview
Health Ikigai was developed through a user-centered design pro-
cess comprising persona creation, journey mapping, and feature
ideation. We defined our target users as adults aged 50–65 who

nals feeding both user and coach interfaces
are managing weight and daily health routines in relation to age-
related conditions or lifestyle transitions. These users may own
wearable devices but find complex numerical dashboards difficult to
interpret during routine daily activity, consistent with prior work
on technology acceptance among older adults [3, 19].
We developed a persona around a small businessman in India
aged 50–65, who wants to improve health through regular walking
but finds wearable dashboards confusing, has limited time for rou-
tine data review, and prefers simple meaningful feedback revealing
design priorities around autonomy, dignity, non-judgmental lan-
guage, and advice that fits real routines. Journey mapping across
daily routines, informed by prior work on routine-based mobile
sensing [14], revealed that the system should support reflection
and offer small achievable actions rather than issue instructions in
response to baseline deviations, with feedback that fits naturally
into daily rhythms. Observations and insights were converged into
six design requirements: feedback should be meaning-first, metric-
second; interpretation should be grounded in personal baselines
rather than population norms; prompts should be non-judgmental
and respectful; interactions should be lightweight and easy to parse;
the dashboard should support coach oversight without burdening
users; and the system should preserve the user’s sense of con-
trol and independence. These themes are consistent with literature
showing that technology acceptance and sustained use among older
adults are shaped by perceived simplicity, social meaning, and align-
ment with personal values and routines [7, 19]. Ideation produced
proposals across three areas: coach-facing dashboard support, mo-
tivational feedback for users, and interaction design emphasising
plain language over medicalized phrasing and emotionally safe
communication that avoids blame [12].
Health Ikigai presents meaning-first interaction to users while
preserving reviewability for coaches through dashboard inspection
and details-on-demand (see Figure 2). Health Ikigai uses the seman-
tic layer to drive a short conversational behavior-change support
flow. In the current proof-of-concept, this flow follows a reflect-and-
evoke principle. Once a trigger has been detected, the system begins
by reflecting the observed state back to the user in simple language.
For example, instead of saying “your steps are below baseline by
180,” the system may say “This week feels a little lighter than usual
on movement.” It then follows with a reflective question such as

“What has been different today?” If the user replies with contextual
information such as “I have been busy,” the system shifts into an
evocation mode. Rather than prescribing a single response, it offers
two or three feasible alternatives and asks which feels most realistic.
For example, it may suggest a short walk after lunch, a few brief
movement breaks between tasks, or another low-burden option
compatible with the user’s routine. If the user selects one of these
options, the system reinforces the choice and intentionally stops
encouraging manageable action.
In the current proof-of-concept, the prototype uses approxi-
mately one month of step-count records generated by smart watches
and stored in the health app, collected via a custom logger. These
records are replayed sequentially rather than streamed in real time,
allowing the prototype to demonstrate the full interaction pipeline,
from activity data to trigger to semantic message to conversational
exchange without requiring a live wearable connection. The fol-
lowing subsections describe each layer of the pipeline in turn. The
architecture was developed around behavior-change-oriented inter-
action design, with emphasis on semantic interpretation, feasible
action suggestions, and lightweight coaching support [4, 23]. The
main contribution of Health ikigai is the interplay and communica-
tion from numerical layer to the semantic layer.
The semantic abstraction layer translates numerical trigger con-
ditions into plain-language health signals before user-facing in-
teraction begins. For example, a date with above-baseline activity
produces a positive acknowledgment with no intervention; a below-
baseline date generates a low-activity trigger, a dashboard alert,
and a conversational prompt. The user can respond in natural lan-
guage, receiving two or three feasible alternatives, after which the
system reinforces the chosen option and encourages real-world

Study Design
We conducted a preliminary formative evaluation to assess whether
the prototype’s semantic and conversational design was under-
standable, actionable, and perceived as supportive by the target
population. Ten participants were recruited from India, all in the
50–65 age range (eight men, two women). Several participants re-
ported managing everyday health conditions including diabetes

and high blood pressure, reflecting characteristics relevant to the
broader target population for cardiometabolic preventive support.
Recruitment inclusion criteria required that participants had prior
difficulty interpreting numerical health information directly, as
assessed through a short screening question. Informed consent
was obtained from all participants before they interacted with the
prototype and completed the survey.
Participants interacted with the prototype individually through a
facilitated session in which a low-activity scenario was replayed and
the conversational interface was demonstrated. Each participant
engaged with the prototype directly, responding to the system’s
prompts as they would in everyday use. Following the interac-
tion, participants completed a ten-item questionnaire and provided
open-ended verbal feedback. Sessions were conducted in a lan-
guage comfortable to each participant. All participants provided
informed consent prior to participation. The study focused on as-
sessing whether participants could understand the semantic feed-
back, perceive the suggested actions as feasible, and experience the
interaction as supportive, lightweight, and easy to follow.
4
Preliminary Results
Responses were coded on a five-point scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5
= Strongly Agree), with an overall mean of 4.76 across all items. Se-
mantic clarity (Q1–Q2) returned a mean of 4.60 (𝑆𝐷= 0.516, 95% CI
[4.23, 4.97]), with participants reporting that plain-language word-
ing helped them understand health states without interpreting raw
numbers or trend charts. Action design and tone (Q3–Q6) scored
a mean of 4.80 (𝑆𝐷= 0.422, 95% CI [4.50, 5.10]), with participants
valuing multiple distinct action options as more motivating and
respectful than a single prescribed directive. Conversational design
items showed the strongest endorsement: Q7 (“chatbot stopped at
an appropriate point”) achieved a perfect mean of 5.00 with zero
variance, and Q8 (“stopping helped focus on action”) returned 4.70
(𝑆𝐷= 0.483, 95% CI [4.35, 5.05]), suggesting the turn limit was
perceived as an intentional and effective design feature rather than
a restriction. Overall experience items (Q9–Q10) returned means of
4.70 and 4.80 respectively. The preference item for semantic conver-
sational feedback over numerical dashboard presentation was also
highly rated within the demonstrated prototype context, scoring
4.80 (see Figure 5).

Discussion and Conclusion
Health Ikigai reframes wearable health feedback as a sensemaking
problem rather than only a visualization problem. By separating
backend numerical computation from frontend communication, the
prototype translates step-count deviations into plain-language feed-
back while preserving numerical transparency for coach review. In
response to RQ1, this design suggests one possible way to shift in-
terpretive work away from users without removing the dashboard
entirely. Instead, the dashboard is repositioned as a coach-facing su-
pervisory layer where coaches can inspect baseline settings, trigger
states, conversation summaries, and underlying step records when
needed. The preliminary formative results suggest that participants
perceived the semantic conversational interaction as clear, support-
ive, and actionable, particularly when the conversation stopped

In response to RQ2, the design highlights conversational-semantic
requirements for low-burden wearable feedback in contexts such
as India, where language, health literacy, digital confidence, daily
routines, and cardiometabolic risk may shape how personal health
data is understood and acted upon. The current work remains an
early-stage proof-of-concept. The prototype uses replayed wearable-
derived step-count records rather than live wearable streams, fo-
cuses on a single activity metric, and was evaluated with a small
formative sample without a comparison condition or health out-
come measures. Future work should evaluate Health Ikigai with
larger and more diverse groups, live multimodal wearable data, and
controlled comparisons with conventional user-facing dashboards.
