# Don’t Sleep, Keep Watch: Proactive Perception for Computer-Use Agents 

Jiaqi Su 1, Jie-Jing Shao 2, Yichen Liu 3, Long-Kai Huang 4, Haiyan Yin 21National University of Singapore 2A*STAR Centre for Frontier AI Research (A*STAR CFAR)

Abstract 

Computer-use agents (CUAs) built on vision–language models are increasingly entrusted with long-horizon tasks in live desktop, web, and mobile environments, where the screen keeps changing on its own. However, current CUAs lack real-time situational awareness: they perceive the environment through discrete snapshots, and the substantial inference latency of VLMs, compounded by highly dynamic interfaces, opens a perception–action gap in which the observed state frequently becomes stale before the corresponding action is executed. To bridge this gap, we present NightWatch , a plug-and-play framework that endows any CUA with continuous, temporally grounded perception without changing the agent’s model, prompt, or action space. NightWatch couples the VLM agent with NightWatch-0.8B, a lightweight System-1 model that performs streaming monitoring of the interface state and keeps the agent synchronized with the evolving environment at low computational cost and latency, thereby grounding each decision in the current state rather than an outdated snapshot and reducing both stale actions and idle waiting while preserving task success. Experiments on desktop and dynamic web environments (OSWorld-Verified and DynaCU-Bench) show that NightWatch, driven by a watcher of only 0.8B parameters, reduces non-reasoning wall-clock time by 33% and wasted time by 53% on OSWorld while keeping task success on par, raises success on DynaCU-Bench from 39.5% to 44.2% with the large VLM agent, and is more efficient than passive-watching baselines such as AOI and PUSV under the same screenshot-only input, as well as a System-1 decision model more than 30 × its size. Overall, NightWatch demonstrates that the timing of perception is as critical as its content, marking a step toward agents that maintain continuous awareness of their environment rather than perceiving it turn by turn.

## 1 Introduction 

Computer-use agents (CUAs) built on vision–language models (VLMs) now operate real desktops, browsers, and phones [44, 30, 27, 49]. Unlike a single image, these screens move on their own: pages load, dialogs pop up, notifications flash and vanish, and animations settle on schedules that the agent does not control. Yet almost every CUA still perceives its world through the same loop: take a screenshot, reason, act, sleep for a fixed delay, and repeat. This loop is reactive , or time-triggered: the agent looks only when its own clock says so, which has two consequences. First, the agent is blind between screenshots. The gap from observation to execution averages 6 .51 s on OSWorld [45] and reaches 4 .18–15 .43 s on Android [26]. A pop-up arriving within this gap can lower task success by 47% [53], and agents that act on an outdated screen rarely recover from it [50]. Second, how long to wait is set by hand, with no principled signal that the screen is ready. Across the official agent scripts of OSWorld [44], the post-action sleep ranges from 0 to 10 s, a twentyfold spread on a single benchmark. A short sleep captures a half-rendered transition, whereas

Recent work has begun to watch the screen between steps, but the watching remains passive (Figure 1b). AOI [17] samples frames between steps yet only attaches them to the next scheduled observation, and PUSV [45] checks the screen once, right before dispatch. Event-triggered waits and monitors read DOM or trajec-tory text rather than pixels [24, 38], while pre-compiled policy trees reuse the same large VLM as their observer [10]. None of them lets the screen decide when the agent looks, and none can stop reasoning that the screen has already made obsolete. We argue that CUAs need proactive percep-tion : the perception layer, rather than the agent’s fixed schedule, decides when the agent observes, and whether its ongoing reasoning and pending action are still valid. The agent still pursues the task it was given, but no longer waits for its own clock to no-tice that the world has changed. Proactive perception is close in spirit to streaming video models that decide when to respond without being asked [7, 37, 25], and to event-triggered control, which samples a system when its state demands rather than at fixed intervals [4, 33]. Realizing it is hard: judgments must arrive in time, must separate changes that affect the pending action from unrelated animations, and err both ways, as a needless interrupt discards finished reasoning and an early wake shows an unready screen. To this end, we present NightWatch , a plug-and-play framework that equips any CUA with proactive perception. A lightweight watcher continuously looks at the screen and answers a fixed set of event questions, and deterministic rules turn its answers into three interventions (Figure 1c): interrupting 

reasoning that a screen change has made obsolete, voiding a pending action whose target has gone stale, and waking the agent the moment the screen settles, in place of the fixed sleep. System-1 decision models [16, 3, 48] are cheap enough to watch every frame, because they answer many multiple-choice questions from one encoding instead of generating text. Recent work has begun to hand agent decisions to such models, which choose the next tool or action in place of the large model [57]. Our small model instead never chooses what the agent does, but watches the screen and tells the agent when to look and whether its current reasoning or planned action still holds. To our knowledge, NightWatch is the first framework to bring proactive perception to CUAs, letting the screen decide when the agent looks and whether its reasoning and pending action still hold. The design rests on three further observations. The questions that matter, such as whether a dialog has appeared or whether the screen is still loading, are generic across applications, so the questions and their thresholds can be fixed once and reused across applications. In addition, small models read states reliably but decide poorly, so the watcher only reads the screen while code makes the decision. Finally, a quiet screen is not necessarily a settled one, since a page may pause while it loads, so the watcher learns readiness from hindsight labels, read from what the screen does next in recorded 

2trajectories, rather than from stillness alone. Our watcher, NightWatch-0.8B, is trained on desktop interaction trajectories from independently generated tasks and on procedurally generated synthetic interfaces, without any benchmark task, file, or trajectory. With screenshot-only input on 100 OSWorld-Verified tasks, NightWatch reduces non-reasoning wall-clock time by 33% and wasted time by 53% with an unchanged large VLM agent, while task success stays on par with the unmodified agent (68 .0% vs. 66 .3%). On the 49 dynamic web tasks of DynaCU-Bench, the same frozen watcher raises success from 39 .5% to 44 .2% with the large VLM agent and keeps it with the small one. Moreover, NightWatch is more efficient than passive-watching baselines such as AOI [17] and PUSV [45], and in a separate comparison of watchers, NightWatch-0.8B is faster and more successful than a System-1 decision model more than 30 × its size. Our contributions are as follows. • We identify reactive perception as an overlooked bottleneck of CUAs, formulate proactive percep-tion, and quantify the cost of the time-triggered loop through a wall-clock ledger of idle waiting, premature observations, and stale actions (Section 3.1). • We propose NightWatch, a plug-and-play framework in which a cheap watcher interrupts, voids, and wakes an unmodified agent, and train NightWatch-0.8B without benchmark data, releasing the code, data, and weights. • Experiments on desktop and dynamic web environments show that NightWatch saves 33% of non-reasoning time on OSWorld-Verified with success on par, raises success by 4 .7 points on DynaCU-Bench with the large VLM agent, and that a small trained watcher is both faster and more successful than a decision model more than 30 × its size and a closed System-1 API. 

## 2 Related Work 

Perception in computer-use agents. Benchmarks such as OSWorld [44, 49] and AndroidWorld [30] drive VLM agents [27] through a discrete observe–reason–act loop, with a fixed pause after every action. A growing body of work shows that this loop breaks once the screen changes on its own. Pop-ups and environmental distractions mislead agents [53, 22], and injected anomalies, notifications, and fake loading pages cause large drops in success [8, 40, 21, 41]. Single screenshots miss intermediate states of dynamic interfaces [20, 47], agents trust outdated structure over current pixels [50], and the delay between observing and acting opens a window for stale or even adversarial actions [45, 26], which time-sensitive and real-time agent benchmarks also penalize [12, 39]. Several methods address parts of the problem. AOI [17] attaches keyframes sampled between steps to the next observation, whereas AAPT [10] pre-compiles a policy tree while the screen is idle and executes branches when changed frames match them. PUSV [45] validates the screen before dispatch, SentinelBench [24] replaces sleeps with waits on DOM changes, and step-level monitors escalate to a stronger model on stuck or milestone events read from text [38]. Action-induced visual differences have also been used for reflection [23], and GUI testing has long replaced fixed waits with rendering-completion detectors [11]. Beyond GUIs, streaming video models decide frame by frame when to respond [7, 37, 25, 9, 36, 54, 55], as rewarded by benchmarks of timely answers [18, 19], but learn this gate end to end inside the model. NightWatch instead leaves the agent untouched: a cheap visual watcher in front of it decides, from the pixels and at every frame, when the agent looks, and can interrupt reasoning or void actions instead of only buffering or checking once. 

Fast watchers for slow reasoners. Pairing a fast monitor with a slow planner is common in robotics, where cheap checks of constraints or progress trigger replanning by a VLM [56, 14, 2, 31], and real-time execution overlaps inference with action [6, 29, 42]. Asynchronous LLM agents let events interrupt or redirect ongoing generation [13, 46, 32]. System-1 decision models answer many typed questions from a single shared encoding at a fraction of the cost of a VLM call [48], and have been 

3used as fast executors for mobile agents [51]. These ideas echo event-triggered control, which samples a system when its state demands rather than periodically [4, 33, 15], and active perception, which treats when and how to sense as part of the task [5]. NightWatch brings them to computer use, with a visual System-1 as the watcher and fixed thresholds that turn its answers into decisions.

## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Evaluation benchmarks. We evaluate NightWatch in two computer-use domains with different dynamics. ① Desktop : OSWorld-Verified [44], on a random sample of 100 tasks that covers all ten application domains. ② Dynamic web : the 49 dynamic tasks of DynaCU-Bench [17] that do not require audio, such as live dashboards, carousels, and games, in which the page changes while the agent acts. Each task is run three times. OSWorld is a largely static benchmark on which generous waits hide timing failures [52], so we expect savings in wall-clock time with success on par, whereas DynaCU-Bench tests whether the same watcher also helps success when the screen does not wait for the agent. 

7Baselines. All methods share the same agent, prompt, action space, and step budget, and differ only in how the agent perceives the screen. We compare against two paradigms. 

■ Time-triggered : the official loop of each benchmark ( Vanilla ), which observes after the fixed pauses of the benchmark’s official setting, such as AOI’s 2 s interval on DynaCU-Bench. 

■ Passive watching : AOI [17], which samples frames at 3 Hz between steps and attaches pixel- and CLIP-gated keyframes with a visual narration to the next scheduled observation, and PUSV [45], which verifies the screen around the target once before each click and lets the agent re-decide when the check fails. Section 4.4 compares other schemes and watchers. 

Implementation. Our harness follows the official setting of each benchmark: the agent is OSWorld’s screenshot-only prompt agent [44] on OSWorld and AOI’s standard agent [17] on DynaCU-Bench, each with two unchanged backbones: MiMo-V2.6-Flash [43], a hosted large VLM, and Qwen3.8-27B (FP8) [28], a small open-weight VLM. Tasks allow at most 100 executed steps on OSWorld and 50 on DynaCU-Bench, and all methods receive screenshots and the trajectory only, so AOI’s DOM element list is disabled. On DynaCU-Bench, all methods share AOI’s harness, with its agent, prompt, action parsing, failure feedback, and judge, and each baseline is reproduced as described in its paper (Section D). NightWatch-0.8B is initialized from d3-lite [35], a 0.8B System-1 decision model, fine-tuned with LoRA on 360 generated OSWorld tasks, and used frozen, with the same thresholds, on both benchmarks. It runs on one NVIDIA L40S GPU over a 10 Hz frame stream, and a settledness check takes about 0.3 s. Sections C and D detail the training data, the harnesses, and the judge. 

Metrics. We report task success averaged over runs (avg@3) and the share of tasks solved in any run (pass@3). For efficiency, we report the non-reasoning time per task, i.e ., wall-clock time minus the time spent in agent LLM calls, which isolates the part of the loop that perception controls, and the wasted-time share , the fraction of loop time spent idle, on premature observations, or on stale actions. For timeliness, we report the share of steps with a premature observation or a stale action, as defined in Section 3.1 and measured from screen changes (Section E).

4.3 Cost Efficiency 

End to end (Table 2), NightWatch is the fastest method with both agents: a task takes 614.7 s against 666.0 s for Vanilla with the large VLM agent ( −8%) and 625.4 s against 701.9 s with the small one (−11%). Table 4 removes one intervention at a time on DynaCU-Bench, where all three fire often: NightWatch-0.8B wakes the agent early on about half of the steps, and interrupts and voids on 15 and 6 of every 100 steps. ❸ Watcher against pixel quiescence on OSWorld. On OSWorld, where NightWatch interrupts only 0.48 times and voids 0.03 actions per task, the wake carries most of the gain. A pixel-only variant that uses the same harness but wakes the agent on pixel quiescence alone, and never interrupts or voids, spends 179.8 s per task outside reasoning and 770.8 s in total, against 113.1 s ( −37%) and 611.5 s ( −21%) for NightWatch in a run with the same agent, and NightWatch is faster on two thirds of the tasks (Table 10). Judging whether stillness is final, rather than waiting for it to last, is thus what shortens the wait.

## 5 Conclusion 

We presented NightWatch, a plug-and-play framework for proactive perception: the evolving screen, rather than a fixed schedule, decides when an unmodified CUA observes and whether its reasoning 

11 and pending action remain valid. A 0.8B watcher, faster and more successful than a model over 30 ×

its size, answers generic questions about the frame stream, and fixed thresholds turn its answers into interrupts, voids, and wakes. NightWatch reduces non-reasoning time on OSWorld-Verified by 33% with task success on par and raises success on DynaCU-Bench from 39.5% to 44.2% with the large VLM agent. These results identify the perception–action gap as a central bottleneck of CUAs and proactive perception as a practical remedy. 

Limitations. NightWatch changes when the agent perceives, not what it can do: on the 11 OSWorld tasks that fail in every run of both Vanilla and NightWatch, the agent mostly ends the task itself with an incorrect result. Interrupting and voiding restart the reasoning on the current screen but do not make it faster, so on interfaces that change faster than one generation, such as the fastest toasts and games of DynaCU-Bench, no observation is still valid when the action is dispatched. NightWatch therefore suits interfaces that change more slowly than the agent reasons, while faster ones need a faster agent (Section H).

## C Watcher Training 

Training tasks. Ttrain contains 360 tasks over the ten OSWorld application domains: 20 each for Thunderbird and VLC, and 40 for each of the other eight. An LLM writes the instructions, and each task starts from a freshly launched application on the OSWorld system image, without benchmark files, pages, or profiles. An instruction is discarded when its string similarity to any OSWorld instruction is at least 0.6, so that no evaluation instruction is reused. 

Decision points. Running the agent on these tasks yields 344 valid trajectories (16 are skipped because of errors). They give 14,693 training decision points (2,540 during reasoning, 4,198 before dispatch, and 7,955 after execution) and 1,512 validation decision points. 

Labels. We set 𝛼 = 0.3 in Equation (9), and the teacher is a hosted Jev model. A gold label is available only where the pixels are unambiguous. 

Synthetic interfaces. We add 15,000 examples from procedurally generated web pages with five layouts and nine event families (dialogs, toasts, errors, loading, navigation, layout shifts, delayed enabling, long operations, and ambient changes), with exact ground truth at every frame. 

Settledness examples. With a horizon of ℎ = 1 s in Equation (10), the recorded trajectories give 6,646 training and 670 validation examples of the moments at which the wake rule decides: 2,470 and 263 after a change, and 4,176 and 407 after an action that has not changed the screen. 

Optimization. We fine-tune with LoRA of rank 16 on the attention, MLP, and Gated DeltaNet projection layers, keeping the vision tower frozen and training the readout head in full. Images are resized to at most 768 px, 𝜆 = 1 in Equation (11), and the temperature fitted on the validation split is 0.60. We use AdamW with a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5, linear warmup, and linear decay, and the validation split holds out 10% of the training tasks.

OSWorld-Verified. The sample of 100 tasks contains 9 Chrome, 9 GIMP, 11 Calc, 16 Impress, 8 Writer, 26 multi-application, 8 operating-system, 6 Thunderbird, 5 VLC, and 2 VS Code tasks. The screen is 1920 × 1080; the agent receives screenshots downscaled to 1280 × 720, the coordinates in its actions are scaled back to the screen, and it receives the last three screenshots as its history. The 60 s environment reset and the 20 s wait before evaluation are the same for all methods and are excluded from loop time. 

19 DynaCU-Bench. Of the 100 dynamic tasks of DynaCU-Bench, we keep the 49 that do not require audio, since audio is disabled for every method: all carousel, transient-notification, and game tasks, 9 of the 10 dashboard and 8 of the 10 video tasks, and one task each of meetings and collaborative editing. All methods share AOI’s harness: AOI’s standard agent with its observation text, prompt, action parsing, and failure feedback, with AOI’s DOM element list removed before it reaches the agent. Vanilla and AOI are run as released, and PUSV and NightWatch are added to the same harness, PUSV following its paper. Every task allows 50 steps instead of the 15 of AOI’s evaluation, so that agents are not cut short. The budget does not drive the results: with MiMo-V2.6-Flash, no successful run of AOI, PUSV, or NightWatch uses more than 15 steps and one of Vanilla does, so a 15-step budget would change success by at most 0.7 points. Non-reasoning time is reported for PUSV and NightWatch, whose logs separate the agent’s loop from environment setup and scoring. All tasks are scored by AOI’s evaluator, as in the original protocol; since the Gemini 2.0 Flash judge of the original evaluation is no longer served, we use Gemini 2.5 Flash and keep the rest of the evaluator unchanged. PUSV’s third layer inspects operating-system alert windows, which a browser page lacks, so here we use its first two layers.

PUSV. PUSV checks every click before dispatch, comparing a fresh screenshot with the one the agent reasoned on, with the three layers of its paper [45]: the SSIM of the 160 × 160 patch at the click falls below 0.92; more than 0.2% of the pixels outside that patch differ by more than 20 gray levels; or a new window whose title contains an alert keyword appears. ## E Metric Definitions 

All timing is computed from the event log of each episode and the 10 Hz frame stream, which is the screen’s timeline. A frame-to-frame change is significant when the pixel-level gate of Section 3.2 fires (𝜒 = 1). 

Loop and non-reasoning time. Loop time runs from the first observation to the end of the episode. Non-reasoning time is loop time minus all LLM generation, including generations that were interrupted or voided. 

Wasted time. For each executed action, the time from dispatch to the next screenshot is split at the last significant change before that screenshot. The part after it, up to the screenshot, is idle . The wasted-time share is the sum of idle time, discarded generations, and the non-idle time of timing-caused no-effect steps, divided by loop time. A step has no effect when no significant change follows its dispatch, and the no-effect step is timing-caused when the screenshot it acted on was premature or stale. Thus, the cost 𝑐 of a premature or stale step in Equation (1) is measured per step, not set by hand. 

20 Premature and stale. Since the validity 𝑣 in Section 3.1 cannot be read from the logs, the ledger uses observable proxies. Both are measured on the screenshot the agent acted on, i.e ., the last one before the dispatch, which is a new one when the agent observed again after an interruption or a voided action. A step is stale when the screen changes significantly between that screenshot and the dispatch. A step is premature when that screenshot was taken while the screen was still moving, 

i.e ., significant changes occur both in the 0.5 s before it and between it and the dispatch. Both are reported as shares of executed steps. These proxies count any significant change on the screen; the stricter target-region mismatch below counts only changes at the target and ranks the methods alike (Section F). 

Statistical tests. We compare a method with Vanilla per task, averaging its three runs, and test the mean difference over the 100 OSWorld tasks with a paired sign-flip permutation test (20,000 permutations). 

Mismatch. A targeted action is mismatched when at least 5% of the patch around its target differs between the agent’s screenshot and dispatch.

Beyond the cases of Figure 5, we describe one more interrupt and one more void on DynaCU-Bench. 

Interrupt. On a DynaCU-Bench page that shows a sequence of error toasts and asks for the third error code, the agent observes the screen 6.5 s after the start of the task, when only two codes have appeared. A screenshot-based plan formed on this frame cannot contain the answer. A second interrupt at 10.9 s follows the next toast, and the agent then submits ERR-7712. NightWatch solves this task in all three runs, and Vanilla in two of them. 

Void. In a DynaCU-Bench whack-a-mole game, the agent sees a mole in the center hole at 4.9 s and generates a click on it, at (640 , 350 ). By 9.7 s, when the action is about to be dispatched, the mole has moved, so the watcher reports that the target is no longer present and the action is discarded. The next observation, at 9.8 s, shows the mole in the lower-left hole, and the agent clicks it at (480 , 510 ),scoring its first point. A stale click at the center would have hit an empty hole. NightWatch solves this task in all three runs, whereas Vanilla fails in all three. 

22 H Failure Analysis and Scope 

Failures caused by the framework. A task that Vanilla solves in all three runs and NightWatch in none would point to a failure introduced by the framework. Among the 149 tasks of the two benchmarks there is one such task, on OSWorld, and conversely two OSWorld tasks are solved in all runs of NightWatch and in none of Vanilla. 

Insufficient agent capability. On OSWorld, 11 of the 100 tasks fail in all runs of both Vanilla and NightWatch, 6 of them multi-application tasks. Timing is not what limits them: in 27 of the 33 runs of Vanilla and 26 of the 33 runs of NightWatch the agent ends the task itself with an incorrect result, and 6 runs of each method reach the step limit. On these tasks NightWatch lowers premature observations from 5.9% to 3.5% of steps. Because NightWatch does not change the agent, it cannot supply a capability that the agent lacks. 

Reasoning latency of the agent. On DynaCU-Bench, 21 of the 49 tasks fail in all runs of both methods: 6 transient-notification, 5 carousel, 5 game, 4 video, and 1 dashboard task. Here, 53 of the 63 runs of NightWatch reach the task’s time limit, and in the other 10 the agent ends the task with an incorrect result. The agent spends 9.1 s of LLM generation per executed step on these tasks, while the toast, frame, or game state that it needs often changes faster than it can respond. Interrupting and voiding restart the reasoning on the current screen but do not make it faster, so when the screen changes faster than one generation, no observation is still valid when the action is dispatched. NightWatch therefore suits interfaces that change more slowly than the agent reasons, while faster ones need a faster agent. 

Time efficiency of waking. Complementing the examples in Section G, the wake intervention shortens the loop whether or not the outcome of a task changes. On OSWorld with MiMo-V2.6-Flash, execution and waiting together take 3.17 s per step, against 5.58 s for Vanilla. 

## I Analysis of Streaming Wake

Variability of settling times. In the OSWorld runs of Vanilla (MiMo-V2.6-Flash), settling durations have a mean of 2.14 s, a median of 1.22 s, and a 99th percentile of 15.8 s, so no single delay suits all steps. In the same runs, 9.6% of the executed steps of Vanilla and 2.6% of those of NightWatch are premature (Section E), so NightWatch lowers idle time and premature observations together.
