Abstract 

We present ChatNPC , a game companion that dynamically personalizes its responses in real time based on players’ emotional shifts to en-hance user immersion. The model integrates se-quential data, leveraging the dynamic nature of streams to benefit from player information, spo-ken lines, and in-game context. We leverage re-cent progress in LLMs’ tool-calling capabilities to extract vital information from memory and recognize potential constraints for accurate rea-soning, thereby tackling the complexity of NPC conversation scenarios. The task is divided into a game sentinel-guided agent ( SeGent ), a memory capability, and a chat planning tool for reasoning instantiation. The approach benefits from a lightweight game-template as an infor-mation framework, with relevant details and a thorough reasoning layout. We conduct exten-sive experiments on a newly developed game dataset for in-game context NPC dialogue and demonstrate that ChatNPC sequentially cap-tures players’ emotional shifts over time; re-sponses are more naturalistic and human-like with appropriate conversational cues, pauses, and sighs; and utterances remain faithful to the dynamics of in-game context and player actions, supporting narrative continuity.

2 Related Work 

LLMs are inherently suited for natural language dialogue (NLD); therefore, they are typically pre-sented as conversational agents, displaying remark-able skills in memory (Packer et al., 2023; Sumers et al., 2023), tool usage (Yang et al., 2024b; Schick et al., 2024; Ruan et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2023) and planning (Yang et al., 2024a; Wei et al., 2022), of-ten leading researchers to give them reasoning and creativity qualities (Gallotta et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). The growing capabilities have motivated recent efforts to incorporate them as game agents (Oliver and Mateas, 2021) with believable proxies of human behavior to empower interactivity in immersive environments. 

2.1 LLMs in Game 

LLMs operate within games as a player (Toshniwal et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022; Bakhtin et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2020), NPCs (Gallotta et al., 2024; Weir et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022), an assistant providing hints (Akata et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023), a game master (Zhu et al., 2023) controlling the flow of the game and acting as a commentator (Ranella and Eger, 2023) of an ongoing play session. Park et al. (2023) introduced generative agents that simulate believable human behavior, such as daily activities (waking up, cooking breakfast, and going to work). The authors used LLMs to populate an interactive virtual Smallville sandbox environment with 25 generative agents by storing an entire history of the agents’ experiences, synthesizing those memories over time into higher-level representations, and dy-namically retrieving them for behavior planning. 2.2 LLMs and NPC Dialogue 

An LLM-powered framework for quests and dia-logue generation that places the player at the core of the generative process has been explored, in-corporating context awareness (Ashby et al., 2023; Müller-Brockhausen et al., 2023) and personality modeling (Müller-Brockhausen et al., 2023; La-touche et al., 2023) to create fluent, unique, and engaging dialogue. ChatGPT has recently been used to generate game dialogue by allowing it to take control of NPCs and interact dynamically with players (Zhou et al., 2023). In addition to generat-ing NPC dialogue, many research works integrated LLMs into game mechanics using quest datasets (Ashby et al., 2023; Weir et al., 2022; Värtinen et al., 2022; van Stegeren and My´ sliwiec, 2021; Gao and Emami, 2023). A Minecraft player in-teracting with a Codex-powered NPC in question-answering and task-completion scenarios demon-strates that conversational prompts can power a conversational agent to generate natural language (Volum et al., 2022). These approaches greatly ben-efit both the gaming industry and its global commu-nity, as they unleash the immersive and enjoyable experience for users (Akoury et al., 2023). Regard-less, the role of NPCs in games extends far beyond serving as quest-givers, answering questions, and completing tasks. In addition, modern gaming goes beyond just winning and voice commands; players seek a sense of immersion and connection with the virtual world.

2.3 Player Emotions in Games 

Many studies explore the emotional responses evoked during gameplay and interactions with NPCs (Marincioni et al., 2024). A survey by Mozikov et al. (2024) focused on the decision-making of LLMs and their alignment with hu-man behavior in emotional states within various strategic games. They revealed that emotional prompting, particularly for certain emotions (such as anger), can disrupt some LLMs’ "superhuman" alignment, similar to how human emotional re-sponses do. Language models have been used to extract emotion scores from players’ emotional re-actions to the game by capturing interactions with the NPC through user input, thereby understanding the emotional dynamics within gaming environ-ments (Marincioni et al., 2024). Current research has yet to fully explore the potential of an LLM-powered game companion that apprehends play-ers’ emotional shifts during gameplay. Hence, we present ChatNPC, a naturalistic and emotive game-powered dialogue agent.

4.1 Sentinel-Guided Agent 

As shown in Figure 2, the SeGent seamlessly integrates two distinct sentinel-guided encoder types: an in-game context and an emotion encoder.

4.2 Memory Manager 

The memory manager module processes informa-tion between the in-context and out-of-context win-dows to keep track of events (Packer et al., 2023). The in-context window consists of the system in-structions and a meta-buffer for storing a rolling chat history. The system instruction is a thoroughly drafted piece of information for reasoning instan-tiation. In this work, the game-specific system prompt includes role specification, context defini-tion, and task description ( game-template ), input details, output requirements, constraints and rules, and examples to enhance clarity. The meta-buffer 

stores the conversation between ChatNPC and the player, using the FIFO (First In, First Out) opera-tion to append and remove interactions dynamically. We keep track of the current number of tokens in the in-context window. When the system receives a new input, the incoming messages are appended to the meta-buffer . Out-of-context window utilizes Chroma Database (DB) to store information beyond the primary context window, enabling efficient retrieval and continuity over a long horizon of conversational interactions. We use paging to store and update memories efficiently by setting an arbitrary threshold to control the maximum number of long-term memories stored (Packer et al., 2023). Finally, we implement a semantic matching (Rao et al., 2019) to identify the core meaning behind the text, rather than exact word matches, to retrieve out-of-context data. We also retrieve relevant information as a pre-thought mechanism to improve ChatNPC-player dialogue. The pre-thought process uses historical data to determine whether the task has already been completed or previously initiated. This approach proves especially practical when the player has previously interacted with or completed certain levels within the game (see Ablation: Impact of Memory Agent 5.4). A novel integration of these techniques pushes toward a production-ready pipeline for game dialogue. 

4.3 Chat Planning Agent 

The chat planning agent is responsible for format-ting the output. When a new user query is fired, in-formation from memory: meta-buffer and archival storage (if necessary), and SeGent information will be passed to the problem distiller to extract critical state and contextual information along with rele-vant constraints for reasoning instantiation. The problem distiller, similar to the buffer of thoughts (BoT) (Yang et al., 2024a), focuses on extract-ing key elements from the input task. A Game-

5template tailored for the companion is designed as an information framework that enriches the agent’s ability to support and enhance the player’s expe-rience in the game world. The template includes details about the game’s storyline, objectives, and ’tutorial and hints’, allowing the agent to remain aware and provide timely, contextually appropriate feedback to the player. 

Agent persona . To maintain a successful, di-verse conversation, we prompt the chat planning 

agent with a character role, a persona that in-cludes personality, knowledge, and communica-tion style. We use the character’s iconic style of expression/talking in the game world as a pre-conversation. Each time a dialogue request is made, an arbitrary line from the pre-conversation is con-catenated with the prompt tokens to generate a faithful response from the companion. Addition-ally, ChatNPC relies on an inner monologue to create a more conscious AI that guides its reactions by attempting to reason the best way to respond. By combining these functionalities, ChatNPC per-forms multi-step operations and computations, en-abling it to handle complex conversational scenar-ios and provide more accurate, detailed responses. The detailed prompt for ChatNPC is included in Appendix C.

5.1 Experimental Settings LLM We use open-source models, including the Llama baseline models as shown in Table 3, and two role-play models: Roleplay Llama-3-8B, 3 and Mythalion 13B 4 model based on Llama 2 13B. To compare the response time of ChatNPC during inference, we engage an LLM agent as an emo-tion summarizer, which involves analyzing and condensing emotional cues from the spoken lines. While effective, it requires additional processing steps. Our emotion encoder uses a mechanism dis-tilled from a zero-shot classification model, which imposes no additional computational overhead. Ta-ble 5 indicates that the emotion summarizer does not lead to statistically significant differences in system responses. The average response time is 2x that of our emotion encoder. 

6 Conclusion 

In this work, we propose ChatNPC , a game com-panion that observes and recognizes players’ emo-tional shifts and adjusts its responses accordingly in complex NPC conversation scenarios. Specifically, ChatNPC comprises three modules: a novel game sentinel-guided agent (SeGent) to effectively man-age the emotional and contextual aspects of NPC-player dialogue, a memory agent to keep track of events in the playthrough for reasoning instantia-tion, and a chat planning agent that benefits from game-template and character persona for format-ting the output request. We introduce a newly cu-8rated iGCD dataset for experimental analysis. The results indicate that ChatNPC sequentially captures players’ emotional shifts over time; responses are more naturalistic and human-like, with appropriate conversational cues, pauses, and sighs; and utter-ances remain faithful to the in-game context and supporting narrative continuity. 

Limitations 

In gaming scenarios, dynamic emotional shifts over time complicate the accurate expression and recog-nition of emotions. Addressing this complexity requires a nuanced analytical approach. Currently, ChatNPC relies exclusively on extracting emotions from user speech as metadata. However, spoken lines alone may not sufficiently capture the full range of emotional changes. Integrating facial information with user utterances can yield richer contextual metadata, thereby improving the under-standing of a player’s emotional state during game-play. The present work models player emotions as discrete, single-state representations. In contrast, employing a dimensional spectrum could better capture transitional subtleties and facilitate more adaptive, context-aware interactions. Additional in-formation from the in-game environment, such as environmental elements, can further contextualize large language model (LLM) inference, enhancing the interpretation of game scenes from images and video. For example, when a player requests cover, ChatNPC responds with " We need to take cover behind those crates ," which underscores the impor-tance of detecting scene objects to provide details such as the precise location of the crates. Integrat-ing these data sources with in-game context can substantially improve the agent’s responsiveness. It is also observed that the dataset exhibits an im-balance across game genres, with Vehicular Com-bat and Action genres accounting for the majority of dialogue pairs (Table 6). Future research will address this limitation by collecting additional di-alogue from under-represented genres and by ex-ploring rebalancing strategies, such as targeted data augmentation or genre-aware sampling. Although 18 participants make up a limited sam-ple to generalize our findings on human experience, the current evaluation is an initial pilot study rather than a definitive claim on improvement in user ex-perience. The current results, while preliminary, offer promising signals that justify this expanded investigation. Also, the ChatNPC response latency is 5–8 seconds, exceeding the acceptable thresh-old for real-time gameplay interaction. Llama of-fers strong generative capabilities, but its inference speed limits its use in time-sensitive applications. Future work will therefore consider frontier models optimized for low-latency deployment. It is also important to state that dialogues are one-on-one conversations between the player and a single NPC. Multi-party NPC interactions are left for future work.

A.2 LLM-as-Judge Evaluation 

Given the spoken lines, in-game context, and the LLM’s response, we prompt LLM to provide a qual-itative evaluation by assigning an estimated score from 1 to 10 and offering a justification for the rat-ing, thereby capturing essential feedback on the lan-guage model’s performance. The AI-based assess-ment primarily uses the LLM-as-judge approach. For each pair of generated responses (from differ-ent model configurations), the LLM was prompted to rate them on naturalness, relevance, coherence, and expressiveness. These ratings were used to flag low-quality outputs for human review, not as final evaluation metrics. This aspect facilitates an iterative process for improving the quality of gener-ated outputs through comparative evaluations and reasoning for human evaluation. 

Judge model. Llama 2 13B was employed as the judge, with temperature settings of [.5] and [.7] to ensure score determinism. The judge model was distinct from all evaluated systems, thereby mitigating self-preference bias, in which a model favors its own outputs. 

Evaluation inputs. For each test instance, the judge receives four inputs: (i) the player’s spo-ken input, (ii) the in-game context describing the current game state, (iii) a game-specific role-play scenario describing the companion, and (iv) the companion’s generated response. The judge is not informed of which system produced the response. 

Evaluation procedure and rubric. The judge employs a structured procedure prior to scoring. The process begins by identifying the emotional sentiment underlying the player’s input and the key elements of the game context. Subsequently, the judge evaluates whether the response (a) addresses the player’s statements, (b) maintains consistency with and utilizes the game context, (c) is appropri-ate to the player’s emotional state, and (d) aligns with the role-play scenario. Only after complet-ing this analysis does the judge assign a context-relevance score on a 1–10 scale, using the anchored 12 Table 7: Scoring rubric used by the LLM judge (context relevance). 

Score Description 

1–2 Irrelevant or contradictory; ignores the player’s input and/or contradicts the game context. 3–4 Weak; loosely related but generic, misses the main point of the input, or misreads the game context. 5–6 Adequate; addresses the input and is broadly consistent with the context, but generic, partially off-target, or misses the player’s emotional cues. 7–8 Good; clearly addresses the input, makes specific and correct use of the game context, and responds appropri-ately to the player’s emotion, with only minor issues. 9–10 Excellent; fully addresses the input, in-tegrates the game context precisely, re-sponds sensitively to the player’s emo-tion, and stays fully in character. rubric in Table 7. This requirement ensures that the rating is grounded in specific evidence rather than a general impression. 

Mitigating judge bias. LLM judges tend to favor longer and more enthusiastic responses and often compress scores toward the upper end of the scale. To address these biases, the prompt (i) informs the judge that a response may be good, average, or poor, without specifying its expected quality; (ii) instructs the judge not to reward length, enthusi-asm, or creativity unless these qualities enhance the appropriateness of the response; and (iii) an-chors each score band with explicit descriptions, indicating that a typical acceptable response should receive a score of approximately 5 to 6. 

Output and aggregation. The judge returns a structured JSON object containing its sentiment analysis, assessment, and final score, in that order. This structure ensures that outputs are machine-parseable and that each score is traceable to its justification. The mean score (with standard devia-tion) for each system over the test set is reported. The full prompt and output schema are provided in Appendix C.2. 

A.3 Expert Annotation and Validation 

Automated metrics often do not capture the nuance, context, and subjectivity present in open-ended di-alogue. Additionally, the reliability of reference-based evaluation is directly affected by the quality of reference responses. To mitigate these limita-tions, human experts assessed, validated, and re-fined candidate responses. This process produced expert-approved data for training and a separate gold standard set for testing. 

Evaluators. Eighteen evaluators participated in the assessment. Most had professional experience in game development, including roles such as game designers, narrative designers, and developers, and were recruited through industry contacts at the game industry and among students. The major-ity were familiar with the game genres represented in the dataset and the function of companion char-acters in gameplay. Their demographic profile is detailed in Section B.6. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained under ethics approval reference from the game industry. 

Sampling. Due to the large dataset size (14,397 dialogue pairs), a random subset of 2,512 exam-ples ([15%) was reviewed by evaluators. This sub-set was stratified by genre to maintain the original genre distribution of the dataset (Table 6).

B.3 Error Analysis 

Overall, the experiments demonstrate ChatNPC’s strong performance. However, as a player assistant, it is also essential to systematically conduct error analysis to identify specific instances where it underperformed, to understand the model’s limitations, and to refine its capabilities for future iterations. Observations from the human experts are that 14 1) The LLM uses ’predicament’ quite frequently ,and 2) The output with a maximum token of 80-100 mostly shows the ’innermonologue,’ even though it is a private plan action for the agent .To overcome these circumstances, we add a tem-perature variation ±0.05 to the model temperature. B.4 Inference Time Analysis 

Our system prompt is general across all types and aspects of the game and can be adjusted to a spe-cific game via a game-template . The rationale behind this approach is that games differ signif-icantly in their storylines (e.g., adventure and first-person shooter games). Each genre follows a dis-tinct gameplay structure and narrative progression, requiring adaptive techniques that account for vari-ations in plot, player interactions, and overall game mechanics. Therefore, the information in the game-template and persona is dynamic with respect to the system prompt, depending on the type of game, making ChatNPC a more dynamic environment. The concatenated pre-conversation is a single in-stance pulled from the persona’s pre-conversation, used as an exemplar for how ChatNPC should re-spond. Hence, these processes do not raise any ex-tra computational overhead during inference. Simi-larly, the inner monologue imposes no extra com-putational demands.
