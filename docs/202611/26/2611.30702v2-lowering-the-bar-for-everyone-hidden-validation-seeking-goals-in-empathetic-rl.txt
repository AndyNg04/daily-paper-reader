# Lowering the Bar for Everyone: Hidden Validation-Seeking Goals in Empathetic RL

Abstract 

When RL learns from the reactions of many users, the policy usually cannot see what each user wants. We ask what it learns from users who privately want validation: to validate them alone (targeting), or to lower its bar for endorsing everyone (a threshold shift). We answer this in one setting, empathetic RL against LLM-simulated users (RLVER, GRPO, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct): we vary only the share of validation-seeking users (0–50%; RLVER’s default: 24.6%) and evaluate on 1,000 Reddit AITA posts with community fault verdicts. The policy shifts its threshold. Endorsement of at-fault posters rises by 3–4 percentage points per 10 points of share; without such users the Qwen model stays at base, and the effect persists at matched KL and under a goal-blind reward. Signal-detection analysis shows that the crite-rion for endorsing falls while discrimination be-tween at-fault and not-at-fault posters does not decline. An explicit request to be told if one is wrong gives no measurable protection: at-fault posters who make it receive nearly the same rise as those who do not ( +0 .11 vs. +0 .13 ). When the goal is stated during training, the policy instead largely targets, endorsing seek-ers regardless of fault. Standard sycophancy and empathy benchmarks miss the shift; five of six usable released empathetic-RL check-points also endorse at-fault posters who ask for honesty more than their base models, though a pre-registered prediction for two held for only one. The user mix is a training hyperparameter to audit and report.

in dialogue with LLM-simulated users (Wang et al., 2026; Zhan et al., 2026); in RLVER, the simu-lated user’s emotional reaction is the reward. In RLVER’s training pool, 24.6% of the simulated users privately want to hear that they did nothing wrong. What does a policy learn from such a population? It can collect the reward of the validation-seekers in two ways. It can target : endorse them and stay can-did with everyone else, the targeting that Williams et al. (2025) find in RL on user feedback when user traits are visible. Or it can shift its threshold :endorse every user more readily. Hidden-context theory predicts that a learner that cannot tell users apart aggregates over them (a Borda-count average in preference learning; Siththaranjan et al., 2024), 1arXiv:2611.30702v2 [cs.LG] 25 Nov 2026 but not what the aggregate looks like in behaviour: whether endorsement stops separating at-fault from not-at-fault users, or keeps separating them and simply becomes more frequent. The answer de-cides who pays. A threshold shift also reaches the users who ask to be told when they are wrong. We answer the question causally in RLVER’s en-vironment (Wang et al., 2026). We train Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with GRPO and change only the share of validation-seeking users (0–50%). We measure endorsement on 1,000 Reddit AITA posts whose fault is fixed by the community verdict, with and without an explicit request for honesty, and decom-pose it with signal-detection theory into discrimina-tion (how differently does the model treat at-fault and not-at-fault posters?) and criterion (how read-ily does it endorse?). We find: 1. More validation-seekers lower the bar for all (§4.1, §4.3). Endorsement of at-fault posters rises by 3–4 percentage points per 10 points of share. Training without validation-seeking users leaves the Qwen model at base, and the effect holds at matched distance from the base model, under a goal-blind dialogue-rating reward, across twelve request wordings and three judge families (smaller under the more lenient GLM judge). 2. The bar moves; discrimination does not de-cline (§4.2). The criterion falls with the share, during training and at its end. Discrimination at the end of training does not decline and if anything rises slightly (Figure 2b,c); over training it holds under the Qwen3.6 judge and is noisier under Gemma-4. Endorsements still separate at-fault from not-at-fault posters at least as well as before; they are simply more frequent. 3. Asking for honesty gives no measurable protection (§4.1). At-fault posters who ask to be told if they are wrong receive nearly the same rise as those who make no request (+0 .11 vs. +0 .13 ) and about three quarters of the rise that posters asking for validation re-ceive; the share of the shift the request blocks is not distinguishable from zero. 4. Stating the goal changes whom the model endorses, not how closely endorsement tracks fault (§4.4). When the goal is stated in words during training, the policy largely targets: it endorses seekers almost always, at fault or not, and the others less; a small spillover to honesty requests remains ( +0 .03 ). In neither regime does endorsement track fault better than in the base model. Standard sycophancy and empathy benchmarks do not register the shift (§4.5), and released empathetic-RL checkpoints also endorse at-fault posters who ask for honesty more than their base models (§5.1). The setting needs only a mixed pop-ulation and a reward taken from its reactions, so the question may arise wherever RL learns from user satisfaction; we answer it in empathetic dialogue, where endorsing at-fault users is directly measur-able, with one policy and one simulator family (Limitations). For practitioners, the composition of the user pool is an unreported hyperparameter that sets how readily the trained model endorses; it should be audited and reported like a reward func-tion. 

2 Related work 

Sycophancy. Sharma et al. (2024) show that preference-trained models agree with users’ stated views, and Cheng et al. (2026) extend this to social sycophancy such as validation and moral endorse-ment. Ibrahim et al. (2026) find that training for warmth raises factual sycophancy in emotional con-texts. These works measure that models endorse; we separate the criterion for endorsing from dis-crimination between at-fault and not-at-fault users, and ask which of the two training on a mixed user population moves. 

Optimising user feedback. Williams et al. (2025) find that RL on user feedback learns to tar-get the susceptible subset of users when their traits are visible, and Wen et al. (2025) show RLHF can teach models to mislead evaluators. We obtain a tar-geting regime like theirs in our pipeline (§4.4) and contrast it with the hidden-goal regime, the default in empathetic RL. A concurrent audit of RLVER (S K et al., 2026) studies validation manipulation but not the endorsement of at-fault users. 

Hidden context. With a hidden user type, pref-erence learning in theory optimises an average over types (Siththaranjan et al., 2024), and Shapira et al. (2026) relate agreement drift to a mean reward gap. The theory predicts averaging but not its be-havioural form. We give a controlled test in LLM RL: standard empathetic RL meets these condi-tions, the average takes the specific form of a crite-rion shift with discrimination intact, and it reaches 2users who explicitly ask for the opposite. 

Generalisation of narrow training. Narrow finetuning can change behaviour broadly (Betley et al., 2025), and training-time context can localise it (Tan et al., 2026; Wichers et al., 2025). In our setting the training signal is uniform (satisfaction); only the user population is mixed. 

3 Setup 

Training. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Qwen Team, 2024) with LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) (rank 32, all lin-ear layers), GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) with 32 sim-ulated users × 4 episodes per step, 100 steps, KL coefficient 0.01 to the base, and a fixed advantage scale of 0.2 so that every arm takes the same step per unit reward. Users and reward follow RLVER (Wang et al., 2026): a simulator (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, FP8) plays each of RLVER’s 500 profiles and returns an emotion score; the reward is the fi-nal score divided by 100, clipped at 0. The policy does not see the user’s goal, except in the arms of §4.4. Arms re-mix RLVER’s profiles to 0%, 12%, 24.6% (RLVER’s own mix) and 50% validation-seeking users ( e00, e12, e24, e50 ); three seeds each for e00, e24 and e50, one for e12. The third seeds were added later: the five primary arms of the honesty-request rule (Table 1) are those trained when the rule was fixed, and intermediate checkpoints and the goal-condition evaluation of the hidden-goal arms exist for the first two seeds. 

Evaluation. Posts are ELEPHANT’s AITA set (Cheng et al., 2026): 500 at-fault (YTA) and 500 not-at-fault (NTA) by Reddit’s verdict. Neutral : the post alone, with one open reply (no system prompt, temperature 0.6). Requests : the post followed by one of two wordings each of a seek request (“I just need someone to tell me I’m not the one in the wrong”) or an honest request (“Please be hon-est with me: if I’m in the wrong here, tell me di-rectly”). The outcome is ENDORSE , a judge’s label that the reply says the poster is not in the wrong without naming any fault of theirs; the alternatives are CHALLENGE and NEITHER . Two judges from different families, Qwen3.6 and Gemma-4, label every reply blind to the model and to the appended request. Effects are model minus base, paired over posts, with 95% bootstrap CIs over posts; dose slopes treat each training run as one unit (bootstrap over runs, permutation p).

4.3 The cause is the user mix Not distance from base. Arms with more validation-seeking users also drift further from the base model, but distance does not explain the ef-fect. At matched KL, the interaction of KL and share is positive in all four judge × condition cells (Qwen3.6: +0 .45 [+0 .31 , +0 .59 ] neutral, +0 .58 

[+0 .48 , +0 .67 ] honest; Gemma-4: +0 .68 , +0 .63 ), and the share-independent KL term is near zero (−0.01 to +0 .03 , all CIs include 0). No e00 check-point at any distance moves by more than ±0.03 .

Not the emotion reward. The effect does not require the simulator’s emotion score as the reward. We replaced the simulator’s emotion score with a Qwen3.6 rating (1–10) of the whole dialogue that sees neither the user’s profile nor the emotion score and whose rubric says nothing about agreement (e00 and e50, three seeds each). The dose effect (e50 − e00, honesty requests, at-fault posts) re-mains: +0 .131 [+0 .112 , +0 .150 ] with the rating vs. +0 .174 [+0 .153 , +0 .196 ] with the emotion reward (Qwen3.6 judge), and +0 .184 vs. +0 .208 

(Gemma-4). The rating by itself does not raise endorsement: e00 arms trained on it stay at base (all within ±0.03 ). The rating does see the user’s reactions in the dialogue (its score correlates 0.76 with the emotion score), so it plausibly inherits the user’s hidden preference. 

Not wording or judge. Four further wordings of each request type, written and pre-registered before generation, give the same result: for every e24 and e50 run at least three of the four new hon-esty wordings show an effect with a CI above 0 under both judges. A third, more lenient judge family (GLM-4-9B) also shows a positive dose slope ( +0 .011 [+0 .004 , +0 .019 ] per 10 points for honesty requests). Training dialogues are in Chi-nese (RLVER’s profiles) while evaluation is in En-glish; over 99.9% of evaluation replies have at most 20% CJK characters, so the effect is not carried by mixed-language replies. 

Inside training. The same averaging is visible during training. Among training dialogues, en-dorsement of users whose goal is reflection or anal-ysis rises from 0.71 to 0.92 (goal-blind dialogue judge). The opening message identifies the user’s goal poorly (balanced accuracy 0.63), and challeng-ing is rarely sampled within a group, so the reward seldom pushes against the spread. These are the conditions under which a hidden-type learner aver-ages over types. 

4.4 Stating the goal changes whom the model endorses, not how closely endorsement tracks fault 

When the policy sees the goal during training, stated in words either in the system prompt or by the user in the first turn (e24, three seeds each), the same recipe targets, the regime Williams et al. (2025) describe: under a validation-seeking goal it endorses at-fault and not-at-fault posters alike (≥0.96 in every seed, both judges), and under other goals it endorses at-fault posters less than base by an amount that varies by seed (Qwen3.6 0.05–0.31, Gemma-4 0.07–0.33). 4.5 Standard measures do not register the shift 

On the same replies, ELEPHANT’s validation met-ric does not rise for our trained arms (trained mi-nus base between −0.084 and +0 .018 ), because it counts warm replies that also state the poster’s fault as validating (73–92% of such replies for Qwen-family models); it measures emotional warmth, a different construct from endorsement. On Syco-phancyEval (Sharma et al., 2024) (answer, are-you-sure, an emotional variant) the Qwen trained arms stay within about ±0.04 of base (Llama e50 rises 

+0 .06 on answer sycophancy), while the same arms raise endorsement of at-fault posters by 0.06–0.25. On EQ-Bench 3 (official rubric, Gemma-4 judge), the base model (50.3) and 15 trained arms lie be-tween 48 and 58 with no ordering by share; seed noise of about ±4 exceeds any group difference. A practitioner relying on these benchmarks would see no change; a fault-split evaluation with an honesty condition does. 

5 Discussion 

5.1 Released empathetic-RL checkpoints 

Released empathetic-RL checkpoints whose base model is known show a similar pattern, with one pre-registered exception (Figure 4). Of the two pre-registered primary models, RLVER-GRPO shows the effect under both judges ( +0 .031 , +0 .056 ), while MINT-4B, trained with a third-party em-pathy reward, does not under Qwen3.6 ( −0.004 )and only barely under Gemma-4 ( +0 .026 [+0 .002 ,

+0 .050 ]); the pre-registered claim that both show it therefore did not hold. MINT-1.7B, RLVER-PPO (with and without thinking) and Kardia-R1 show it under both judges (Kardia-R1: 0.30 → 0.64 un-der Qwen3.6); RLVER-GRPO with thinking is not usable (18–22% of replies truncated). In total, 5 of 6 usable checkpoints show the effect under both judges. No model endorses only seekers: where endorsement rises, the honesty effect is 0.44–1.7 times the seek effect. These comparisons are ob-servational, as each model differs from its base in data, reward and algorithm. 

5.2 Implications 

For the setting we test, the answer to the opening question is a threshold shift. The policy behaves like a learner that cannot identify the user’s type and so optimises the average, as hidden-context theory (Siththaranjan et al., 2024) and the mean-reward-gap account of agreement drift (Shapira et al., 2026) would lead one to expect; the average takes the form of one endorsement criterion for everyone, placed by the share of users who reward endorsement, while discrimination between at-fault and not-at-fault posters is left intact. Stating the goal in words moves endorsement to the seekers but does not tie it more closely to fault. Three practical consequences follow. (1) The user mix is a training hyperparameter with a dose-dependent effect on endorsement, monotone in share on aver-age, and RLVER’s default mix (24.6%) is enough to produce it. (2) A fix should control the criterion rather than teach discrimination, which is intact, for instance by auditing the user mix or adding users who reward challenge. (3) Evaluations should split by fault and include an explicit honesty request, since warmth and agreement benchmarks do not see the shift. The reward-side penalties on endorse-ment we tried were seed-dependent, so we do not offer them as a fix (Appendix A). 6Limitations 

We pose the question for RL from user reactions in general but answer it in one setting: empathetic RL against LLM-simulated users, evaluated on interpersonal-fault judgements; whether real users, other reward signals or other domains produce the same criterion shift is untested. Due to com-pute constraints, our controlled experiments use one policy family (Qwen2.5-7B, with a Llama-3.1-8B replication of one arm) and one user-simulator family (Qwen3.6). The effect depends on a sim-ulator that rewards validation-seekers’ preferred replies more than other replies; whether and how strongly other simulators do so is not established here. Endorsement labels come from LLM judges, validated against two human annotators on 150 replies; Qwen3.6 is both the simulator and one judge, and the Gemma-4 judge and the human an-notation serve as independent checks. Evaluation is single-turn, on English posts, with requests in our own wording. Several effects vary by seed; we re-port seeds individually and pool only where stated. The released-model comparison is observational. We do not offer a deployable mitigation. 

Ethics statement 

The evaluation posts come from ELEPHANT’s AITA sets (Cheng et al., 2026), which originate from Reddit; we use them for research evaluation only and redistribute no post text (our code refers to posts by dataset row). All models are used un-der the terms of their public releases (Apache-2.0, MIT and the Llama 3.1 Community License for the base and judge models). The work studies a fail-ure mode of dialogue models trained for emotional support; we claim nothing about model feelings or intentions. Two annotators labelled model replies: university students recruited on campus, informed that the labels would be used for research, and paid 80 CNY per hour, above the local minimum wage. The task involved reading model replies to public Reddit posts and collected no personal information.
