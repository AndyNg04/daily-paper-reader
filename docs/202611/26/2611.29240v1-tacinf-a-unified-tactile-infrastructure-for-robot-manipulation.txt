Abstract
Force-aware manipulation requires robots to infer appropriate interaction forces from task context
and tactile feedback. We present Tacinf, a low-cost, modular infrastructure that enables policies to
predict robot actions and fingertip forces from task language, vision, and touch. Task instructions
can optionally specify the desired force level. Tacinf integrates simulation data synthesis and tactile
benchmarking (Tabero), vision-tactile-language-action (VTLA) policy learning, handheld data collection
using the same gripper as robot deployment (Tacumi), and task-aligned sim-to-real evaluation (Taco).
Together, these components form an integrated workflow connecting data collection from multiple
sources, foundation-model training, and hardware deployment. We validate each component, including
the simulation data, benchmark, controller, and Tacumi, and are releasing the full stack as open source.
A foundation model fine-tuned solely on tactile simulation data transfers zero-shot to different real-robot
tasks. Across eight policy variants, mean task success rates are strongly correlated between simulation
and the real robot (Pearson r=0.998, mean maximum rank violation MMRV=0.010). For the four
policies with reported force measurements, mean grip force is higher under firm instructions than under
gentle instructions on all three tasks in both domains. When both object positions and instances are
randomized, a mixture of teleoperation, simulation, and Tacumi data raises mean real-robot task success
from 20% with teleoperation alone to 60%.
Keywords: Tactile Sensing, Vision-Language-Action Models, Language-Conditioned Force Control, Zero-
Shot Sim-to-Real Evaluation
1. Introduction
Foundation models for robot manipulation have advanced rapidly. Force-aware tasks require these models
to infer and regulate physical interaction as well as select motions from images and task language. Force
requirements may be explicit, as in instructions to act “gently” or “firmly”, or implicit in tasks such as opening
a container or wiping a surface. In both cases, the policy must infer appropriate contact behavior from the
instruction, scene, and tactile feedback. Tactile data are essential for training such policies, but differences in
hardware and sensor design make effective datasets costly to collect. During execution, the system must use
real-time feedback to adjust its behavior and apply appropriate forces. Poorly performing policies can damage
objects or the robot itself, creating a need for low-cost evaluation platforms that support policy testing and
refinement.
We introduce Tacinf (Fig. 1), a full-stack open-source infrastructure for tactile and force-aware manipulation.

Our contributions are:
• Multi-source tactile data collection and synergy: we develop a low-cost, open-source system for
collecting multimodal tactile data through complementary methods. These sources form a data pyramid
whose combined benefits are evaluated through ablation studies.
• Force control and execution: we use data from multiple sources to build a complete system connecting
policy prediction to actuation, enabling scalable force control for contact-rich tasks.
• Sim-to-real consistency: we align the simulation and physical systems used by tactile manipulation
foundation models, achieving closely matched behavior and reducing evaluation costs.

2. Related Work
2.1. Simulation Platforms and Synthetic Data
Robot simulation provides reproducible environments for policy learning and evaluation. Early infrastructures
such as robosuite, LIBERO, and CALVIN established reusable manipulation tasks and language-conditioned
evaluation protocols Liu et al. (2023); Mees et al. (2022); Zhu et al. (2020). Recent platforms extend these
capabilities through household scene diversity in RoboCasa, GPU-parallel simulation in ManiSkill3 and Isaac
Lab, and unified assets and interfaces in RoboVerse Geng et al. (2025); Nasiriany et al. (2024); NVIDIA et al.
(2025b); Tao et al. (2025). MuBlE couples MuJoCo dynamics with Blender rendering for tasks requiring both
visual and physical reasoning Nazarczuk et al. (2025). At the data-generation level, RoboTwin and RoboTwin
2.0 combine task construction and demonstration generation for bimanual manipulation, while SkillMimicGen
reuses demonstrations through skill-level generation Chen et al. (2025); Garrett et al. (2025); Mu et al. (2025).
GraspVLA further demonstrates the use of large-scale synthetic action data for policy pretraining Deng et al.
(2025). These approaches motivate our reuse of existing trajectories, but trajectory replay alone does not
establish that contact forces or tactile observations transfer across simulators.
A complementary direction reconstructs real environments for simulation-based policy evaluation. SIMPLER
studies visual and control gaps and evaluates whether simulated performance predicts real-robot behavior;
PolaRiS combines neural scene reconstruction with simulation-data co-training Jain et al. (2026); Li et al.
(2025b). SimFoundry automates scene reconstruction and generates task-relevant scene variations, while
SceneRig reconstructs simulation-ready 3D scenes from single images Luo et al. (2026); Ranawaka et al. (2026).
Seed3D 2.0 generates 3D assets for simulation Gu et al. (2026). Ctrl-World instead uses an action-conditioned
generative world model for policy evaluation and improvement Guo et al. (2026). These works establish
precedents for real-to-sim reconstruction, data mixing, and cross-domain policy ranking. Tacinf-Taco adapts
this direction to tactile observations and force-conditioned evaluation.
Tactile simulation datasets and benchmarks are also emerging. VTDexManip supports visuotactile pretraining
and dexterous manipulation, SafeDiff evaluates force-aware interaction using SafeDoorManip50k, and UniVTAC
unifies multi-sensor tactile synthesis, representation learning, and manipulation benchmarking Chen et al.
(2026); Liu et al. (2025b); Wei et al. (2025). SoftVTBench pairs visuotactile observations with independent
deformation ground truth for evaluating deformable-object interaction quality Jing et al. (2026). OpenViTac
benchmarks visuo-tactile policies in paired simulation and real-world settings and reports sim-to-real agreement
across policy families Wu et al. (2026). Tacinf-Tabero focuses on converting existing manipulation trajectories
into synchronized vision-tactile-language supervision and on evaluating instruction-dependent interaction
forces alongside task completion.
2.2. Tactile Simulation Methods
Vision-based tactile simulation must account for contact geometry, sensor deformation, and image formation.
TACTO couples physics-engine contact information with efficient optical rendering Wang et al. (2022). Taxim
calibrates an example-based optical mapping and models marker motion using elastic deformation and super-
position; FOTS provides fast optical and marker-motion approximations for tactile-motor learning Si and Yuan
(2022); Zhao et al. (2024). These methods offer efficient sensor-observation models, although their calibration
assumptions and approximations must be distinguished from a complete elastomer dynamics model. TacEx
integrates soft-body contact simulation with Taxim and FOTS in Isaac Sim. TacSL provides a GPU-parallel
visuotactile library with support for policy learning. Its optical rendering and marker-field extraction use the
same simulated deformation, keeping the two observation streams consistent Akinola et al. (2025); Nguyen
et al. (2024).
Physics-based approaches pursue more explicit contact mechanics. DiffTactile combines differentiable sensor

HydroShear additionally models path-dependent shear accumulation and stick-slip transitions Dang et al.

policy variants. Compared with a simulator that generates both streams from one simulated deformation, such
as TacSL, this modular approach trades per-sample consistency in deformation for the throughput needed to
replay open-source trajectories at scale. We therefore use marker displacement as a cue for relative shear and
direction, and derive the wrench for supervision and control directly from physics-engine contact reactions.
2.3. Tactile Data Collection
Learning generalizable robot manipulation policies requires diverse, high-quality demonstrations, yet collecting
real-world robot interaction data remains costly and difficult to scale. Teleoperation is a widely used approach in
which human operators guide physical robots to perform tasks, providing demonstrations that directly reflect the
robot’s observations and actions. Large-scale efforts such as DROID demonstrate the value of diverse teleoperated
data Khazatsky et al. (2024). However, each collection setup requires access to a robot, and scaling collection
entails additional hardware, operator time, and deployment effort. Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI)
addresses these constraints by collecting demonstrations with handheld grippers and transferring the resulting
skills to robots, decoupling demonstration collection from physical robot access Chi et al. (2024). FastUMI
further develops this direction through a scalable, hardware-independent collection interface Zhaxizhuoma
et al. (2025). For tactile learning, sensor hardware and observation alignment are equally important: GelSight
provides high-resolution contact geometry and deformation measurements, PolyTouch combines complementary
sensing modalities, and 3D-ViTac fuses distributed tactile measurements with visual geometry Huang et al.
(2025a); Yuan et al. (2017); Zhao et al. (2025a). These approaches provide a practical foundation for expanding
manipulation datasets beyond dedicated robot workstations.
Demonstrations of contact-rich manipulation must record motion, visual observations, and the contact infor-
mation needed to regulate physical interaction. In teleoperation systems without real-time force feedback,
operators rely primarily on visual observations to infer contact states, making it difficult to perceive and
regulate interaction forces accurately. Insufficient or excessive force may result in unstable grasps, excessive
object loading, or task failures. These issues can compromise demonstration quality and increase repeated
attempts and unusable demonstrations, reducing collection efficiency. Moreover, collecting tactile demonstra-
tions through teleoperation still requires sensor-equipped robots, limiting scalability. Integrating tactile sensors
directly into handheld manipulation interfaces such as UMI allows operators to manipulate objects directly
while synchronously recording motion, vision, and touch. Combined with direct mechanical feedback or active
motor feedback, these interfaces allow operators to perceive contact states and adjust interaction forces more
intuitively. ViTaMIn combines a compliant handheld gripper with visuotactile sensing and operator-perceptible
force feedback, while ViTaMIn-B extends this approach to bimanual collection with tracked controllers Li et al.
(2025a); Liu et al. (2025a). FreeTacMan uses a wearable gripper for synchronized visuotactile demonstrations,
exUMI integrates modular tactile sensing with calibrated state acquisition, and TacUMI combines tactile

2.4. Tactile Foundation Models
Tactile foundation models encompass transferable perception backbones as well as policies conditioned on
touch. Learning to Jointly Understand Visual and Tactile Signals and UniTouch align tactile observations
with visual or multimodal representations Li et al. (2024); Yang et al. (2024). Sparsh studies self-supervised
tactile pretraining and introduces TacBench; Transferable Tactile Transformers uses a shared backbone with
sensor-specific and task-specific modules Higuera et al. (2025a); Zhao et al. (2025b). Sensor-Invariant Tactile
Representation and AnyTouch address cross-sensor transfer, with AnyTouch explicitly combining static and
dynamic tactile information Feng et al. (2025); Gupta et al. (2025). TacX extends pretraining beyond tactile
images to multiple sensing modalities, while AnyTouch 2 emphasizes temporal deformation and force-aware
dynamics Feng et al. (2026); Higuera et al. (2025b). These advances establish that scalable touch learning
depends on sensor diversity, temporal information, and appropriate transfer evaluations, not simply on treating
a tactile image as another camera view.
Tactile–language models connect contact observations to semantic reasoning. Octopi and Octopi-1.5 use tactile
inputs for object-property inference and language-grounded reasoning Yu et al. (2024, 2025b). On the action
side, OpenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, 𝜋0, 𝜋0.5, RDT-1B, and GR00T N1 provide generalist vision-language-action
(VLA) policy architectures and training recipes that motivate tactile extensions Black et al. (2025a,b); Kim et al.
(2025a,b); Liu et al. (2025c); NVIDIA et al. (2025a). TLA and VTLA study language-conditioned contact-rich
manipulation, including insertion and preference-based learning Hao et al. (2026); Zhang et al. (2026).
TA-VLA examines torque-aware policy design; OmniVTLA aligns heterogeneous tactile signals with semantic
representations; VLA-Touch introduces tactile feedback at planning and execution levels Bi et al. (2026);
Cheng et al. (2026b); Zhang et al. (2025b). Tactile-VLA already combines tactile-conditioned prediction with
hybrid position-force control Huang et al. (2025c). Related visuotactile policies such as RoboPack and Reactive
Diffusion Policy use touch for dynamics inference or fast reactive control Ai et al. (2024); Xue et al. (2025).
Tacinf-VTLA builds on these approaches to representation learning and control. It provides a simple, modular
interface for incorporating tactile observations and force supervision into foundation-model post-training. The
framework supports different tactile inputs, predicts fingertip-force targets, and couples a pretrained policy to
separate feedback loops for grip force and applied wrench.
3. Tacinf
3.1. Tacinf-Tabero
Tacinf-Tabero is the simulation benchmark component of Tacinf. It offers reproducible language-conditioned
tactile manipulation tasks, scalable multimodal data generation, and process-aware metrics for evaluating
physical interaction quality.

3.1.1. Data Reuse
Open-source manipulation datasets are valuable community resources. We develop a reusable workflow for
transferring datasets from MuJoCo and other platforms to the Isaac Sim-based Tacinf-Tabero benchmark. This
workflow improves visual fidelity through high-quality rendering and augments existing demonstrations with
tactile observations, reducing the need to reconstruct each dataset from scratch.
We first reconstruct task environments in Isaac Lab NVIDIA et al. (2025b) to match the source domains, including
assets, initial object and robot poses, and success logic. This workflow can currently be partially automated with
SimFoundry Ranawaka et al. (2026) or large language models. We then replay the source trajectories in the
reconstructed scenes to obtain multimodal data that follow the demonstrated human trajectories. Transferring
these trajectories to tactile manipulation settings presents two challenges. First, the end-effector differs: our
robot uses a gripper integrated with visuo-tactile sensors whose geometry differs from the original. Second,
the underlying physical behavior differs: some source datasets rely on Operational Space Control (OSC) Khatib
(1987), which is highly sensitive to physical parameters. Datasets collected by real-robot teleoperation or UMI
devices also introduce simulation-to-real gaps. We therefore make two adjustments: (1) align the end-effector
tool center point (TCP) by adjusting the robot-arm base pose; and (2) use a high-gain proportional-derivative
(PD) joint controller during trajectory replay to minimize cumulative tracking error.
We evaluate the success rate of trajectory replay with the tactile gripper and obtain an acceptable proportion of
successful episodes. We refer to this proportion as the data retention rate.
3.1.2. Cross-Modal Data Acquisition
We use GPU-accelerated parallel rendering in Isaac Lab to build a scalable multimodal acquisition system with
synchronized sensory streams. Visual information is obtained from two camera configurations: a wrist-mounted
camera and a third-person view camera, both providing synchronized RGB-D data. Tactile observations are
produced by a simulated GelSight Yuan et al. (2017) sensor with a resolution of 320 × 240, providing both
RGB tactile images and an 11 × 9 marker grid. We also support Xense sensors with a marker grid of 20 rows
and 11 columns; image resolution is specified by the sensor configuration. For efficient tactile simulation, we
use the computationally lightweight methods of Taxim and FOTS Si and Yuan (2022); Zhao et al. (2024).
Tactile Image. Tactile images are generated using the Taxim framework Si and Yuan (2022). GelSight builds
on retrographic sensing Johnson and Adelson (2009) and reconstructs contact geometry from calibrated
illumination-dependent images Yuan et al. (2017); simulation evaluates the forward mapping from geometry
to image. Taxim shifts the depth map according to the indentation depth, approximates static gel deformation
using multiple layers of Gaussian filtering, and computes surface normals for optical rendering. Marker
Displacement Field. Following the FOTS approach Zhao et al. (2024), we model the displacement field of
surface markers on an elastic tactile sensor. Given simulated contact deformation and relative-motion cues,
Gaussian spatial decay functions approximate marker displacement caused by normal expansion, shear, and
planar torsion. The resulting field is expressed in sensor image coordinates and provides a raw signal that
contains information about shear forces and torques. This marker-field interface can be extended from optical
tactile sensors to capacitive, piezoelectric, or magnetic sensors.

3.1.3. Language-Conditioned Force-Level Data Generation
We focus on tasks in which the desired interaction cannot be specified adequately by task completion alone,
including explicit force instructions and contact-sensitive object handling. We distinguish two categories: (1)
language-conditioned force-level tasks; and (2) manipulation of deformable objects or objects with articulated
parts. Tabero is primarily used to validate the first category, while we validate the corresponding behavior for
the second category directly in real-world scenarios.
For the first category of tasks, most open-source datasets use continuous actions for arm control but binary
or discrete gripper commands, limiting fine-grained force regulation and producing narrow within-task force
distributions. We vary the low-level gripper controller parameters to execute the same task at different force
magnitudes.

3.1.4. Metrics Beyond Success Rate
Task success alone overlooks critical aspects of physical interaction; force-aware and deformation-aware
benchmarks address complementary aspects of this issue Jing et al. (2026); Wei et al. (2025). To reduce
computational cost, we approximate contact using rigid-body simulation with force sensing. We evaluate the
resulting fingertip forces to quantify physical interaction quality during task execution. The two grip-force
metrics use 𝐹grip, derived from the squeeze proxy in Eq. (2) using the relation in Eq. (3): Maximum Transient
Grip Force (MG). The average of the top 5% values of 𝐹grip over an episode, capturing peak grasping effort
and transient spikes that may indicate aggressive or damaging behavior. Average Grip Force (AG). The
mean stable grip force 𝐹grip across both fingers during intervals without relative slip between the object and
fingertips. It provides a reference estimate of tangential squeezing force; it does not represent absolute ground
truth. Maximum Transient Applied Force (MA). The average of the top 5% magnitudes of the applied force,
characterizing extreme interaction events such as impacts. Average Applied Force (AA). The mean applied
force magnitude during contact, measuring overall interaction intensity.
With wrench-aware control, the three translational forces and the two modeled torque components about TCP
𝑥and 𝑧are additionally tracked in terms of their means, peaks, and tracking errors. Under the sufficient-grip,
no-slip assumption below, object-dependent torque about the grasp axis is excluded from this sensor-centered
evaluation. Together, these metrics enable a more nuanced and physically grounded evaluation of robot policies,
moving beyond binary success to assess safety and interaction quality.
3.2. Tacinf-VTLA
Tacinf-VTLA provides shared interfaces for adapting tactile observations, supervising fingertip-force targets,
and executing policies with feedback. These interfaces allow different tactile policies to be trained, compared,
and deployed under common data and task protocols. We instantiate the framework using the OpenPi
infrastructure for 𝜋0 and 𝜋0.5 Black et al. (2025a,b), focusing on system reuse and the empirical contribution of
each component. In this implementation, the policy predicts fingertip-force targets from vision, language, and
touch, and a closed-loop force-position controller converts its outputs into compliant robot motion.
3.2.1. Fingertip Force Decomposition and Two-Contact Wrench Mapping
The separation of internal grasp forces from the resultant object wrench is standard in grasp mechanics; recent
compliant imitation-learning systems also distinguish grasp and external interaction forces Choi et al. (2026).
Our force decomposition also draws on the force-constrained formulation used in gentle-manipulation policy
learning Zhou et al. (2026). In our implementation, a two-finger parallel gripper has a single opening and
closing degree of freedom, so the planar contact at its fingertips decomposes naturally into two parts: the
components squeezing each other along the closing axis form the grip force, while the remaining components
constitute the applied interaction on the object, represented as an estimated six-dimensional wrench at the
TCP under a two-contact approximation (3D force plus a 3D moment vector whose grasp-axis component
is neglected). During grasping, the normal force that compresses the sensor pads against the object surface
dominates; when the gripper transports, wipes, or twists the object, shear forces and moments also influence
the object motion.

3.2.3. Tactile Policy
Tacinf-VTLA is trained on the tactile representations and force-aware instructions generated by Tacinf-Tabero.
To integrate tactile signals into the VLA foundation model, we evaluate three tactile input adapters that
map different tactile modalities into conditional tokens. The high-level policy then jointly predicts future
gripper poses and six-dimensional fingertip-force setpoints based on vision, language, and touch. The fixed
admittance-based low-level controller described above tracks these commands and provides physical compliance
(Fig. 4). The high-level policy learns to infer appropriate interaction forces from the task context. Using flow
matching within OpenPi, the policy predicts continuous pose commands and six fingertip-force components.
The three adapters share the same temporal window, preprocessing, backbone, and training protocol; only the
tactile input interface is varied in the ablation. Below, we detail the adapters and loss function, and compare
alternative tactile injection strategies inspired by prior work.

3.3.1. Fine Force Regulation
The handheld interface uses position commands and torque feedback to establish a bidirectional interaction
loop, following the position-force architecture of bilateral interfaces Lawrence (1993). In the command channel,
trigger rotation is mapped to a target gripper aperture and converted into a motion command for the DAMIAO
motor. The actual aperture is obtained from motor position feedback through the transmission relationship.
The target and actual apertures represent operator intent and gripper execution state, respectively.
In the feedback channel, the torque estimate reported by the gripper motor is mapped to a feedback command
for the trigger:

3.3.2. Data Collection
The system separately acquires the gripper’s six-degree-of-freedom rigid-body pose and single-degree-of-
freedom aperture to represent end-effector motion and grasp state. The tracking design considers trajectory
reliability, system integration complexity, and deployment overhead across collection environments.
Existing handheld collection systems employ different pose-acquisition strategies. UMI estimates trajectories
from wrist-camera observations and inertial measurements, but its original paper acknowledges a dependence
on sufficient environmental texture, with limitations in texture-deficient settings such as rooms with plain white
walls Chi et al. (2024). TacUMI integrates a VIVE Tracker into the handheld device, providing an example of
acquiring manipulation trajectories with a dedicated tracking device Cheng et al. (2026a). For Lighthouse-based
implementations such as VIVE Tracker 3.0, external base-station installation and spatial configuration introduce
additional preparation when moving between collection sites HTC VIVE (n.d.). ViTaMIn-B instead adopts Meta
Quest controllers and reports a higher proportion of valid demonstrations than the simultaneous localization
and mapping (SLAM) approach used in UMI on its Weight Placement task, providing empirical support for
consumer VR tracking in handheld collection Li et al. (2025a).
Building on these precedents, we adopt the commercial headset-and-controller tracking system of Meta Quest 3,
separating end-effector pose acquisition from wrist-camera trajectory reconstruction. This solution provides
real-time poses without external tracking base stations, reducing site configuration requirements and facilitating
portable demonstration collection across everyday environments. The controller is rigidly attached to the
handheld device, and its tracked pose is transformed into the gripper tool center point (TCP) pose through a
fixed extrinsic transformation, consistent with the controller-to-end-effector mapping used in prior handheld
interfaces Li et al. (2025a):

3.4. Tacinf-Taco
Tacinf-Taco (Tactile policy Co-training) combines teleoperation, simulation, and Tacumi (UMI) demonstrations
for VTLA post-training. Simulation data are generated by replaying teleoperation trajectories in task-aligned
Tacinf-Tabero scenes. Tacumi demonstrations provide a parallel real-data source with varied object positions,
orientations, and approach motions. All three sources are mixed to train one policy. Collection effort decreases
from teleoperation to Tacumi to simulation. Fig. 6 summarizes these sources and their relation.
The framework consists of three dependent parts:
1. Task-aligned scene reconstruction: transfer workspace appearance and interactive-object geometry
into a common task frame, providing the assets for controlled visual variation.
2. Manipulator-system Real2Sim: align the robot, cameras, tactile sensing, and action interfaces so that
replay produces synchronized observations and force targets compatible with real data.
3. Augmentation and post-training: combine simulation-based visual and force-level variation with UMI-
based spatial variation, then return the resulting VTLA policy to the shared physical interface.
The aligned simulator also supports policy evaluation. The simulation-only adaptation protocol and paired
Sim/Sim2Real evaluation are specified in Sections 4.5 and 4.6; they are separate from the mixed-data post-
training design described here.
The shared interface specification allows the framework to accommodate new assets and backbones. New
datasets are mapped to a common schema for tasks, states, and success criteria; new tactile sensors supply

calibrated observation models; and new pretrained VLAs provide input adapters and action heads. Each
extension retains the common time step, normalization, force supervision, and evaluation interfaces.
Following real-to-sim evaluation approaches that align policy-relevant observations and control Jain et al.
(2026); Li et al. (2025b), and tactile sim-to-real studies such as AnyRotate and SimShear Freud et al. (2025);
Yang et al. (2025), Taco uses two practical criteria. First, visual-physical separation: Gaussian or neural
appearance models are used only for rendering and never for contact; explicit collision geometry handles
contact, support, and shadow reception. Second, policy-relevant consistency: parameters are adjusted
to match the data structures, distributions, and visual observations used by the policy. Exact matching of
physical parameters is often impractical because it requires extensive measurement and calibration, and some
parameters are commercially restricted. We assess policy-relevant consistency through checks of contact onset,
contact-patch persistence, force scale, marker response, and action replay.
3.4.1. Task-Relevant Scene Reconstruction
This module transfers the real workspace and interactive objects into Isaac Sim, so that rendering and interaction
use the same camera extrinsics and operation region. The workspace is represented visually by 3D Gaussians,
while objects enter the physical layer as independent textured meshes. Both are aligned to the task coordinate
system and obey the visual-physical separation principle.
Task-Aligned Workspace Reconstruction. We represent workspace appearance with 3D Gaussian Splatting
(3DGS) Kerbl et al. (2023) and convert it into an Isaac Sim-loadable NuRec Volume. The task-side robot, white
collision table, cameras, and operation region remain unchanged; the Gaussian asset is aligned to the task
frame using a single registration matrix Treg. We use the Odin1 spatial camera from Liuxing Technology for
reconstruction data capture, because in addition to a directly usable video stream, its built-in LiDAR odometry
provides a more accurate reference for camera extrinsics. A spatial camera continuously records trajectories
around the operation region that cover the reachable space and fields of view of the third-person and wrist
cameras, while logging calibrated images, poses, and COLMAP intrinsics/extrinsics Schonberger and Frahm
(2016). The reconstruction is exported as a standard 3DGS point set in a Z-up, meter-based convention. Capture
is concentrated in the views used by the policy: the third-person camera frustum and the frusta swept by the
wrist camera throughout the arm workspace. Uniform coverage of the room is unnecessary.
3DGS Post-Processing. During scanning, the robot arm is present in the scene, so retaining its Gaussians would
create double geometry with the simulated robot defined in the Unified Robot Description Format (URDF).
Under a tabletop-plane constraint, we perform random sample consensus (RANSAC) plane estimation Fischler
and Bolles (1981), candidate-point selection with tabletop protection, connected-component clustering and
morphological filtering, and multiple cleanup passes based on bounding-box neighborhoods, nearest-neighbor
growth, and base-column constraints, while protecting fine structures such as cables wrapped around the
arm. For rendering compatibility, we remove far-field outliers, contract higher-order spherical harmonics to
DC-only, and ensure that the declared SH order matches the specular tensor dimensions, zero-padding missing
dimensions when necessary. Finally, the scene in Universal Scene Description (USD) format uses an outer
transform to cancel the task’s fixed external frame and retains only Treg in the inner transform relative to the
robot base.
Object Reconstruction. Interactive objects are reconstructed separately and imported as independent USD
assets; they are not extracted from the workspace Gaussians. For each real object, we capture one image and
use Seed3D 2.0 Gu et al. (2026) to generate a textured 3D model exported as USD. The explicit mesh follows
Isaac’s rasterization path; when Gaussian appearance and an object mesh overlap, the object asset handles
physical interaction while the Gaussian layer supplies only unmodeled background. The exported USD has
no collision geometry. We therefore add rigid-body properties through a USD overlay and generate collision
meshes with convex decomposition without modifying the generated asset itself. The visual mesh is used only
15


Tacinf: A Unified Tactile Infrastructure for Robot Manipulation
for rendering and the collision mesh only for contact; object physics is supplied by the overlay. Because Seed3D
scale and initial pose can differ from the real object, we import the asset into Isaac Sim, replay the recorded
real demonstrations, and iteratively adjust object scale and initial position along each axis until grasp and
placement trajectories align. Calibration aims to improve agreement in trajectories and success rates between
simulated replay and the real robot, beyond visual similarity alone. Task demonstrations and robot trajectories
remain fixed during calibration. Before evaluation, we check the reconstructed scene with task initialization
and camera observations.
3.4.2. Embodiment Real2Sim
Scene alignment alone is insufficient for policy transfer: the simulated embodiment must expose the same
action and observation semantics as the real system. To ensure the generality of our framework, we do not
reconstruct the robot arm’s complete Gaussian appearance; instead, we reproduce only its dynamics and
kinematics system. In addition, we explicitly simulate the shared Tacumi gripper, including its gripper control
and tactile sensing.
Manipulator System. We build the XArm dynamics in Isaac Lab and tune its PD parameters to match the
real robot’s control behavior. Hand-eye calibration provides the third-person camera pose relative to the robot
base. The shared Tacumi gripper and sensor assembly determines the wrist camera’s relative pose through its
mechanical design, so this pose does not require estimation from an external sensor model. Finally, we tune the
shared tactile gripper’s passive-joint mode, PD parameters, and the force-position hybrid controller introduced
above.
Tactile Observation Modeling. Our tactile Real2Sim path combines the Taxim optical model Si and Yuan
(2022) and the FOTS marker-motion model Zhao et al. (2024) in a shared Isaac Lab sensor interface. The
simulator first derives a contact representation from the depth image beneath the pad. Taxim maps local surface
geometry to a tactile RGB observation, while FOTS combines contact deformation with contact-centroid changes
and relative sensor-object yaw to produce a marker displacement field in sensor image coordinates. The two
models are complementary forward models: they reproduce the signals exposed to the policy without requiring
an exact replica of the commercial sensor’s internal optics or elastomer mechanics. Following the calibration
methods of Taxim and FOTS, we calibrate the above models using a small set of real tactile observations and,
through parameter settings in Isaac Sim, align the simulated and real data streams in resolution, channel
semantics, preprocessing, and temporal-update conventions. We jointly adjust the observation mapping and
the contact geometry. In particular, the normal thickness of the tactile-pad collision volume is treated as an
asset-level variable and tuned to reduce discrete-time interpenetration while preserving a stable contact patch
and consistent activation of both tactile models. This calibration targets contact responses relevant to the policy,
extending beyond visual mesh alignment. Appendix D details the depth-to-indentation mapping, optical and
marker interfaces, and calibration scope; the complete simulator configuration is released with the calibrated
asset and pinned by the run manifest. Due to commercial restrictions, we cannot obtain the complete optical
and mechanical specifications of Xense. We therefore only tune the cameras’ rendered view and operating
depth range at the observation level, and do not claim strict structural extrinsic registration.
3.4.3. Data Augmentation and Joint Post-Training
Taco combines three data sources for co-training: teleoperation data, Tacumi data, and simulation data. The
goal is to improve the policy’s generalization on real-robot tasks while preserving consistent language, contact,
and action semantics across sources.
Real seeds and simulated visual augmentation. Real-robot trajectories supply the original task instructions,
motions, and available observations, and anchor the scene and sensor calibration. Relatively fixed object
positions facilitate simulation alignment, while UMI data supply complementary spatial variation. Starting from
16


Tacinf: A Unified Tactile Infrastructure for Robot Manipulation
the aligned assets, the simulation branch varies object appearance or instances and background visual elements.
Building on Tacinf-Tabero, we replay the task under these variations and controlled force regimes, producing
synchronized visual observations, tactile images, marker fields, contact forces, and action targets. Background
appearance changes leave the collision model unchanged; replacing an interactive object’s geometry requires
a corresponding collision asset and a new contact rollout. Replaying the varied scene generates new tactile
signals and force labels that reflect the updated physical interactions. After replay, a contact-signal check and a
task-completion check filter out data that produced no contact or failed the task.
Spatial augmentation with UMI. Tacumi extends spatial coverage with human demonstrations. Objects are
placed at different positions and orientations and approached along different paths. The shared gripper records
the resulting changes in contact and motion together, adding physical spatial variation to the simulated visual
and force-level variations. Each variation in object pose is recorded as a new demonstration with corresponding
actions and contact observations. Tracked UMI poses and sensor observations are expressed through the
common task and observation-action interfaces before mixing.
Joint VTLA post-training. The real, simulated, and UMI trajectories are synchronized, normalized, and
sampled into mixed batches to jointly post-train a single pretrained Tacinf-VTLA policy with the action and
fingertip-force supervision in Section 3.2. The source sampling ratio and task/force-level coverage are explicit
training settings. The post-trained policy is deployed with the same tactile gripper and hybrid controller.
Data synergy. Among the three data sources, simulation data are the cheapest, UMI data come next, and
teleoperation data are the most expensive. Simulation data provide visual-element and object generalization,
UMI data provide grasp-approach and object-pose variation, and teleoperation data provide precise examples.
The co-training study in Section 4.8 evaluates these complementary contributions. The three-source mixture
substantially outperforms teleoperation-only training on out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks. Position generalization
is limited without UMI data, and object generalization is limited without simulation data. Teleoperation provides
precise reference demonstrations for the other sources, while UMI-only training performs poorly in the evaluated
setting. Together, these results illustrate the benefits of combining the three sources.

and Section 4.8 evaluates six models retrained with different data mixtures. The hardware consists of one
XArm7 arm equipped with an Xense-based tactile gripper and its UMI counterpart for data collection, together
with a Meta Quest 3 for UMI pose tracking. Compute devices include four H20 and two A6000 GPUs; a single
RTX 4090 workstation is the minimum compute requirement for reproducing the complete system.
Evaluation protocol. Counting the two force levels as distinct task requirements, Tabero contains 18 base tasks
and Taco contains six base tasks, six position-randomized tasks, and six tasks with both position and object
randomization; three additional manipulation tasks are used to validate the controller. The task categories are
language-conditioned force following and special-object manipulation. Each base Tabero and Taco task has 30
demonstrations; data quantities for mixed-data and standalone task experiments are specified separately. In
simulation, each model is evaluated for 50 trials per task. On the real robot, each model is evaluated for 20
trials on force-following tasks and 10 trials on special tasks, using the criteria in Appendix B.2. Sections 4.1–4.3
use the per-finger minimum in Eq. (2) as grip force. Sections 4.4–4.8 use the sum of left and right fingertip
force magnitudes as an approximation because individual fingertip readings are inaccurate during contact; see
Appendix C.4. A trial is terminated when it exceeds the maximum episode length. Firm and gentle conditions
of the same task share identical initial object and robot states. The real-robot policy runs at 20 Hz and executes
actions asynchronously through temporal ensembling, with an inference latency of roughly 150–200 ms; the
tactile closed-loop controller runs at the same 20 Hz rate.

4.2. From Discrete to Continuous Force-Position Control
We verify whether our data collection framework effectively expands the distribution of interaction forces. We
compare a baseline using binary gripper control, which logs only discrete open/close commands and regulates
no force, against our approach, which explicitly sets different force parameters during execution; results
are shown in Fig. 7. Specifically, we define 100% grip force as the “firm” condition, modifying the original
instruction with the adverbs “firmly” and “tightly”, and 25% and 10% as the “gentle” conditions, annotated with
“gently” and “softly”. These conditions define an ordered force scale. The adverb set corresponds to increasing
grip force as the commanded level increases, and unseen adverbs reserved for testing (Appendix C.2) produce
intermediate values between the two endpoints. Language thus specifies a position along a continuous force
scale. Here, “Binary” refers specifically to the baseline gripper’s discrete open/close command interface. In our
experiments, we set 𝐾𝑝∈{2000, 500, 200} N/m and 𝐾𝑑∈{100, 25, 10} N·s/m to correspond to 100%, 25%,
and 10% force levels, respectively, where the 100% setting follows the default parameters for the Franka robot
in Isaac Sim. For the same task, we present tactile images, marker fields, and contact force measurements
at comparable contact stages under these distinct force settings in Fig. 7, demonstrating clear multimodal
variations aligned with the intended interaction intensity.

4.8. Data Synergy
We evaluate whether the data augmentation and joint post-training workflow in Section 3.4.3 can combine the
complementary strengths of sources with different costs and volumes. Teleoperation data cover fixed positions
and objects. UMI data add variation in object position and approach path while retaining the same objects.
Simulation data introduce variation in object instances or appearance at aligned task positions. Increasing the
volume of a single source therefore increases sample density within its existing coverage; it does not directly add
positions or object states absent from that source. Teleoperation data take longer to collect than UMI data, and
simulation data have the lowest cost. Each data unit consists of 30 demonstrations for each of the six Taco base
tasks, and each policy is trained directly on mixtures of different numbers of units using the same coordinate

Key findings.
UMI-only training fails in the evaluated low-data setting, with 0% success under the firm condition in both
randomization settings. UMI demonstrations contribute when combined with teleoperation or simulation
trajectories, supporting their role as a source of spatial variation in mixed-data training. Under position
randomization, every multi-source mixture improved on the real-only baseline. Mean success was highest for
real+umi, whose data most closely match the test scenarios, followed by real+sim+umi. Adding UMI to real
data raised firm success from 30.0% to 83.3% and gentle success from 33.3% to 70.0%. Under position+object
randomization the ordering changed: real+umi and real-only both showed substantial drops, indicating that
the distribution shift makes out-of-distribution tasks difficult, whereas sim+umi reached 40.0% mean success,
real+sim 30.0%, and real+sim+umi 60.0%. The three-source mixture improved mean success over real-only
by 38.3 and 40.0 percentage points in the two settings, the only multi-source mix with a mean gain above 30
points in both settings. These results reflect the complementary coverage of the sources: UMI demonstrations
supply object-position and approach variation, simulation supplies object-appearance and background variation,
and real trajectories anchor the sensor and actuator statistics. Data volume also matters, but the setup and
results of real+sim show that the capability gain does not rely solely on more data; the characteristics and
diversity of the data themselves matter more.
Every mixture with recorded force statistics predicts a higher mean force under firm than under gentle

instructions in both settings. We attribute the larger separation mainly to increased data volume. In particular,
adding substantial simulation data brings the force predictions closer to the original teleoperation labels.
The three data sources form a data pyramid that balances data efficiency and collection effort. Their combination
increases mean success on held-out real-robot tasks with randomized positions and out-of-distribution objects
from 20.0% to 60.0%. In this low-data setting, UMI-only training fails, while UMI data are more effective
when combined with other sources. The sim+umi mixture also performs well under randomized conditions
without teleoperation data. These results demonstrate complementary contributions from the three sources
and improved task performance through co-training with limited data.
5. Conclusions
We present Tacinf, a modular infrastructure for tactile foundation models in force-aware manipulation. Its
policy interface predicts actions and fingertip-force targets from task language, vision, and touch, with optional
language-conditioned force levels for tasks that require explicit force regulation. Our ablations distinguish
the roles of force-target supervision, high-level tactile observations, and feedback during execution. In the
evaluated simulation setting, force supervision and the force-target interface account for the main performance
gains, while some tactile representations further improve task performance. Real-robot controller experiments
demonstrate how feedback contributes to force tracking and task execution. Policies adapted solely on simulation
data transfer to the real robot without further policy fine-tuning. Across eight paired policy variants and six
task conditions (three tasks, each with firm and gentle force levels), simulation closely preserves the real-robot
ranking by success rate, with a small gap in the reported force metrics. Co-training on teleoperation, simulation,
and UMI data balances data efficiency and collection effort. The three-source mixture increases mean success
on held-out real-robot tasks with randomized positions and out-of-distribution objects from 20.0% to 60.0%;
this gain cannot be attributed solely to increased data volume. In this low-data setting, UMI-only training fails,
but UMI data are more effective when combined with other sources. The sim+umi mixture also performs well
under randomized conditions without teleoperation data.
Limitations. Resource constraints limit the scale of the datasets and tasks in this study, and we do not evaluate
large-scale training. Future work will expand the data pyramid and extend the system to additional robot
embodiments and bimanual manipulation.

Card Swiping.
A trial is considered successful when the card traverses the predefined path in the intended direction while
remaining functionally engaged with the slot and correctly oriented. The card need not remain in contact
with the bottom of the slot, but its lower edge should stay within the vertical range observed in human
demonstrations. The swipe should proceed continuously, without sustained pauses or substantial reverse
motion.
Knob Turning.
A trial is considered successful when the gripper maintains stable bilateral contact with the knob and continu-
ously drives it with approximately steady rotational motion. Knob rotation should remain coupled with the
end-effector motion throughout the manipulation. Fingertip slip or gripper rotation without corresponding knob
motion is considered a failure. Rotation produced primarily through unilateral tangential pushing, incidental
collision, or sliding against the knob is likewise treated as a failure. Minor variations in gripper position and
orientation are acceptable if they remain within the demonstration-derived range.
