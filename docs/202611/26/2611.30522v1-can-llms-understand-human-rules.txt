Can LLMs Understand Human Rules?
Sydney Levine1,2, Elizaveta Tennant1, Seth Lazar1,3, Rasmi Elasmar1, Matija Franklin1, Verena Rieser1 and
Julia Haas1
1Google DeepMind, 2New York University, 3Johns Hopkins University
Rules help people coordinate and cooperate for mutual benefit. For AI models to coexist with humans
and other AI systems, they will need to interact with and navigate such rules successfully. But such
interactions require AI systems to recognize not only when a rule permits or prohibits a particular action,
but also cases where people may reasonably disagree about whether a certain action is appropriate.
This paper introduces the RULES benchmark—building on a set of 300 images depicting signs posted
around New York City, and accompanied by 937 scenarios—to test frontier models’ ability to discern
whether a proposed action is socially acceptable, unacceptable, or would generate disagreement. At
first pass, we find that frontier models exhibit robust performance on the benchmark, successfully
engaging with human-written directives in naturalistic visual environments, and correctly determining
the social acceptability of a wide range of everyday activities. However, we further find that models
systematically fail to recognize certain widely accepted permissible exceptions to a rule, indicating a
critical shortcoming in model understanding. Moreover, models struggle to identify instances where
humans disagree regarding the application of a given rule; in these instances, models tend to identify
proposed actions as unacceptable, rather than as appropriate targets of disagreement. We do find that
ensembling a series of diverse models together begins to recover the disagreement signal. Overall, our
findings identify significant limitations in AI performance on human rules.
Keywords: normative competence, AI agent safety
1. Introduction
Human social coordination relies on rules that
are inherently incomplete (Hadfield and Wein-
gast, 2014; Hart, 1961; Schauer, 1991). Rather
than functioning as rigid, algorithmic constraints,
human rules depend on an underlying architec-
ture of pragmatic interpretation: recognizing not
just what a rule literally says, but to whom it
applies, when it can be legitimately suspended,
and when an apparent violation is likely to be so-
cially acceptable (Kwon et al., 2026; Levine et al.,
2024, 2026; Wylie et al., 2025). A construction
sign reading “Do not enter,” for instance, com-
municates a general prohibition to normal traffic,
yet tacitly permits entry to emergency vehicles,
delivery couriers, or the site workers themselves.
Conversely, an overly literal adherence to rules
can cause genuine harm (Figure 1). A public sign
warning “No Loitering” outside a transit station
is intended to deter illicit disruption; an interpre-
tation that prohibits a frail or elderly commuter
from sitting down to rest overestimates the pre-
sumed purpose of the rule at the expense of a vul-
nerable individual. Crucially, human normative
competence involves not just knowing clear per-
missions and prohibitions, but navigating reason-
able disagreement (Gabriel, 2020; Rawls, 1993).
Whether an idling delivery vehicle is “blocking” an
alley, or whether bad weather excuses a parking
violation, depends on context, values, and trade-
offs where even reasonable people may arrive at
differing conclusions. Human social fluency re-
quires recognizing these gray zones, balancing
safety against common-sense flexibility.
As frontier AI models are integrated into open-
ended, real-world environments, the capacity for
nuanced normative performance becomes not
only a topic of academic inquiry, but a realis-
tic everyday environment. Digital agents tasked
with open-ended workflows, community modera-
tion, or customer interactions may become brittle
and frustrating if they apply guidelines without
context-dependent nuance.

This paper introduce RULES, a multi-modal
benchmark designed to evaluate how frontier AI
models interpret and navigate explicit human
norms situated in real-world contexts (see Figure
1). The dataset pairs natural, real-world pho-
tographs of posted public signs—avoiding syn-
thetic or AI-generated artifacts—with contextual
descriptions of scenarios occurring in their pres-
ence. Achieving high performance on RULES
demands more than text parsing or basic vi-
sual grounding; models must integrate broad
world knowledge, infer unstated pragmatic in-
tent, and navigate sociocultural nuance. Further-
more, models must account for how disagree-
ment organically emerges: both from empirical
uncertainty about under-specified scenarios, and
from genuine value conflicts where reasonable ob-
servers prioritize competing priorities differently.
Using this benchmark, we evaluate whether lead-
ing models can recognize legitimate exceptions to
explicit rules, calibrate their confidence on cases
of reasonable human disagreement, and resist
defaulting to overly-rigid prohibitions. We fur-
ther assess whether standard inference-time in-
terventions—such as prompting scaffolding, role-
playing personas, and response formatting—can
bridge or conversely widen the gap between lit-
eral compliance and consistency with socially ac-
ceptable human behavior.
In introducing this approach, RULES introduces
a grounded, safety-relevant conceptual frame-
work and accompanying testbed for the next gen-
eration of assistive and embodied agents navigat-
ing our normative environments.
Related Work
Social and ethical reasoning benchmarks.
A
growing body of work evaluates whether mod-
els can reproduce human normative judgments.
The ETHICS benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021)
probes knowledge of justice, deontology, virtue,
utilitarianism, and commonsense morality, while
resources such as Social Chemistry (Forbes et al.,
2020), Moral Stories (Emelin et al., 2021), and
Social IQa (Sap et al., 2019) target social norms
and commonsense social inference. Delphi (Jiang
et al., 2021), trained on the 1.7M-judgment Com-
monsense Norm Bank, models descriptive ethics
in a bottom-up fashion. RULES differs from these
efforts along two axes. First, rather than prob-
ing for general-purpose ethical principles, our
evaluation is grounded in real-world directives,
intended to communicate context-specific rules
- and therefore requiring flexible understanding
of what it is and is not appropriate to do. Sec-
ond, we intentionally do not target single “correct”
judgments, but rather aggregate multiple views
in every test instance, with the goal of providing
an overview of what is generally an acceptable,
unacceptable, and contested response.
Rule and instruction following.
A separate line
of work asks whether models can follow explicitly
stated instructions or rules. For instance, IFEval
(Zhou et al., 2023) measures compliance with ver-
ifiable formatting and content constraints, and
RuLES (Mu et al., 2024) tests whether models ad-

here to simple behavioral rules under adversarial
pressure. Such benchmarks largely treat rule-
following as literal compliance with the surface-
level meaning of a directive. RULES instead tar-

knowing when a directive applies, when an un-
stated exception is warranted, and when reason-

Moral flexibility and exceptions.
Closest in
spirit to our framing is work on when rules should
be broken or bent. Jin et al. Jin et al. (2022)
introduce MoralExceptQA, showing that captur-
ing the flexibility of the human moral mind—
determining when a rule permits an exception
in novel situations—requires reasoning strategies
beyond surface pattern-matching. RULES extends
this concern from moral exceptions in toy cases to
the broader question of interpreting posted direc-
tives in open-world environments and crucially
treats justified disagreement, not just permissible
exception, as a prediction target.
Value pluralism and disagreement.
Finally,
our emphasis on reasonable disagreement con-
nects to work on pluralistic alignment and anno-
tation disagreement. Sorensen et al. (Sorensen
et al., 2024b) formalize Overton, steerable, and
distributional modes of pluralistic alignment, ar-
guing that a single majority response is often
inadequate when multiple defensible values are
in tension (see also Sorensen et al., 2024a). A
parallel NLP literature argues that annotator dis-
agreement carries signal rather than noise and
should be modeled rather than aggregated away
(Plank, 2022; Röttger et al., 2022). Whereas this
work largely studies disagreement as a property
of labels, RULES asks whether models can predict
when disagreement will arise—distinguishing
genuine normative contestation from cases with
a clear answer.
2. RULES Dataset
The RULES dataset is composed of 300 photos of
street signs captured in New York City in 2024.
Each image contains a sign that was posted in
a public space, e.g. a sidewalk, or semi-public
space, e.g. a store, and some of the immediate
surroundings. Each image is also accompanied
by a text-based description written by this paper’s
authors of a hypothetical scenario that might take
place in the presence of the sign, see Figure 1. The
scenario ends by posing a question, which asks if
the proposed action is acceptable. 3-4 scenarios
were constructed for each image, yielding a 937
total image/scenario pairs. (For details on dataset
creation, see Appendix 6).

2.1. Author Annotations
Each image/scenario pair was classified by the
authors into theoretically-motivated categories:
sign authority, conformity to directive, putative
rule-breaking justification, and risk level. The
most significant annotation categories are de-
scribed here, but see §6 for further details and
worked examples in Fig 1.
Conformity
This annotation indicates whether
the user’s proposed action complies with, does
not comply with, or is it neutral with respect to
the directive in the sign. For the examples in
Figure 1, the user proposes to not comply with
the directive.

Justification
In cases of non-compliance with
the sign’s directive, we check for and classify pu-
tative justifications for non-compliance present in
the case (e.g., the presence of an individualized
waiver to the rule, compliance with the spirit-
but-not-letter of the rule, other moral reasons
overriding the need to comply, and undue cost of
compliance).
2.2. Human Participant Annotations
Participants were recruited to annotate the cases
for ground-truth social acceptability. Participants
responded to each scenario by indicating whether
the proposed action was acceptable or unaccept-
able. This provides a gold standard label because
the nature of social acceptability is that if people
judge an action is socially acceptable, it neces-
sarily is (as long as the people making the judg-
ment are representative of the social group in
question) (Bicchieri, 2006; Cialdini et al., 1990;
Hart, 1961; Searle, 1995). Each case was as-
signed a ground-truth label based on the pro-
portion of participants judging the action accept-
able: Acceptable (> 66.7%), Disagreement
(33.3%–66.7%), or Unacceptable (< 33.3%).
This categorization strategy led to 276 cases
of disagreement (29.5%), 288 acceptable cases
(30.7%), and 373 unacceptable cases (39.8%).
Appendix 10.3 explains the reasoning behind this
categorization strategy; in §3.2, we explore a
range of alternative methods for converting par-
ticipant responses into ground truth labels and
discuss the implications for our findings.
2.3. Experimental Procedure
Models tested on the RULES dataset were
prompted to answer whether the proposed ac-
tion was acceptable, unacceptable, or whether
people could reasonably disagree about this (see
Appendix 7 for exact text). A range of prompt
wordings were piloted, and no notable differences
emerged in model accuracy (see Appendix 8.3
for details).

This successful performance implies an impres-
sive facility with a range of normative capacities
that are necessary for success on the benchmark:
models have seemingly 1) identified the presence
of a sign with a relevant directive in the visual
environment, 2) compared the directive to the

knowledge of norms to determine social accept-
ability of the action. The latter step itself involves
determining whether the user’s course of action
complies or doesn’t comply with the directive,
and then determining the normative authority
and jurisdiction of the rule, identifying potential
rule-breaking justifications offered by the user, de-
termining if the putative justification is legitimate,
and integrating all this to make a final judgment
about social acceptability.

Stimulus Ablations Produce Predicted Perfor-
mance Drop
We compared ablations of the de-
fault image/scenario pairs to check whether our
dataset was truly testing model ability to inte-
grate the directive of a sign with the user’s pro-
posed action to make a social acceptability judg-
ment. These included: text only (replacing the
image with a natural language description of the
sign and the surroundings), text only / no sur-
roundings (replacing the image with only the
text of the sign without further description of
the surroundings), text only / no surroundings
/ no sign (removing the content of the image
entirely; scenario description only is included).
Critically, when the text of the sign is not pro-
vided in the input, there is a significant drop
in performance across all models. McNemar’s
tests compare the version where the sign is re-
moved against all other versions, for each model
(all 𝑝s < .001; Holm–Bonferroni corrected). The
average within-model accuracy drop was approx-
imately 14 percentage points (13.9 percentage
points from text-only to no-sign; 12.9 percentage
points from default to no-sign), confirming that
access to the sign’s text is critical to model perfor-
mance on this evaluation. This importantly indi-
cates that the scenario descriptions and questions
are not giving the answer away or are answerable
independently of knowing the rule that is being
communicated by the sign. (See §10.2 for further
details.)
3.2. Robustness Tests
Prompt Robustness
To determine whether our
results were contingent on specific prompting de-
cisions, we tested a selection of models on a range
of prompt perturbations and no notable differ-
ences in accuracy were found. See §10.1 and
§10.8 for details.
Determining Thresholds for Ground Truth La-
bels
While human agreement exists on a con-
tinuous spectrum, downstream AI systems ulti-
mately require distinct categorical labels because
an AI agent must make a discrete operational
decision: whether to act, refrain, or seek clar-
ification. Binning into labels is thus necessary,
but arbitrary. Here, we explore whether arbitrary
threshold setting depresses model accuracy and
whether tuning these thresholds can remedy it.
In Appendix 10.3, we discuss our reasons for
determining the mapping between human re-
sponses (proportion of participants responding
that an action is acceptable) and ground truth la-
bels (acceptable, unacceptable, disagreement) as
we did. However, in this section, we explore how
accuracy of model performance would change if
the thresholds for assigning labels were optimized

for model success.
It is possible that a model may be responding to
our task in a highly reliable way, but simply being
more conservative or more liberal in a way that
is systematic, detectable, and even justified with
respect to the underlying distribution of human
participant responses. Put another way, perhaps
a model is (functionally) correctly predicting the
distribution of human responses to a scenario, but
simply applying the labels (acceptable, unaccept-
able, disagree) in a different way (i.e., according
to a different threshold). A model might reason-
ably label any case as unacceptable that it infers
will have less than 50% support, for example.
Or otherwise it might reasonably label cases as
disagreement if as few as 10% of participants
disagree with the modal response. These (and
other) possibilities would be internally coherent
ways of responding to our task, and reasonable
for various downstream applications as well.
To explore this possibility, we allowed the
threshold for assigning ground truth labels to
vary for each model, determined what the opti-
mal threshold would be for maximizing model
performance, and recomputed the accuracy score
across all 25 evaluated model configurations (see
§10.3 for details). Strikingly, models that improve
most when thresholds are optimized for perfor-
mance require the shrinking of the disagreement
category nearly out of existence. Put another
way, the way to improve model performance is to
change the task so that they only rarely have to
predict that a case should be labeled as disagree-
ment (see § 3.3 for further analysis of disagree-
ment cases). This suggests that the overall model
performance we report (and, in particular, model
failures on predicting the DISAGREEMENT label)
is not substantially skewed downward due to the
decision about how to map the human data to
ground truth labels.
3.3. Failures on Disagreement Cases
The fact that optimizing thresholds only boosts
accuracy if we virtually eliminate the ’Disagree-
ment’ bin highlights that model performance is
deeply tied to how LLMs handle normative ambi-
guity. In the following section, we examine these
disagreement cases directly to understand why
models struggle with contested rules and how
their errors are structured.
Models Struggle to Recognize Normative Am-
biguity
Recognizing when a morally charged
case would result in disagreement is a key feature
of normative competence — and as AI-powered
agents begin to navigate the human social world
autonomously, being able to predict disagreement
will be key to making decisions that respect the
plurality of values held by the wide variety of
people they interact with. Yet it is this capacity
where models struggle most. Fig 5 shows the

When Models Fail on Ambiguity, They Default
to Over-Prohibition
Moreover, errors in mak-
ing predictions about the disagreement cases are
not distributed uniformly. When models make
incorrect predictions about disagreement cases,
the majority of them predict that the action is
unacceptable.
We examined the distribution of errors on dis-
agreement cases — that is, when a model failed
to predict “disagreement,” whether it was more
likely to predict “unacceptable” than “acceptable.”
Per-model binomial tests (Holm–Bonferroni cor-
rected across all 24 models) confirmed that 17 of
24 models showed a significant bias toward pre-
dicting “unacceptable” over “acceptable” when
they erred on disagreement cases. This bias was
particularly pronounced among Gemini models
(65–86% of errors; 8 of 8 models significant),
GPT models (52–79%; 6 of 7 models significant,
with 63–79% among the 6 significant models),
and Open Source models (64%; 2 of 2 models
significant), while Claude models (38–57%; 0 of
4 significant) and two of the three Grok models
(34–54%; 1 of 3 significant overall, with Grok 4.5
at 74%) showed more balanced or even reversed
error distributions (see Fig 6. For examples of
errors, see Appendix 10.9.)
The fact that models tend towards prohibition
in disagreement cases reveals that they struggle
to infer overall when non-compliance with a rule
could be acceptable. The majority of the disagree-
ment cases (84%) are proposals to not comply
with the directive in the sign, indicating that most
of the errors detected here reflect model’s ten-
dency to say that non-compliance is unacceptable.

Models Struggle Similarly on Different Kinds
of Disagreement
While there are many reasons
people might disagree about the answer to a nor-
mative question (or why a single person might be
uncertain as to the answer), there are two broad
categories of disagreement widely acknowledged
by philosophers: normative and descriptive. Nor-
mative disagreement is that which depends on
a normative claim that is disputed or hard to
resolve (e.g. whether one value should trump
another in this particular case). For example,
people may disagree about whether or not it’s OK
for a non-customer to use a bathroom designated
for “customers only” in an emergency — even if
they agree about all the facts. In a case like this,
they disagree (roughly) about whether the rights
of the property owner take precedence over ac-
commodating the needs of someone in a difficult
situation.
Descriptive disagreement, on the other hand,
emerges when people disagree (or are uncertain
7

about) some salient unknown fact (e.g., the down-
stream consequences of some proposed action;
or additional contextual information that is not
available). For instance, people might disagree
about whether it is permissible to touch a wall
labeled “Do not touch, wet paint” because they
have different beliefs about whether the paint is
actually wet.
We used an LLM judge to classify each of
the disagreement cases into NORMATIVE or DE-
SCRIPTIVE. See Appendix 9.1 for further details
of LLM judge. Fig 23 reveals that some models
(such as Claude Sonnet and Opus) may be bet-
ter at identifying normative disagreement than
descriptive disagreement. However, most mod-
els struggle about equally across the categories,
meaning that identifying factual uncertainty that
leads to disagreement might be just as impor-
tant as being able to recognize when value con-
flicts cause disagreement. Fig 24 shows the break-
down.
3.4. Models Possess Latent Disagreement Sig-
nals
Are models responsive to the underlying distri-
bution in disagreement cases?
The preceding
analysis shows that models struggle when forced
to output a discrete “disagreement” label. How-
ever, it is possible that models possess latent sensi-
tivity to the distribution of human judgments, but
simply struggle to operationalize it through the
three-way categorical choice we posed to them
(e.g., see Fig 7.).
To investigate this question, we mapped each
model’s prediction to an ordinal numerical value
(unacceptable
=
0.0, disagreement
=
0.5,
acceptable = 1.0) and evaluated them against
the raw proportion of human participants judg-
ing each scenario acceptable.
We compute the Wasserstein distance between
a model’s trinary judgment and the raw propor-
tion of participants that judged the case accept-
able. We compute the mean absolute error (MAE)
between each model’s numeric prediction and
the human proportion of “acceptable” ratings
for each scenario. This metric penalizes errors
proportionally to their severity: predicting “ac-
ceptable” for a unanimously unacceptable sce-
nario (cost = 1.0) is penalized more heavily than
predicting “disagreement” (cost = 0.5), unlike
the main accuracy measure, which treats all er-
rors equally. All 24 models achieved MAE scores
significantly below the random baseline of 0.43
(lower is better; permutation tests, all 𝑝s < .001,
Holm–Bonferroni corrected), with a mean MAE of
0.25 (range: 0.19–0.31). Notably, MAE was con-
sistently lowest on unacceptable cases (mean =
0.17) and highest on disagreement cases (mean
= 0.38), further confirming that models’ pri-
mary source of error is their difficulty recogniz-
ing normative ambiguity. A theoretically perfect
trinary classifier — one that maps each human
proportion to the nearest of the three categories
— would achieve an MAE of 0.11, suggesting
meaningful room for improvement (see Fig 25
and §10.4 for further analysis.)

Fig. 5 shows that models are more accurate in la-

action proposed in the scenario would comply,
not comply, or be neutral with respect to the rule

shows that model performance falters for accept-

4. Discussion
The findings from the RULES benchmark reveal
an important gap in the normative competence
of current frontier models. While AI systems are
increasingly expected to assist humans in com-
plex, dynamic environments, our results indicate
that they struggle where human social interac-
tion is most nuanced: in identifying instances of
disagreement and recognizing permissible excep-
tions to strict rules. Instead of navigating the grey
areas of human normativity with flexibility, mod-
els exhibit a strong bias toward prohibition when
confronted with situations that elicit human dis-
agreement, and they fail to recognize cases where
humans overwhelmingly agree that rule-breaking
is socially acceptable. The failure of a wide range
of prompting scaffolding, role-playing personas,
and formatting interventions to mitigate these
limitations suggests that robust normative compe-
tence cannot be easily engineered through minor
inference-time adjustments, but rather demands
more fundamental advances in model training
and architecture.
Our findings suggest that current safety-
alignment paradigms create a fundamental ten-
sion in normative reasoning: By heavily penaliz-
ing Type I errors (false leniency) during safety
fine-tuning, models learn an asymmetric, risk-
averse policy. However, this over-indexing on
false-positive avoidance does not grant special
competence on true high-risk scenarios (§ 3.5),
nor can it be patched via inference-time scaf-
folding (§ 3.6). Instead, it yields brittle, over-
regulated AI agents that lack the pragmatic flex-
ibility required to navigate real-world human
norms.
The practical implications of these limitations
span several immediate and high-stakes use cases
for AI deployment. First, as autonomous agents
are increasingly tasked with navigating digital
worlds—such as managing online communities,
content moderation, or executing open-ended
workflows—an inability to handle rule ambigu-
ity risks producing overly rigid, frustrating, and
brittle user experiences. Second, this rigidity be-
comes a physical safety and operational concern
for robots navigating the human world. A hu-
manoid robot that treats every posted sign as an
absolute, context-independent command may fail
to act rationally in common edge cases. Third,
these limitations directly impact assistive tech-
nologies such as Google’s Project Astra and other
glasses-based platforms designed to provide real-
time visual assistance for the visually impaired. If
a wearable assistant misinterprets the local social
permissibility of a posted sign, it could offer overly
conservative or socially awkward guidance, alien-
ating the user from the naturally flexible norms
of their immediate community.
To build upon the baseline established by the
RULES dataset, several avenues for future work
are critical. A primary technical extension is to
transition from still images to dynamic videos.
Human social evaluation is inherently temporal;
signs are read in motion, and the proposed ac-
tions themselves unfold over time with critical
behavioral cues—such as speed, intent, and sub-
tle environmental contexts—that static images
cannot fully capture. Evaluating models on video
data will provide a more realistic test of their sit-
uational awareness and dynamic norm parsing.
Furthermore, future research must deeply in-
vestigate the underlying mechanisms of “reason-
able disagreement.” When humans disagree on
whether an action is acceptable, where does that
disagreement originate? One possibility is that
disagreements largely devolve into uncertainty
about the facts of the situation (e.g. what is likely
happening just outside the frame, or what would
happen if a particular action were executed). An-
other possibility is that even if all the facts were
agreed upon, disagreement would emerge from
fundamental disputes over what is valuable and
how to trade off competing values. Disentangling
these two components is essential to discover-
ing whether language models are systematically
better or worse at resolving factual ambiguity
versus balancing value trade-offs, which in turn
will inform more targeted alignment strategies.
Finally, we acknowledge several limitations in

6. Appendix: Dataset Construction
6.1. Overview
Each case in our dataset consists of an image con-
taining a sign and the sign’s immediate surround-
ings (which was photographed by our research
team in and around New York City in 2024). Pho-
tos were captured on an iPhone and unaltered
by AI and do not contain images of people or PII
information.
The image is also accompanied by a user query
proposing some course of action that might take
place in the presence of the sign. The sign and
the associated question are considered a “case”
or “scenario.” Each case also has associated tags
that describe its normative properties. Tags were
generated according to the four-step procedure
described below. Note that these labels were not
provided to the human participant nor to the AI
being assessed and were used only for dataset
creation and analysis.
6.2. Preliminary Considerations
Case Construction
All cases are constructed to satisfy the following
criteria. First, each case clearly proposes a course
of action and asks whether it is acceptable (e.g.,
“Is it ok for me to X?”). Cases do not invoke spe-
cific normative vocabulary beyond “acceptable”
or “ok”—for instance, they do not explicitly ask
whether something is legal. This constraint en-
sures precision and comparability across cases.
Second, cases are phrased in the first person, as
though a real user is interacting with the model.
(However, we later created textual variants of
each case, which take up different perspectives.
See §8.4 for details.) Third, the user’s text is con-
sistent with what is shown in the accompanying
image; where an apparent inconsistency exists, it
is explained in the query (e.g., “I took this photo
earlier”). Fourth, every image contains a sign.
Fifth, for our purposes, a sign is interpreted as
providing a directive as long as it recommends
for or against some course of action; not all signs
contain or imply directives.
6.3. Tagging Procedure
Step 1: Normative Authority of the Sign
The first step is to classify the normative authority
of the sign—that is, the grounds on which one
might have reason to comply with the directive
it issues. Where a directive sits on the “ladder
of normative authority” is roughly proportional
to the level of sanction to which non-compliance
might expose the user. This step is based solely on
the image. The categories, ordered from strongest
to weakest authority, are as follows.
Law. The directive is grounded in the law and
backed by legal sanction; non-compliance
could lead to punishment at law, such as a
fine or points on one’s license.
Private Property. The directive is grounded in
private ownership rights. Non-compliance
could expose the user to exclusion from
the owner’s property—for example, a store
owner may ask the user to leave, and refusal
would constitute trespass.
Moral Norm. The directive is grounded in a
legally unenforceable moral norm. The user
is being asked to do something for everyone’s
sake; if the user does not comply, and the
sign’s directive is reasonable, third parties
would be justified in morally criticizing the
user.
Request. The directive is grounded in an appeal
from a particular second party. The user is
being asked to do something for the sake of
the person or people who placed the sign. If
the user does not comply, and the request is
reasonable, the person who placed the sign
would be justified in morally criticizing the
user, but would have limited recourse beyond
reproach.
Advice. The directive is grounded in the user’s
own self-interest and functions more as a
recommendation. If the user does not com-
ply, we might criticize them for being foolish,
but they would not have acted in a socially
unacceptable way.

Information. The sign is purely informational
and cannot reasonably be interpreted as issu-
ing a directive. Because there is no directive,
there is nothing to comply or not comply
with. This category always leads to the no-
directive tag in Step 3.
Step 2: Social Acceptability
The second step is to assess the social accept-
ability of the user’s proposed action, considering
the image, scenario, and user’s question. Social
acceptability is the ultimate standard for evalu-
ating normative competence. Ground truth for
social acceptability is ultimately established by
querying the relevant population—which is taken
up by our experiment with human participants,
see §7. However, in this stage, the researchers
tagged the cases as an initial starting point for
case construction. Cases are tagged with one of
three labels.
Acceptable. The user’s proposed course of action
is socially acceptable.
Unacceptable. The user’s proposed course of ac-
tion is socially unacceptable.
Uncertain. It is uncertain whether the user’s pro-
posed course of action is socially acceptable.
This tag is applied when there are good ar-
guments on both sides or when critical infor-
mation is missing. (This category becomes
“Disagreement” once experimental data is
collected.)
Step 3: Conformity to the Directive
The third step assesses the relationship between
the user’s proposed action and the sign’s directive.
The user’s action may comply with, fail to comply
with, or be neutral with respect to the directive.
Comply
The proposed action complies with the
sign’s directive. Cases of compliance are further
tagged with one of two sub-tags.
Control. A standard case where the norm clearly
applies and compliance is acceptable. These
straightforward cases are tagged accept-
able, comply, control.
Note, how-
ever, that compliance can occasionally be
unacceptable—for example, if obeying a “no
jaywalking” sign would prevent the user from
rescuing a child in immediate danger across
the street at very little personal cost.
Misapplication. The directive does not actually
apply to the user, even though their proposed
action is consistent with it. For example, a
sign reading “Employees must wash hands”
does not apply to a non-employee. If such
a user asks whether they should wash their
hands, their action complies with the direc-
tive and is acceptable, but the directive is
misapplied.
Not-Comply
The proposed action does not com-
ply with the sign’s directive. In these cases, we
check for putative justifications—that is, we are
interested in both successful and attempted-but-
unsuccessful justifications for non-compliance.
Where multiple justifications apply, the one closer
to the bottom of the following list describes the
“better” justification and should be selected.
1. Control. A standard case of non-compliance
with no justification offered or inferable. The
user proposes not to comply and gives no
excuse. These are clear violations.
2. Misapplication. The directive does not apply
to the user in this situation. For example, a
restaurant employee visiting on their day off
sees a sign saying “Employees must wash
hands.” If they ask whether it is acceptable
not to wash their hands, the rule does not
apply because they are off duty, making non-
compliance acceptable.
3. Permission. The directive does not require
any particular course of action, nor does it
involve any meaningful sanction. It is there-
fore up to the user whether to adhere to it.
This justification is nearly always associated
with advice-authority signs, since ignoring
advice may be irrational but is not socially un-
acceptable. (Permission can be understood
as a special case of waiver in which the per-
son with the power to waive compliance is
17

the user themselves.)
4. Waiver. Someone with the power to waive
compliance has putatively done so. For ex-
ample, a sign may prohibit outside food or
drink, but the user claims the manager has
given explicit permission. This appeal can be
either successful or unsuccessful.
5. Interpretation. The proposed action is pu-
tatively consistent with the spirit, if not the
letter, of the norm. The user’s action might
seem facially non-compliant, but is in fact
consistent with the underlying purpose of
the directive. For example, a sign prohibiting
“outside food” was not intended to preclude
breastfeeding. Conversely, pedantic attempts
to reinterpret a rule that clearly applies are
unsuccessful appeals to interpretation.
6. Override. Other moral reasons putatively
override the directive.
The user’s action
is justified because some competing moral
consideration—often involving the welfare
of others—outweighs the reason to conform.
For example, disregarding a sign in order to
help someone in danger. An unsuccessful
appeal to override occurs when the reason
cited is not weighty enough to justify non-
compliance.
7. Cost. Compliance would putatively cause
a disproportionate personal cost to the user.
The user’s non-compliance is justified (or per-
haps excused) because adhering to the direc-
tive would impose an unreasonable burden.
For example, failing to clean up after one’s
dog because doing so would cause the user to
miss a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity may be
a successful appeal to cost, whereas simply
disliking the task would not be.
Neutral
The proposed action is orthogonal to
the sign’s directive—it neither complies with nor
violates it. Neutral cases are further tagged with
one of two sub-tags.
Misapplication. There is a directive, but it does
not apply to the user or their situation, and
the proposed action is orthogonal to it. For
instance, an off-duty employee sees a sign
reading “Employees must wash hands” and
asks whether it is acceptable to brush their
teeth. The directive does not apply (because
they are off duty), and the proposed action
(brushing teeth) is unrelated to the directive
(washing hands). Note that the proposed
action in a neutral-misapplication case can
still be independently unacceptable.
No-Directive. The sign does not contain or imply
any directive, so there is nothing to com-
ply or not comply with. These cases are
nearly always associated with informa-
tion-authority signs. The user’s proposed
action can still be acceptable or unacceptable
on its own merits.
Step 4: Source of Uncertainty
If a case was tagged uncertain in Step 2, a
final tag is applied to identify the source of the
uncertainty.
Descriptive Uncertainty. The evaluation of the
user’s proposed course of action depends on
some salient unknown fact. The case can-
not be resolved without additional empirical
information.
Normative Uncertainty. The case depends on a
normative claim that is disputed or hard to
resolve, and about which there is reasonable
disagreement.
Step 5: Risk Level
This tag captures the real-world impact of not
complying with the sign’s directive, as proposed
by the user in the case. Cases were tagged as
“high risk” (21.0%) if the proposed action posed
a risk of significant property damage, a signif-
icant chance of moderate to severe bodily or
psychological harm (including to animals), in-
volved general safety precautions (e.g., emer-
gency exits, instructions about weapons, instruc-
tions about masks, construction zones, parking/-
driving safety), other sorts of universally rec-
ognized emergencies (e.g., EMTs called to a
scene, presence of fire), or included any safety-
relevant sign (e.g.: a action that proposed going
through an emergency exit when no emergency
was present.)

8.4. Details of Variant Manipulations
In addition to our default prompting strategy,
we attempted a range of methods to scaffold
the model’s performance on the RULES dataset,
attempting to boost accuracy with lightweight,
inference-time interventions.
These included: (1) instructional variants,
which augmented the baseline system instruc-
tions with specific guidelines regarding moral
reasoning (“moral instructions”, “morebench in-
structions”); (2) persona and role-playing vari-
ants, which modified the initial system prompt
to evaluate whether framing the task through a
specific lens shifted the model’s judgment profile;
(3) answer format variants, which adjusted the
structural output demands on the model. This in-
cluded a “justification” condition requiring the
model to generate a paragraph-long rationale
prior to its final answer, and a “distribution” con-
dition, which reframed the categorical prediction
into a continuous probabilistic estimation task by
asking the model to predict the percentage of 100
regional residents who would deem the action ac-
ceptable; (4) scenario framing variants, which
altered the contextual norms or cultural back-
ground assumed by the model. These included
a geographic anchor instructing the model to an-
swer as a resident of the tri-state area (“tristate”),
a semantic shift focusing on holistic judgment
(“all things considered”), and an explicit norma-
tive nudge stating that “rule-breaking is often

text (like foot traffic, staff proximity, or ambient
safety conditions) forcing models to rely heavily
on the text scenario. Because real-world norma-
tive assessments are dynamic and unfold over
time, future work should expand to multi-view
imagery or video streams to test whether richer
visual context resolves these underlying ambigui-
ties.

10.3. Computing Ground Truth
As discussed in §2.2, to establish ground truth
labels for social acceptability, we collected ac-
ceptability judgments from human participants
for each case (for experimental details, see §7).
The number of participants varied across cases
(typically around 30), so we adopted percentage-
based thresholds rather than fixed count cutoffs.
We used a 33%/66% threshold to partition the
space of acceptability judgments into three cate-
gories. If greater than 66.7% (> 2/3) of partici-
pants judged the proposed action acceptable, the
ground truth label was set to acceptable. If
fewer than 33.3% (< 1/3) of participants judged
it acceptable, the ground truth label was set to
unacceptable. If between 33.3% and 66.7%
(1/3–2/3) of participants judged it acceptable,
the case was labeled as disagreement.
We considered three theoretically-motivated
alternative approaches to setting these thresholds
before arriving at the 33%/66% partition.
10.3.1. Optimizing for overlap with expert an-
notations
One natural approach would be to select thresh-
olds that maximize agreement between the
crowd-sourced ground truth labels and the la-
bels assigned by our expert annotators (see §6,
“Step 2: Social Acceptability”). In principle, this
29

has appeal as a way of splitting the difference be-
tween expert judgment and population-level opin-
ion. In practice, however, this approach yielded
an unacceptably wide range for the accept-
able category, effectively treating cases as ac-
ceptable even when they attracted substantial
disagreement among participants. This result un-
derscored that expert annotators, however care-
ful, are themselves drawn from a particular de-
mographic distribution and should not be treated
as a gold standard for social acceptability judg-
ments. We therefore opted not to calibrate our
thresholds to expert agreement.
10.3.2. Generous disagreement threshold
A second approach would be to set a low thresh-
old for the disagreement category (e.g., ap-
proximately 17%, or around 5 participants out
of a typical 30), thereby expanding the range of
cases classified as contested. There is a principled
motivation for this: including the voice of the mi-
nority is important, and a generous disagreement
band ensures that cases with even modest dissent
are flagged rather than collapsed into a majority-
wins label. However, given what we observed
about model performance (i.e. the disagree-
ment label is hardest for them to predict), such a
generous threshold would classify a large propor-
tion of cases as disagreement, substantially de-
flating model accuracy scores in a way that would
obscure meaningful performance differences. We
judged this to be uncharitable to the models and
uninformative for evaluation purposes.
10.3.3. Optimizing for model performance
A third approach would be to select thresh-
olds that optimize model performance on our
evaluation—for example, by choosing boundaries
that maximize a given model’s accuracy on ac-
ceptability judgments. This option we explored
extensively (see §3.2 in the main paper and fur-
ther analysis below).
We allowed the threshold for assigning ground
truth labels to vary for each model, determined
what the optimal threshold would be for maxi-
mizing model performance, and recomputed the
accuracy score across all 25 evaluated model con-
figurations. Fig 20 (left panel) shows the im-
provement in model accuracy when thresholds
are optimized for an individual model’s perfor-
mance, sorted by amount of improvement. Fig 20
(right panel) depicts the thresholds that were
selected. The average accuracy gain is approxi-
mately 10.3%, though one model (Gemini 3.1
Flash Lite) improves by nearly 25% (24.8%)!
Strikingly, models that improve most when thresh-
olds are optimized for their performance require
the shrinking of the disagreement category nearly
out of existence. Put another way, the way to im-
prove model performance is to change the task so
that they only rarely have to predict that a case
should be labeled as disagreement (see § 3.3 for
further analysis of disagreement cases). If we re-
strict the possibility of shrinking the disagreement
category (for instance, enforcing that it must span
at least 15% of the distribution), performance im-
provements decrease (see Fig. 21, with a mean
performance increase of 7.9%). This suggests
that the overall model performance we report is
not substantially skewed downward due to the
decision about how to map the human data to
ground truth labels.
Second, we repeated this exercise but searched
for the one threshold that would optimize per-
formance across all models.
The new thresh-
old again minimized the disagreement category
(lower bound = 61.7%, upper bound = 62.3%;
see Fig 22 - left panel), and resulted in an aver-
age accuracy gain of only 7.9%. When ensuring
that disagreement spanned at least 15 percent-
age points, a 5.5% increase in accuracy remained
(ideal threshold: lower bound = 56.5%, upper
bound = 71.7%; Fig 22 - right panel). Again, this
suggests that no major accuracy gains would be
seen by manipulating the label mapping.
In conclusion, we found that setting thresholds
by optimizing for model performance minimized
the disagreement category, effectively com-
pressing the space of contested cases to a nar-
row band. This occurred because the models per-
formed poorly on disagreement cases, so any
optimization procedure that rewarded model ac-
curacy naturally shrank the category that models
found most difficult. We ultimately rejected this
approach because it would tailor the ground truth

10.5. Understanding Model Over-compliance
10.6. Investigating model justification detec-
tion
In §3.5.1, we discuss the fact that models de-
tecting justifications for rule-breaking sometimes
helps them make the correct judgment. Here we
describe further details of the analysis presented
in the main paper.
To investigate the source of model over-
compliance we investigated the explicit reason-
ing of a subset of models that either provided
reasoning traces (or summaries of traces) or pro-
vided explicit reasoning as part of their answer.
Only models that provided reasoning longer than
20 words on at least 100 cases were included
in this analysis (12 models total: Claude Opus
4.6, Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5 Mini, Gemini 2.5
Flash, Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite, Gemini 2.5 Flash
Lite (Think), Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 3.1 Flash
Lite, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 3.5 Flash, Grok 4.1
Fast, Grok 4.1 Fast (Reason).)

the target model had identified the putative jus-
