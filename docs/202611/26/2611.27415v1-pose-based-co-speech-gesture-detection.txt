Abstract — Co-speech gesture detection identifies the spans in which a speaker gestures (e.g., beat or semantic gestures) in video. As a first step in gesture annotation, it is crucial for studying human communicative behaviour. Despite the availability of automatic tools and models, co-speech gesture detection remains underexplored, and its current state of the art is unclear: existing methods have been evaluated using different datasets, experimental setups, and metrics, making their performance difficult to compare. To address this gap, we benchmark existing detectors using the same cross-validation folds across three datasets (MULTISIMO, GESRes, and CABB-S), with evaluation at the frame, gesture-span, and window levels. Our results show that previously reported performance does not consistently transfer to this common evaluation setting. Performance varies substantially across datasets, particularly with gesture frequency, indicating that the task remains far from solved. We further propose a CTR-GCN + BiLSTM model that outperforms the previous best method, MPSL, on CABB-S. Our results provide a more consistent basis for comparing existing approaches and highlight key challenges for future research on co-speech gesture detection. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Co-speech gestures are an essential means of communica-tion: performed alongside speech, hand and arm movements emphasise what is said or convey meaning that complements it [21], [34], [11]. How people produce and interpret gestures with speech is still largely an open question, and answering it at scale requires large annotated corpora. Annotating gestures in video, however, is labour-intensive, typically taking a human annotator several hours per hour of video. The first step in gesture annotation is to identify gesture spans in continuous video [27]. Several pose-based detectors have reported promising results, including a gesture recall of .87 for SPUDNIG [43] and frame recalls of up to .98 for LightGBM [17] (Table I). However, a more recent LightGBM-based tool reported a recall of .69 [41]. Since these methods were evaluated on different data, often in-house, with different objectives and metrics, their results are not directly comparable, leaving the state of the art unclear and the practical reliability of existing detectors uncertain. In this paper, we address this gap with the following contributions. (1) We build a framework that evaluates existing gesture detection methods on the same data and with the same objectives: rule-based, pre-trained and trained methods are run on the same cross-validation folds of three datasets, MULTISIMO, GESRes and CABB-S, and scored per frame, per gesture span and per window. (2) We show that the early reported success could not be replicated, because performance varies strongly across datasets, above all with how frequent gestures are. (3) We show that models trained on the target data remain the most reliable, while cross-domain training and co-training with silver labels help only as far as the training data matches the test data. (4) We propose a CTR-GCN + BiLSTM model that outperforms the previous best method, MPSL [10], on CABB-S. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Co-speech gestures 

Co-speech gestures are an integral part of human com-munication and convey information that complements and, in some cases, extends spoken language [34], [23]. For example, beat gestures can emphasize or highlight parts of an utterance, while deictic gestures direct the listener’s attention toward specific locations or objects [22]. In contrast to con-ventionalized emblems , such as the OK sign, which can carry relatively stable meanings independently of speech [21], co-speech gestures are typically interpreted in conjunction with their accompanying speech and communicative context [24], [23]. For instance, the metaphoric gesture of presenting one palm and then the other can convey the contrast expressed by “on the one hand, on the other hand” only in combination with the corresponding speech. Compared with conventionalized gestures, co-speech ges-tures are more idiosyncratic and variable in form, reflecting differences across speakers and communicative contexts [33], [23]. The relationship between gesture and speech is not necessarily one-to-one: gestures may convey information that is not explicitly expressed in speech or may provide com-plementary information [36], [35]. For example, a shoulder shrug and open hands accompanied by an utterance of “sure” suggest the speaker is actually unsure. Despite the importance of co-speech gestures, relatively few studies in computer vision have addressed their interpre-tation (see, e.g. [14], [51], [39]). Understanding how humans produce and interpret gestures, on the other hand, remains an active area of research in the study of human communica-tion and behavior (e.g. [48], [16]). Such studies commonly rely on manual annotation of gesture recordings [27], [3], [2]. A fundamental step in this process is identifying the temporal boundaries of gesture units in continuous motion streams. Each identified span can then be annotated with its gesture type (e.g., beat , iconic , or deictic ), kinematic properties (e.g. hand position, handshape, and trajectory), phases (e.g., preparation, stroke, hold, and retraction), and communicative function or meaning. Accurate gesture-unit detection is therefore essential for subsequent annotation and analysis. arXiv:2611.27415v1 [cs.CV] 20 Nov 2026 B. Co-speech gesture detection 

Manual annotation of co-speech gestures is time-consuming, limiting the scalability of gesture analysis to larger datasets. Nonetheless, automatic gesture annotation remains largely unexplored 1 and only a limited number of studies have investigated the automatic detection of co-speech gesture units. At first glance, co-speech gesture detection may appear straightforward as it only involves a binary classification be-tween gesturing and non-gesturing periods. Early approaches reported encouraging results. For example, SPUDNIG [43] is a heuristic-based method that detects movements relative to estimated rest positions. A recall-based agreement against manual annotation of up to 0.92 was reported, although preci-sion was low [43], [18]. In addition, [17] trained a LightGBM classifier [20] on body keypoint features, achieving a macro-F1 score of up to 0.98. In an active learning setting, the authors reported that an F1 score of at least 0.85 could be achieved by annotating 27% of the data. In contrast, recent models reported more moderate perfor-mance. [41] introduced a gesture-detection tool, Envision-HGdetector (ENVISION), that is trained on multiple gesture-annotated corpora using LightGBM and CNN models. Their reported F1 scores ranged from 0.52 to 0.76 on the com-bined evaluation set. [10] formulated gesture detection as a multi-phase sequence-labeling (MPSL) task, combining a Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN) specialized for sign language recognition [49], [19] with a transformer. In subsequent work, they investigated strategies for integrating skeletal features with speech representations extracted from mel-spectrograms [9]. Both methods achieved a similar window-level F1 score of about 0.70. Table I summarizes major studies on co-speech gesture detection in the literature. Overall, these studies establish the feasibility of learning-based gesture detection. However, their task objectives, datasets, and evaluation protocols largely differ. A systematic benchmark is necessary to enable a fair comparison of existing approaches and to assess their respective strengths and limitations. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

The objective of this study is to assess the current state of the art in automatic co-speech gesture detection. To this end, we systematically evaluate existing open-source methods under a common experimental setup, using multiple datasets and consistent evaluation protocols. Specifically, we compare four existing approaches: the rule-based SPUDNIG algorithm [43], the trainable LightGBM model proposed by [17], the pretrained ENVISION framework [41], and the MPSL framework proposed by [10]. Furthermore, we introduce a new model based on the Channel-wise Topology Refinement Graph Convolutional Network (CTR-GCN) [6] to investigate whether techniques that have proved effective

A. Data 

Among the limited datasets with manual gesture annota-tions, we selected three for the current study: MULTISIMO [28], GESRes [15] and CABB-S [42]. The MULTISIMO cor-pus contains 23 videos (approximately 4 hours) of discussion on questions in a similar format as the game Family Feud . We use videos of both the participants and the moderators, who are recorded individually in a seated position. The spans and types of gesture units were fully annotated by Rater 1 and partially annotated by Rater 2 . We only use the annotations by Rater 1. GESRes contains 12 videos by politicians, clinicians, and educators – three speakers each. Each video was annotated from the beginning but only partially, covering approximately 3.5 of the 7 hours and resulting in 2373 coded gesture spans.The videos originate from existing recordings captured from a front camera angle. The MULTISIMO and GESRes datasets depend little on the visual context. For evaluation on general co-speech ges-ture detection, we prioritize them over interactions that rely heavily on task-specific environments, such as ECOLANG [13], which involves manual manipulation with physical objects. However, we also experiment with CABB-S, asmaller-scale variant of the CABB corpus [7]. It contains 19 dyads (approximately 5 hours) of task-oriented interactions in Dutch, in which one participant instructs the other to identify complex 3D objects. Participants are recorded using front-facing cameras while standing. This is the only dataset that allows for the implementation of the multi-phase se-quence labelling model [10] and our proposed variant, which require fine-grained annotation of gesture strokes. The original videos of all three datasets are openly avail-able (unlike e.g. SaGA [32]), allowing for different mark-erless tracking techniques and facilitating reproducibility. The recording conditions are rather stable, compared with the frequent camera changes or cuts in other datasets such as TEDM3D [45] and the EVA corpus [37]), which may interfere with pose tracking.

X = ( x1, x2, . . . , xN )

Y = ( y1, y 2, . . . , y N ), yn ∈ { 0, 1}

where yn = 1 indicates that frame n belongs to an annotated gesture unit, and yn = 0 denotes a non-gesture frame. All compared methods predict per sliding window, assign each prediction to a frame, and smooth the resulting frame labels into gesture spans with tool-specific minimum durations and gap thresholds (Table I). The spans are con-verted back to frame labels on the normalised 29.97 fps grid for evaluation.

C. Proposed method 

Our proposed method targets the per-window classification objective and builds on the MPSL model [10], which embeds each skeletal time window with an ST-GCN, contextualises the window sequence with a Transformer encoder, and decodes labels with a linear-chain CRF. Our model keeps the general architecture, but replaces the ST-GCN and CRF components, as shown in Figure 1. Instead of ST-GCN, which convolves over a single fixed skeleton graph, we use CTR-GCN [6], which refines the joint topology separately per channel. It contains a Multi-Scale Temporal Convolutional Network (MS-TCN) [31] that captures both fine-grained and long-range patterns: 

E = CTR-GCN (W)

In addition, the Transformer outputs C are not decoded with a CRF. Following [4], they are fed to a BiLSTM [12]

In place of the CRF objective ( LCRF ) [8], we minimize the cross-entropy loss ( LCE ) plus a temporal-consistency loss (LTC ) (weight λ = 0.02). Adapted from prior temporal con-sistency frameworks [30], [47], this loss penalizes prediction divergence between consecutive windows sharing the same ground-truth label while enforcing a sharp probability shift at class boundaries. IV. E XPERIMENT 

A. Model setting 

The compared models differ in how, and on which data, they are trained. SPUDNIG [43] is rule-based and requires no training. ENVISION [41] is used off the shelf with both of its released backbones, a CNN and a LightGBM classifier; neither is retrained here. LightGBM [17] is trained and evaluated separately on each dataset by cross-validation. We use the default parameter setting of each tool (see Table I). The MPSL model is re-trained using the implementation released with [10]. Similar to the MPSL model, our proposed model is also trained with the multi-phase objective, and then mapped to binary gesture labels per window. Training follows the setup of [10]: SGD [44] with Nes-terov momentum of 0.9, a batch size of 64, an initial learning rate of 0.1 and a weight decay of 10 −4, for a fixed number of 80 epochs with a 20-epoch linear warm-up and a 10-fold learning-rate decay at epoch 50. Due to architectural difference, our model is not initialized on the sign language GCN [19], but trained from scratch with a batch size of 16. On top of training on the same corpus, we also tried a cross-domain setting: we train LightGBM (MediaPipe features) on one dataset and test it on the other, i.e. train on all of GESRes and test on MULTISIMO, and vice versa. Furthermore, we use the cross-domain model to label the training-fold videos of the test dataset; these silver labels, together with the out-of-domain data, are used to train a 

co-training model, e.g. trained on GESRes + silver MUL-TISIMO and tested on MULTISIMO. These setups test how well a trained detector transfers to new data. 

B. Data preprocessing 

All videos are resampled to 29.97 fps; lower-frame-rate recordings (23.97 fps in GESRes) are upsampled by frame interpolation. Each model uses the pose estimator it was originally developed with, which has been found to outper-form the alternatives [18]: OpenPose [5] (25 keypoints from BODY 25) for SPUDNIG and LightGBM; MediaPipe [50] (543 body, hand and face landmarks) for ENVISION, and additionally for LightGBM as a comparison; and MMPose [40] (133 keypoints) for the MPSL model and ours. Each model requires a different subset of the tracked keypoints (see original papers for details). We remove corrupt samples and tracking artifacts, and filter frames in which static background equipment is detected as a person. Seven MUL-TISIMO sessions are excluded because of tracking failures and occlusions. 3

CABB-S provides per-window binary labels derived from stroke annotations. MULTISIMO and GESRes annotate each gesture-unit span with a category labels. We treat spans la-belled symbolic , beat , iconic , deictic , metaphoric or emblem 

as gestures. All other spans, namely N/A (MULTISIMO), 

adaptor (GESRes), non-gesture (CABB-S) and unannotated spans, are treated as non-gestures. We construct speaker-disjoint cross-validation splits for MULTISIMO (4 folds) and GESRes (3 folds). For CABB-S, the MPSL models use the preprocessed multi-phase data and five folds of [10], which are also split by speakers. LightGBM is trained on the original per-frame annotations with the same folds. SPUDNIG and ENVISION are run on each complete dataset and each sample appears in exactly one test fold. C. Evaluation 

On MULTISIMO and GESRes, we evaluate on the per-frame objective. The MPSL model and our proposed model are excluded, as they require training on stroke annotations. These two datasets are also part of ENVISION’s training data, and since its train-test split is not publicly available, our test folds may overlap with its training data. Its scores on these two datasets may therefore be inflated and should be interpreted with caution. LightGBM, in contrast, is always evaluated on folds held out from its training data. We additionally evaluate span-level performance by merg-ing consecutive frame predictions into gesture and non-gesture spans. Following [43], a predicted span matches a ground-truth span if their temporal overlap is at least 60%. Span-level evaluation captures the detection of individual gestures, regardless of duration. This evaluation better aligns with the objective of manual annotation, as short gesture spans are no less relevant than longer ones. We report precision, recall, and F1-score for the gesture class at the corresponding prediction level. On CABB-S, all models are evaluated on the per-window objective: frame-level predic-tions are aggregated with the any-frame rule. V. R ESULTS

Both SPUDNIG and ENVISION label most windows as gesture, resulting in a high recall (82–97%) and a very low precision (14–23%; Figure 2). ENVISION’s reported window F1 of .76 (LGBM) and .52 (CNN) [41] is thus not reached on CABB-S. LightGBM, which tends to under-detect gestures on the previous two datasets, misses most gesture windows on CABB-S, resulting in a higher precision but a low recall. The two multi-phase models are clearly better than both groups. MPSL [10] reaches .617 window F1 with the highest accuracy (.928) 5, and our model (CTR-GCN + BiLSTM) reaches the highest window F1, .666 ± .03, with an accuracy of .918. In particular, our model recovers more gold gesture windows than MPSL (78% vs. 56% recall).

VI. A NALYSIS 

Across all models and datasets, the best gesture F1 does not reach 0.7. Contrary to the optimistic scores reported in prior work (Table I), co-speech gesture detection is therefore far from solved. In this section, we examine typical errors. Figure 3 shows two frames from GESRes that all com-pared models detect as gestures, although they are not annotated as such (false positives; Table IV). On the left, the speaker is not gesturing, but because she is holding a mi-crophone, her pose resembles a gesture in the pose-tracking data. On the right, the speaker holds his hands in mid-air at the end position of his previous gesture. Depending on the annotation guidelines, and on how consistently annotators follow them, such a hold may or may not be annotated as part of the preceding gesture; here it was not, yet all models detect it as a gesture. Conversely, Figure 4 shows two CABB-S participants gesturing subtly with their hands while their arms are held low in front of the body, a typical rest position. Most models miss these spans; only our CTR-GCN-based model detects them. All in all, these examples illustrate the ambiguity and challenge of the task. Another likely source of error are adaptors, such as adjusting clothing or a microphone. Figure 5 shows the proportion of gesture, adaptor and other non-gesture frames that each model labels as gesture. The models follow a similar pattern: they label more adaptor frames as gestures on GESRes than on MULTISIMO, largely in line with the overall rate at which they predict gestures on each dataset. The exception is ENVISION-CNN, which is the only method that labels adaptors as gesture hardly more often than other non-gesture frames It is the only method that uses adaptor annotations during training, by learning a separate class for non-gesture movement. This suggests that adaptors are distinguishable from gestures given suitable training labels, and that future work should make use of adaptor annotations where available.

Identifying gesturing spans appears to be a simple binary task, yet even models trained specifically on the target data reach only moderate F1. The earlier optimism may therefore come partly from evaluation practice rather than from the methods themselves, since each was tested on its own data, with its own unit of evaluation and its own gesture ratio. This matters for how the tools are meant to be used. They were not proposed to replace human annotation but to speed it up, by pointing annotators to candidate gestures. Yet where gestures are rare, most of the candidates they return are not gestures (SPUDNIG: a span precision of 28% on MULTISIMO and a window precision of 14% on CABB-S), so they do little to filter the material an annotator has to inspect. Also, motion alone may not be sufficient. Beat-like ges-tures are defined largely by their alignment with speech, and whether a movement has a communicative role may only be clear from the speech context. Adding speech, however, is a design choice that depends on how the annotations are used: if the detected gestures are later used to ana-lyze gesture-speech synchronization, a detector that favours speech-aligned gestures could produce biased data. The main application of gesture detection is annotation for the study of human behaviour, but it could also serve the evaluation of gesture generation. Co-speech gesture gener-ation systems are still mostly evaluated through subjective human comparisons, and the inconsistency and limitations of these evaluation methods are increasingly being discussed [38]. A reliable gesture detector and annotator could provide an objective, categorical measure of generated gestures, for example whether a system produces gestures at a plausible rate and at the right moments. A more interpretable representation of movement may help to understand why detection breaks down across datasets. In future work, we will explore learned discrete pose representations, such as a Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE) [46], which would allow us to inspect which movement patterns a detector treats as gesture and to test whether these representations transfer better across datasets and annotation schemes. ETHICAL IMPACT STATEMENT We collect no new data and use three existing datasets under their distributors’ terms; consent and ethical review were handled by the original studies. We release only the data splits (fold time stamps) and the keypoints tracked from the original videos. Gesture detectors could be misused to monitor people without consent and, given their frequent errors, could bias research or assessments.
