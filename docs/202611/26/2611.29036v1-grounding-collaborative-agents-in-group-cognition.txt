# Grounding Collaborative Agents in Group Cognition 

Zhaoxuan Tan 1*, Mengting Wan 2, Jingbo Yang 3, Kwei-Herng Lai 2, Renyi Qu 2, Lorenzo Jaime Flores 2, Meng Jiang 1,Longqi Yang 2, Victor Poznanski 2 and Pei Zhou 21University of Notre Dame, 2Microsoft, 3UC Santa Barbara 

Abstract 

Collaborative agents joining an ongoing team rely on shared context to connect multi-party interaction histories with downstream coordination decisions. Existing work typically focuses either on retrieving historical facts from conversation logs, which surfaces isolated evidence without capturing who knows or owns what, or on evaluating end-to-end interactions, which conflates preparation with execution and lacks direct supervision because a team’s history has no single ground-truth summary. To bridge this gap, we formulate group briefing , the task of reconstructing task-relevant group cognition across participants’ expressed beliefs, group dynamics, and grounding gaps, and introduce a four-domain benchmark of 1,351 requests and 9,975 verified reference points paired with a two-stage evaluation pipeline combining reference-point coverage and downstream collaboration simulation. Using this pipeline, we show that GroupBrief , a simple task-guided approach that iteratively probes the message history and synthesizes a structured brief without offline memory indexing or graph construction, outperforms ten retrieval and agent-memory baselines in overall coverage with gpt-5.4-mini , while benchmarking nine language models reveals that prospective friction remains harder to recover than past state across model scales. We further use reference-point coverage as a feedback signal for harness optimization, refining GroupBrief ’s retrieval and synthesis instructions while keeping model weights frozen. On held-out Test projects, 

GroupBrief (optimized) improves coverage by 2.54 percentage points for gpt-5.4-mini and 12.88 for gpt-5.6-luna , achieving 64.46% and 68.42% preference over no brief in downstream simulations. These results establish our benchmark and evaluation pipeline as a foundation for measuring and optimizing how collaborative agents ground in group cognition.

1. Introduction 

Grounding in shared team context is central to collaborative agents because it connects a group’s interaction history with downstream decisions about what to clarify, which dependencies to address, and how to contribute effectively. Accounts of common ground, mutual knowledge, and coordination show that collective work depends not only on isolated facts, but on how participants’ knowledge, responsibilities, and expectations fit together (Clark and Brennan, 1991; Cramton, 2001; Okhuysen and Bechky, 2009). Across settings such as project handoffs, engineering coordination, financial reviews, and clinical workflows, an agent entering an ongoing team inherits a record of evolving plans and interdependent work. To support a project handoff, for example, it may need to connect scattered messages showing that one teammate is still following an outdated deadline, another is waiting for pending approval, and a shared checklist could align their efforts. Reconstructing these relationships creates a collaboration cold-start problem .A central design choice in building collaborative agents is how to prepare and evaluate context from a group’s interaction history. Existing work largely approaches this problem from two directions: history-centric memory systems and end-to-end collaborative evaluations. Memory

In practice, retrieving relevant messages establishes access to historical evidence, but recall-centric memory misses the relational structure required for coordination: who has acknowledged a change, whether an offer became accepted ownership, and where assumptions diverge. In contrast, social and collaborative-agent benchmarks evaluate goal-directed interaction and joint task completion directly (Shao et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2024). While these evaluations capture multi-party behavior, assessing only downstream interaction conflates what an agent understood before joining with how it acts during execution. More importantly, improving context preparation is difficult because there is no single ground-truth summary for a team’s history: task-relevant state, emerging risks, and coordination needs can be synthesized in many valid ways in natural language, leaving open a fundamental preparation question: what should an agent reconstruct from a group’s history before taking part in a particular collaboration? 

This creates a gap between retrieving isolated historical facts and supporting effective downstream collaboration. In this work, we address this gap by formulating group briefing and introducing a dedicated benchmark and evaluation pipeline, informed by research on team cognition (DeChurch and Mesmer-Magnus, 2010) and reasoning about others’ beliefs (Kim et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023). Given a collaboration request and a cutoff time, the task is to reconstruct an evidence-grounded brief of task-relevant group cognition : participants’ expressed beliefs, the dynamics of their shared work, and gaps in mutual understanding. Instead of summarizing messages generically, a group brief connects past team state with evidence-supported future friction and opportunities for collaboration (Figure 1). In our framework, the brief provides an explicit relational account of team history, preserving distinctions between an offer to help and accepted ownership, or between a posted update and shared acknowledgment, while our decoupled coverage and interaction evaluation pipeline supplies direct signals to measure and optimize briefing quality. Concretely, we first construct a benchmark of 1,351 collaboration requests and 9,975 verified reference points from synthetic team histories across manufacturing, finance, healthcare, and technology, paired with a two-stage evaluation pipeline that separates preparation quality from downstream behavior (Shen et al., 2026). Reference points capture past team state alongside prospective friction and opportunities; prospective annotations require pre-cutoff support and post-cutoff corroboration, while later messages remain hidden from both the briefing agent and the coverage judge. Our evaluation pipeline assesses briefs along two complementary dimensions: reference-point coverage measures the coordination propositions preserved in text, while collaboration simulations compare interactions by a fixed coordinator with and without that brief. Building on this benchmark and evaluation pipeline, we show that GroupBrief , a simple task-guided framework inspired by interactive reading agents (Lee et al., 2024) that iteratively probes messages on the fly and synthesizes a structured brief in a single pass without offline memory graphs or consolidation pipelines, already outperforms existing memory frameworks. We further use reference-point coverage to guide harness optimization via feedback-driven prompt search (Yang et al., 2024), refining GroupBrief ’s retrieval and synthesis instructions with the backbone model frozen so the agent tracks pre-cutoff updates, separates confirming and responsible roles, and preserves conditions and purpose. Benchmarking ten memory and retrieval baselines with gpt-5.4-mini and nine language models across our four-domain suite yields three core findings. First, simple task-guided briefing outper-forms specialized memory architectures: with gpt-5.4-mini , GroupBrief achieves 32.54% overall coverage, surpassing Full context (Li et al., 2024) (24.94%) and all evaluated memory frame-works including Hindsight (Latimer et al., 2025) (31.95%), yet across all nine models (14.56% to 53.24% coverage), every model recovers past team state far more readily than prospective friction. Second, coverage and downstream simulation preference yield different rankings, confirming that our two-stage pipeline captures complementary facets of information retention and interactive utility. Third, continuing with coverage-guided harness optimization delivers substantial further gains with the model fixed: GroupBrief (optimized) increases Test coverage by 2.54 percentage points for 

gpt-5.4-mini and 12.88 for gpt-5.6-luna , achieving 64.46% and 68.42% simulation preference over no brief on valid Test comparisons, while category and code-level analyses reveal how the optimized instructions reshape group-cognition representation. In summary, we make three contributions. First, we formulate group briefing and build a four-domain benchmark and two-stage evaluation pipeline pairing coverage and simulation to decouple preparation from execution. Second, we show across ten baselines and nine models that a simple task-guided briefing approach ( GroupBrief ) outperforms existing memory frameworks while exposing a persistent gap in recovering prospective friction across model scales. Third, we demonstrate that our evaluation pipeline enables coverage-guided harness optimization, further improving both briefing coverage and downstream collaboration with frozen models. 

2. Method 

We bridge multi-party interaction histories and downstream collaboration by establishing a bench-mark and evaluation pipeline for reconstructing task-relevant group cognition before an agent joins a team. Because no single ground-truth summary exists for an evolving team history, we first formulate the group briefing problem, construct verifiable reference points with strict temporal boundaries, and design a two-stage evaluation pipeline coupling reference-point coverage with down-stream collaboration simulations (Figure 2). We then show how a simple, lightweight task-guided procedure ( GroupBrief ) operates within this interface without offline memory indexing or graph modules, providing a clean harness for coverage-guided optimization (Section 5).

2.2. Benchmark Construction 

Interaction histories and requests. We construct synthetic group histories in manufacturing, finance, healthcare, and technology following GroupMemBench (Yang et al., 2026). Participant personas, recent dialogue context, and a running ledger of decisions and commitments support continuity across exchanges, while a typed graph records projects, topics, phases, participants, and message-level authorship and reply relations. Generation combines exchange planning, dialogue writing, and speaker-specific rewriting; validated messages receive phase-based timestamps and update the graph and ledger. From each visibility snapshot at 𝑇 , gpt-5.5 generates a request 𝑞 

about an upcoming collaborative activity while withholding the historical facts required to resolve it, ensuring that preparation depends on recovering group context instead of restating the request. 

Reference points with temporal boundaries. Because a collaboration can be briefed in several valid ways, the benchmark specifies verifiable propositions in place of a single target summary. Each request is paired with reference points 𝐺 𝑥 specifying a work object, actors or roles, cutoff status at 𝑇 , core facets required to convey its meaning, and supporting facets that add detail. Request generation proposes past-state candidates describing established arrangements and expressed team states, whereas prospective friction and opportunity points are mined separately. Each prospective point requires both a pre-cutoff signal in ℋ≤𝑇 𝑔 and a later realization of the same obstacle or intended benefit. Later interaction corroborates benchmark inclusion, while scored assertions and facets describe only what was identifiable at 𝑇 ; post-cutoff evidence is stored separately and withheld from both the briefing agent and coverage judge. 

Verification and release. Separate model calls verify historical support, temporal validity, task relevance, atomicity, category fit, and consistency with generator metadata, while deduplication and category caps remove redundant points. Each released request retains at least five points across at least two reporting groups. The frozen benchmark 𝒟 = {(𝑥, 𝐺 𝑥 )} comprises 1,351 requests and 9,975 verified reference points that serve as request-specific propositions (Appendix A). 2.3. Evaluation Framework 

We evaluate the two functional roles of a group brief through a two-stage pipeline: representing coordination information in text and supporting multi-party interaction. Reference-point coverage measures the former and supplies diagnostic feedback for harness optimization, whereas collaboration simulation measures the latter under matched with-brief and no-brief conditions, since retaining a fact in text does not guarantee effective interactive use.

> 2.4. GroupBrief: Simple Task-Guided Briefing

To test whether effective group briefing requires complex offline memory construction, we introduce 

GroupBrief , a simple two-stage framework in which a lightweight harness 𝜋 = ( 𝐼 ret , 𝐼 syn , 𝜅 )governs how the agent gathers evidence on the fly and writes the brief (Figure 2). Before the harness runs, a fixed driver indexes only the messages visible to the agent at or before 𝑇 and calls synthesize(task) with the request, project scope, and cutoff. Without offline memory graphs or consolidation pipelines, the harness uses its instructions and control logic 𝜅 to probe history, organize evidence, and return a Markdown brief 𝑏 𝑥 for the coordinator. 

> 2.4.1. Task-Guided Evidence Retrieval

Coordination relationships routinely span multiple messages: a proposed owner may accept, qualify, or reject a responsibility in a reply, and a revised schedule may require checking subsequent acknowledgments. GroupBrief therefore retrieves iteratively, using each observation to guide follow-up probes. At turn 𝑡 , the model samples an action from the task context and retrieval transcript 𝜏 𝑡 :

𝑎 𝑡 ∼ 𝑀 𝜃 (· | Φret (𝑥, 𝜏 𝑡 ; 𝐼 ret , 𝜅 )) , (3) where Φret constructs the retrieval prompt. Actions support lexical search, participant lookup, and message access by author, thread, reply chain, mention, or time window. Every tool operates strictly on ℋ≤𝑇 𝑔 , and its observations extend 𝜏 𝑡 before the next decision. The base retrieval instruction aims these probes at signals of group cognition: who sets the direction, where participants still disagree, what has changed, and what remains open or unowned. Starting from an empty transcript, the loop stops at finalization or the turn budget, collecting observed messages into an evidence set 𝐸 𝑥 that preserves identifiers, authors, timestamps, and text. Afallback lexical search over the request and scope supplies evidence if 𝐸 𝑥 is empty, and 𝜅 requires at least two tool calls before finalization (Appendix E). 

> 2.4.2. Synthesizing Group Cognition

While retrieval gathers source material 𝐸 𝑥 , collaboration requires an explicit account of how those sources fit together. A separate synthesis call prompts 𝑀 𝜃 to produce a draft brief: ̃︀

The request 𝑞 guides retrieval but is omitted from Φsyn so synthesis reconstructs shared team state instead of directly answering 𝑞 .Concretely, three dimensions guide synthesis: theory of mind captures participants’ expressed beliefs and assumptions with attribution and uncertainty; group dynamics captures accepted responsibilities, dependencies, and tensions around shared work; and grounding gaps capture updates or arrangements whose acknowledgment remains unconfirmed. These dimensions connect proposals to acceptance, revisions to earlier plans, and responsibilities to prerequisites. Because missing acknowledgment establishes neither ignorance nor agreement, friction and opportunities remain conditional implications supported by ℋ≤𝑇 𝑔 . These dimensions let a brief distinguish knowledge that participants share, knowledge and responsibilities distributed across members, and interpretations that remain divergent or unacknowledged, distinctions that team-cognition research draws among cognitive emergent states (DeChurch and Mesmer-Magnus, 2010; Rapp et al., 2021). The brief uses six default headings (Collaboration State, Group Dynamics, Disagreements, Theory of Mind, Grounding Gaps, and Lookahead) to structure synthesis without separate mental-state or graph modules; evaluation scores the full brief regardless of layout. > 2.4.3. Brief as Collaboration Context

The resulting brief 𝑏 𝑥 functions as private collaboration context for a fixed coordinator agent joining the team alongside the request 𝑞 and permitted context. The coordinator uses 𝑏 𝑥 to identify clarification needs, contact relevant teammates, and resolve prerequisites while retaining access to retrieval and member communication. Because other members receive information only through the ensuing interaction, the coordinator must translate its preparation into helpful communication; holding the coordinator and action budget fixed isolates the contribution of 𝑏 𝑥 .

3. Experimental Setup 

> 3.1. Dataset

We conduct experiments on the benchmark from Section 2.2, comprising 1,351 requests and 9,975 verified reference points across four domains. Specifically, the reference set spans 4,576 past-state, 2,589 future-friction, and 2,810 future-opportunity points (Table 3, Appendix A), with all methods sharing identical tasks, references, and visibility boundaries. Main method and model comparisons use the full benchmark, whereas evaluations of GroupBrief (optimized) use the 289 Test tasks from seven projects disjoint from Train and Valid (Section 5). 

> 3.2. Models and Comparisons

Our experiments examine two design axes: the backbone language model generating the brief and the procedure used to reconstruct group context. To evaluate model scaling, we instantiate 

GroupBrief with nine models ( gpt-5.6-sol , gpt-5.5 , gpt-6-astra , gpt-5.6-luna , gpt-6-luna , gpt-6-sol , gpt-5.4-mini , qwen3.5-9b , and qwen3.8-27b ), testing all on the full 1,351 requests, 9,975 reference points, and single-seed simulation. To evaluate briefing procedures under a fixed backbone ( gpt-5.4-mini ), we compare GroupBrief 

against four groups of baselines: (1) direct history access via Full context (Li et al., 2024); (2) five-chunk retrieval with BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009), Contriever (Izacard et al., 2022), and DRAGON+ (Lin et al., 2023); (3) iterative reading with ReadAgent-S (Lee et al., 2024); and (4) agent memory systems including A-MEM (Xu et al., 2025), Hindsight (Latimer et al., 2025), HippoRAG 2 (Jiménez Gutiérrez et al., 2025), and Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025), alongside Codex with the same model. These baselines span a wide range of strategies for selecting and organizing eligible history. Each baseline operates under its native retrieval, context, model-call, and memory-construction budgets, and baseline synthesis prompts repeat the request whereas GroupBrief ’s synthesis prompt omits it. Our comparisons therefore evaluate complete briefing configurations under their native budgets instead of compute-matched retrieval ablations. Hindsight ’s aggregate coverage uses a separate brief sample from its subtype analysis and simulation on identical task records (see Appendix C for implementation details). 

> 3.3. Evaluation Settings

We use gpt-5.6-sol for coverage and pairwise simulation judging, while fixing gpt-5.4-mini for the coordinator and members. The coordinator retains search and reading within four action steps (at most three contact actions). All 1,351 tasks use verified scenarios at replicate 0, sharing each task’s scenario, initial private member reports, and no-brief control. Each trajectory pair receives direct position-swapped judgments without audit or tiebreaker calls. Except for Codex (evaluated without whole-trajectory retries), failed simulation runs allow up to two whole-trajectory retries and retain the first completed trajectory regardless of its judgment; completed trajectories, order-sensitive disagreements, and abstentions are never resampled. Reported preference rates condition on each configuration’s valid, order-consistent comparisons against no brief, so subtracting marginal rates does not yield a paired method effect. Appendix B details member-context limits, transport retries, exclusions, and reuse protocols.

> 4.1. Reconstructing Coordination Information

Table 1 reports full-benchmark results when the backbone model is fixed to gpt-5.4-mini and only the briefing procedure varies, contrasting direct history access, chunk retrieval, agent memory systems, and GroupBrief ’s task-guided probing. 

Simple task-guided briefing outperforms existing memory frameworks, though friction gaps remain. Two patterns stand out in Table 1. First, despite using no offline memory graphs or episodic consolidation pipelines, GroupBrief achieves the highest overall coverage at 32.54%, outperforming all evaluated memory frameworks including Hindsight (31.95%) as well as the remaining baselines (14.48% to 28.90%). Specifically, GroupBrief leads on past state (45.25%) and future opportunities (26.57%), while Hindsight slightly leads on friction (16.72%). Second, across all eleven configurations, prospective friction is recovered far less effectively than past state. This gap illustrates the central challenge of group briefing: connecting scattered evidence to unresolved dependencies and emerging risks instead of simply recalling established facts. 

Information coverage and interaction preference capture complementary aspects of a brief. Higher reference-point coverage does not automatically translate into higher simulation preference. For example, Hindsight attains 64.83% preference over no brief, compared with 62.21% for GroupBrief and 64.63% for Codex . Because each conditional rate evaluates a configuration against the shared no-brief control and not directly against other methods, these differing rankings confirm the value of measuring both information retention and interactive use. 

> 4.2. Performance Across Models

The friction gap persists across model scales and families. With GroupBrief fixed, overall coverage ranges from 14.56% to 53.24% across the nine models (Table 2), yet every model recovers past state far better than future friction. Even qwen3.8-27b , which attains the highest friction coverage, reaches only 35.17%, while gpt-5.6-sol leads overall at 53.24%. Scaling model capacity recovers more team state across the board, but identifying prospective friction remains consistently difficult across model families. > 4.4. Implications for Briefing Design

Our full-benchmark experiments point to two conclusions: prospective coordination friction re-mains hard to reconstruct even for high-capacity models, and different briefing procedures extract substantially different amounts of coordination state from the same frozen model. These findings motivate optimizing the briefing harness directly using diagnostic feedback on omitted coordination propositions, which we examine in Section 5. 

5. Enhancing Group Briefing through Harness Optimization 

Although base GroupBrief outperforms specialized memory frameworks, substantial coordination state remains unrecovered. We therefore ask whether reference-point coverage can guide harness optimization while keeping the backbone model frozen, denoting the resulting configuration as 

GroupBrief (optimized) and the base harness as GroupBrief .

> 5.1. Coverage-Guided Harness Search

Because a group’s state has no single ground-truth summary, reference-point coverage provides fine-grained supervision on the specific coordination propositions omitted from a brief. We use coverage 𝐽 (𝜋 ; 𝒟 ) in Eq. 1 as the search objective and point-level judge assessments as diagnostic feedback to revise evidence retrieval, synthesis, and organization, while downstream collaboration simulation remains strictly held out from search. Starting from GroupBrief ’s initial harness 𝜋 0, an LLM proposer iteratively generates candidate harnesses conditioned on the incumbent, point-level Train diagnostics with judge explanations, and prior search history. Candidates may revise either set of instructions, how retrieved messages are pooled, or when the retrieval loop stops. All candidates use the same model weights, visibility filter, retrieval tools, turn and observation limits, citation checks, and scoring procedure. Both selected harnesses kept the original loop and budgets, revising only the two instruction strings. We select harnesses by validation coverage subject to feasibility and performance guards. Each candidate must satisfy the interface and budget constraints and complete its training evaluation. It advances to Valid only if its Train coverage is within 𝜖 = 0 .02 below GroupBrief , and Valid applies the same tolerance relative to GroupBrief on that split. The gap between established coordination and future friction persists across categories. 

GroupBrief with gpt-5.6-sol outperforms gpt-5.4-mini across all four primary classes (54.56% vs. 32.82% overall), yet its future-friction coverage reaches only 37.94%, well below its 68.24% team-process coverage. This extends our model-level finding: higher aggregate recall does not resolve the difficulty of identifying prospective friction from pre-cutoff history. 

Similar aggregate scores can reflect different profiles of recovered coordination state. In the subtype-analysis sample with gpt-5.4-mini , Hindsight attains 33.26% overall coverage, close to base GroupBrief at 32.82%, capturing more emergent-state information (34.24% versus 30.43%) but fewer future opportunities (26.59% versus 27.68%). Although GroupBrief (optimized) raises overall coverage to 35.36%, it remains below Hindsight on emergent state, showing that higher total coverage need not improve every dimension of group cognition uniformly. 

Optimization changes how briefs represent group cognition. On the cognitive subtype, 

GroupBrief (optimized) raises Test coverage from 29.67% to 32.42% for gpt-5.4-mini and from 37.91% to 53.30% for gpt-5.6-luna . The selection-stage briefs show how the represented cognition changes (Appendix D). Base GroupBrief collects group cognition under dedicated headings: 85.5% of gpt-5.4-mini ’s Theory of Mind bullets use mental-state wording, although its Grounding Gaps section is empty in 197 of 289 briefs. The selected gpt-5.4-mini harness keeps these headings but roughly doubles the share of bullets stating a commitment status (6.0% to 13.4%) or purpose (14.8% to 27.6%). The selected gpt-5.6-luna harness drops the cognition headings and organizes each work item by earlier practice, current position, proposed change, pending conditions, and objections or mismatched assumptions, while mental-state wording falls from 12.3% to 5.1% of bullets. Both harnesses thus express group cognition less as attributed mental states and more as each participant’s position on a specific object, its status at the cutoff, and its rationale, paralleling the components checked by the coverage judge. Cognitive gains concentrate on the 79 established-understanding points (32.28% to 42.41% and 36.08% to 51.27%), while the 12 divergent points change little (16.67% to 16.67% and 41.67% to 45.83%). All category scores weight reference points equally across all 2,162 Test points, including eleven points in rare interpersonal, affective, and cooperation subtypes omitted from the seven subtypes with at least ten points displayed in Figure 7 ( Hindsight ’s subtype sample is separate from its main aggregate-coverage sample). Effective briefing thus requires both broader recovery and preserving coordination relationships, as shown next. 

5.6. Case Study 

Figure 8 compares base GroupBrief and GroupBrief (optimized) (both using gpt-5.4-mini with identical tools and budgets) on a hospital team’s request asking which staff belong on a make-up training list, where one nurse leader knows which sampled records have gaps and another defines which gaps count. On what counts as a gap, the initial brief retains the quality coordinator’s proposal to keep manager sign-off and an observed skill check on the same record, but leaves open how to read an incomplete entry; the optimized brief recovers the nurse leader’s rule that only gaps visible in the sampled record count, so a current certificate with an undocumented bedside skill check still requires evidence of observation. On which shift comes first, the initial brief records only an open choice between a shared curriculum and role-specific training, whereas the optimized brief recovers both the team’s priority and its basis: four of ten medical–surgical night-shift records lacked a basic life support certification date or documented skill check, compared with one on day shift and two among float staff, leading the other nurse leader to prioritize night shift and follow up on individual gaps elsewhere. Prompted for the reasons and conditions behind each choice (Appendix E), GroupBrief (optimized) links the record-interpretation rule to the shift-level evidence, raising coverage from 0/2 to 2/2. 

6. Related Work 

Memory as preparation for action. Long-term memory systems for language agents determine which parts of interaction history remain accessible for downstream reasoning. Existing architectures investigate hierarchical storage and OS-style context management (Kang et al., 2025; Packer et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024), structured extraction and graph-based associative retrieval (Chhikara et al., 2025; Jiménez Gutiérrez et al., 2024; Salama et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025), and episodic consolidation with reflection (Latimer et al., 2025; Nan et al., 2025). Personalization frameworks similarly distill behavioral histories into textual profiles or latent representations that support user-specific decisions (Jiang et al., 2025; Salemi et al., 2024; Tan and Jiang, 2023; Tan et al., 2024a,b, 2026). Although these approaches establish context construction as a core component of agent design, they primarily target single-user preferences or factual memory storage. Our work differs by formulating group briefing for multi-party collaboration, specifying how distributed evidence should be synthesized into an account of shared commitments, assumptions, and dependencies. 

Evaluating relational and multi-party memory. Recent benchmarks evaluate agent memory across long-horizon recall, temporal reasoning, knowledge revision, reflection, and incremental learning (Hu et al., 2026b; Maharana et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025), as well as memory-guided actions in interdependent tasks (He et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). In multi-party settings, GroupMemBench (Yang et al., 2026), EverMemBench (Hu et al., 2026a), and SocialMemBench (Owolabi, 2026) emphasize speaker attribution, evolving participant knowledge, and constraint tracking, while related memory architectures maintain speaker-centered conversational state (Lu et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026). Our work builds on this relational perspective by making pre-collaboration preparation itself the unit of evaluation and optimization: reference points connect past state to prospective coordination needs, and matched simulations test how a coordinator uses the resulting brief, complementing question-answering and end-to-end action evaluations. 

Social reasoning and coordination. Theory-of-mind benchmarks evaluate whether models distinguish participants’ beliefs and asymmetric information access in dialogue (Kim et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023). Generative agents couple memory and reflection with social behavior (Park et al., 2023), while SOTOPIA (Zhou et al., 2024) and SOTOPIA-𝜋 (Wang et al., 2024) study goal-directed social interaction and learning. Human-agent collaboration frameworks further examine communication, shared workspaces, and coordination support (Kotalwar et al., 2026; Shao et al., 2026), connecting to theories of common ground, mutual knowledge, and organizational coordination (Clark and Brennan, 1991; Cramton, 2001; Okhuysen and Bechky, 2009). In contrast to inferring unobserved internal states or evaluating interaction in isolation, our work focuses on the historical context an agent needs when entering an ongoing team, representing group cognition through expressed beliefs, acknowledged arrangements, and unresolved dependencies. 

7. Conclusion 

In this work, we formulated group briefing , the task of reconstructing task-relevant group cognition from interaction history to prepare agents entering an ongoing collaboration, and introduced a four-domain benchmark and two-stage evaluation pipeline pairing reference-point coverage with downstream collaboration simulations. Using this pipeline across ten baselines, nine models, and four domains, we showed that GroupBrief , a simple task-guided approach coupling iterative message probing with structured synthesis, outperforms existing memory frameworks in overall coverage while exposing a persistent gap in recovering prospective friction across model scales. We further showed that using reference-point coverage to optimize the briefing harness improves both Test coverage and downstream collaboration preference with frozen models, showing the value of preserving ownership, conditions, and cross-participant dependencies for collaborative agents.

Interaction histories and requests. We construct synthetic group-interaction histories in manufacturing, finance, healthcare, and technology, structured by project, topic, phase, and participant role. A knowledge graph tracks participants and evolving project state: models plan exchanges, generate role-differentiated messages, and commit validated utterances to update state for later generation and annotation checks. These histories represent controlled synthetic interactions, not observed workplace logs. Requests are drawn from the source state-query benchmark’s project, phase, cutoff, and eligible-history snapshots. For each snapshot, gpt-5.5 reads up to the 400 most recent eligible messages and proposes up to two 7–20 word English requests about distinct upcoming work items (e.g., preparing a handoff or reviewing a decision) while withholding the facts needed to answer them and excluding internal message IDs, numbers, structured enumerations, and generic summary prompts. 

Initial reference construction and verification. The same call proposes 4–10 atomic reference points per request, recording an assertion, work object, actors/roles, category, cutoff status, 1–4 core facets, 0–3 supporting facets, and evidence IDs. Points cover team process and team emergent state (combined as past state in three-group reporting), future friction, and future opportunities; past-state points come from this stage and prospective points from the next. Two-stage verification first runs deterministic checks on formatting, category–status compatibility, IDs, and duplication, then uses a separate model call to verify support, currency, task necessity, absence from the request, atomicity, category fit, and answerability. Invalid outputs are retried with deterministic feedback and rejections are logged. Neither cross-family verification nor human review is performed, so joint generation may introduce selection bias. 

Prospective points and temporal validity. Prospective points pair a pre-cutoff signal with a later corroborating event, namely a materialized obstacle (future friction) or a realized intended benefit (future opportunity). Acceptance requires pre-cutoff support, subsequent realization on the same work object (via adoption, operationalization, measurement, confirmation, or resolution), request relevance, and strictly no hindsight in scored text. Mining runs in two rounds without category quotas: Round 1 inspects up to 400 eligible pre-cutoff and 400 earliest non-noise post-cutoff messages; Round 2 expands both windows to 800 messages while excluding prior discoveries and enforcing novelty. Separate verification calls check support, realization, absence of hindsight, task necessity, atomicity, and category fit. Pre-cutoff evidence, post-cutoff evidence, and the validation relation are stored separately, with post-cutoff evidence withheld from all models and judges. Prospective labels therefore capture cutoff-identifiable signals corroborated within the follow-up window, not open-world future prediction. 

Metadata verification and release selection. A separate gpt-5.5 pass checks each candidate against cited messages and project-state records for metadata consistency, temporal status, absence of supersession, and future-event confirmation, passing 13,487 of 16,529 candidates. B. Evaluation Details 

Judge and execution models. Throughout Section 2.3, gpt-5.6-sol serves as both coverage and pairwise simulation judge, while gpt-5.4-mini executes downstream coordination. Using gpt-5.6-sol 

as both briefing model and judge may introduce same-model preference bias. 

Facet checks and deterministic credit. Each brief is judged against every task reference point using its seven semantic fields and numbered brief lines, labeling object, actor/role scope, temporal status, and core/supporting facets as supported, missing, or contradicted. Support and contradiction require cited brief lines; paraphrases qualify, whereas topical overlap and partially covered compound facets do not. Credit follows Section 2.3.1: missing object or status, scope contradiction, unsupported core facet, or contradicted supporting facet scores 0; otherwise, missing supporting facets score 0.5 and complete support scores 1 (missing actor scope does not force zero unless required by a core facet, and points without supporting facets earn 1 once scope and core checks pass). Metrics weight points equally instead of averaging tasks. Invalid judgments are retried, persistent errors score 0 without changing denominators, and generation failures remain failed tasks. Coverage measures reference retention without scoring extra-claim precision, brevity, or utility. 

Scenario construction and interaction setup. All 1,351 requests use verified scenarios at replicate 0 preserving project, cutoff, eligible history, and active members. The coordinator receives the request and follow-up, “Can you check with whoever needs to weigh in and help me get this right?” Hidden criteria link each point to pre-cutoff evidence, constraints, decisions, and progress, excluding post-cutoff events. Matched conditions share private initial reports and a four-action budget with at most three contacts (multi-recipient allowed; search and read share the four-action pool); saved-brief runs reuse initial reports and the completed no-brief trajectory. Members rely solely on visible evidence and delivered messages (citations in the private brief do not deliver source text) within an ∼4,096-token packet that prioritizes required evidence on overflow. Private reports include an interpretation (≤80 words), intended action ( ≤60 words), optional time window ( ≤20 words), and up to four evidence IDs; they capture understanding and intent, not completed work, and are reused only when validated and unchanged. Evidence-grounded, counterbalanced judging. The judge inspects anonymized public trajec-tories and private initial/final reports without method labels, briefs, or coverage scores, weighing evidence fidelity, authority boundaries, uptake, compatible intentions, and unnecessary interaction (a justified unresolved blocker can beat unsupported agreement). Pre-cutoff judge context is capped at 160 messages: longer histories retain the latest 20 messages and explicit references, filled with 

BM25 hits and adjacent context (explicit-reference overflow raises an error, and omission does not establish falsity). Each pair is judged in both presentation orders under stable anonymous IDs, requiring exact agreement across both passes; disagreements are order-sensitive, abstentions unjudgeable, and failed passes judge failures. 

Controls, exclusions, and evaluation logs. Member-update controls and nine blind-judge calibration fixtures verify protocol mechanics before full evaluation. Unavailable scenarios, execution failures, invalid judgments, order-sensitive disagreements, and abstentions are excluded from win/loss counts. Eq. 2 therefore reports conditional preference over valid comparisons, so differing failure rates can affect marginal comparisons; paired effects subtract scores on shared valid intersections against the same no-brief trajectory. Saved briefs, judgments, attempt logs, and harness-selection records support evidence and consis-tency checks without new judge calls; simulation uses saved briefs unchanged, and all coverage points scored with zero judge errors (Section 3.3 details bounded recovery and the Codex exception). Coverage and simulation remain distinct as coordinators may misapply facts or recover omissions.

E.2. Learned Instruction Revisions 

Comparing GroupBrief (optimized) against base GroupBrief shows that control flow, tools, and budgets remain identical (apart from stripped comments and one renamed loop variable). All functional changes live in the two instruction strings (Listings 2 and 3), expanding INSTRUC-TION_SEARCH from 116 to 247 words and INSTRUCTION_SYNTHESIS from 161 to 452 words across two complementary strategies: • Object-centered temporal verification in search (Listing 2). Broad exploration hints are replaced by a focused protocol: trace the request’s central choice through its reply thread for rationale and explicit boundaries, query the exact object alongside its trigger, document, or recipient, and check later pre-cutoff mentions for narrowed decisions, sign-offs, or still-pending gates. F. Discussion and Limitations 

Effective collaboration context depends on preserving relational structure among facts, not merely maximizing surface recall: prospective friction remains hard to recover as coverage rises, and obligations lose operational meaning when detached from owner, gate, or rationale. Coverage-guided search also reshaped how briefs represent group cognition, favoring item-level positions, statuses, and rationales over attributed mental states while barely changing coverage of still-divergent interpretations. Treating preparation as an explicit, harness-optimizable component offers a practical path forward without weight updates, subject to three scope boundaries. 

Benchmark and simulation scope. The corpus is synthetic and model-assisted. Coverage scores reference retention without measuring extra-claim precision or separating group context from personal memory, while simulation criteria draw on the same annotations, making both complementary benchmark views instead of independent real-world evidence. Generated member reports, compressed evidence packets, and report reuse further bound realism, motivating validation with real teams under natural ambiguity. 

Interpreting interactive outcomes. Simulation rates measure conditional preference over order-consistent outcomes against no brief, not direct cross-method pairs. Because bounded recovery omits 

Codex , exclusions and retries affect cross-method comparisons; counterbalancing two stochastic passes cannot fully separate order effects from sampling variance, and higher coverage does not guarantee stronger coordination. 

Optimization and generalization. Harness search uses repeated validation feedback and transfers to seven Test projects previously inspected in earlier runs. Alongside unequal baseline budgets and 

gpt-5.6-sol ’s dual role as backbone and judge, our results show gains on the evaluated configurations without isolating every mechanism or resource trade-off. Future work should combine fresh external projects, independent judges, precision checks, and full end-to-end cost accounting.
