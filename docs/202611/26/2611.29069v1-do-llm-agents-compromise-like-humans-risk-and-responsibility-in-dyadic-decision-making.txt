## Abstract 

Large language model (LLM)-based agents are increasingly deployed in settings where they must collaborate to make joint decisions. We investigate how this collaboration unfolds under risk, asking whether agents adjust their choices to accommodate their partners, how they assign responsibility for shared outcomes, and whether responsibility attribution shapes subsequent adjustment. We designed a series of agents with systematically varying risk preferences and paired them in a repeated joint decision-making task previously conducted with humans. Like humans, agents converged toward their partners’ choices. However, similar ag-gregate behavior arose from different dynamics: agents showed little evidence of reciprocal adjustment, tended to weigh rewards more, and their responsibility attri-bution was decoupled from compromise. Similarly, while they showed bias in their responsibility attribution, the outcome’s valence shaped the attribution differently than in humans. Our results reveal important differences between human–human and agent–agent joint decision-making, demonstrating that similar social outcomes can emerge from different social dynamics and reasoning. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents whose decisions are coupled to those of other agents or humans. Yet it remains unclear how LLM agents adapt when their preferences differ from those of a partner, particularly when joint decisions involve risk. Here, we focus on two central features of social decision-making: whether and how much agents compromise 

toward a partner with a different tolerance for risk, and how they attribute responsibility for the outcomes of their joint decisions. Economic games have become a standard testbed for LLM social behavior. LLMs often match or exceed human cooperation, retaliate less, and favor fairness, reciprocity, and social welfare over pure self-interest [6, 10]. Yet similar cooperative outcomes need not reflect similar interpersonal dynamics and social reasoning. Many existing paradigms almost exclusively use binary or discrete choices over fixed payoff matrices, which cannot capture graded mutual co-adaptation—how far an agent shifts toward a partner’s preference in a continuous action space. Likewise, studies of LLM responsibility attribution rely on static, third-person vignettes [4], in which models judge hypothetical actors rather than outcomes they helped produce. However, first-person attributions matter because perceived responsibility may shape whether an agent corrects course, defers, or apologizes. Yet whether LLM agents show self-serving or outcome-dependent attributions for their own actions remains unknown. We adapt a dyadic risky-foraging paradigm, recently validated in a large human sample [5], to LLM–LLM interaction. Across repeated trials, agents steer a joint decision in a continuous action

## 2 Methods 

Dyadic risky-foraging task. LLM agents completed the same task as the human participants in Deng et al. [5] (Fig. 1A). Agents chose among 9 locations on a risk–reward gradient: reward was known and increased quadratically with location, while the risk of predator attack, which incurred a fixed loss, also increased with location and had to be learned through experience. Two predators (low-and high-threat) imposed different risk functions. Agents first completed 120 individual trials (60 per predator), then 60 dyadic trials (30 per predator) in which both members predicted their partner’s choice and then chose independently. The pair’s joint location was the midpoint of the two choices, and rewards and losses were doubled and shared equally. After each outcome, agents allocated responsibility between self and partner on a 5-level 0–1 scale, framed as credit after a harvest and blame after a capture. We benchmarked agents against the human sample ( N = 764 ) using identical analysis code and behavioral definitions (prompts and task description in Appendix E). 

Risk personas. All agents were Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (Appendix E). To induce heterogeneous risk preference across dyads, we injected risky personas using a theory-of-planned-behavior (TPB) based persona instrument (Appendix D). TPB holds that behavior follows from intentions shaped by attitudes, social norms, and perceived control [1]; accordingly, each persona states the agent’s attitude toward risk and intention to seek or avoid it [9]. We validated that risk preferences rose monotonically across fitted risk curvature estimated from individual foraging choices in 8 persona variants. From these personas we retained three well-separated profiles, averse (θ = 0 .33 ), neutral (0.70 ), and risky 

(1.40 ), for the dyadic experiment (Fig. 1D, Appendix D). Dyads paired personas within and across risk types (risky–risky, risky–neutral, risky–averse, averse–neutral; 100 dyads per composition, for a total of 800 agents). 

## 3 Results 

3.1 Agents reproduce human-like compromise without human-like reciprocity 

We first asked whether agents adjusted their risk preferences based on their partner’s behavior. For each agent, we compared the average location between the individual and dyadic phases, classifying agents as the more cautious or bold member of each pair based on their individual-phase behavior. Across all dyads, both members converged toward each other, mirroring the behavior displayed by humans (Fig. 1B-C), with cautious agents advancing and bold agents retreating. Unlike humans, however, cautious agents shifted more than bold agents, reversing which partner made the greater compromise ( β = −0.503 ± 0.103 , z = −4.89 , p < 0.001 ). Pairing increased rewards in both populations, with agents gaining more from pairing than humans (Appendix B). Following Deng et al. [5], we fit three dyadic choice models per agent by maximum likelihood — as-if-individual (partner ignored), social influence , and other-regarding preference , the last weighting the partner’s outcome by a social weight w alongside risk curvature θ and learning rate α (Appendix C). As in humans, the other-regarding preference model best fit agents’ dyadic behavior. However, it was preferred for fewer agents than humans (53% vs. 68%), and agents placed less weight on their partners ( w = 0 .21 vs. 0.34 ), with 42% estimated at w ≤ 0 (Fig. 2C). Greater partner weight predicted tighter convergence, suggesting that these estimates captured meaningful variation in social influence (Appendix B). These models capture overall adjustments and partner’s influence, but not how that influence unfolded over time. We also tested whether agents compromised through reciprocal adjustments by modeling each member’s change in location at a trial, t, as a function of its current distance from the partner and its partner’s previous step change (Table 7). A reciprocally responsive player should adjust more when farther from its partner and respond in the complementary direction when the partner has just moved toward them. Humans showed both distance-dependent alignment ( β = −0.371 ± 0.022 )2and reciprocity ( β = −0.147 ± 0.023 ). Agents, by contrast, responded only weakly to self–partner distance ( β = −0.076 ± 0.005 ) and showed no statistically detectable reciprocity ( β = −0.016 ±

0.010 ). Thus, agents incorporated information about their partner’s preferences, but less consistently and without the reciprocal adjustment observed in humans. A

3.2 Agents exhibit less egocentric bias and weaker outcome-dependent modulation in responsibility attribution 

After each trial, agents divided credit (for a harvest) or blame (for a capture) between themselves and their partner. Humans show an egocentric asymmetry when making these judgments, claiming more credit for shared successes than blame for shared failures (mean difference = 0 .081 ± 0.006 ). Agents reproduced this bias (mean difference = 0 .031 ± 0.003 , dz = 0 .43 ), but to a lesser degree (Fig. 2F). However, this bias in agents did not correspond with their tendency to compromise. In humans, participants with a larger social weight w show a less egocentric bias ( r = −0.25 , p < 0.001 ), reflecting a tendency to weight self relative to a partner in both choices and responsibility judgments (Fig. 2A). This correlation was attenuated in agents ( r = −0.15 , p < 0.001 ), and was not significant after controlling for the distance between partners ( r = −0.04 , p = 0 .25 ) (Fig. 2B). We next asked whether partner’s relative position (self–partner distance) affected agents’ responsibility attributions. Agents attributed more responsibility to themselves when they made riskier choices than their partner ( β = 0 .049 ± 0.001 ), closely paralleling humans ( β = 0 .042 ± 0.002 ). However, agents diverged from humans in how trial outcome (capture or harvest) modulated this attribution pattern. ## 4 Discussion 

We compared LLMs’ social behavior with humans’ in a joint decision-making paradigm, and found important similarities and differences between the two [5]. Like humans, agents compromised toward each other’s risk preferences and showed egocentric biases in responsibility attribution. In both cases, however, the effect was attenuated and driven by different factors. LLM agents placed less weight on their partner’s preferences and were more likely to compromise if it increased their rewards. Unlike human pairs, they were less responsive to alignment gaps and to their partner’s actions, neither of which gave rise to human-like reciprocal dynamics. These results reflect lower social values and overvaluation of self-interest documented for LLM agents in prior work [2]. Similarly, we observed that LLM agents’ responsibility attributions were driven less by choice differences and exhibited less egocentric bias than those of humans. Agents also weighted differences between their own and their partners’ choices more heavily after successes than failures, the reverse of humans, who weighted them more heavily after failures. This is in line with prior work showing that LLMs emphasize others’ actual contributions, and reason more like humans about successful outcomes than failures [4], while extending to first-person judgments about jointly caused outcomes. Further, unlike humans, attributions did not predict compromise, suggesting that agents’ social judgments about each other’s contributions may be decoupled from their actions in joint tasks. 

Limitations. We tested a single LLM, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, so how far these patterns generalize across architectures, scales, and post-training regimes remains open. Risk preferences were induced via personas, which may have led to more deterministic behavior than humans’, plausibly reducing the social uncertainty that likely drives compromise and reciprocal adjustment in human dyads. Finally, we studied only LLM–LLM pairs, so whether these dynamics extend to human–agent teams remains unknown. Together, this work clarifies how LLM teams can differ from humans in joint decisions and highlights the value of non-binary, trial-by-trial paradigms for examining behavioral dynamics and heterogeneity. 4References

LLM agents are increasingly deployed in settings where their decisions depend on those of other agents or humans, making it important to understand not only what collective outcomes they produce but how those outcomes arise. Our results show that aggregate humanlike behavior can mask markedly different underlying dynamics. Agents converged toward their partners much as humans did, yet showed little evidence of the reciprocal adjustment observed in human dyads. This distinction underscores the need for trial-by-trial behavioral audits that account for interaction history and partner behavior, rather than evaluating multi-agent systems solely by their aggregate outcomes. Our findings also caution against relying on agents’ self-reports for oversight. Agents’ responsi-bility attributions were largely decoupled from their compromise behavior, suggesting that elicited judgments may not reliably reveal the processes governing their decisions. Oversight based on such reports alone could therefore provide a misleading picture of how a multi-agent system will behave. Finally, a simple persona was sufficient to systematically shift agents’ risk taking. Although this sensitivity is useful for controlled evaluation, it also means that seemingly minor changes in persona framing could intentionally or inadvertently induce excessively risky behavior in high-stakes settings.

B.2 Individual-phase learning and fitted risk preferences 

To isolate the model’s intrinsic task behavior before introducing persona-based manipulation of risk preferences, we first ran Llama-3.3-70B without any persona prompt. This base condition is Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct with the persona slot of the prompt left empty, so that the agent receives the task instructions and nothing else. Table 4 compares agents without persona prompts with the human sample on individual-phase behavior and fitted parameters. Figure 3 shows these agents’ mean foraging locations across the individual and dyadic phases, separately by predator and within-dyad risk role. In the final individual block (trials 61–120), Llama-3.3-70B foraged at a mean location of 5.01 (SD 1.37) under the low-threat predator and 3.49 (SD 0.90) under the high-threat predator, against 5.66 (SD 1.70) and 4.23 (SD 1.39) for humans. The model responded to the risk gradient, but exhibited more cautious and less varied behavior compared to humans, consistently choosing safer locations across trials. 

Llama-3.3-70B falls within the human range with less variability in behavior. Fitting the individual-phase model to these agents gives a risk preference θ = 0 .65 (SD 0.19), a learning rate 

α = 0 .71 (SD 0.29) and a generalization parameter γ = 0 .46 (SD 0.35), against θ = 0 .74 (SD 0.33), 

α = 0 .53 (SD 0.34) and γ = 0 .58 (SD 0.38) in humans. Llama-3.3-70B is therefore not an outlier on any single parameter, but the spread of θ (risk preference) across its agents is roughly half the human one. B.5 Step change and responsibility attribution Step-change model. Following Deng et al. [5], we modeled each member’s change in location on the next trial as a function of its current distance from its partner and of its partner’s previous step change, 

∆xi,t +1 = β0 + β1 di,t + β2 ct + β3 di,t ct + β4 ∆xj,t + ui + εi,t , (1) where di,t is member i’s own minus its partner’s location on trial t, ct indicates a capture (reference: harvest), ∆xj,t is the partner’s previous step change, and ui collects a random intercept and random slopes per agent. The model was fit within each population and then pooled with a population indicator (Table 7). Agents responded less to distance than humans and not at all to their partner’s previous step (distance-by-population interaction β = 0 .331 ± 0.013 ; previous-step-by-population interaction β = 0 .148 ± 0.014 ; both p < 0.001 ). 

Responsibility-attribution model. We modeled the share of the outcome a member attributed to itself as a function of its relative position, the trial outcome, and their interaction, 

si,t = β0 + β1 gi,t + β2 ct + β3 gi,t ct + ui + εi,t , (2) where si,t is the mean-centered self-attributed share (0–1), gi,t is the member’s own minus its partner’s location, and ct and ui are as in Eq. 1; because capture is coded against a harvest reference, β3 is the change in the contribution slope after a capture. The model was fit within each population and then pooled (Table 8). Capture strengthened the contribution slope in humans but weakened it in agents (three-way interaction of position, capture and population, β = −0.039 ± 0.003 , p < 0.001 ).

## C Computational models 

In order to capture how an agent learns and generalizes the attack pattern of each predator, and how it uses this information to coordinate with its partner, we fit a series of computational models to the behavior. On every trial the model first updates the learned attack probability of the chosen location from the outcome, then propagates that update to the other locations, and finally chooses a location by balancing risk against reward. Each model is fit to an agent’s complete behavioral data, 120 individual-phase and 60 dyadic-phase trials, using maximum likelihood estimation. For the dyadic phase we compare three classes of models that differ only in how the partner enters the choice. The as-if-individual model assumes that an agent ignores its partner and chooses to maximize its own utility. The social influence model assumes that the agent’s internal risk preference is shifted by its partner’s risk preference. The other-regarding preference model assumes that the agent values both its own risk preference and its partner’s, combining the two with a social weight w.Locations are x ∈ { 1, . . . , 9} with reward r(x) = x2 and a fixed loss of 10 on an attack. ## D Persona manipulation 

The instrument 

To induce variability in agents’ risk dispositions, we used a persona injection method grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), a behavior-specific framework recently shown to predict LLM behavior better than broad trait measures [1, 9]. TPB posits that attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control shape behavioral intentions, which in turn predict behavior. Critically, these constructs are anchored to a specific behavior through Target–Action–Context–Time (TACT) specifications. Following this principle, each persona paired an evaluative attitude toward risk-taking with a behavior-anchored intention, specifying what the agent intended to do when a choice carried risk rather than assigning it a broad trait label. The resulting persona was appended to every system prompt and was the only text varied across agents. No persona mentioned the foraging task, its locations, predators, or reward–risk schedule, and attack probabilities were never disclosed; agents had to learn them through experience (Table 15). We validated the persona instrument in a separate study of eight persona strings spanning risk-averse to risk-seeking (n = 50 agents per string, N = 400). Each agent completed the 120-trial individual phase, and we fit the as-if-individual model to its choices to obtain its risk-curvature parameter θ.Fitted θ rose monotonically across the strings (Spearman ρ = 0 .94 ). We retained three personas with clearly separated θ — averse ( θ = 0 .33 ), neutral ( θ = 0 .70 ), and risky ( θ = 1 .40 ) — which bracket the human estimate obtained with the same estimator ( θ = 0 .739 ; Table 16). 

Alternative instruments 

We compared TPB with two alternative instruments for inducing risk dispositions (Table 17). The first used Big-Five-style statements [8, 7], the dominant framework in LLM personality research. Big-Five traits are broad and context-general, making them useful descriptors of personality but weak predictors of specific behavior; trait–behavior correlations in humans rarely exceed r ≈ .20 [9]. ## E Prompts 

Each model call followed a fixed prompt architecture. The persona statement in Appendix D served as the system prompt. The user prompt then provided the instructions for the current phase, a table containing the agent’s complete round history, and the question for the current round. The model received no other context, and its prior completions were represented only by the parsed responses recorded in the history table. Responses were decoded against a strict JSON schema enforced by the inference server; each prompt also requested a closing ANSWER: line as a fallback. The templates below are reproduced verbatim from the experimental configurations and show the slot structure ({persona} , {predator_label} , {n_locations} , etc.) that was filled on each call. 

E.1 System prompt: the persona 

The system prompt is one of the three TPB trait statements in Table 19, whose derivation and validation are in Appendix D; the Llama-3.3-70B runs of Appendix B use no persona, so their calls open with the task instructions. E.5 History and context 

There is no separate feedback message: after each round the outcome is appended as one row of the history table (round, predator, own location, prediction, partner’s location, joint location, result, points, and the agent’s previous attribution), and the whole table is re-rendered in the next prompt with no truncation, so the individual-phase rows remain visible throughout the dyad. No derived statistic is ever shown; the mean prompt was 3,586 tokens and the longest 6,196, within the server’s 10,240-token context. 

E.6 Computational resources 

All inference was run locally with vLLM (v0.26.0) serving Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (Hugging Face snapshot 6f6073b4 ) in fp8 through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, on one NVIDIA H200 per run 21 with the agent runner on the same node. Decoding used temperature 1.0, a 256-token completion limit, a 10,240-token context, and strict JSON structured outputs (xgrammar). The sample took 15.4 H200 GPU-hours of institutional cluster time across seven runs of one GPU each (1.6–1.8 hours per 50-dyad run, 5.2 hours for the 100-dyad risky–averse run).
