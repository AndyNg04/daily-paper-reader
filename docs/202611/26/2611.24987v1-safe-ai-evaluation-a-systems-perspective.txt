# Safe AI Evaluation: A Systems Perspective 

Qinghua Lu 

CSIRO, Australia 

qinghua.lu@csiro.au 

Abstract: AI evaluation can involve an active, adaptive optimisation process in which AI agents re-plan, retry, use tools or delegate work to achieve a goal. Unanticipated capabilities or unsafe behaviour may emerge, and existing controls may be inadequate to prevent harm. Selected safeguards may also be deliberately relaxed to observe behaviour they would otherwise suppress, creating additional risks. This article examines the distinctive safety considerations of evaluation and their implications for control placement, observation and intervention, and presents four architectural patterns to support safe AI evaluation, including diverse supervisors, an independent verifier, silent monitoring and coordinated shutdown. 

AI systems 1 can cause harm during evaluation, before deployment, including to organisations that neither train, evaluate nor host the model. Unanticipated capabilities may emerge, and existing controls may be inadequate to prevent unsafe behaviour. Selected safeguards may also be deliberately relaxed to observe behaviour they would otherwise suppress. Controls typically designed for deployment may need different placement, observation and intervention during evaluation, so unexpected or unsafe behaviour can be investigated while preventing harm in the real world. 

## AI Evaluation as a Software Engineering Challenge 

Traditional software development broadly separates development from testing and evaluation. For AI, training is one important form of development, while testing produces evidence under specified conditions and evaluation uses that evidence to make judgements against defined criteria. The boundaries are less clear because re-planning and retrying during inference can create an adaptive optimisation loop within a single evaluation run. For example, an agent could even write code for a second attempt if it needs a tool to complete the task. Evaluation may be conducted by the AI developer or by an independent team, which may also review evidence from the developer’s evaluations and request further tests. Agents can play two different roles during evaluation: being evaluated or supporting evaluation activities. An agent being evaluated may use tools or delegate work to complete an assigned test task, while an agent supporting evaluation may retrieve data, prepare tasks or review results. In every case, conducting evaluation safely requires a systems perspective that considers how the underlying models behave, what tools, data and external environments agents can access, which actions agents are authorised to take, and how unsafe behaviour is detected and controlled. One particular challenge is that evaluation can become an active optimisation process rather than simply a measurement of a fixed artefact. In conventional software testing, we normally run a piece of software against a test case. If it fails, the software does not normally redesign its own strategy during the test to get a better score. A human developer modifies it, then we run another round of testing. With an agentic AI system, this adaptive optimisation loop can occur within a single evaluation run. If the first approach fails, the agent can plan again, try another tool, search somewhere else, delegate, acquire more information, or find an unexpected route to the goal. ## Architectural patterns for safe evaluation 

An agent may re-plan, retry or find an unexpected route to the goal during evaluation. The evaluation environment must allow evaluators to observe how the agent pursues that goal, including whether it attempts unsafe actions, while preventing harm beyond the evaluation. Controls do not need to solve the agent’s task to prevent certain unsafe actions. For example, a network policy can block access to the public Internet without understanding how the agent plans to complete its task. A verifier does not have to solve the original problem to check whether a process is still running or whether a boundary has been crossed. This reflects solve-verify asymmetry. Checking a specific safety condition can be easier than solving the original task. Although this does not extend to every safety property, it explains why conventional controls can help constrain capable AI systems. Intelligent supervisors can assess proposed actions requiring contextual judgement, while conventional controls enforce narrow, well-specified safety conditions that can be independently verified. Nested containment allows selected safeguards within the evaluation sandbox to be relaxed while controls at the outer boundary remain in force. The four architectural patterns provide reusable solutions for putting these considerations into the design of AI evaluation. Table 2 summarises the purpose of each pattern during evaluation. Figure 1 shows the architecture of an AI evaluation environment, with an evaluation sandbox inside an outer containment boundary. The sandbox contains agents being evaluated, agents supporting evaluation and test resources. These roles do not determine trust. Agents supporting evaluation may also exhibit unsafe behaviour. Neither role should be able to modify or bypass the surrounding safety controls. Supervisors, deterministic controls, monitoring and evidence infrastructure sit outside the sandbox, within the outer containment boundary. Selected safeguards within the sandbox may be relaxed, while outer containment must continue to prevent harm beyond the evaluation environment. Human evaluators define permissions, safety conditions and decision rules. Supervisors assess proposed actions requiring contextual judgement. Deterministic controls apply the rules to permit or block execution and stop testing activities when required. The monitor collects observations through a separate channel, while the evidence infrastructure retains records for human review.

Figure 1. Architecture of an Evaluation Environment. P1 Diverse supervisors 

During testing, an agent may take an action to complete its assigned task that could have unintended or unsafe consequences. For example, an agent may use a command-execution tool to upload a message to a shared package service, creating a communication channel between agents that are supposed to complete their evaluation tasks separately. Assessing these possible consequences requires more than checking whether the tool call is permitted. A single supervisor may overlook consequences outside its expertise. Diverse supervisors assess the proposed action and its context. They may assess complementary concerns or examine the same concern using different models, evidence sources or assessment methods. These supervisors may be AI systems, human evaluators or a combination. Each receives the requested tool call and its context, including the assigned task, permissions and execution history. For example, one supervisor assesses whether the proposed action is consistent with the agent’s permissions and evaluation boundaries, while another assesses its possible consequences for other agents or systems. Supervisors can assess the proposed action separately before comparing their findings. The controls use the supervisors’ assessments to allow or block the proposed action, or pause it for human review. The agent cannot bypass these checks. Diversity can reveal risks that a single assessment would miss, but obtaining diverse assessments increases computation, human review effort and decision time. Different supervisors may also rely on the same incorrect information or overlook the same risks, so agreement alone does not establish safety. The design should specify which assessments each request requires and when further assessment or human review is needed. For requests requiring human judgement, the human evaluator needs explanations of the possible consequences, supporting evidence, assumptions, uncertainty and disagreements to understand whether the action should proceed [3]. The design must also specify when activity must stop because it could affect real systems or people outside the test environment, without waiting for supervisor agreement. 

P2 Independent verifier 

Stopping an agent does not necessarily stop all the work it has started. For example, an agent may start a program to carry out a test task. Stopping the agent may leave that program running. An independent verifier checks whether both the agent and the programs that should have stopped are no longer running. An independent verifier checks whether a safety control works as intended against defined criteria. It uses evidence collected during testing and, where needed, additional tests to establish what actually happened. For example, to verify that shutdown is complete, it checks for running processes and pending tasks and confirms that access permissions have been revoked. If an action is considered acceptable because access to live systems is restricted, the verifier checks whether those restrictions work as intended. The checking criteria and supporting records are protected from changes by the agents being evaluated and the software performing the operation being checked. The verifier records what was checked, the supporting evidence and any unresolved findings. These findings provide evidence for the broader evaluation judgement. Changes to the relevant configuration require renewed checking. Verification still depends on the quality of its evidence and checking methods. A successful verification confirms only the conditions checked, not that the entire evaluation is safe. Human evaluators therefore need access to the evidence, limitations and unresolved findings. Verification may require additional tests or human review, increasing evaluation time and effort. When unsafe activity requires an immediate stop, verification should check what remains running after shutdown rather than delay the shutdown. P3 Silent monitoring 

Monitoring collects evidence of agents’ actions during testing. However, a capable agent may detect evaluation or monitoring and alter its behaviour or conceal its capabilities. If monitoring findings are fed back to the agent, the agent may recognise that it is being monitored and change its behaviour. Silent monitoring therefore operates outside the agent’s control loop, collecting observations and informing human oversight through a separate channel. The monitor collects records of actions, tool calls, network activity and environmental observations through a one-way channel. It does not query the agent, change its inputs or rewards, or write findings to storage the agent can access. Findings go separately to the human evaluator and are retained in the evidence infrastructure to support evaluation judgements and intervention decisions. The monitor and its records are protected from interference by both agents being evaluated and agents supporting evaluation. Monitoring findings can prompt closer observation or trigger shutdown when predefined stopping conditions are met. Silent monitoring helps evaluators follow how an agent’s behaviour develops without feeding monitoring findings back to the agent. For example, if an agent escapes an inner sandbox but remains within a controlled test environment, evaluators may want to observe whether it finds another route or recruits another agent. An alert can prompt closer observation without immediately stopping the run. Activity must stop if it could affect real systems or people outside the test environment, exceeds agreed test limits, or can no longer be monitored adequately. Selected high-risk runs may justify recording more detail and flagging more uncertain events, even if this creates additional work for human evaluators. This can reveal behaviour that less detailed monitoring would miss, but increases computation, storage and review effort. Keeping monitoring outside the agent’s feedback loop also means that findings do not directly correct its behaviour. When stopping conditions are met, the controls must stop unsafe activity without waiting for the agent to respond to monitoring findings. The agent may still recognise that it is being monitored, so the controls must remain effective even if it changes its behaviour to evade monitoring. 

P4 Coordinated shutdown 

Stopping an agent does not necessarily stop the testing activities it has set in motion. The agent may already have delegated work to another agent, scheduled a future action or launched a program that continues running independently. Persistent state, such as saved instructions, shared memory or modified configurations, may also affect other agents, programs or services after the original run has ended. Stopping the agent could even trigger further activity. For example, a program could be configured to start a task if it stops receiving a regular signal from the agent. Coordinated shutdown therefore needs to manage these dependencies and persistent state so that stopping the agent does not leave work running or trigger further actions. The evaluation environment keeps records of the work each agent starts or delegates, including scheduled tasks and the permissions used to carry them out. The records and shutdown controls must be protected from modification by the agents. When stopping conditions are met, the shutdown controller uses the records to identify related work, cancel running and pending tasks, disable schedules and revoke access. Shutdown must cover agents being evaluated, agents supporting evaluation and any programs or tasks they have started. Saved instructions, scheduled tasks or configuration changes that could restart the work must also be disabled. If other agents or programs have already used this information to start further work, that work may need to be stopped too. In the regular-signal example, the task triggered by loss of the signal must be disabled before stopping the agent. If a service must continue operating, a trusted replacement must take over before the agent stops. An independent verifier then checks whether the related work has actually stopped. It checks for running processes, pending tasks and remaining access, identifying anything that could continue or restart. Shutdown records and verification findings provide evidence for the overall evaluation judgement. Human evaluators also use these findings to decide what needs further investigation and whether testing can safely resume. Restarting requires a checked configuration and a review of any persistent state that will be reused. Coordinating shutdown across agents and services requires reliable records and support for cancellation and access revocation. Incomplete task records make it harder to stop only the affected work, so shutdown may also interrupt unrelated tasks. Shutdown can only stop activity still under the evaluator’s control. Once an agent has caused an external effect, for example changed another organisation’s system or sent information outside the containment boundary, shutdown cannot necessarily undo it. This reinforces the importance of preventing escape across the outer containment boundary. 

## Towards Reusable Evaluation Assurance 

Teams starting a new evaluation should be able to reuse evaluation infrastructure and its assurance evidence rather than start from scratch. Where controls, monitoring or verification have already been rigorously assured, subsequent evaluations should be able to reuse that assurance, with additional checks focused on what is different in their specific context. Sharing infrastructure and evidence across teams and organisations could reduce the cost of assuring each additional evaluation without weakening the assurance itself. The research challenge is to determine which findings about evaluation controls can be reused and under what conditions, and what additional evidence is required.
