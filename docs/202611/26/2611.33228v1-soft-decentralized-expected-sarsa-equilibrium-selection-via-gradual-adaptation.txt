Abstract 

We introduce Soft Decentralized Expected SARSA (SDES), a modification of Decentralized Q-learning in which agents maintain persistent exploration and update their acting Q-values gradually rather than switching directly to newly learned best responses when updating. We prove convergence to ϵ-greedy Nash equilibria in weakly acyclic stochastic games for sufficiently large batch sizes. We then show, in the repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma with memory, that soft updating can reshape basins of attraction without changing the equilibrium set. In the deterministic limit, we derive analytical basin bounds governed by the Q-value differences between actions in the target policies and show that reducing the policy learning rate can favor either cooperative or competitive equilibria. These results suggest that soft updating is not merely a stabilization device but also a mechanism of equilibrium selection in decentralized learning. 

Keywords: Soft policy updating, weakly acyclic games, best-response dynamics, decen-tralized multiagent reinforcement learning, basins of attraction

1 Introduction 

When implementing multiagent reinforcement learning (MARL) algorithms in real-world environments, two common features are that policy updates are incremental and that exploration persists throughout learning. Incremental updates are typically motivated by the stability they provide, whereas persistent exploration is motivated, in part, by the adaptability it affords in potentially changing environments. Incremental policy revision, or soft updating, is a widely used technique in modern reinforcement learning (RL). Rather than immediately replacing a value estimate or policy parameter with its latest learned version, a soft update interpolates between the current estimate and a newly computed target according to 

x ← (1 − α)x + αx target ,

where α ∈ (0 , 1) is a learning rate; the case α = 1 recovers a hard replacement. In deep RL, this device was introduced for target networks in Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) (Lillicrap et al., 2016), in contrast to the periodic hard copies of the target network used by the original Deep Q-Network (DQN) (Mnih et al., 2015), and was retained in Twin Delayed DDPG (TD3) (Fujimoto et al., 2018) and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) (Haarnoja et al., 2018). Modern implementations of DQN also commonly support soft target updates as an alternative to periodic hard copies. Soft updates are typically motivated as a stability mechanism: they slow the movement of bootstrap targets and reduce training variance. Throughout this paper, soft updating refers to the incremental interpolation above, applied between batches to each agent’s acting Q-values. It is unrelated to ϵ-soft exploration policies (Sutton and Barto, 2018), although our agents use ϵ-greedy policies, and the maximum-entropy (“soft”) objective of SAC. Persistent exploration is likewise a recurring feature of multiagent learning algorithms. Rather than allowing exploration to vanish, an agent may retain a positive probability of selecting every action, for example, through a fixed ϵ-greedy policy or a smooth best-response rule. Such persistent randomization has been studied most extensively for smooth Q-learning, where it can regularize the learning dynamics and induce convergence to quantal-response equilibria in important classes of games (Leslie and Collins, 2005; Leonardos et al., 2021b). More broadly, the exploration rate can affect both proximity to equilibrium and payoff performance in multiagent Q-learning dynamics (Hussain et al., 2023). The literature on perturbed decentralized learning further shows that continued experimentation can determine the long-run distribution over equilibria and other recurrent outcomes (Marden et al., 2014; Meylahn and Sch¨ afer, 2026). Persistent exploration is therefore more than a means of collecting samples: it can regularize the learning process, preserve responsiveness to change, and influence which outcomes are observed in the long run. Throughout this paper, persistent exploration means that agents use ϵ-greedy policies with a fixed ϵ > 0 during and between batches so that every action retains positive probability. Establishing convergence guarantees and understanding equilibrium selection are central challenges in decentralized MARL. Although substantial progress has been made on conver-gence, much less is known about how algorithmic design choices affect which equilibrium is ultimately reached. A major contribution in the former direction is Decentralized Q-learning (DQ) by Arslan and Y¨ uksel (2017), which converges to Nash equilibria (NE) in weakly

acyclic stochastic games with arbitrarily high probability for sufficiently large batch sizes. DQ, however, uses hard policy updates: whenever an agent revises its policy, it switches immediately to a newly learned best response. In this paper, we introduce Soft Decentralized Expected SARSA (SDES), a generalization of DQ in which agents update their acting Q-values gradually, at a policy learning rate α,while maintaining persistent exploration and learning within each batch using Expected SARSA. We prove that SDES converges to an ϵ-greedy NE in weakly acyclic stochastic games for every fixed ϵ ∈ (0 , 1), provided that the batch size is sufficiently large. Thus, SDES retains the convergence mechanism of DQ while removing the small-exploration restriction required there. The use of Expected SARSA is central to this extension: it evaluates the exploratory policy that the agent will actually continue to use, rather than learning an unrestricted greedy best response and treating exploration as a perturbation. Our second main result is that soft updating can have a consequence beyond stability. Although the policy-revision graph and its equilibrium set are independent of α, the policy learning rate can change the basins of attraction of the learning dynamics and, consequently, the probabilities with which different equilibria are learned. For the repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma with one-period memory, we show that, in the deterministic soft-update limit, key conditional basins and the resulting bounds on the aggregate basins are determined by the target Q-value differences of competing best responses. We refer to the collection of these differences as the Q-value spectrum . In this limit, the soft-update dynamics form a piecewise-linear flow whose basin geometry can be expressed in terms of the relative magnitudes of these differences. Our simulations indicate that this geometry remains predictive for small and intermediate policy learning rates. The analysis therefore suggests a broader hypothesis: in more general settings, the relative magnitudes and configuration of target Q-value differences may help determine the basins of attraction of competing equilibria. Thus, at least in the setting studied here, soft updating is not only a smoothing device but also a mechanism of equilibrium selection. We establish these results in the repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma with one-period memory. We characterize how the payoff parameters, discount factor, and persistent exploration rate determine the ϵ-greedy best-response graph and identify the parameter region in which the cooperative Grim-Trigger equilibrium coexists with the All-Defect equilibrium. Within this region, we derive bounds on their basins of attraction and show how these bounds depend on the policy learning rate and the relevant target Q-value differences. In particular, reducing α can increase the probability of convergence to Grim Trigger when the Q-value spectrum favors that equilibrium, whereas the same reduction increases the probability of convergence to All-Defect when the spectrum favors defection. Whereas previous analyses of MARL dynamics have predominantly focused on stateless repeated games, explicit analytical basin calculations remain scarce for multistate learning dynamics. Our analysis provides such a calculation for a repeated game with endogenous state transitions, yielding explicit conditional basins and bounds on the aggregate basins of the Grim Trigger and All-Defect equilibria.

1.1 Related Work Provably Convergent Learning in Games. Qin and Yu (2024) provide a systematic review of learning in normal-form and Markov games. Foundational approaches include fictitious play (Brown, 1951; Robinson, 1951), with convergence in zero-sum and potential games (Monderer and Shapley, 1996), and no-regret methods such as regret matching (Hart and Mas-Colell, 2000), online mirror descent (Nemirovski et al., 2009), and multiplicative weights for attaining coarse correlated equilibria (Freund and Schapire, 1999). Since the foundational work of Littman (1994), Markov games have become the standard framework for MARL. In zero-sum games, minimax Q-learning (Littman, 1994) converges asymptotically (Szepesv´ ari and Littman, 1999), but requires observed actions, knowledge of the game structure, and the solution of a matrix game at each step. Convergence was subsequently established beyond zero-sum games for several Q-learning variants under coordination assumptions (Littman, 2001; Hu and Wellman, 2003; Greenwald and Hall, 2003), and for a two-timescale risk-sensitive Q-learning algorithm converging to a generalized NE (Borkar, 2002). Independent policy-gradient methods converge at a polynomial rate in Markov potential games (Leonardos et al., 2021a), while continuous-time best-response and discrete-time fictitious-play dynamics converge in zero-sum Markov games (Sayin et al., 2022); the latter use two timescales to address non-stationarity but require opponents’ strategies or actions. By contrast, radically uncoupled learning can achieve convergence and rationality (Sayin et al., 2021), in line with the information settings of Foster and Young (2006); Leslie and Collins (2005). More recently, fully decentralized bandit learning was shown to converge almost surely in continuous-action general-sum Markov games under strong monotonicity, using only the public state and each agent’s realized rewards (H¨ ofer and Soner, 2026). Recent work also extends potential-game theory: Arefizadeh et al. (2024) characterize potential, ordinal-potential, and generalized ordinal-potential games; Guo et al. (2025) introduce Markov 

α-potential games and algorithms for approximate equilibria; and Guo and Zhang (2025) characterize dynamic potential games through value-function decompositions and symmetry of functional derivatives. For linear-quadratic games, Hosseinirad et al. (2026) show that potential structure is restrictive under full-state feedback but richer with decoupled dynamics and information. Other scalable directions include mean-field learning (Perrin et al., 2020; Elie et al., 2020) and decentralized MARL (Zhang et al., 2021). Overall, this literature studies equilibrium learning under realistic information and interaction constraints. Within this landscape, the present paper belongs to the class of graph-theoretic policy-revision methods: convergence properties are read off a graph on the finite policy space whose edges are induced by the revision dynamics, with weak acyclicity as the key structural condition; see Yongacoglu et al. (2024) for a recent generalization of better-response paths and weakly acyclic games in this framework. Within this class, Altabaa et al. (2023) model the idealized policy updates as an absorbing Markov chain on the joint policy space and obtain closed-form convergence probabilities to each equilibrium as a function of the revision rule and the best-reply graph alone. This is the framework underlying DQ (Arslan and Y¨ uksel, 2017). Building on Arslan and Y¨ uksel (2017), subsequent work has studied decentralized learning under local control and global state information in stochastic teams and games (Yongacoglu et al., 2022), introduced satisficing paths to analyze independent MARL

dynamics (Yongacoglu et al., 2023), and provided a two-timescale analysis of unsynchronized DQ under asynchronous updates (Yongacoglu et al., 2025). DQ has also been applied to urgency-constrained workload offloading in edge computing (Fox et al., 2025). SDES operates within this framework: its convergence guarantee (Theorem 13) is a statement about paths on the ϵ-BR graph on the policy space, while the basin analysis of Section 3.1 adds a quantitative selection layer, which path is taken, and with what probability, on top of the same graph. 

Dynamical Systems. From the perspective of dynamical systems, MARL can be inter-preted as a high-dimensional, non-equilibrium process in which agents continuously update their policies in response to environmental feedback and each other’s evolving behavior, seeking to explain how macroscopic patterns such as cooperation emerge from local interac-tions among heterogeneous agents Barfuss et al. (2025); Helbing (2012). A first family of models, grounded in evolutionary game theory Nowak (2006), nonlinear dynamics Strogatz (2018), and network theory Jackson (2008), makes minimal behavioral assumptions and offers tractable insight into when cooperative dynamics arise and stabilize Perc et al. (2017), but abstracts away from the algorithmic complexity and cognitive capacities of real learning agents. MARL closes this gap by instantiating agents with learning algorithms capable of temporal credit assignment, policy optimization, and strategic adaptation in complex, often partially observed environments Rahwan et al. (2019), and a growing body of work integrates this algorithmic perspective with mechanistic insight from dynamical systems Baker et al. (2017); Ma et al. (2023). 

Deterministic Learning Dynamics. Barfuss et al. (2019) derive deterministic continuous-time limits of temporal-difference learning in stochastic games, covering Q-learning, SARSA, and actor–critic methods and enabling dynamical-systems analysis of learning attractors (Bar-fuss et al., 2019). This framework has been linked to a minimum free-energy principle and approximated by an online sample-batch TD algorithm (Barfuss, 2022), extended to infinite-horizon memory (Barfuss, 2020), and generalized to partial observability and perceptual uncertainty (Barfuss and Mann, 2022). In the iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma with one-period memory, also studied here, finite-batch fluctuations substantially promote cooperation, approximately doubling the frequency of cooperative outcomes relative to the deterministic limit (Barfuss and Meylahn, 2023). SDES belongs to this class of sample-batch TD algo-rithms but replaces the hard best response at each batch boundary by moving the acting Q-values toward the valuation Q-values at rate α. Thus, α can reshape the deterministic basins of attraction independently of finite-batch fluctuations (Barfuss and Meylahn, 2023), while SDES additionally provides almost-sure convergence guarantees. 

Algorithmic Collusion. Tacit collusion among autonomous learning agents is a relatively recent but serious concern in decentralized MARL. Simulations show that independent Q-learners may converge to collusive outcomes in repeated pricing games (Calvano et al., 2020b,a), including sequential pricing environments (Klein, 2021). More generally, Askenazi-Golan et al. (2026) establish local convergence of Q-learning and gradient-based dynamics to strict finite-recall equilibria in repeated games, yielding a learning-based analogue of the Folk Theorem that delimits the range of potentially collusive outcomes; notably, they identify the size and geometry of the basins of attraction of these equilibria, the natural equilibrium-selection mechanism, as an open problem. In the same local spirit, Possnig 

> 5Soft Decentralized Expected SARSA

(2026) studies the stability of learned outcomes through a limiting differential equation and derives conditions under which monitoring and market primitives steer learning toward competitive or collusive equilibria in repeated Bertrand competition. The generality of these findings is still debated: den Boer et al. (2026) argue that collusion in Calvano et al. (2020b) requires implausibly long convergence times, and den Boer and Meylahn (2024) argue that what constitutes algorithmic collusion needs to be properly defined, so that algorithmic collusion remains an open question regarding generality, empirical relevance, and theoretical foundations. Methods for quantifying collusion show how algorithmic parameters affect its likelihood (Meylahn and Janssen, 2022; Meylahn, 2025, 2026). The analysis of the present paper is global rather than local: for the repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma we compute (bounds on the) basins of attraction in closed form and identify the Q-value spectrum as a factor that governs their size, extending this quantification program, which measures the likelihood of learning collusive strategy equilibria under hard policy updates, to soft policy updates.

2.2 Illustrative Environment 

To show how the algorithmic hyperparameters can influence the probability of converging to different NE, we require a simple environment in which multiple NE are possible. The iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma (IPD) with a particular choice of state space is ideal for this purpose. In the IPD, the players have two possible actions A = {C, D } (‘cooperating’ and ‘defecting’), and the stage-game payoff matrix is 

C DC R, R S, T D T, S P, P ,

where we assume T > R > P > S ≥ 0. To reduce the dimensionality of the parameter space, and since we are predominantly interested in the effect of algorithmic hyperparameters on the probability of reaching different NE, we will set T = 1 and S = 0 for most of the analysis. Recent work has studied this game when the players make use of RL and a state space consisting of all possible one-period histories, i.e., 

S = {(D, D ), (D, C ), (C, D ), (C, C )}.

Here, the first entry of a state tuple represents the action taken by the first player in the previous round, and the second entry represents the action taken by the second player. This enhances the strategy space available to the players to include strategies such as Tit-for-Tat, which repeats the opponent’s action in the previous round. In the full state space, S, there exist three NE, all of which are symmetric: Win–Stay, Lose–Shift (WSLS), Grim Trigger (GT), and All-Defect (AD) (Usui and Ueda, 2021; Meylahn and Janssen, 2022). Note that GT refers to a one-period memory version of the usual Grim Trigger strategy. That is, the player cooperates when both players cooperated in the previous round and defects otherwise. When the strategies are implemented in an ϵ-greedy fashion, this means that it is possible to re-establish cooperation after defection when both players play GT. This choice of state space leads to a total of 256 possible strategy profiles with three NE. Our analysis will not be feasible with that many strategy profiles, and since we only require two NE to study the effect of hyperparameters on the probability of reaching different NE, we will consider a lower-dimensional state space. We can achieve this by partitioning the full state space, and we will refer to such a partitioning as a state space abstraction. There are multiple options for reducing the state space dimensionality; however, we require an

4 Discussion 

In this section we interpret the theoretical and empirical results and draw out the structural message they carry. The starting observation is an invariance. The ϵ-BR graph is built entirely from the stage payoffs, the discount factor γ, and the exploration rate ϵ: its vertices and edges encode which unilateral policy revisions are available from each strategy profile, and the learning parameters α and λ do not enter it. At the level of the graph, changing α therefore changes nothing; the equilibria, the admissible revision paths, and the convergence guarantee of Theorem 13 are all unaffected. Indeed, for hard updates the selection probabilities are fully determined by the revision rule and the best-reply graph (Altabaa et al., 2023); a soft update is not expressible at that level, since the dynamics leave the discrete policy space. Yet Theorem 17, together with the simulations of Section 3.2, show that α is a primary determinant of which equilibrium is selected. Equilibrium selection is thus a layer of structure that the best-response graph alone does not resolve. In the two-state Prisoner’s Dilemma studied here, the additional information needed to describe this selection is contained in the relative target Q-value differences ∆ qπ−i 

> i

separating actions. We refer to the indexed collection of these differences as the Q-value spectrum .In the soft-update limit, policy revision becomes a deterministic, piecewise-linear flow in Q-space whose basins of attraction are explicit functions of these gaps (Section 3.1). As 

α → 0 the discrete soft-update dynamics approach the deterministic piecewise-linear flow analyzed in Section 3.1. The simulations show that the basin geometry of this limiting flow is predictive for sufficiently small α and often remains informative at intermediate values. has an additional geometric effect that λ does not share. We still require λ > 0 in the general convergence proof. The continuous-time analysis nevertheless suggests that, as 

α → 0, simultaneous policy changes become unlikely because they require simultaneous boundary-crossing times. Whether this observation permits the inertia requirement to be weakened or removed for suitable classes of games is an open question. 

Policy learning rate and the Q-value spectrum: Soft updating between batches is typically motivated by stability, yet we find that it changes which equilibrium is selected. In our environment the effect is not monotone toward cooperation: the favored attractor is the one favored by the limiting vector field of ∆ q differences. Conditional basins such as ˆB, ˆC, and ˆD are explicit functions of those differences (Section 3.1). For favorable payoff configurations satisfying the conditions of Theorem 17(iii), the limiting lower bound on the basin of πGT 

can reach 3 /8, compared with the hard-update value 1 /4. Thus, even the guaranteed lower bound can be 50% larger than the hard-update basin. Simulations indicate still larger shifts in some regimes (Figure 8), always in the direction predicted by the Q-difference geometry. For example, the bottom left plot of Figure 8 shows and increase of close to 150% from the baseline as α approaches zero. 

Implications for algorithmic collusion: Our results indicate how soft updating could influence the learning of collusive strategy equilibria. The basin of attraction of an equilib-rium is the probability that independent learners converge to it, the quantity proposed as an equilibrium-selection mechanism, and left open, by Askenazi-Golan et al. (2026), and the quantity measured for hard policy updates by Meylahn (2025). In pricing environ-ments, equilibria supported by reward-punishment strategies analogous to πGT are natural candidates for collusive strategy equilibria. Our analysis suggests that the probability of learning such equilibria is governed not only by market primitives and monitoring, which enter through the payoffs, γ, and ϵ and fix the revision graph, but also by an internal algorithmic parameter, the policy learning rate, which is invisible to equilibrium analysis. Slow policy adjustment could substantially increase the probability of learning a collusive strategy equilibrium when the Q-value spectrum favors it, and equally suppress it when the spectrum favors competition. A seemingly innocuous stabilization choice is therefore relevant to the ongoing debate on the plausibility and regulation of algorithmic collusion (den Boer et al., 2026; den Boer and Meylahn, 2024). 

Persistent exploration and the equilibrium concept: SDES uses Expected SARSA to evaluate the exploratory policy that will actually continue to be played, so its learning target and equilibrium concept are both defined within the fixed ϵ-greedy policy class. This alignment removes the small-ϵ condition needed for convergence when a greedy best response is learned while an exploratory policy generates the data. The resulting guarantee is convergence to an ϵ-greedy NE, not to an NE. Persistent exploration therefore enters not only as a sampling device but also as part of the solution concept. As ϵ changes, both the target Q-values and the best-response graph may change, as illustrated by the critical thresholds in Proposition 14. 

Limitations. The convergence and basin results operate at different levels of generality. Theorem 13 applies to weakly acyclic stochastic games under the stated communicating and non-degeneracy assumptions, but provides an arbitrarily-high-probability guarantee for sufficiently large finite batches; it does not assert almost-sure convergence for every fixed batch size. The explicit basin analysis is considerably more specialized. It uses a

two-player, two-action Prisoner’s Dilemma with a two-state abstraction, uniform Q-value initialization, and the deterministic limit K → ∞ and α, λ → 0. Moreover, parts (ii) and (iii) of Theorem 17 provide bounds on the aggregate basin rather than a complete closed-form characterization. The simulations examine the idealized SI ϵBRD through value iteration and show that the limiting geometry remains informative away from the strict limit, but they do not yet quantify how finite-batch estimation noise modifies the basins of SDES itself. 

5 Conclusion 

We introduced Soft Decentralized Expected SARSA (SDES), a generalization of Decentralized Q-learning in which agents maintain persistent exploration and revise their acting Q-values gradually at a policy learning rate α. We showed that SDES converges to an ϵ-greedy NE in weakly acyclic stochastic games for sufficiently large batch sizes. Our analysis of the repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma with one-period memory shows that soft updating can also affect equilibrium selection. Although α does not alter the ϵ-best-response graph or its equilibria, it changes the basins of attraction. In the deterministic soft-update limit, key conditional basins and bounds on the aggregate basins are determined by the relative target Q-value differences between competing actions. Consequently, reducing α can enlarge the basin of either Grim Trigger or All-Defect, depending on the underlying Q-value geometry; soft updating does not intrinsically favor cooperation. These results show that soft updating can be more than a stabilization device: in the setting studied here, it is also a mechanism of equilibrium selection. They further suggest that, more generally, the Q-value information omitted by the best-response graph may help determine which equilibria decentralized learners select. Establishing the scope of this mechanism in larger stochastic games with more agents and actions is an important direction for future work.

B.2 Proof of Theorem 17(i) 

We proceed by constructing what we call the asynchronous best-response graph corresponding to IBR graph G2 in Figure 9. This graph contains nodes representing all possible policy profiles. The directed edges represent the transitions that can occur if one or both players changes their strategy towards the best-response strategy given their opponent’s strategy in one or both components. For example, consider the profile ( AC, AD ). Given IBR graph G2,we see that the best response for the first player is to switch to playing AD while the first player is already best responding. Switching from AC = ( C, C ) to AD = ( D, D ) requires changing the greedy action in both states. In the asynchronous BR graph, we thus add directed edges to the profiles reached by changing the strategy AC towards AD in one or both states. That is we add the edges from ( AC, AD ) to ( GT, AD ), (AGT, AD ), and πAD .If this graph contains an edge from one strategy profile to another, it is possible to observe that transition in the SI ϵBRD. To see this, note that the SI ϵBRD allow for transitions that change a profile towards the profile in which both players are playing a best response to their opponent’s previous strategy in any subset of agent-state pairs. These are precisely the edges present in the graph. Therefore, we can calculate a lower bound for the BoA of πGT 

by taking the fraction of nodes from which it is only possible to reach πGT and an upper bound by taking the fraction of nodes from which it is at all possible to reach πGT .

Lower bound: The πGT node has eight incoming edges (including the self-loop), coming from seven other strategy profiles. From all seven of these there also exists a path to πAD .Therefore, the fraction of nodes from which only πGT can be reached is 116 .

Upper bound: Since the game is weakly acyclic, the fraction of nodes from which it is at all possible to reach πGT is one minus the fraction of nodes from which it is only possible to reach πAD . Now the node πAD has eleven incoming edges (including the self-loop), coming from ten other strategy profiles. Of these, it is possible to reach πGT from all nodes except from ( AGT, AD ), ( AD, AGT ) and ( AGT, AGT ). These in turn are reachable from ( AC, AC ), (AC, AGT ), (AGT, AC ), (AC, AD ), (AD, AC ), (AGT, GT ), and ( GT, AGT ).
