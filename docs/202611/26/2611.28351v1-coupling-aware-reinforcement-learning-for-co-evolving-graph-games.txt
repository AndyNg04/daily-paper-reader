## Abstract 

Building hydrogen station networks, electric vehicle charging grids, or vaccine distribution systems requires coordinating two separately operated graphs, demand-side facilities and the supply-side networks they depend on, where each operator’s payoff depends on the other’s choices. We formalize this as the Co-Evolving Graph Game (CEGG), a setting that sits between facility location, interdependent-network analysis, and graph-based multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) but is not jointly addressed by any of them. We propose Cross-Graph Attention with Policy Mirror Descent (CGA-PMD), pairing cross-graph attention, which lets each agent read only the partner-graph nodes structurally coupled to its own, with a policy mirror descent update for stable training. Across three reward domains and a range of practical network scales, we find that the structure of the coupling selects which method wins: at small scale most methods are competitive, but as scale grows or coupling becomes irregular, CGA-PMD is the one method that stays reliable: it attains the highest success count on each of five real city networks, while own-only, unmasked-attention, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)-based variants degrade in distinct ways. Our theoretical analysis addresses the optimizer side: the Kullback-Leibler (KL)-regularized update admits an iteration-uniform cumulative drift bound, whereas the clip update admits no such bound on the iterations where the clip does not bind. The implementation is available at 

https://github.com/Mina-Kim-Github/cegg-cga-pmd .

## 1 Introduction 

Consider two agents that must sequentially expand separate graphs (Figure 1): Agent 1 adds nodes to graph G1 and Agent 2 to graph G2, and a node on G1 becomes operational, starting a diffusion process on its neighboring edges, only when a coupling-partner node on G2 has also been activated. This structure arises across many infrastructure-expansion problems, hydrogen refueling stations need reachable production plants, electric vehicle (EV) chargers need grid capacity, vaccination sites need cold-chain logistics, 5G towers need fiber backhaul, so each agent’s optimal placement depends on the other’s choices, yet the two agents act on different graphs. We use hydrogen as the primary case study but the formulation is domain-agnostic. Existing methods do not directly address this setting. Facility location operates on a single network topology, not on two separate coupled graphs expanded in tandem. Interdependent-network analysis studies how fixed coupled networks fail under perturbations, not how multiple decision-makers should expand them. Standard multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) assumes either a shared observation space or full decentralization, neither of which captures the structured coupling across

This paper closes this gap with the following contributions: 1. Problem formalization. We define the Co-Evolving Graph Game (CEGG), a class of two-agent sequential network-expansion problems sitting in the gap between facility location, interdependent-network analysis, and graph-MARL. 2. Algorithmic proposal and empirical regime. We propose CGA-PMD, pairing Cross-Graph Attention with a Policy Mirror Descent update, and benchmark it against five primary base-lines covering own-only, coupling-masked, and unmasked-attention architectures crossed with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Policy Mirror Descent (PMD) optimizers, across three reward calibrations and a range of practical network scales. CEGG instances partition into difficulty zones in which different inductive biases win, and CGA-PMD is the method that stays reliable at large scale and under irregular coupling. 3. Theoretical analysis. We prove an iteration-uniform cumulative drift bound for the idealized KL-regularized update under noisy cross-graph advantages, and the complementary negative statement for the clip update on the iterations where the clip does not bind. A neural-architecture extension ties the bound to inter-graph coupling cardinality, and a bias–variance decomposition of the masked context, measured on trained models, says when the Φ mask helps. 

## 2 Related Work 

The CEGG sits in a gap among coupled-network analysis, sequential network design, and graph-MARL (Table 1): no prior setting captures two separately evolving networks, each expanded by its own agent, where each agent’s reward depends on the other network’s state. This prevents direct application of interdependent-network analysis [Artime et al., 2024, Buldyrev et al., 2010] (no agency), Mei et al. [2026]’s heterogeneous-attention MARL on fixed coupled networks, whose attention we adapt with a coupling-aware mask as CGA and use unmasked as the MAPPO-Het baseline, facility-location and reinforcement learning for combinatorial optimization on a single graph [Dai et al., 2017, Darvariu et al., 2024, Golovin and Krause, 2011, Guo et al., 2025, Laporte et al., 2019], including RL siting of refueling infrastructure [Kim et al., 2026b], or graph-MARL with shared observation [de Witt et al., 2020, Jiang et al., 2020, Lowe et al., 2017, Rashid et al., 2020, Sintes and Buši´ c, 2025, Yu et al., 2022]. Cross-graph attention with a coupling-aware mask is our architectural response. ## 4 Experiments 

We instantiate the CEGG across three reward calibrations: hydrogen freight (Agent 1 places hydrogen refueling stations on a freight logistics network G1, Agent 2 places hydrogen production plants on 

G2; reward via fuel-cell-truck Bass diffusion), EV-grid (Agent 1 places direct current fast chargers on 5a road network G1, Agent 2 places grid substations on G2; reward via EV-adoption Bass diffusion), and vaccination (Agent 1 places clinics on a demand network G1, Agent 2 places cold-chain depots on G2; reward via vaccine-uptake Bass diffusion). These instantiations are stylized: the Bass form, its parameters and the costs are literature estimates, not operator data, and deployment would need calibration against an operator’s records (App. E.5 varies them). For network instances we use open-source Transportation Network Test Problems (TNTP) [TNR Core Team, 2026], a curated repository of real city road networks in which nodes are intersec-tions and edges are road segments. We use breadth-first search (BFS) subsamples of ChicagoS-ketch, nested subgraphs grown by breadth-first expansion outward from the highest-degree node, at N ∈{ 50 , 100 , 150 , 200 , 250 , 300 } for controlled scaling, and five real city networks spanning 

17 × in size (Sioux Falls 24 to BerlinMitte 397 nodes). Supply graphs use top-degree nodes (i.e., 

N2=⌈N1/3⌉, with coupling radius dmax =2 — Φ( v) = {u ∈ V2 : dG1 (v, u ) ≤ 2}, the supply nodes within graph-hop distance 2 of demand v). The six methods of Table 2 are evaluated on every cell with 10 seeds per (method, network) cell. The comparison is a factorial over architecture and optimizer rather than a survey: HAPPO [Kuba et al., 2022], MADDPG [Lowe et al., 2017], and QMIX [Rashid et al., 2020] assume a shared observation or a joint action space over one graph, so they do not apply to two separately expanded graphs. We use patience-based early stopping (patience 100, max 1000 episodes); the full network list, hyperparameters and the significance protocol are in App. B. A seed counts as a “success” if the trained policy’s evaluation reward exceeds a productive threshold (5·10 7 at hydrogen calibration; scaled by κ for EV-grid and vaccination). Sub-threshold seeds are dominated by a cold-start collapse in which the policy hits a no-learning floor (typically −4.8·10 7:installation cost without adoption) and never escapes; this is failure of the trained policy, not training-time non-convergence (early stopping does fire). “ k/n ” notation in figures and tables denotes k

successful seeds out of n. No seed lands between 4.54 ·10 7 and 8.46 ·10 7, so the counts do not depend on where the line is drawn, and reward gaps are significant in 23 of 24 cells by paired Wilcoxon (App. E.1). The experiments are organized along three borderlines: scaling at fixed coupling structure (Section 4.1), coupling structure at fixed scale (Section 4.2), and method-by-method behavior on real city networks across all three reward domains (Section 4.3).

## 6 Conclusion and Discussion 

The Co-Evolving Graph Game (CEGG) captures sequential co-design over two coupled graphs, in a setting not addressed by facility location, interdependent-network analysis or graph-MARL individually. We propose CGA-PMD: Φ-masked cross-graph attention paired with a deep-RL realization of policy mirror descent. The two are a pairing: the attention introduces the partner-graph noise the KL regularization controls and the grid ablates it. CGA-PMD keeps a 10 /10 success rate through N =250 and 9/10 at N =300 , and attains the highest success count on each of the five real city networks, where the unmasked DGN-PMD falls to 1/10 

on two. On the BFS sweep it also leads on mean reward from N =150 upward, by 1.1–1.9× once collapsed seeds are removed from every method. These results establish that CGA-PMD is scalable and reliable at large action-space scale. There are limitations to this study that naturally open avenues for further work. Theorem 1 is proved for the idealized multiplicative update, and Lemma 2 bounds single-step drift rather than a convergence rate. Proposition 1 is scoped to the iterations on which the clip does not bind. Extending the bound to the implemented update is the natural next theoretical step. None of these qualifications affects the comparison the experiments support: as scale grows or coupling becomes irregular, CGA-PMD is the method that stays reliable across three reward domains and five real city networks. The CEGG formulation and the evaluation protocol introduced here give that continuation a setting to build on.

Hyperparameters. All GNN policies use 3-layer GraphSAGE with hidden dimension 64. CGA uses 4-head attention. Learning rate: 3 × 10 −4 (Adam) for all methods. Supply network construction. For each demand network G1, the supply network G2 is constructed from the top-N1/3 highest-degree nodes. Supply edges connect nodes within given distance in G1.

Coupling function. Φ( v) = {u ∈ V2 : dG1 (v, u ) ≤ dmax } where dmax is the coupling tightness parameter. Default: dmax = 2 . The distance dG1 (v, u ) is measured in graph hops (unweighted BFS), not physical distance. Extending CGA to physical-distance-based coupling is straightforward by replacing the BFS in Φ’s definition with a weighted shortest-path computation, and is left for domain-specific deployments. 

Environment parameters. We use Bass-diffusion innovation and imitation rates p=0 .004 , q=0 .3

(within ranges reported for energy-technology adoption in the original Bass-diffusion analysis [Bass, 1969] and standard hydrogen/FCEV adoption-modeling literature), with a background capacity growth rate of 0.02 per step. Reward coefficients (hydrogen-on-logistics instantiation): κ = 8 ,500 

(Agent 1 per-unit carbon-saving benefit, USD), pH = 1 .75 × 10 5 (Agent 2 per-unit hydrogen sale price), c1 = 10 6 (demand-side installation cost), c2 = 5 × 10 5 (supply-side installation cost). Horizon: T = 30 . The regime map is robust across the three reward calibrations defined in Section 4.3 ( κ∈{ $500 , $1 ,275 , $8 ,500 }, a 17 × range): all three reproduce the qualitative regime structure (App. D). The specific calibration sets absolute reward magnitudes, not the qualitative regime boundary.

We implemented all baselines from scratch in PyTorch rather than using existing MARL libraries, because our environment (coupled two separate graphs and the coupling function Φ) is not a standard interface. This also ensures a fair architectural comparison: all methods share the same 3-layer GraphSAGE backbone, hidden dimension 64, and PPO hyperparameters.
