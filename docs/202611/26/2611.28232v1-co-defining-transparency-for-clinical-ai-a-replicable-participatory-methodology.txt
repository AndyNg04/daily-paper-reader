## Abstract 

Transparency is the most invoked safeguard for clinical AI, yet frameworks (e.g. the EU AI Act, FUTURE-AI) are specified top-down by regulators and technical experts, not bottom-up by the frontline users whose trust and adoption they should calibrate. This disconnect exacerbates the central deployment failure of clini-cal AI, miscalibrated reliance and adoption (such as automation bias, deskilling, or disuse), which technical explanation alone does not resolve. We argue that “how much transparency, of what kind, and for whom” is an empirical, context-dependent question, and present a replicable participatory methodology to answer it, instantiated at a large European university hospital. A cumulative chain of six workshops engages an inter-professional panel (such as clinicians, patient and caregiver partners, and students) in agnostic, open-ended elicitation: 275 stakeholder verbatims and external benchmarks (eCH-0272, EU AI Act, Stan-ford FMTI) yield 20 indicators (18 prioritised and 2 recovered at later stage), rated across five clinical scenarios of graduated intervention before and after tech-nical briefings, discussed with legal experts, and translated into concrete imple-mentation features. As an existence proof, the use case yields decision-useful signals: the human review of automated decisions is the single most critical trans-parency indicator (84% of participants), and required transparency for adoption rises monotonically with the perceived risk of intervention (AI informational tools to AI treatment tools), corroborating World Health Organisation (WHO) risk-proportionality. In the local setting, a short explainability technical briefing did not substantially shift participants’ transparency requirements. Of 151 evaluated AI transparency solutions, 79 were accepted as-is, covering (at least partially) 17 out of the 20 selected indicators. We position the six-workshop protocol (not its single-site outcomes) as the transferable artefact and release all instruments to support replication. 

## 1 Introduction 

Generative and predictive AI is being embedded across the clinical pathway, from patient-facing chatbots and ambient scribes to triage, diagnosis and treatment recommendation. Across ethics guidelines, regulation and governance strategies, transparency is the single most cited safeguard [Franzke, 2022, Kiseleva et al., 2022]. Yet transparency is not one thing: depending on context it de-notes data provenance, model interpretability, traceability, uncertainty communication, or disclosure of limitations, each with distinct technical and organisational costs. International references such as the EU AI Act [European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024b], the Council of Europe Convention [Council of Europe, 2024], the FUTURE-AI guideline [Lekadir et al., 2025] and

the Foundation Model Transparency Index [Bommasani et al., 2024] give valuable normative struc-ture, but they are expert-driven and top-down: they prescribe what transparency should be rather than establish what it must accomplish for the people who act on the system’s output. The purpose transparency must accomplish, we argue, is to calibrate reliance, trust and adoption. The dominant deployment problem for clinical AI is not raw accuracy but whether the human re-lies on the system when and only when it is right [Lee and See, 2004, Schemmer et al., 2023]. Both failure modes are documented in medicine: over-reliance through automation bias and an-choring [Khera et al., 2023, Jabbour et al., 2023], longer-term deskilling under routine AI exposure [Budzy´ n et al., 2025], and (less discussed but empirically more prevalent in some settings) under-reliance/disuse, where clinicians discard correct advice [Cabitza et al., 2026, Küper et al., 2025]. Crucially, adding explanations does not reliably repair this: explanations can fail to reduce over-reliance and sometimes increase it [Bansal et al., 2021, Buçinca et al., 2021], help only when they lower the cost of verification [Vasconcelos et al., 2023], and do not neutralise reliance on biased 

models even for AI-literate physicians [Jabbour et al., 2023, Qazi et al., 2026]. AI Transparency is therefore one important element impacting on the clinical stake, the user, and the healthcare work-flow, which should be carefully designed and evaluated. The co-designed AI transparency solutions should later be evaluated in prospective randomised controlled trials to assess, on clinically relevant indicators such as Patient Reported Outcomes (PROMs) or TTF (time-to-treatment-failure), whether this additional transparency is beneficial to the healthcare system. Transparency perception is contingent; it cannot be settled by a fixed checklist; we propose to elicit it from stakeholders against concrete uses. Our contribution is methodological : a replicable, cu-mulative participatory protocol that turns the abstract goal “transparency for trust” into prioritised, scenario-specific, and implementation-oriented requirements, together with the reasoning that makes each requirement decision-useful. We deliberately foreground the method over the numbers. Asingle hospital panel cannot yield population estimates, and we do not claim it does; instead we show that the protocol can produce decision-relevant evidence (such as stable priorities, a reliance-calibrated gradient, a catalogue of accepted/contested solutions) and that we argue is part of what an institution needs to know about AI transparency perception for clinical AI deployment at the benefit of the healthcare system and patient. 

Contributions. (1) We frame clinical-AI transparency as an instrument for calibrated reliance and derive from the reliance literature why requirements must be co-defined and context-specific. (2) We present a replicable six-workshop methodology, with its instruments and analysis, designed to be transferable across institutions. (3) We report a European university-hospital use case as an existence proof, reading the results as signals about the method rather than as proven findings. (4) We discuss what the method enabled locally, and its limits, for GenAI adoption and governance. 

## 2 Background and related work 

Appropriate reliance is the human’s ability to accept correct AI advice and reject incorrect advice [Schemmer et al., 2023, Lee and See, 2004, Parasuraman and Riley, 1997]. It decomposes into avoiding over-reliance (following the AI when it is wrong) and avoiding under-reliance (overrid-ing it when it is right); good outcomes require both [Küper et al., 2025, Cabitza et al., 2026]. In clinical settings, the balance is fragile and expertise-dependent: novices tend to over-rely because they detect errors less well, while experts may under-rely [Logg et al., 2019, Tschandl et al., 2020, Küper et al., 2025]. A randomised vignette study found that systematically biased model predictions degraded clinicians’ diagnostic accuracy, and that standard explanations did not prevent this harm [Jabbour et al., 2023]; an accompanying analysis framed automation bias as a first-order safety risk [Khera et al., 2023]. Recent evidence extends the concern to skill retention—endoscopists’ unaided detection performance fell after routine exposure to AI assistance [Budzy´ n et al., 2025]—and even AI-literacy training did not eliminate automation bias in LLM-assisted diagnostic reasoning [Qazi et al., 2026]. Conversely, controlled studies of second-opinion systems find disuse to be the more prevalent and consequential failure, with appropriate reliance well below chance [Cabitza et al., 2026]. The natural hope is that explanation calibrates reliance, however explanations do not auto-matically yield complementary human–AI performance [Bansal et al., 2021]; they frequently fail to curb over-reliance because people adopt low-effort heuristics rather than engaging analytically, and cognitive-forcing designs help only at a usability cost [Buçinca et al., 2021]. There is a broad “participatory turn” in AI design [Delgado et al., 2023, Kallina and Singh, 2024], and healthcare-specific reviews document a range of empirical methods for public and patient in-volvement in AI research [Frost et al., 2024, Lammons et al., 2023], with a growing case for in-clusive co-creation in digital health [Nickel et al., 2024]. Two constituencies are easy to omit yet central to calibrated reliance. Patients and informal caregivers are increasingly the direct users of generative tools (symptom chatbots, portals) and bear the consequences of clinician reliance; their notion of “enough transparency” rarely matches a model card. Students and trainees are the pop-ulation whose reliance habits are being formed now and who are most vulnerable to over-reliance and deskilling [Budzy´ n et al., 2025, Küper et al., 2025]; embedding them in requirement-setting is simultaneously a data source and an AI-literacy intervention, complementing efforts to build AI literacy into medical curricula [Huang, 2025, Singla et al., 2024]. Transparency is routinely invoked as an ethical, regulatory or technical requirement, yet it is rarely defined together with the full range of people it concerns. Existing efforts are mostly top-down (ex-pert definitions, checklists, XAI metrics) or one-off (an isolated focus group, a public consultation without follow-up), and the few participatory approaches remain tied to a single product or use case. Classical technology-acceptance models—the Technology Acceptance Model [Davis and Grani´ c, 2024] and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology [Venkatesh et al., 2003]— identify the levers of adoption (perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, social influence, facil-itating conditions), and their relevance to healthcare is well documented [Holden and Karsh, 2010, Kühne et al., 2025]. They explain, however, why a system is accepted or rejected rather than what 

should change for a given audience. Value-Sensitive Design (VSD) [Friedman et al., 2013] takes the opposite route: it starts from stakeholders’ values (autonomy, intelligibility, safety, fairness, trans-parency), translates them into concrete design criteria (which information, in what form, at what moment), and involves users throughout the design process. Our methodology follows this logic, 3applied to transparency and carried across a cumulative sequence of workshops rather than a single product. Our aim is not to replace FUTURE-AI [Lekadir et al., 2025], the EU AI Act [European Parlia-ment and Council of the European Union, 2024b], the FMTI [Bommasani et al., 2024], national e-government standards, ISO/IEC 42001 [International Organization for Standardization, 2023] or WHO guidance [World Health Organization, 2021, 2023, 2024], but to operationalise them: to de-termine which of their many provisions a given stakeholder community treats as essential, how that changes with clinical stake, and which concrete implementations they will accept. The pre-sented methodology co-defines AI transparency with a broad set of profiles (clinicians, technical staff, patients, caregivers and students) at the same table, in order to find solutions to operationalise transparency solutions and indicators in health institutions. The protocol borrows established com-ponents (open-ended focus groups and grounded-theory coding, MoSCoW card-sorting, iterative rating as in Delphi studies, and VSD), but combines them differently. Unlike a Delphi or nominal-group process, it is deliberative and mixes lay and expert participants at the same table. We chose this design because AI transparency must be acceptable to non-experts, yet feasible and lawful. 

## 3 Methods 

3.1 Design principles 

Four principles shape the protocol. (P1) Agnostic/open-ended start. The first workshop imposes no framework or technical primer, so that spontaneous stakeholder representations—not the facil-itators’ ontology—seed the indicator set. (P2) Cumulative chain. The six workshops are not in-dependent modules; each consumes the previous output and feeds the next (Figure 1), so priorities set early are stress-tested against scenarios, technical knowledge, law and concrete solutions. (P3) Reliance-oriented elicitation. Requirements are elicited against concrete uses that vary the depth of AI intervention, and are phrased as minimum transparency needed to accept the use , aligning the measure with use-case AI transparency solutions acceptability rather than with abstract approval. 

(P4) Every verdict is a result. Whether a solution is judged satisfactory, in need of rework, or unacceptable, all three are informative for design; the goal is to document what is acceptable, not to validate a predetermined best practice. 

3.2 The six workshops 

In high-pressure environments such as university hospitals, getting time and longitudinal adherence from stakeholders is hard. The 6 workshops are designed to get the maximum valuable information from a minimal timeframe (such as the lunch break), while staying comfortable enough to permit reflection and conversations toward either a consensus or a clearer understanding of the divergent perceptions. 

W1 – Exploration (agnostic and open-ended). Stakeholders answer seven open prompts on sticky notes (what they like/dislike about AI; candidate transparency and comprehension indicators; solu-tions; free remarks), followed by group discussion. A dense pre-workshop questionnaire anchors individual baselines; a post-workshop questionnaire captures cool-down reflections. Verbatims are analysed with open, axial and selective coding in the grounded-theory tradition [Strauss and Corbin, 1990], with three independent coders reconciled collectively as triangulation. Output: a thematic map of spontaneous representations for AI transparency. 

W2 – Prioritisation (card-sort). Candidate indicators are distilled from three external references (the Swiss e-government standard eCH-0272, the EU AI Act [European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024b], and the Foundation Model Transparency Index [Bommasani et al., 2024]) cross-referenced and reduced to a working catalogue, each item tagged with its source. In a simultaneous individual card-sort, participants place each randomly-ordered indicator into a MoSCoW-style tier ( Essential / Useful but not essential / Accessory ) [Clegg and Barker, 1994], with a brief argument round after each card. Two rules govern the sort: “the impossible does not exist” (judge relevance independently of feasibility) and “AI in general” (flag any indicator that partici-pants think must be treated differently by system type). Output: a prioritised catalogue, grouped post-hoc into interactional , algorithmic and social families [Haresamudram et al., 2023]. 4Table 1: The five scenarios span increasing depth of AI intervention in the care pathway. W4 – Effect of technical knowledge. The same instrument is re-administered after a short ( ≤20-minute) briefing on the “black box,” post-hoc explainability [Ribeiro et al., 2016, Lundberg and Lee, 2017, Turbé et al., 2023], interpretable-by-design models [Turbé et al., 2024], retrieval-augmented generation [Lewis et al., 2020] and chain-of-thought [Wei et al., 2022, Korbak et al., 2025], with each method’s limits. Because W4 measures thresholds after participants understand what transparency tools can and cannot deliver, its values (not W3’s) are used to prioritise the final workshop. Output: 

pre/post threshold change following the technical briefing. 

W5 – Legal round table. Rather than a focus group, domain lawyers answer stakeholder questions raised in W3–W4, organised into themes (liability and causation under algorithmic opacity; medical-device status under Medical Device Regulation (MDR) [European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2017]; the limits of legally required explainability [Wachter et al., 2017]; medical vs. industrial secrecy; secondary use of health data and consent [European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2016, 2024a]; hospital governance; expected legal evolution). Output: legal constraints and vocabulary that are then cited inside concrete solutions. 

W6 – Concrete solutions. For each prioritised indicator, the team proposes implementation solutions—some a common baseline (identical across scenarios) and some organised as a per-scenario matrix. Participants vote each solution on a 0/1/2 scale (unacceptable / to rework / sat-isfactory). Participants discussed the proposals in mixed-profile subgroups, assigned a consensus rating to each solution, and could suggest modifications or alternative solutions. Indicators recov-ered at a later stage (data confidentiality; patient information, consent and choice) are evaluated in parallel to decide their adoption and priority. Solutions explicitly encode W5’s legal lessons. Out-put: an acceptability map over concrete solutions, and a final indicator set aligned to FUTURE-AI [Lekadir et al., 2025]. The six-workshop structure was defined in advance, while W1 and subsequent workshop outputs progressively shaped the content and format of downstream workshops. More fundamentally, Work-shops 2–6 were not fixed in advance: they were derived from the themes W1 surfaced. Because W1 is deliberately agnostic and open-ended, it functions as a local needs-assessment whose emergent concerns dictate what the rest of the chain must do—rank them (W2), test their context-dependence (W3), probe the effect of technical understanding (W4), adjudicate their legal dimension (W5) and turn them into concrete solutions (W6). This makes the chain site-relative : at another institution W1 may foreground different dominant concerns, and the downstream workshops should follow. A workshop may be dropped, merged or replaced (a site where algorithmic fairness or reimbursement dominates might add a dedicated audit workshop; a panel indifferent to environmental impact might retire those indicators; a jurisdiction with settled case law might fold the legal round table into a short briefing. What transfers is therefore not the particular six workshops or their numbers but the 

derivation rule : run an agnostic and open-ended elicitation, then let its themes define the chain. We treat W1 as the methodological core and the process. 5Figure 2: W1 coding and transition to W2. 3.3 Analysis and outputs 

The protocol yields both quantitative and qualitative streams: card-sort tier proportions (W2); per-scenario risk and threshold means with non-parametric context tests (W3–W4); pre/post thresh-old deltas (W4); solution-acceptability distributions (W6); and a pre/post participant questionnaire matched by individual identifiers for longitudinal comparison. Each numeric result is paired with a discussion explaining why participants set a threshold or rejected a solution, so that the outputs are actionable rather than merely descriptive. The core methodological principles are not site-specific. The external references at W2 can be swapped for the jurisdiction of interest; the scenarios are templates parameterised by care pathway; the legal round table adapts to local law; and the solution catalogue is meant to be re-authored per institution. The method is specified as a step-by-step facilitator guide with fixed timings, materials and analysis recipes, so a different hospital or a non-clinical GenAI setting can run it with fidelity while adapting content. 

## 4 Use case: a European university hospital 

We instantiated the methodology at Geneva University Hospitals. We stress the framing of Sec-tion 3: with a single self-selected panel these results are an existence proof that the method produces decision-useful evidence, not population estimates. We therefore report effects qualitatively where the sample is small and flag uncertainty explicitly. 6Panel. Of 44 applicants, 31 were retained; to preserve diversity after attrition, four participants joined from W4 onward, and attendance ranged 20–29 per session. The panel spanned 11 health professionals, 4 software developers, 2 communication and 4 administrative staff, 1 researcher, 4 patient partners, 1 informal-caregiver partner, 1 parent partner and 3 medical students, an inter-professional mix by design (§2). At each workshop, participants were divided into two to four mixed-profile focus groups of roughly five to ten people, each session lasting about one hour. Profiles were mixed within groups rather than segregated, so that every subgroup carried several viewpoints; presentation order of materials was counter-balanced across groups where relevant. In the solution-evaluation workshop (W6), each of the four groups was further split into two subgroups, yielding eight subgroups whose votes were pooled. 

Baseline (W1). All respondents (29/29) had used AI personally and 24/29 professionally, yet 13/29 (45%) had never encountered the notion of AI transparency ; nonetheless 25/29 rated it im-portant or very important. Coding of 275 verbatims produced 74 categories under 10 themes; the most cited spontaneous indicators were source provenance, disclosure of AI use, the nature of data used, and the existence of a human review (Figure 2). 

What stakeholders prioritise (W2). From 26 candidates, 18 indicators were retained. The card-sort (Figure 3) shows a clear hierarchy: the ability to request human review of an automated decision 

is essential far above all else (84% Essential, 0% Accessory), followed by explaining the determining factors, documenting training data and stating model confidence (68% each). Environmental-impact indicators sit at the bottom (4% Essential). The ordering places human-in-the-loop and intelligibility (the levers most tied to reliance) above institutional and technical disclosures. 

A reliance-calibrated gradient (W3–W4). Required minimum transparency for adoption rises monotonically with the depth of AI intervention (from informing a patient, to informing a clinician, to triage, diagnosis and treatment; see Figure 4) mirroring a proportionality-to-risk logic [World Health Organization, 2023]. Perceived risk peaks earlier, at triage, while the transparency require-ment keeps climbing toward treatment: the two are related but not identical, and transparency tracks 

consequence for the patient more than momentary risk. The scenarios also surfaced qualitatively distinct concerns that a scalar score hides: anticipated deskilling for the clinician-facing tool (S2) [Budzy´ n et al., 2025], anchoring once a diagnosis is proposed (S4) [Jabbour et al., 2023], and the shift of human oversight from a safety net to a precondition in triage (S3). 

Technical literacy does not reassure (W4). Re-rating the same scenarios after a short ( ≤20-minute) briefing on what explainability methods can and cannot do produced no detectable quanti-tative change: per-scenario shifts were small (about +0 .1 to +0 .2, at most +0 .25 on the 10-point scale) and far below the response dispersion (per-indicator SD ≈2–4), so we do not read them as a genuine change in thresholds (Figure 4 and Table 6). The informative result was qualitative: un-derstanding the possibilities and limits of explainability did not relieve participants’ concerns, and it did not meaningfully change what they asked for. For this audience, transparency needs appear anchored in the clinical stake and the demand for human oversight rather than in technical familiar-ity. This argues against treating literacy training as a substitute for building transparency into the system, and is consistent with automation bias persisting under AI literacy [Qazi et al., 2026]. 

From principles to solutions (W6). Of 151 solution evaluations, 79 were accepted as-is, 44 flagged for discussion, 26 for rework and 2 rejected; 14 reached perfect unanimity. Rejections and rework are as informative as approvals (P4): e.g. a proposal to record AI involvement in the prescription/care plan was rejected, and a right-to-a-human-contact solution weakened precisely at the highest-stake scenarios where participants doubted its feasibility. The two recovered indicators behaved sensibly: data confidentiality escalated to top priority in the more engaging clinical scenar-ios, whereas patient-consent priority was context-dependent. Several accepted solutions carried the legal round table’s fingerprints (data-handling notices, an AI-governance charter, reinforced exter-nal audit). Reading the two workshops together (Figure 3) shows the principle-to-solution link is real but not mechanical: nearly every prioritised indicator also yielded solutions judged acceptable (mean vote ≥1.5 on the 0–2 scale), and human review—top-ranked in W2—was also among the most acceptable. Longitudinal trust. We compare two cross-sectional snapshots: a pre-workshop baseline (T1, 

N = 29 ) and a post-workshop questionnaire (T2, N = 22 ) (not a matched within-participant sam-ple), so these are descriptive signals rather than a paired change (collection incomplete at analysis). Trust in clinical AI stayed moderate at both times: the two middle categories (“moderately” and “fairly confident”) dominated and grew (72.4% at T1 → 90.9% at T2), while the extreme categories emptied and the share reporting low trust was smaller at T2 (20.7% → 9.1%). We read this less as a rise in trust than as a shift toward a more conditional, reasoned trust, which is consistent with the verbatims, where T1 responses stress knowing the sources and the fact that AI is used, whereas T2 responses invoke traceability, auditability, human oversight and system documentation. 

## 5 Discussion 

What the method enabled The use case shows the protocol delivering four things a checklist cannot: (i) a ranked set of requirements anchored in what stakeholders treat as essential; (ii) a 

context function —how the requirement scales with clinical stake—rather than a single threshold; (iii) a pre/post assessment of whether technical information changes requirements, useful for de-8Figure 4: Minimum transparency required to accept each use after the technical briefing (W4, 1–10 scale), per indicator and scenario, with the per-indicator mean, 27 participants. Squares indicate the W2 family (interactional, algorithmic, social). ciding whether to invest in literacy vs. built-in transparency; and (iv) an acceptability map over concrete implementations, including the instructive failures. Each output maps directly onto a de-ployment decision (what to build first, where to demand more, what users will reject), which is what the GenAI4Health adoption agenda needs and what generic transparency guidance leaves under-specified. 

Relation to the reliance literature. The participants’ stated priorities are consistent with findings from the reliance literature: the levers they rank highest (human review, intelligible factors, confi-dence) are those tied to when to trust , and their appetite for transparency grew rather than shrank with technical understanding—echoing that explanation is not a reliability guarantee [Bansal et al., 2021, Buçinca et al., 2021, Jabbour et al., 2023]. Involving students and patients was not tokenistic: trainees’ deskilling fears (S2) and patients’ consent boundaries (S1/S4) shaped the scenario-specific requirements, supporting the case for co-definition (§2). 

## 6 Limitations 

Statistical power : a single site with 20–29 participants per session cannot support inferential gen-eralisation; scenario means and deltas are indicative, and small differences should not be over-read. 

Technical literacy : was probed with a single and very short 20-minute briefing; a longer or dif-ferently delivered intervention might shift requirements, so the null result concerns this dose and format only, and should be studied more specifically. 

Self-selection : volunteers are likely curious about AI. 

Single institution and jurisdiction : priorities and legal framing are country-specific; the method 

transfers, the local outcomes may not. 

Declared thresholds : workshop-elicited minimums are stated preferences, not measured reliance 9behaviour; connecting them to behavioural over-/under-reliance is future work. 

Longitudinal data is incomplete N = 22 out of N = 29 .These limits are the reason we present the contribution as a methodology validated by demonstration rather than as a powered study. 

Future work: within a single institution, the next step closes the loop from stated to behavioural reliance: instrument AI tools already deployed at the hospital with the accepted solutions, measure appropriate-reliance outcomes [Schemmer et al., 2023, Cabitza et al., 2026], and scale elicitation through an institution-wide survey seeded by the workshop catalogue. Across institutions, running the same protocol at multiple sites would show which indicators and perceptions are stable and which vary. Because clinical AI is deployed globally, understanding these regional dependencies (such as culture, legal frameworks, and care local processes) is key to designing transparency that is itself calibrated to the setting, and thus to calibrated reliance on AI tools. 

## 7 Conclusion 

Transparency keeps a central role in trustworthy clinical AI in calibrating reliance, trust and adop-tion. What counts as the right transparency depends on who uses the system, how much is at stake, and where it sits in the workflow. Rather than settle this with a fixed checklist, we built a partic-ipatory methodology that elicits the requirements from the people who act on the system’s output. In our instantiation, 275 agnostic and open-ended stakeholder verbatims were read against three external references (the Swiss e-government standard eCH-0272, the EU AI Act, and the Stan-ford Foundation Model Transparency Index) to assemble a working set of transparency indicators. Participants then rated those indicators across five clinical scenarios of increasing algorithmic in-volvement, before and after a short briefing on what explainability tools can and cannot do. A legal round table tested the requirements against liability, consent and governance, and a final workshop turned the surviving priorities into concrete, votable implementation features. And indicators about environmental cost were consistently set aside next to immediate clinical stakes. The single-site study produced evidence an institution can act on, and two needs stood out. Human review emerged as the highest-priority safeguard in W2 (84% of participants rated it Essential) and it remained highly rated across all five clinical scenarios. The second is proportionality: the mini-mum transparency people asked for rose steadily with the depth of the intervention, from roughly 

6.5/10 for a public information tool to above 7.7/10 for treatment-influencing AI, in line with WHO guidance that scrutiny should follow risk [World Health Organization, 2023]. Two secondary results are worth keeping in view. Teaching participants how explainability methods work neither reas-sured participants nor changed their requirements, so technical literacy seems not to be a substitute for building transparency into the system. Of the 151 concrete solutions evaluated, 79 were ac-cepted without change, and the exercise closed with a catalogue of 20 indicators mapped onto the FUTURE-AI framework [Lekadir et al., 2025]. We therefore offer the six-workshop protocol, and not the particular figures from one hospital, as the part worth reusing. The numbers are local; the way of producing them might be implemented in another healthcare facility. To make that usable, we release the full instruments, the coding scheme and the analysis recipes, so other teams can run the same elicitation in their own setting, adapt the scenarios and legal questions to their jurisdiction, and reach requirements their own stakeholders will actually accept.

## C General format, panel and ethics 

Panel. We recommend roughly 30 participants, split into three to four groups of five to ten, chosen for stakeholder diversity (health professionals, technical staff, administrative/communication staff, patient and caregiver partners, and students/trainees) rather than statistical representativeness. Group size can be adapted to local resources; the invariant is diversity plus small-enough groups for active participation. Each workshop runs 60 minutes. 

Ethics, consent and data management. Ethical and institutional requirements are verified locally before recruitment, and consent follows the procedures of the host institution. Each participant receives a pseudonymous identifier ( p01 , p02 , . . . ); the table linking identities to identifiers is stored separately from research data and accessible only to authorised staff. The same identifier matches pre/post questionnaires without revealing identities in the analysed data. 

Fidelity vs. adaptation. Replicating teams are asked to keep the sequence , the elicitation logic 

(agnostic and open-ended start; minimum-transparency-to-accept framing; before/after technical briefing; “every verdict is a result”) and the analysis recipes, while adapting the content : indicator wording and terminology, the clinical scenarios, the legal material, and the implementation solu-tions. 15 D Facilitator guide (summary)

## J Guidance for multi-site replication 

For a comparable second site we suggest: (1) keep the pseudonymous-ID and pre/post-matching scheme so results can be pooled; (2) reuse the indicator catalogue (Table 5) as the W2 starting set, swapping the national standard for the local one and letting W1 add site-specific candidates; (3) keep the five-scenario spine but re-word illustrative uses to local care pathways; (4) reuse the risk (1–5) and transparency (1–10) scales verbatim so gradients are comparable across sites; (5) localise the W5 legal questions to the relevant jurisdiction. Because the chain is derived from W1, a site may add, merge or drop a workshop (e.g. a dedicated fairness-audit session, or folding the legal round table into a briefing where case law is settled). Reporting the per-indicator W2/W4/W6 values in the format of Tables 5–6 would make cross-site comparison and eventual meta-analysis straightforward. 18
