# An Open-Source ROS 2 Framework for Shared Control in Surgical Teleoperation: Integrating the Haply Inverse3 with the da Vinci Research Kit 

Balint Varga, Member, IEEE, Sebastian Fritz, Gergely Kiss, Kristof Takacs, Peter Galambos, Senior Member, IEEE, 

Abstract —Surgical teleoperation deprives surgeons of touch, forcing visual inference of tissue forces; the commercial platforms now restoring force feedback re-main closed, preventing open research on haptic shared control. This paper provides a system-contribution: it presents an open-source, ROS 2-native framework that integrates the compact, three-degrees-of-freedom (3-DOF) Haply Inverse3 haptic device with the da Vinci Research Kit (dVRK). The framework includes a novel mapping strategy that combines 3-DOF translational device input with separately tracked VerseGrip orien-tation into 6-DOF patient-side manipulator commands. Two virtual-fixture-inspired guidance modes serve as reference implementations to evaluate the framework in a within-subjects user study on a teleoperated hot-wire benchmark task in simulation, and the archi-tecture is further demonstrated on physical dVRK hardware. Relative to unassisted manual control, linear guidance significantly reduced both the number of wire contacts and the task completion time, whereas the progressive-stiffness guidance produced consistent but statistically non-significant trends in the same direction. Subjective ratings of workload, agency, and satisfaction showed favorable descriptive trends for the guidance modes. The results demonstrate the feasi-bility of haptic shared control with a compact device coupled to the dVRK and provide an openly available, reproducible platform for future research. They further suggest that simpler linear feedback may be more robust for inexperienced operators than more complex profiles. 

Index Terms —Haptic Shared Control, Collabora-tive Robotics, Robot-Assisted Surgery, Teleoperation, Da Vinci Surgical System, Haply Inverse3.

Although da Vinci 5 introduces force feedback, the platform remains closed, which limits experimental access to the feedback architecture, custom control designs, and alternative haptic rendering strate-gies. The primary goal of this work is the system integration itself: providing the research community with an open, ROS 2-native solution for using compact haptic devices with the dVRK. The novelty lies in the end-to-end integration of a compact 3-DOF haptic device with a da Vinci-derived teleoperation platform. The haptic shared-control evaluation presented in this paper serves as a means of validating this platform rather than as an end in itself; the evaluated feedback modes are intentionally simple implementations. The hot-wire task is used as a benchmark because it emphasizes path following, speed regulation, and collision avoidance, all of which are relevant to surgical teleoperation. II. State of the Art 

This section reviews the state of the art across the five relevant interrelated themes and identifies the research gaps that motivate the present work. 

A. Haptic Feedback in Robot-Assisted Surgery 

Early controlled experiments demonstrated that restor-ing force feedback to the operator in robot-assisted surgery can reduce tissue-damage errors by a factor of three [15], establishing the fundamental value proposition of haptics in surgical robotics. On the other hand, some studies suggest that 3D stereo vision in da Vinci systems can effectively replace haptic feedback with sufficient train-ing [16]. Subsequent work examined force sensing, display technologies, and the persistent engineering tradeoffs that continue to shape the field [1], while systematic analy-ses identified the stability–transparency tradeoff as the central bottleneck [17]. The instrumentation challenges – miniaturization, biocompatibility, sterilizability, and cost – that explain why earlier commercial systems lacked haptic feedback have been comprehensively surveyed [4]. A meta-analysis synthesizing 56 studies confirmed large effect sizes for haptic feedback across multiple performance dimensions [3]. Despite this strong evidence base, the question of how to move beyond passive force rendering toward cooperative guidance remains open. 

B. From Haptic Feedback to Haptic Shared Control 

A conceptual shift occurred when the research com-munity moved beyond passively reflecting environmental forces and toward actively guiding the operator through cooperative control. Haptic shared control was formalized as a paradigm in which guiding forces at the master device encode a desired trajectory while the human retains override authority [18]; experimental evidence showed that such guidance yields faster, more accurate control with lower effort and reduced visual attention demand. A comprehensive taxonomy later distinguished traded, cooperative, shared, and supervisory control paradigms and provided general axioms for design and evaluation across several domains, including surgery [19]. The benefits of haptic guidance during telemanipulation were experimentally validated even when direct environ-ment feedback is limited [20], positioning shared control as a viable alternative to improving haptic transparency alone. However, guidance inaccuracies can degrade per-formance when model errors are significant [21] - a critical consideration for surgery where anatomical variability lim-its guidance-model fidelity. In the surgical domain, a dual-user framework for hap-tic telesurgical training with a variable authority factor and H∞ robust stability guarantees was proposed early on [22]. Haptic shared control was subsequently extended to dual-arm telerobotic manipulation with passivity-based stability analysis [23], and a broader survey situated these efforts within the wider autonomy landscape of physical human–robot interaction [24]. The dominant mechanism for enforcing shared control in surgical robotics is the virtual fixture, originally intro-duced as a computer-generated perceptual overlay that constrains or guides telerobotic manipulation [25], [26]. Guidance virtual fixtures decompose forces into preferred and non-preferred directions, while forbidden-region vir-tual fixtures prevent workspace boundary violations; the analytical framework addressing stability and passivity for both types remains foundational [27]. A comprehensive survey consolidated the field by covering constraint def-inition, evaluation, and enforcement across domains [28]. Ensuring stable interaction under time-varying impedance has been addressed through energy-tank-based control architectures [29], extended to passive task-prioritized shared control with haptic guidance [30]. On the dVRK, adaptive virtual fixtures with online parameter generation have been demonstrated [31]. These approaches provide a mature control-theoretic toolkit, but their application has been tied to specific device–platform combinations. 

C. Haptic Interfaces for Surgical Teleoperation 

The quality of haptic shared control is fundamentally constrained by the master device through which the sur-geon perceives and generates forces. The Force Dimension sigma.7 – a 7-DOF device with 20 N Cartesian force capability, gravity compensation, and fully actuated trans-lation, rotation, and grasping – set the standard [32] and has become the most widely used research-grade haptic master for surgical teleoperation. A direct comparison of the sigma.7, the 3D Systems HD 2, and the SensAble PHANToM Premium 3.0 showed that device kinematics resembling the human upper extremity improve teleop-eration performance [33]. Kinematic redundancy in hap-tic masters reduces apparent inertia and improves soft-tissue stiffness discrimination [34], while cutaneous and tactile interfaces offer stability advantages over grounded kinesthetic devices [35]. Despite this landscape, the set of haptic devices actually integrated with surgical teleopera-tion platforms remains remarkably narrow, dominated by the sigma.7 and the PHANToM family. More generally, using generic 6-DOF devices as human–machine interfaces can be challenging for task-specific robotic applications because their kinematics are not necessarily matched to those of the controlled robot [36]. Compact 3-DOF haptic devices may therefore offer a more adaptable alternative for selected surgical teleoperation tasks, motivating the present work, which integrates and evaluates such a device with a surgical robotic platform. 

D. User Study Design and Evaluation Methodology 

Rigorous evaluation of haptic shared control in surgical contexts demands standardized tasks, validated instru-ments, and careful experimental design – yet the literature reveals considerable inconsistency in tasks, metrics, and participant populations [3]. A single-blinded randomized controlled study with residents performing both simulated and live bariatric surgeries demonstrated significantly lower NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) workload scores with haptic feedback [37]. Sensorless haptic feed-back on the dVRK has been evaluated in palpation and peg-transfer tasks using NASA-TLX, interaction forces, and completion times [38], while the combination of haptic feedback with haptic guidance has been assessed using both NASA-TLX and the System Usability Scale [39]. Multi-modal feedback on the da Vinci IS1200 achieved approximately 50% grip force reduction [40], and skin-deformation tactile feedback was compared against force feedback and combined modalities [41]. While these studies represent methodological best prac-tice, evaluation tasks vary widely, making cross-study comparison difficult. The hot-wire task – a well-established precision benchmark in haptics and teleoperation with a clear binary error metric and straightforward completion-time measurement – has not yet been employed as a bench-mark for haptic shared control in surgical teleoperation. 

E. The da Vinci Research Kit as Integration Platform 

The dVRK repurposes retired first-generation da Vinci hardware with custom field-programmable gate array-based electronics and open-source software, providing re-searchers at over 40 institutions with complete access to all control levels of a clinical-grade surgical robot [12]. A comprehensive review catalogued the first decade of dVRK research spanning haptic feedback, bilateral teleoperation, virtual fixtures, force sensing, and external haptic device integration [13]. Closing the haptic loop requires either adding sen-sors or estimating interaction forces. A non-invasive trocar-embedded sensor was proposed that measures instrument-tissue forces without modifying the surgical instrument [42], while sensorless four-channel bilateral teleoperation using deep neural networks for dynamics identification has been shown to achieve high-performance haptic teleoperation without hardware modifications [43]. On the master side, the dVRK’s original master tool manipulators have been replaced with sigma.7 devices, and a user study demonstrated that force reflection signif-icantly improves palpation and incision performance [44]. Published studies coupling an external haptic device to the dVRK remain very limited. Two observations follow: the dVRK ecosystem remains tightly coupled to a small set of established haptic devices without a ROS 2-native mod-ular integration, and the vast majority of dVRK haptic studies focus on rendering environment forces rather than providing cooperative guidance forces in a shared-control paradigm. 

F. Research Gaps 

The literature shows substantial progress in haptic feed-back and shared-control methods, yet critical gaps remain. First, dVRK studies are dominated by established master devices like the sigma.7 and PHANToM family [33], [43], [44], while compact devices such as the Haply Inverse3 lack systematic integration. Moreover, existing integrations rely on legacy middleware rather than modular ROS 2-native architectures. Second, most research focuses on reflecting environment forces for transparency rather than cooperative haptic guidance [38], [39], [44]. Although ma-ture control-theoretic tools exist [18], [27], [28], [31], their application to real-time assistance with compact haptic devices remains insufficiently explored. Third, evaluation methodology is fragmented across diverse tasks, compli-cating cross-study comparison [3]. Specifically, the hot-wire task has not been systematically used to evaluate hap-tic shared control. Collectively, these gaps indicate a need for a unified framework combining accessible hardware, cooperative strategies, and standardized benchmarks. These limitations of the state of the art motivate and necessitate this work. The following sections detail the system architecture and experimental results required to bridge these identified limitations.

In addition, a mapping strategy was implemented to enable haptic shared control of the dVRK using the Inverse3 equipped with the VerseGrip orientation tracking addon, allowing for both force-based and position-based control modes. IV. Design of the Reference Guidance Modes 

In the following, we present two guidance modes for the hot-wire task: M1, which provides linear guidance based on the distance to the desired trajectory, and M2, which increases the stiffness of the feedback force as the user approaches the target path.

Linear and Progressive Stiffness Characteristics 1) Mode 1: The linear force feedback mode (M1) applies a restoring force proportional to the effective penetration distance d, measured from the inner boundary of the haptic zone di to the wire surface. The maximum force is limited by the outer effective radius do. The force increases monotonically as the operator moves further into the zone, providing continuous guidance back toward the wire center: 

F = k d ˆn − b v. (8) where F is the force applied to the user, k is a constant stiffness coefficient, and d is the distance from the desired trajectory. V. User Study 

The user study is designed to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed haptic shared control strategies under limited spatial visualization, since only a standard 2D monitor is used for visual feedback. Two implementations of the shared control strategy are evaluated, referred to as M1 and M2. The manual control mode without active haptic guidance is denoted M0 and serves as the baseline. 

A. Working Hypotheses 

The working hypotheses for the user study are as fol-lows:  

> •

H1: The haptic shared control strategies (M1 and M2) lead to a significant reduction in the number of contacts with the wire compared to the manual control mode (M0).  

> •

H2: The haptic shared control strategies (M1 and M2) result in significantly shorter task completion times compared to the manual control mode (M0).  

> •

H3: A haptic shared control strategy leads to sig-nificantly better subjective user experience ratings compared to the manual control mode (M0): The cognitive workload, the sense of agency, and overall satisfaction are significantly improved under haptic shared control. 

B. Study Design 

Twenty test subjects (7 female, 13 male) were recruited from a pool of volunteers, ranging in age from 25 to 65 years. Prior experience with teleoperation systems varied across participants, from none to advanced, providing a heterogeneous sample. The study followed a within-subjects design in which each participant completed the hot-wire task under all three control modes (M0, M1, M2). To mitigate learning effects, the order of modes was counterbalanced across participants using a balanced Latin square arrangement. Prior to the actual runs, the participants underwent a five-minute training session for each mode, resulting in a total familiarization time of 15 minutes per participant before data collection began. Visual feedback was provided throughout the task via a color change of the wire upon contact, indicating a collision event. No additional auditory or haptic warning was given at the moment of contact beyond the mode-specific haptic rendering already active in M1 and M2. Visual feedback was restricted to a standard 2D monitor, providing no stereoscopic depth cues to the participants. It should be noted that the ring used in the hot-wire task may become disengaged from the wire during the trial. In such cases, no haptic feedback is rendered until the participant manually repositions the ring onto the wire, as the shared control forces depend on proximity to the wire geometry. This results in a temporary interruption of the haptic feedback loop and was handled consistently across all three control modes. All participants took part voluntarily and provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the ethics commit-tee, approval no. 596. Participants were informed of the purpose and procedure of the study and of their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. No personally identifiable information was recorded, and all data were anonymized prior to analysis. The simulated study used the same software stack as the physical system in Section VII: the Haply driver, the Haply–dVRK bridge with the joint-space mapping, the guidance modes of Section IV, and the PSM1 command interface. Only the physical PSM and the endoscopic camera were replaced, by a kinematic model of the PSM and a virtual hot-wire environment rendered in RViz. The study therefore evaluates the complete integration chain except the PSM dynamics and the physical camera. 

C. Experimental Setup Details 

The hot-wire benchmark used in the user study is defined by the following specifications. The Haply Inverse3 haptic device is capable of generating forces between 0 N and 1 .2 N, see [14]; this theoretical range informed the selection of guidance stiffness values for the shared-control strategies. The wire itself has a total length of 0 .21 m and is divided into five straight segments. The first, second, third, fourth, and fifth segments measure 0 .04 m, 0 .03 m, 0 .05 m, 0 .05 m, and 0 .04 m, respectively. These dimensions that reproduce the physical prototype illustrated in Fig. 5. A guiding ring attached to the wire possesses a diameter of 0 .02 m. Control-mode parameters are defined as follows.  

> •

Mode 0 (baseline): Manual control without guidance forces, i.e., F = 0. 

> •

Mode 1 (linear guidance): Restoring force according to (8) with constant stiffness k = 80 N/m and viscous damping coefficient b = 2 N s/m.  

> •

Mode 2 (progressive-stiffness guidance): Restoring force according to (9)-(11) and with piecewise-linear stiffness, i.e., k1 = 40 N/m in the inner region and 

k2 = 120 N/m in the outer region, and the same viscous damping coefficient b = 2 N s/m. The virtual-fixture zone is bounded by an inner radius 

di = 0, an outer radius do = 0 .015 m, and a midpoint 

dm = ( di + do)/2. The stiffness values k1 = 40 N/m and 

k2 = 120 N/m are derived analytically to guarantee that the maximum force at the outer boundary matches the linear guidance force of Mode 1. These numeric values ensure comparable maximum forces across the two guidance strategies while allowing distinct force-field characteristics within the haptic zone.

VI. Results 

H1 and H2 were assessed using the two objective mea-sures: number of contacts and task completion time. The lack of significance for Mode 2 cannot be attributed solely to statistical power; rather, it indicates that increased control complexity may negate the benefits of progressive guidance in novice operators. This aligns with the subjective reports, and suggests that simpler linear guidance may be more robust for users with limited teleoperation experience. Beyond the specific guidance profiles, these results primarily validate the platform: the integrated system rendered stable haptic guidance through a compact 3-DOF device coupled to the dVRK, and its open-source architecture allows alternative guidance laws to be sub-stituted without modifying the integration layer. Descrip-tively higher agency and satisfaction ratings under haptic guidance support this interpretation. Planned system-level improvements include a stereoscopic VR display, user-adjustable force-feedback intensity, real-time motion scal-ing with singularity avoidance, and grip-integrated mode switching on the VerseGrip. 

B. Limitations 

Some limitations should be considered when interpret-ing these results. The hot-wire benchmark evaluates tele-operation primitives (path following, collision avoidance, speed regulation), not surgical skills; the results must therefore not be interpreted as validation of surgical performance. Visual feedback was monocular and 2D, unlike stereoscopic clinical consoles; the missing depth cues likely increased the reliance on haptic cues, so the measured benefit of guidance may be larger than in a stereoscopic setup. Conversely, the heterogeneous prior experience of the participants and the short, fixed train-ing phase may have increased inter-subject variance and introduced within-session learning effects that counterbal-ancing cannot fully remove; in this respect, the between-mode differences are conservative estimates. Finally, since no subjective comparison survived Bonferroni correction, the subjective ratings constitute descriptive trends rather than confirmatory evidence. Future studies should employ homogeneous or covariate-adjusted samples, extended or criterion-based training, stereoscopic visualization, and surgically representative tasks. 

VIII. Preliminary Integration on Physical da Vinci Hardware 

At the Antal Bejczy Center for Intelligent Robotics, the proposed haptic shared control framework was integrated with a real dVRK setup, enabling a preliminary demon-stration in a physical teleoperation scenario. The integra-tion process involved interfacing the Haply Inverse3 and the VerseGrip with the dVRK control software, allowing for real-time haptic feedback during teleoperation tasks. Initial tests were conducted using a simplified teleopera-tion task in which the end-effector was guided along a wire trajectory. During these initial tests, the same control-loop ar-chitecture as in the simulation study was used, and the system operated without instability or watchdog-triggered force interruption. Regarding quantitative performance: (i) Control-loop frequency: although the Haply device sup-ports a haptic refresh rate of up to 4 kHz, we matched the controller rate to the dVRK control loop at 1 kHz. (ii) Tracking accuracy: the dVRK’s positioning accuracy is in the range of 1 mm to 2 mm depending on hardware, tools, and workspace position, whereas the Inverse3 posi-tion resolution is 0 .01 mm according to the manufacturer; the tracking accuracy of the overall system is therefore dominated by the robot (slave) side. (iii) Communication delay: without direct delay measure-ments, we can state that the integration did not noticeably increase the generally acceptable maximum latency of approximately 200 ms of the telemanipulation system. (iv) Stability: the viscous damping component was in-cluded to suppress oscillatory behavior; during the phys-ical experiments, no oscillatory instability or watchdog-triggered force interruption was observed. An initial video demonstration of the integrated sys-tem is available online 2, showcasing the real-time haptic feedback and control capabilities of the system. The video illustrates the operator manipulating the haptic device while receiving force feedback corresponding to interac-tions with the virtual environment, demonstrating the feasibility of the proposed haptic shared control framework in a real-world setting. In Figure 5, the demonstration setup is shown, highlighting the physical setup of the dVRK and the Haply Inverse3 haptic device during the teleoperation task. 

IX. Conclusion and Outlook 

This paper presented a haptic shared-control frame-work for surgical teleoperation based on the integration of the Haply Inverse3 with the da Vinci Research Kit. The system combines a ROS 2-based software architecture with mapping strategies and virtual-fixture-inspired hap-tic guidance for a teleoperated hot-wire task. The user study showed that linear haptic guidance improved per-formance over manual control, yielding fewer wire contacts and shorter completion times. In contrast, the progressive-stiffness guidance produced consistent but statistically non-significant improvements in the same direction, sug-gesting that the potential benefits of more complex guid-ance profiles may not outweigh their added perceptual and cognitive demands, at least for novice operators. The framework was also integrated with a physical dVRK setup, demonstrating technical feasibility, although quan-titative hardware validation remains future work. Future studies will examine cognitive load, task diffi-culty, and adaptive assistance strategies. Formal interac-tion models, such as ordinal potential differential games, may support adaptive shared control. Further work will focus on stereoscopic visualization, larger homogeneous user groups, quantitative comparison of simulated and physical setups, and cognitive-load-aware haptic guidance.
