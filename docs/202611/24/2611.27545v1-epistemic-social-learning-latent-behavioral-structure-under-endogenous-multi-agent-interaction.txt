# Abstract 

In multi-agent interaction, a learner’s actions shape the observations from which it infers other agents’ behavior. A behavioral model may predict these observa-tions well yet support poor adaptation when opponents change. We introduce 

Epistemic Social Learning (ESL), which couples fast, interaction-specific Bayesian beliefs with slower online learning of population-shared behavioral prototypes. Belief-conditioned decisions influence subsequent observations and, in turn, the representations learned from them. Under explicit regularity and averaging as-sumptions, we provide a conditional stochastic-approximation characterization of these coupled dynamics. Across twelve synthetic 3 × 3 cooperative, coordi-nation, and adversarial games, ESL achieves the lowest aggregate post-switch regret among the evaluated online latent-adaptation methods, including a switch-aware Bayesian filter. Additional experiments examine observation loss, noise, and asymmetric incentives. These findings illustrate how predictive fit can differ from decision-relevant adaptation under policy-dependent interaction. 

# 1 Introduction 

Adaptive agents interact with partners whose behavior varies and changes. Beyond predicting the next action, they must learn behavioral structure that remains useful as interactions evolve. Multi-agent observations are endogenous: beliefs shape actions, which determine observed responses and update subsequent beliefs. Thus, the learner helps generate its own training data. This feedback creates a subtle failure mode. A behavioral representation can fit the observations generated under the current interaction policy yet omit distinctions that become important after behavior changes. For example, two latent behaviors may appear effectively identical under the actions currently taken by the learner, while implying different best responses once the interaction shifts. Predictive fit and decision-relevant adaptation can therefore come apart. This motivates our central question: how should agents learn shared latent behavioral structure when their own policies influence the evidence available for learning it? 

We propose Epistemic Social Learning (ESL), a two-timescale framework for this setting. ESL maintains learnable, population-shared behavioral prototypes: parameterized components of a behav-ioral model, not prespecified ground-truth opponent types. Their parameters evolve slowly across interactions, while fast, interaction-specific Bayesian beliefs express uncertainty over these com-ponents. Agents choose belief-conditioned logit responses, creating a closed loop in which beliefs shape policies, policies generate observations, and those observations update both beliefs and the shared representation. The resulting structure allows experience to be pooled across interactions while retaining opponent-specific uncertainty. ESL combines established Bayesian modeling and online representation learning in policy-dependent interaction. We characterize its coupled dynamics conditionally using controlled-Markov stochastic

# 2 Related Work 

Latent behavior and opponent modeling. Classical Bayesian opponent modeling maintains be-liefs over candidate models [2]; cognitive-hierarchy approaches describe levels of strategic reason-ing [9, 28, 29]. DRON and DPIQN learn policy-conditioned opponent representations [15, 16], whereas EM/HMM methods represent heterogeneity through latent states [11, 14]. These approaches distinguish belief inference from learning behavioral structure, but do not by themselves specify how an online shared library changes alongside interaction-specific beliefs. Switch-aware filtering and Bayesian online changepoint detection [1] also address nonstationarity. Our SA-FP comparator uses fixed emissions; learned-emission HMMs and changepoint comparators remain future work. Related decision-dependent distribution shifts are studied in performative prediction [24]; ESL focuses on shared behavioral prototypes and opponent-specific inference in repeated interaction. 

In-context and representation-based adaptation. Learned strategy spaces [20] and recurrent or theory-of-mind models [25, 23] support opponent adaptation. OEOM [17] constructs a pretrained opponent population; OMIS [18] uses separately trained components and decision-time search. These systems can draw on models trained before evaluation, whereas ESL learns its shared prototypes from the evaluated interaction stream. Both are applicable in principle, but a controlled comparison would need to match pretraining, inference, and decision-time resources. We make no superiority claim over these systems; resource-matched sequential comparisons remain open. 

Multi-agent learning and strategic adaptation. Fictitious play [8], follow-the-regularized-leader (FTRL), and mirror descent [22] study policy-level learning in repeated interaction. Optimistic mirror descent has been analyzed for convergence under particular game assumptions [3]. LOLA [12] antici-pates other agents’ learning updates, while recursive-reasoning methods [30, 31] model higher-order beliefs. ESL instead couples online learning of shared behavioral prototypes with interaction-specific Bayesian inference and policy-dependent observations. Its stochastic-approximation characterization is conditional, not an equilibrium or no-regret guarantee.

# 4 Method: Epistemic Social Learning 

ESL decomposes the circularity of social learning into two coupled timescales: fast epistemic belief inference per interaction, and slow shared prototype adaptation across interactions. Intuitively, beliefs can respond immediately to each newly observed opponent signal, whereas the shared prototype dictionary aggregates belief-weighted evidence across several interactions before its next update. The two timescales are an architectural separation of update frequencies; a fixed small batch with constant step sizes is not automatically an asymptotic two-timescale convergence regime. Full details are provided in Appendix A. Theorem 1 characterizes limiting behavior without assuming a fixed data distribution. Its ICT sets represent self-consistent regimes of the coupled belief–policy–prototype dynamics and may include recurrent behavior rather than a single fixed point. Game-theoretically, each agent samples a belief-conditioned logit response to its learned prediction of the other agent’s behavior. At finite inverse temperature λ, this is a quantal response; as λ → ∞ , it concentrates on utility-maximizing responses to the learned prediction, which need not equal the other agent’s actual strategy. The theorem is therefore not a Nash-convergence result. Stationary fixed points may admit SCE-like and, under logit response, QRE-like interpretations under additional consistency conditions, but ESL has no general guarantee of convergence to Nash, SCE, or QRE. Under uniqueness of the invariant measure and global stability, the iterates converge almost surely to a fixed point (Appendix A). The theorem concerns asymptotic interaction dynamics rather than exact recovery of ground-truth latent types (Appendix A.8); Appendix F discusses the game-theoretic interpretation in more detail. 

Theory versus practice. Theorem 1 applies to the diminishing-step, regularized Robbins–Monro regime under Assumptions 1–5, including the finite-batch averaging condition in Assumption 4. The main experiments instead use constant prototype-update steps, small fixed batches ( M = 2 in the headline adaptation protocol), and no explicit ℓ2 prototype regularization. Constant steps permit continued prototype updates after later behavioral shifts, while small batches allow frequent updates from newly observed signals. This practical regime is not covered by the theorem: constant steps violate Assumption 2, the boundedness lemma uses nonzero regularization, and fixed M does not by itself establish Assumption 4. Theorem 1 therefore provides no convergence, boundedness, or finite-time guarantee for the headline PSR/DPR/LTE experiments. The diminishing-step experiment in Appendix E is a theorem-facing diagnostic, not a verification of the theorem’s assumptions. 

# 6 Experiments 

Experimental philosophy and comparator selection. We evaluate one focal learner against three switching opponent identities ( N = 4 ) on twelve fixed 3 × 3 games (four each cooperative, coordination, and adversarial). We use A = K = 3 , T = 1000 , H = 100 , switches every 200 rounds plus hazard 0.01 , and ten seeds per game ( n = 120 matched cells). Methods load identical full-precision benchmark JSONs without resampling; construction and per-game results are in Appendix C.6. 6.2 Mechanistic Analysis 

We evaluate calibrated exogenous behavioral shifts on the twelve games (Figure 3; Appendix D.1). A single shift at t = 100 is followed by a 100-round PSR window, so regret magnitudes differ from the headline protocol. The small, medium, and large shifts have total-variation (TV) distances of 

0.10 , 0.30 , and 0.70 , with Jensen–Shannon divergences of 0.010 , 0.052 , and 0.310 . They change the oracle best response in 5, 10, and 26 of 36 game–rotation conditions, respectively. This separates the magnitude of distributional change from its decision-relevant effect. ESL’s advantage is clearest for large shifts, whereas Online EM has the lowest medium-shift mean. This exogenous-shift diagnostic neither isolates a causal mechanism nor directly tests Proposition 1.

# 7 Conclusion 

ESL couples interaction-specific Bayesian beliefs with slowly learned shared behavioral prototypes under policy-dependent observations. We give a conditional stochastic-approximation characteri-zation of the resulting closed-loop dynamics. On twelve synthetic 3 × 3 games, ESL achieves the lowest aggregate post-switch regret among the evaluated online latent-adaptation methods, although SA-FP achieves lower prediction regret. These findings distinguish decision-relevant adaptation from predictive fit; they do not establish convergence or performance guarantees for sequential-state or adversarial co-learning. 

Limitations. Our evaluation remains restricted to structured synthetic repeated normal-form games with a focal learner facing exogenous switching opponents; it does not validate complex sequential environments such as Overcooked, mixed adaptive co-learning populations, or adversarial non-exploitability. The categorical prototype implementation does not directly extend to high-dimensional sequential state spaces, although state-conditioned prototype policies are a possible untested direction. The opponent-count and population-recovery experiments reported in Appendix C.2 assess tracking across identities and latent recovery, not scalability to sequential high-dimensional state spaces. SA-FP uses frozen random emissions; stronger comparators include learned-emission/transition HMMs, changepoint methods, and resource-matched pretrained in-context systems such as OEOM and OMIS. The held-out OMD experiment has limited tuning and does not compare strategic ESL–OMD co-learning. Theorem 1 is conditional on regularity and finite-batch averaging assumptions, including an unverified joint-process averaging condition, and applies to a diminishing-step, regularized Robbins– Monro regime, not the constant-step, unregularized, M = 2 empirical implementation. Finally, K is fixed within each run; adaptive prototype counts and richer continuous, hierarchical, or fully online representations remain future work.

The use of buffered beliefs bsnap introduces a discrepancy between the finite-batch empirical update and an invariant-measure-averaged direction, represented by the tracking bias βm. Assumption 4 requires this discrepancy to become negligible on the stochastic-approximation timescale. Under the stronger law-level geometric-mixing condition developed above, ∥βm∥ = O(M −1 

> m

), and a suitable growing-batch schedule establishes the required window-sum condition. A fixed M generally leaves a bias bound that does not vanish with m and is therefore not covered by this argument. Larger batches improve averaging but reduce responsiveness to recent behavioral shifts. The main experiments deliberately use small fixed batches and constant steps for finite-horizon adaptation; they are outside the asymptotic guarantee. Persistent interaction and strictly positive likelihoods help maintain informative updates, but they do not by themselves establish irreducibility or mixing of the complete joint belief process. 20 A.7 Interpretation of ICT Limit Sets 

Theorem 1 characterizes the limiting behavior of ESL through internally chain transitive (ICT) sets of the differential inclusion (15). In the present setting, these ICT sets correspond to self-consistent regimes of the endogenous belief–policy–data feedback loop: for a given Θ, the fast belief dynamics induce invariant distributions over belief states; these invariant distributions determine averaged update directions in G(Θ) ; and the resulting prototype parameters remain consistent with the data distribution generated by the induced policies. Thus, ICT sets serve as generalized equilibrium objects for ESL under endogenous, potentially non-ergodic dynamics. When the invariant measure is unique, the set-valued dynamics collapse to an ordinary differential equation, in which case the ICT limit sets coincide with invariant sets of the associated ODE, and reduce to fixed points under additional stability conditions. 

A.8 Identifiability of Learned Prototypes 

The convergence result characterizes the limiting behavior of the ESL dynamics. It does not, by itself, imply recovery of any ground-truth behavioral types. Prototype recovery is an identifiability question. When synthetic ground-truth prototypes {θ⋆k}Kk=1 exist, a local separation condition is that there exist neighborhoods Nk of each θ⋆k and an ϵ > 0 such that, for all distinct k, k ′ ∈ [K],

inf  

> θ∈N k, θ ′∈N k′

∥Lk(· | θ) − Lk′ (· | θ′)∥TV ≥ ϵ. 

This condition rules out observationally indistinguishable prototypes locally. Recovery up to permu-tation would require additional identifiability, mixing, and stability conditions that are not proved here. The empirical adaptation and prototype-recovery diagnostics examine different outcomes; they do not establish general recovery or show that identifiability is unnecessary for effective adaptation. Our main theorem instead concerns dynamical limit sets of the coupled belief–prototype process. It is important to distinguish the ICT characterization from classical game-theoretic equilibrium notions. An ICT set need not be a fixed point: it can include limit cycles or more complex recurrent sets, all of which are consistent with the chain-recurrence structure of the differential inclusion. What ICT sets guarantee is internal chain transitivity — any two points in the set are connected by arbitrarily fine pseudo-orbits that remain within the set. This is precisely the topological notion of recurrence that stochastic approximation iterates inherit from their limiting flow [4]. In the ESL setting, the ICT structure arises naturally because the endogenous data distribution need not produce a unique invariant regime: different interaction histories can stabilize different belief– prototype configurations. The theorem makes no claim that all such configurations are equally useful for adaptation; that is an empirical question examined in Section 6.

Consequently, DPR is exactly invariant ( 100 .474 ) across this sweep, while changing λ alters the focal logit policy and its PSR (Table 4). The lower PSR at larger λ is a result of this controlled protocol, not a general closed-loop co-learning claim. In particular, the λ = 2 PSR of 158 .44 here differs from the headline 162 .97 because the experiments use different random streams, even though they share the game suite and nominal parameters.

Game-theoretic interpretation of limit sets. The internally chain transitive (ICT) sets identified by Theorem 1 admit a natural game-theoretic interpretation. Agents act through belief-conditioned logit best-response policies and update their beliefs from realized interactions. The limiting objects of ESL therefore do not necessarily coincide with Nash equilibria. Instead, they are better understood as self-consistent behavioral regimes under bounded rationality [27], in which beliefs, induced policies, prototype parameters, and the resulting data distribution are mutually compatible under the endogenous learning dynamics. The differential inclusion (5) governs the slow-timescale dynamics, and its ICT sets characterize invariant regimes of the system. Fixed points correspond to stationary self-consistent configurations, while more general ICT sets may capture recurrent, non-equilibrium behavior induced by the belief– policy feedback loop. Thus, the theorem characterizes an asymptotic ICT limit set, which need not be a single fixed point. At a fixed point Θ∗, there exists an invariant measure μ∗ ∈ I (Θ ∗) such that 

gμ∗ (Θ ∗) = 0 ,

meaning that the prototype parameters are stationary under a data distribution that they themselves help induce. This admits an Self-Confirming Equilibrium (SCE)-like interpretation [13]: beliefs need only be consistent with observations generated along the realized path of play. Nash equilibrium requires each player’s strategy to be a best response to opponents’ actual strategies. By contrast, SCE permits best responses to beliefs consistent with observed play, even when those beliefs are incorrect about unobserved contingencies. Because agents select actions through logit best responses, stationary ESL configurations also admit a Quantal Response Equilibrium (QRE)-like interpretation [21]. However, they are not generally QRE, because responses may be based on learned, self-confirming beliefs rather than the true strategy profile. Under additional conditions ensuring correct beliefs, mutual consistency, and convergence to a stationary fixed point, the connection to standard logit QRE becomes exact. More general ICT sets need not admit a standard equilibrium interpretation. Instead, they correspond to persistent behavioral regimes in which the system moves through a sequence of mutually compatible configurations. This highlights that endogenous interaction may generate non-convergent yet stable learning dynamics. 

Regret and strategic scope. ESL is not proved to achieve vanishing external or swap regret, and low PSR implies neither property. Conversely, low long-run regret can coexist with substantial regret immediately following a behavioral change, which is the local adaptation cost that PSR is designed to measure. Our experiments use committed switching policies rather than counterparts that adapt to ESL’s learning state. We therefore make no robustness guarantee for such adaptive-counterpart settings. 

# G Representational and Computational Structure 

We distinguish storage for the shared behavioral representation from storage and computation associated with individual interactions. 

Representation and belief storage. In the categorical model, the K population-shared prototypes require O(K|A| ) parameters, independent of N for fixed K and |A| . Beliefs for P tracked ordered agent pairs require an additional O(P K ) storage. Full directed-pair tracking has P = N (N − 1) ,whereas one focal agent tracking its opponents has P = N − 1. Prototype sharing avoids a separate parameterized behavioral dictionary for every pair, but not pair-specific belief storage. The prototype count is fixed within each run and varied in Appendix C.7. 

Per-batch computation. For a batch of Brec observed records, direct dense evaluation of belief-weighted categorical prototype gradients takes O(Brec K|A| ) operations. This is an implementation-level upper bound for the gradient calculation, not necessarily a tight bound for optimized aggregation. Bayesian belief updates, action selection, and interaction processing incur further work. Total 35 computation thus depends on the number of encounters, not only on the shared prototype parameters. Neither total storage nor total computational cost is independent of population size.
