# A Comparison of Heuristic, Human-Sensitive, and Self-Play DRL Policies in Human–Agent Teaming 

Ayman bin Kamruddin, Christopher Lam, Gaurav Patil, Mirco Musolesi, Mario di Bernardo, Michael J. Richardson 

Abstract —Dynamical Perceptual-Motor Primitives (DPMPs) offer a tractable framework for generating human-like movement dynamics in artificial agents (AAs) and have been used to support coordination in human-AI teaming tasks. Traditionally, DPMP controllers rely on heuristic (rule-based) decision policies derived from human behavior to guide goal selection and action sequencing. Although effective in structured tasks, such heuristics can be difficult to generalize to more complex settings, motivating interest in deep reinforcement learning (DRL) approaches for learning decision policies for human-AI interaction. A key chal-lenge, however, is ensuring that DRL policies remain aligned with human strategies, particularly when agents are trained through self-play. To examine this issue, we compared three target-selection policies governing DPMP-based AAs in a first-person herding task: (1) a human-derived heuristic policy, (2) a self-play DRL policy (SP-DRL), and (3) a human-sensitive DRL policy (HS-DRL) trained alongside a heuristic agent. Results showed that heuristic agents most closely matched typical human–human coordination patterns, followed by HS-DRL agents, whereas SP-DRL agents deviated most. These findings highlight the importance of human-informed training when developing DRL-based teammates and suggest that simple heuristic policies may remain an effective approach for designing human-aligned AAs capable of human–AI collaboration. 

Index Terms —human–agent teaming, dynamical perceptual-motor primitives, deep reinforcement learning, human-AI align-ment, heuristic decision policies.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Many real-world tasks require people to coordinate their actions with others in real time under evolving environmental constraints. Concerted success hinges on anticipating oth-ers’ actions and intentions [1], organizing movement spatio-temporally [2], and adapting behavior as interactions unfold [3]. These demands are increasingly relevant as artificial agents (AAs) are introduced into roles alongside humans. Accordingly, growing research in human-agent teaming (HAT) examines whether AAs can exhibit coordination patterns simi-lar to those observed in human-human interaction [4]. In such settings, effective teaming extends beyond task completion, depending on whether an AA’s behavior is predictable, legible, and compatible with human expectations [5]. Modeling human-like behavior in AAs can be framed as a hierarchical control problem, separating action dynamics (i.e., controlling how to move) from higher-order action decisions (i.e., determining which action to perform and when) [6]. From a dynamical systems perspective, behavior emerges through continuous coupling between agents and the environment [7]. Within this framework, dynamical perceptual-motor primi-tives (DPMPs)—elementary sets of low-dimensional nonlinear functions—provide a compact architecture for modeling the continuous dynamics of goal-directed movement [8]–[10]. DPMP models have been widely used to generate human-like behaviors in both solo and multiagent tasks, such as navigation [11]–[13]. When serving as the action dynamics architecture of AA [14], DPMPs specify how an agent moves towards a selected goal state but not which goal or subgoal state to pursued when multiple options are available. Modeling such decision policies is substantially more challenging, as action selection must account for the evolving behavior of other agents and changing environmental constraints [5], [15]. One approach to modeling AA action decisions is to derive heuristic (rule-based) policies from observed human decisions [13], [16]. Such heuristics are interpretable and often align well with human decision patterns, supporting predictable coordination in HAT [5], [14]. However, they can struggle to generalize to more complex or novel task configurations, limiting their flexibility in dynamic settings [16]. As an alternative, deep reinforcement learning (DRL) learns optimized decision polices through simulation-based reward maximization [17]. A common training paradigm is self-play, where agents learn by interacting with copies of themselves rather than human data [6]. While SP-trained agents can achieve high task performance, their strategies often diverge from typical human behavior, reducing suitability for HAT arXiv:2611.23747v1 [cs.MA] 18 Nov 2026 JOURNAL OF L ATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021 2

[18]. Human-sensitive DRL has therefore been proposed to incorporate human behavioral models during training, encour-aging policies that better align with human strategies [19]. The present study builds on prior work [13] and related work currently under review [20], which introduced a first-person herding task for examining action dynamics and decisions in multi-target environments. There, a DPMP-based locomotion model governed by a human-derived heuristic target-selection policy effectively reproduced human-like herding behavior. Extending this approach, we used the same task to exam-ine how different decision-policy designs shape coordination dynamics in human–AA dyads. Specifically, we compared policies: (1) a heuristic policy [13], (2) a Self-Play DRL (SP-DRL) policy, and (3) a Human-Sensitive DRL (HS-DRL) policy trained alongside the Heuristic AA. Because all AAs used the same DPMP controller, the study isolated the effect of decision-policy design on human-AI teaming behavior. In particular, we evaluated whether HS-DRL improves human-alignment between DRL agents and human performance. 

A. DPMP Model of Human Herding Navigation 

The herding task provides a controlled paradigm for study-ing goal-directed navigation, decision-making, and multiagent collaboration. Typically implemented as a video game re-sembling everyday navigation, the task involves one or more participants acting as virtual herder agents (HAs) who corral target agents (TAs; e.g., cattle) into a containment zone (see also Fig. 2). HAs influence TAs by leveraging the TAs’ tendency to move away when approached within a critical distance. Participant HAs in prior work consistently converge onto similar paths, which can be accurately modeled using DPMPs [13], [14], and subsequently used to control AAs’ action dynamics in human-AA herding tasks [21], [22]. The present model builds on the work of bin Kamruddin et al. [13], which extended the DPMP-based steering model of human navigation developed by Fajen and Warren [11] to a first-person herding task. In this study, participants controlled a HA tasked with corralling a single TA into a centrally-located containment zone. Analysis of HA trajectories revealed two distinct phases: (i) an approach phase and (ii) a corral phase (see Fig. 1a). During the approach phase, participants steered the HA to an offset position behind the TA, aligned with the direction of the containment zone and located beyond the HA-TA repulsion threshold to avoid prematurely influencing the TA. Once positioned at this offset, the corral phase began, with the HA advancing toward the TA, leveraging the repulsive force to drive it into the containment area. [13] captured the two phases (Fig. 1b) by modeling the HA’s heading direction ( ϕ), turning rate ( ˙ϕ), and angular acceleration ( ¨ϕ) as a function of the current positions of the TA ( x), HA ( y), and environmental obstacles ( xOi ). B. Target-Selection Policies and Action Decisions 

In herding tasks, this action decision corresponds to which TA should be approached and corralled at any given time, while accounting for the behavior of other HAs and positions of TAs in the environment [6]. We therefore examined different target-selection policies governing these action decisions. JOURNAL OF L ATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021 3

1) Heuristics for Target-Selection Decisions: Research em-ploying the herding paradigm has identified heuristic (i.e., rule-based) target-selection policies that capture the decision sequence used by human HAs when selecting and/or switching between TAs in multi-target environments. In a single-herder three-target task, [13] identified the Successive Collinear An-gle (SCA); a three-rule heuristic target-selection policy. First, a human HA selects the TA closest in angular distance—defined as the angular deviation between the TA-origin and HA-origin vectors—to their initial position relative to the center of the containment zone (i.e., field origin). Second, after corralling a TA, subsequent TAs are selected based on angular proximity to the HA’s current position. Third, when two TAs are collinear relative to the containment zone (within 20 ◦), the HA first selects the TA furthest from the zone and, upon approaching the second TA en route, corrals both by switching between them. The SCA policy accurately predicted target-selection order in approximately 79% of participants’ single-herder, three-target trials and, when integrated with the DPMP model (Eq. 1), reproduced human herding trajectories. Subsequent work extended the SCA policy to collaborative multi-herder scenarios, demonstrating that it captures human-like behavior in human–human and human–AA teams [20]. The interpretability and determinism of heuristic policies make them well suited for integration with DPMP-based models, producing predictable decisions supporting reciprocal coordi-nation in HAT [5]. However, heuristics are simplified approx-imations of human decision-making and lack the flexibility and adaptability required for complex or novel task configu-rations [14], [23]. These limitations motivate the exploration of learning-based approaches for target-selection policies. 

2) Deep Reinforcement Learning for Target-Selection De-cisions: An alternative approach to heuristics for action de-cisions is deep reinforcement learning (DRL), which learns action-selection policies through reward-driven interaction with an environment [17]. In DRL, a deep neural network approximates a policy that maps observed states to actions, and is iteratively optimized to maximize expected task rewards [24]. This framework has increasingly been used to derive decision policies for DPMP-controlled AAs, offering greater adaptability compared to the rigidity of heuristic-based AAs [25]. A common DRL training paradigm is self-play (SP), in which agents learn by interacting with copies of them-selves rather than human partners [6]. While SP-DRL can produce highly effective, able to exceed expert-level human performance, the resulting strategies often diverge from typical human decision patterns [19]. Such divergence can negatively affect human-AA teaming, as human coactors may perceive SP-trained AAs as unpredictable or uncooperative [22]. To address these limitations, human-sensitive DRL (HS-DRL) has been proposed as a training paradigm that incor-porates models of human behavior during learning [19]. In this approach, DRL agents are trained alongside simulated human proxies derived from heuristic or behavioral models, encouraging the learned policy to align more closely with human coordination strategies [25]. Previous work has shown that such human-sensitive DRL training can improve joint ac-tion within human-AI teams compared to traditional SP-DRL agents [19]. However, it remains unclear whether HS-DRL produces more effective HAT than SP-DRL or human-derived heuristic policies. The present study addresses this question by comparing heuristic, SP-DRL, and HS-DRL target-selection policies in a first-person herding task. 

C. Current Study 

Building on [13] and [20], this study aimed to (1) further validate the SCA heuristic policy for controlling DPMP-based HAs in human-AA dyads, and (2) benchmark its efficacy against SP-DRL and HS-DRL policies. While the three types of AAs differed in their target-selection policies, all shared the same DPMP movement model (Eq. 1). This design therefore isolates how different decision-policy approaches influence human-AA coordination. In particular, we examined whether HS-DRL AAs produce more human-compatible behavior than SP-DRL AAs and approach the Heuristic AA’s performance. To evaluate these policies, human-AA dyads completed a multiplayer first-person herding task involving three to five TAs. In a between-subjects design, participants collaborated with either the Heuristic, SP-DRL, or HS-DRL AA. Target-selection decisions and movement trajectories of the human and AAs were analyzed to assess the effects of the policies on HAT. These measures were derived from comparison with human-human dyad data from the same task [20], providing a reference for typical human coordination patterns. Because the SCA was derived from observed human strate-gies, the Heuristic AA was expected to best capture human herding dynamics, reflected in the highest decision and tra-jectory overlap with the human-human baseline. Among the DRL policies, the HS-DRL AA was expected to produce more human-aligned behavior than the SP-DRL AA. Consequently, participants collaborating with the Heuristic AA were pre-dicted to exhibit behavior more similar to the human baseline, followed by those paired with the HS-DRL AA, with the least typical behavior for those paired with the SP-DRL AA. II. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Participants 

Seventy-one students from Macquarie University partici-pated in exchange for course credits. Nine participants were excluded due to incomplete sessions or technical issues, leaving a final sample of 62 participants. Participants were randomly assigned to an AA condition: Heuristic ( n = 22), HS-DRL ( n = 20), or SP-DRL ( n = 20). Sample sizes were comparable to prior herding studies [14], [21]. Ages ranged from 17 to 32 years ( M = 19.03, SD = 2.58). All participants provided informed consent, and the study was approved by the Macquarie University Human Research Ethics Committee. 

B. Apparatus and Task 

The first-person herding task was designed using the Unity game engine (version 3.3.0, Unity Technologies, San Fran-cisco, USA), presented to participants on a 27-inch computer monitor. Participants paired with an AA located and corralled TAs into a containment zone (Fig. 2). The field, enclosed JOURNAL OF L ATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021 4

C. Herder Agent Control 

Participants controlled the HA using a keyboard and mouse. The keyboard controlled movement, with the W, A, S, and D keys moving the HA forward, left, backward, and right, respectively; diagonal movement was possible by pressing two keys simultaneously. Movement speed was fixed at 5 m/s. The mouse controlled the HA’s viewing direction, decoupled from the HA’s motion, allowing participants to adjust their angular orientation independently of movement. The first-person view spanned 60° vertically and 97.6° horizontally. 

D. Movement Dynamics of the Artificial Agents 

The movement dynamics of all AAs were defined by the same DPMP model (Eq. 1), under the multi-herder condition specified in Eq. (2) [20]. The assignment of the coupling terms (ψG , ψ T , ψOi ) was determined by an AA’s target-selection policy. Consequently, all AAs followed the same approach and corral phases, differing only in their action selections. 

E. Target-Selection Policies for Artificial Agents 

Three types of AAs were paired with participants, forming three experimental conditions. While all AAs shared the same DPMP model, they differed in their target-selection policies. Each policy output corresponded to a TA identification (ID) number (1-5; 0 indicating no target). The selected TA deter-mined the offset goal location through the term ψG and the targeted TA in ψT . The containment zone, all remaining TAs, participant HA contributed repulsive obstacle terms in ψOi .

1) Heuristic (SCA): The Heuristic AA used a deterministic rule-based target-selection policy based on the SCA heuristic [13], [20]. The policy selects TAs according to angular dis-tance relative to the containment zone and prioritizes succes-sive targets based on proximity in angular space. When two or more TAs are near-collinear (within 20 ◦) relative to the containment zone, the furthest TA is selected first, with the near TA influenced en route. The SCA policy was adapted for the multi-herder task, accounting for the other HA by prioritizing TAs closer to the agent and further from the other HA [20]. A tabulated summary of the generalized SCA policy is provided in Supplementary Material B. 

2) Self-Play DRL: SP-DRL AAs were implemented using a fully connected multilayer perceptron (MLP) with two hidden layers (128 nodes each), trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization algorithm (PPO; [26]). The state space inputs to the network included the positions (x, y ) and velocities of all HAs and TAs. Rewards were computed at each environment step and included a small time penalty ( −0.0001 ), a positive reward for containing a TA ( 0.6/N TAs ), and a penalty for TA escape ( −1.2/N TAs ). Training was conducted using 10 SP-DRL AAs interacting in self-play across 20 parallel envi-ronment instances running on CPUs at 40 × real-time speed, for a total of 150 million environment steps. The two best performing agents were selected based on completion time across the training trial set. 

3) Human-Sensitive DRL: HS-DRL AAs used the same MLP architecture, PPO training algorithm, and reward struc-ture as SP-DRL AAs. Training was conducted for 150 mil-lion environment steps, with the two best-performing agents selected for the study. HS-DRL AAs were trained using a hybrid approach [17] (i.e., rather than self-play), where each JOURNAL OF L ATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021 5

HS-DRL AA interacted with a Heuristic AA, allowing the policy to adapt to human-like target-selection behavior rather than another DRL agent. 

F. Procedure, Trial Sequence, and Initial Conditions 

Upon arrival, participants provided informed consent and completed a brief questionnaire collecting demographic infor-mation. The experimenter then explained the task, in which participants collaborated with an AA to locate and corral TAs into a containment zone as quickly as possible. Participants first completed a practice session consisting of five single-HA single-TA trials to familiarize themselves with the controls and task mechanics. After a short break, participants completed the experimental session comprising 24 multi-HA multi-TA trials in which they collaborated with an AA. Trials were evenly distributed across conditions with 3, 4, or 5 TAs per trial. Following [20], the first six human–AA trials also served as practice to familiarize participants with the presence of the AA and were excluded from analysis. The remaining 18 trials (Trials 7–24) were analyzed. Trial order was randomized across participants. Each trial began with agents placed at pre-specified initial positions and ended once all TAs stopped within the containment zone. Participants were assigned to either Player 1 or Player 2 ini-tial positions, counterbalanced across conditions to control for starting-location effects. The sequence and initial conditions of the analyzed trials matched those used in the human–human baseline experiment of [20], enabling direct comparison with human dyad performance. Participants were not informed which policy controlled their AA partner. 

G. Measurement of Target-Selection Decisions 

Target-selection decisions was quantified using a dependent variable termed decision overlap , which measured the sim-ilarity between a HA’s target-selection sequence (human or AA) and those of typical human HAs from the human-human dataset reported in [20]. For each trial, a 50 Hz time series was extracted, indicating when the HA was actively corralling a TA. At each timestep a binary vector of dimension NTA was constructed, where each element indicated whether the HA was interacting with a particular TA (1 = engagement, 0 = no engagement). This produced a binary time series representing the sequence of target selections during a trial. The resulting decision sequences were compared with those of the 21 human participants occupying the player role (Player 1 or Player 2) in the human-human baseline dataset [20]. Similarity between sequences was computed using Dynamic Time Warping (DTW). The resulting DTW distance was normalized by the product of the lengths of the two time series, converted to a similarity score by subtracting the normalized distance from 1, yielding values between 0 (no overlap) and 1 (perfect overlap). For each trial, the decision overlap for a HA was obtained by averaging the similarity values computed against the 21 human reference sequences. This produced 18 decision overlap scores per HA across the analyzed trials.

H. Measurement of Navigation Trajectories 

Navigation behavior was quantified using binary trace over-lap , which measured the similarity between a HA’s trajectory (human or AA) and typical human trajectories from the human-human dataset [20]. Binary trace maps were generated from the human-huma dataset by dividing the game field into 

5 × 5 spatial bins and recording the frequency of trajectory points passing through each bin (see Fig. 3 and Supplementary Material B). Bins containing more than 10 trajectory points were marked white, while bins with fewer were marked black. One binary trace map was created for each of the 18 analyzed trials using the corresponding human-human trajectory data. For each trial, a HA’s trajectory overlap score was computed as the proportion of trajectory points falling within the white bins, normalized by the total number of bins visited by the trajectory. Scores ranged from 0 to 1, where higher values in-dicated greater similarity to typical human navigation patterns. III. R ESULTS

This study evaluated whether the SCA heuristic target-selection policy can effectively control DPMP-based AAs in human-AI dyads performing a first-person herding task. To benchmark its efficacy, the SCA heuristic was compared with SP-DRL and HS-DRL policies. Evaluation focused on how closely AA and participant behavior aligned with typical human dynamics observed in human-human dyads [20]. Overall, the Heuristic AA produced the most human-aligned behavior, exhibiting higher decision and trajectory overlap scores than both DRL-based AAs. The HS-DRL AA showed greater human-alignment than the SP-DRL AA, though both remained less human-like than the heuristic agent. For par-ticipants, decision overlap differed across AA coherder con-ditions, whereas trajectory overlap remained consistent. These findings suggest that human-derived heuristic policies can pro-mote human-compatible behavior in AAs, while human part-ners adjust their target-selection decisions across AI policies without substantial changes to their movement trajectories. A. Interpreting Behavior in Human-AI Dyads 

Since all AAs used the same DPMP model, differences in behavioral alignment reflect target-selection policies. The advantage of the Heuristic AA was most pronounced in trials with five targets (Fig. 4, Panels A–B), where demands were highest. These findings support human-derived heuristics for capturing goal-directed behavior in multiagent tasks [14]. This outcome is consistent with prior work showing that the SCA policy captures the dominant target-selection strategy used by human herders in the first-person task [13]. Because the SCA policy was derived from observed human behavior, it naturally aligns with the perceptual and decision constraints underlying human action. When combined with the DPMP model, the heuristic policy enables agents to adapt continu-ously to the positions of targets and partners, reflecting the be-havioral dynamics framework proposed by Fajen and Warren [11]. This coupling between human-derived decision rules and dynamical movement control likely contributed to the strong human-behavioral alignment observed for the Heuristic AA. In contrast, both DRL agents exhibited lower alignment with typical human behavior. Reinforcement learning policies optimize task rewards rather than human similarity, often producing strategies that diverge from typical human decisions [18]. The HS-DRL AA showed greater alignment with human behavior than the SP-DRL AA, consistent with prior findings that incorporating human behavioral models during training can guide policies towards more human-compatible strategies [19], [25]. However, the HS-DRL AA did not outperform the Heuristic AA, suggesting that while human-sensitive training can improve DRL alignment with human behavior, simple human-derived heuristics may remain a highly effective ap-proach for human-AI teaming in structured coordination tasks. Participant decision overlap varied across AA conditions, whereas trajectory overlap remained stable, suggesting that participants adapted their target-selection strategies while maintaining consistent movement patterns. Such flexibility is consistent with theories of joint action, which emphasize that individuals adjust their decisions in response to their coactor’s behavior and intentions to maintain coordination [1]. The similar decision overlap observed in the Heuristic and SP-DRL participant conditions may reflect compensatory behav-ior, where participants adjusted their choices to accommodate the SP-DRL AA’s variable yet less human-like decisions [19]. Lower decision overlap in the HS-DRL participant condition, however, may reflect reduced engagement or strategic difer-ence, potentially arising when participants perceive the agent as highly capable and defer TA selection. This interpretation remains speculative and warrants direct investigation in future work [27]. 

B. Implications for Human–AI Teaming 

The present findings support combining rule-based heuris-tic decision policies with DPMP-based movement models to design AA teammates that exhibit human-compatible be-havior in dynamic human-AI tasks. Such architectures may be particularly useful for synthetic teammates in first-person training environments and collaborative navigation tasks where predictable and interpretable behavior is important [21], [28]. Comparable participant outcomes with DRL-based agents to the Heuristic AA condition suggest that reinforcement learning remains viable for human–AI teaming. Deriving heuristic policies often requires extensive analysis of human strate-gies, whereas DRL methods can learn policies autonomously through simulation [22]. In complex environments where human decision rules are difficult to specify, DRL offers a scalable alternative. JOURNAL OF L ATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021 8

V. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

The present study examined heuristic, HS-DRL, and SP-DRL target-selection policies for AAs collaborating with hu-mans in a first-person herding task. Results showed that the Heuristic AA most closely reproduced human decisions and trajectories, supporting the use of human-derived heuristics for DPMP control. Participants also demonstrated robust adapt-ability across AA conditions, suggesting that DRL policies can still support effective human–AI teaming. Overall, the findings underscore the value of combining human-inspired heuristics with dynamical systems models to develop AA teammates capable of human-compatible behavior. One limitation of this study is the relatively low task com-plexity, with trials containing a maximum of five targets, which may have constrained variability in target-selection decisions. Future work is underway examining larger first-person herding environments with up to 12 targets and three herders to test the generality of the SCA heuristic under greater decision demands. In addition, all AAs used the same DPMP movement model, which may have reduced observable differences in participant coordination across policies; future work could compare the DPMP model with DRL-parameterized move-ment controllers. Finally, supervised machine learning may provide a promising direction for modeling human decision policies, potentially leveraging synthetic pre-training using human-like simulation data generated by Heuristic or HS-DRL AAs when large human datasets are unavailable. As human–AI collaboration become increasingly common, understanding how agent decision policies and movement dynamics shape coordination will be critical for designing effective AAs teammates within human-agent systems.
