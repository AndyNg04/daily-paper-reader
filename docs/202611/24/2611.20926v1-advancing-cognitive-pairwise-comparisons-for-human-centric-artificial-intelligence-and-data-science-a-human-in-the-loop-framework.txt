Abstract. Human-centric artificial intelligence and data science (HCAD) systems increasingly involve human-in-the-loop (HITL) interventions to overcome the limitations of fully automated machine learning algorithms —such as automation bias and the inability to incorporate tacit domain knowledge —and continuously improve predictive performance. Data transformations, feature selection, feature weighting, and model selection usually require laborious human involvement across iterations of artificial intelligence and data science (AI&DS) pipelines, making development a challenging and time-consuming process. The Cognitive Pairwise Comparison (CPC) framework is proposed as a human-centric methodology to elicit preferences and priorities in a structured format. To establish essential facets of human-centricity, this research advances the CPC methodology so that user preference elicitation can be systematically quantified in these challenging AI&DS tasks. To demonstrate the usability and validity of these facets, this study presents several applications within movie recommender systems using the MovieLens dataset. It exemplifies how the extended CPC framework can facilitate interactions between human judgments and data across supervised learning, dimensionality reduction, and unsupervised learning algorithms. 

Keywords : Human-in-the-Loop (HITL), Cognitive Pairwise Comparisons (CPC), Human-Centric AI (HCAI), preference elicitation, machine learning pipelines, feature and model selection, recommender systems. 

1. Introduction 

Emerging Artificial Intelligence and Data Science (AI&DS) technologies broadly disrupt human lives. They reshape how innovation is organized, overcome human constraints in information processing, assist in generating breakthroughs, and exert a transformative impact on the digital structure of organizations (Haefner et al., 2021). Nevertheless, the ubiquity of AI&DS creates significant challenges, largely stemming from the marginalization of human interaction. Everyday phenomena —what we read and watch, how we connect with others and build relationships, and how decisions regarding employment, loans, and insurance are made —are increasingly influenced by mathematical models and their underlying data-driven systems (Thurman et al., 2019). Consequently, AI capabilities are often overhyped; without grounded human input and domain understanding, AI agents act merely as parrots that mimic human language without genuine comprehension. To bridge this fundamental gap, technical development must be anchored within broader human contexts. Highlighting this necessity, Carter et al. (2025) examine the practical mechanics of cross-disciplinary collaboration between AI and Humanities, Social, and Economic Sciences (HSES) experts. Based on an 18-month initiative, they conclude that integrating these fields for responsible AI development demands dedicated resources, a long-term vision, and room for continuous experimentation. 

AI "thinking" is fundamentally a bundle of algorithms created by highly specialized human designers. AI cannot genuinely think or understand; rather, human users justify that AI is "intelligent" only when the algorithms output sound, actionable responses. For example, actionable insights or recommendation choices are not consumed by machines, but by human decision-makers. Because the creation of AI&DS applications inherently requires human intelligence, diverse development teams build AI products based on their own specific selections of platforms, rules, packages, and datasets. In other words, AI&DS products created by humans are seldom perfect and frequently make mistakes —often failing to align with designers' initial assumptions and operational definitions. Consequently, there are diverse popular alternatives among AutoML platforms, such as such as Google Cloud AutoML, currently offered as part of the Google Gemini platform (Google, 2026), Amazon SageMaker AutoPilot (Amazon Web Services, 2026), IBM AutoAI (IBM, 2026), Microsoft Automated ML (Microsoft, 2026), Auto-WEKA (Kotthoff et al., 2019; Thornton et al., 2013), Databricks AutoML (Databricks, 2026), SAS Model Studio (SAS, 2026), and H2O Driverless AI (H2O.ai, 2026). When these platforms are used to solve the exact same problem, they frequently yield different performance results and error profiles, because the underlying automation algorithms reflect the distinct initial assumptions of their human designers. Thus, human involvement remains essential in the decision-making process. 

Broussard (2018) used the term “Artificial Unintelligence” to describe how computer algorithms can fundamentally 

misunderstand the world. Because people often assume computers are more objective than humans, they frequently attribute algorithmic mistakes to human user error rather than questioning the logic within thousands of lines of code or the biases in the sample data. Experiments by Dietvorst et al. (2015) on understanding human confidence in algorithms concluded that people may choose an algorithm if they have not yet seen its results (even if those results surpass their own). However, once people observe an algorithm making mistakes, they strongly avoid using it; conversely, if they can modify the algorithm even slightly after observing its performance, their willingness to adopt it increases significantly. Therefore, combining human judgment with algorithmic processing helps users feel more confident. Building on this, Korowicz and Thommes (2025) evaluated the impact of algorithmic, expert, and hybrid feature selection methods on AI-supported decision-making, revealing that while users strongly prefer a combined human-algorithmic development approach, neither their baseline preferences nor having model-selection choice significantly alters their actual reliance on AI advice. Ultimately, establishing a human-in-the-loop (HITL) framework helps guard against the detrimental effects of erroneous algorithmic recommendations (De-Arteaga et al., 2020 ).

While defining the exact nexus among machine learning (ML), artificial intelligence (AI), and data science (DS) is beyond the scope of this study, these fields are deeply interconnected. ML serves as an essential technique underpinning AI, and AutoML represents a subset of AutoAI, which overlaps heavily with AutoDS. Because these domains collectively rely on "learning from data," this study adopts the unified term AI&DS. 

Despite the recognized conceptual need for HITL frameworks, a critical operational gap remains. While current literature agrees that human expertise must be integrated into AI pipelines, existing methods largely rely on qualitative design guidelines, post-hoc interpretability tools, or simplistic behavioral feedback loops. They lack a rigorous, interval-scale mathematical mechanism capable of translating tacit human judgments into precise numerical weights that can be programmed directly into low-level pipeline operations —such as data transformation, feature selection, and model tuning. 

Cognitive Pairwise Comparisons (CPC) is a mathematically rigorous elicitation method that captures tacit human preferences through structured pairwise evaluations and translates them into explicit, quantitative paired interval-scale weights (Yuen, 2009, 2012). To bridge the specific operational gap identified above, this research extends this baseline CPC method to systematically integrate human knowledge into AI&DS algorithms, forming a sophisticated quantitative approach for solving complex decision problems. The core novelties and main technical contributions of this work are threefold: 

• Mathematical Elicitation for Machine Learning Pipelines : Unlike existing tools that rely strictly on data-driven heuristics or qualitative interviews, this framework provides quantifiable formulas (Eqs. 1 –7) to systematically encode human mental models into low-level pipeline operations. 

• Algorithmic Integration Across Pipeline Stages : Cognitive Pairwise Comparisons (CPC) are extended beyond standard multi-criteria decision-making into programmatic algorithms, including Feature Weighting (Eq. 8), Categorical Encoding (Eqs. 9 –11), Discretization (Eqs. 12 –14), data splitting (Algorithm 1), forward feature selection (Algorithm 2), model selection (Eqs. 18 –20), weighted distances (Eqs. 21 –24), and user-preference-weighted PCA (Algorithm 3). 

• Cross-Paradigm Applicability : A single quantitative preference elicitation technique is demonstrated to apply seamlessly across supervised learning, unsupervised learning, data reduction, and rule mining workflows. 

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews recent literature on Human-Centric AI&DS and Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) paradigms to highlight current methodological gaps and formally motivate this study. Section 3 introduces the proposed CPC-HCAD framework, detailing its conceptual architecture. Section 4 presents the core mathematical foundations of Cognitive Pairwise Comparisons (CPC). Building upon these foundations, Section 5 proposes specific CPC extensions tailored to embed user preferences into key HITL pipeline activities. Finally, Section 6 demonstrates the practical applicability of these proposed algorithms through a case study on a popular movie recommender dataset, followed by concluding remarks in Section 7. 2. Related Work and Motivations 

2.1 Limitations of Auto AI&DS 

The rise of AI&DS applications is transforming existing business processes by automating tasks, augmenting human capabilities, and replacing certain human roles, thereby reshaping educational, social, and business structures. Recent techniques in automating AI&DS creation processes —such as AutoAI or AutoML mentioned in the preceding section —

aim to automate data science tasks, suggesting that an increasing portion of data science responsibilities could potentially be automated. For example, AutoDS is an AutoML system designed to leverage state-of-the-art ML automation techniques to support DS projects (D. Wang et al., 2021). Typically, users need only upload their datasets before systems like AutoDS automatically configure algorithmic hyperparameters, preprocess data, select optimal algorithm combinations, and subsequently train and deploy the model. 

However, a growing body of research explores the human perspective within Auto AI&DS. The fully automated settings in which current AutoML systems operate are rarely a one-size-fits-all solution across diverse users and problem domains (Lee et al., 2020). AutoML systems still rely on considerable human effort to be effective, and even as the underlying technology improves, human oversight remains essential to ensure safety and efficacy (Crisan & Fiore-Gartland, 2021). Consequently, AutoAI systems should be designed to augment, rather than entirely automate, the complex practices of heterogeneous data science teams (D. Wang et al., 2019). While models produced by automated systems often exhibit fewer technical errors, they paradoxically yield lower human confidence scores (D. Wang et al., 2021). Increasing transparency by providing users with clearer insights into AutoML tools significantly improves user trust and understandability (Drozdal et al., 2020). Furthermore, automation bias can lead humans to blindly follow machine recommendations, failing to utilize alternative information sources or independent judgment to override erroneous output (De-Arteaga et al., 2020). To combat this, Li and Fan (2026) demonstrated that actively incentivizing human decision-makers to evaluate and correct algorithmic predictions significantly reduces bias without compromising accuracy, effectively highlighting the indispensable role of human-algorithm collaboration. 

2.2. Human-Centric (or Centered) AI&DS 

The realization of fully autonomous AI&DS remains challenging. Because the high complexity of AI&DS development demands intensive human involvement, formulating a generalized, fully automated solution is difficult; instead, Human-Centric AI&DS (HC-AI&DS) provides targeted solutions on a case-by-case basis. For example, an evaluation of commercial data preparation tools by Hameed and Naumann (2020) revealed that core tasks —such as data discovery, validation, structuring, enrichment, filtering, and cleaning —still require extensive manual intervention. 

Kacprzyk et al. (2019) defined human-centric (or human-centered) computing as developing problem solutions that explicitly incorporate the human perspective —employing tools and techniques for problem formulation, solution design, and result interpretation that align with human cognitive abilities and communication skills. Advancing this paradigm, Natarajan et al. (2025) highlighted the critical distinction between "human-in-the-loop" and "AI-in-the-loop" paradigms, arguing that properly recognizing human experts as primary decision-makers is essential for the effective design and evaluation of collaborative systems. 

Although fully automated machine learning is appropriate for many applications, human users often possess domain knowledge that supplements and constrains available data and analytical solutions (Gil et al., 2019). To leverage this expertise, Gil et al. (2019) proposed human-guided machine learning (HGML) as a hybrid approach that enables users to apply domain knowledge in guiding AutoML systems during algorithm selection and multi-step solution synthesis. Similarly, Lee et al. (2020) outlined a vision for a mixed-initiative machine learning environment (MILE) by rethinking the complementary roles that automation and human supervision play across the ML development lifecycle. To ensure these complex human-machine collaborations operate safely, Abeywickrama and Ramchurn (2024) proposed a formal model-checking methodology to verify, certify, and explain responsible decision-making behaviors —specifically addressing safety, controllability, and ethics within human-agent collectives. Practical implementations, such as Pandora (Nushi et al., 2018), further leverage both human- and system-generated observations to summarize conditions of system malfunction with respect to input content and system architecture. 2.3  Human-in-the-Loop  in AI&DS 

Data science projects are inherently collaborative, requiring teams to engage with diverse stakeholders and tools throughout the development lifecycle (Zhang et al., 2020). The design of interactive machine learning (ML) systems must involve users across all stages —from exploratory phases that reveal human interaction patterns to refinement stages that fine-tune interface details and evaluate alternatives (Amershi et al., 2014). Furthermore, humans actively intervene in data pipelines by discovering, capturing, curating, designing, and creating datasets (Muller et al., 2019). Gil et al. (2019) highlighted several major tasks required of human guides, including variable selection, variable requirements, instance selection, model selection, parameter settings, solution design, model assessment, model comparison, and parameter comparison. While automated systems handle key wrangling decisions on matching, mapping generation, mapping selection, format transformation, and data repair, users must provide the target data schema, partial representative instances aligned with the target, prioritization criteria for populating the target, and feedback on candidate results (Konstantinou et al., 2019). This human involvement is especially crucial during data preparation, which utilizes sequential pipeline steps to transform datasets (Hameed & Naumann, 2020) and consumes approximately 80% of data teams' workflow (Hellerstein et al., 2018; Patil, 2012). To optimize this phase, Rezig et al. (2019) presented a human-centric data-cleaning framework where users interactively detect, repair, and validate data errors while defining structural specifications. 

A persistent challenge in these workflows is the disconnect between data scientists and domain experts, as both groups perceive modeling and business domains differently (Viaene, 2013). Kaur et al. (2020) identified a misalignment between data scientists' understanding of interpretability tools and their intended usage, emphasizing that overcoming this gap requires expertise in the mathematics underlying ML models and in effectively communicating information to users (e.g., through tutorials, visualizations, or interactive tools). Similarly, Hong et al. (2020) conducted 22 semi-structured interviews with industry practitioners and demonstrated that ML interpretability is contextually dependent on practitioners' organizational roles and socially negotiated through stakeholder interactions across development pipeline stages. 

To synthesize these varied human interactions across the pipeline, Mosqueira-Rey et al. (2023) systematically reviewed Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) techniques, categorizing them by the degree of human agency into active learning, interactive ML, and machine teaching. Building on this concept of agency allocation, Borchers et al. (2026) introduced the Agency Allocation Framework to systematically analyze how decision-making authority is distributed between human stakeholders and automated systems, moving away from simple behavioral proxies. Punzi et al. (2026) provided a structured taxonomy of collaborative frameworks focused on uncertainty-driven abstention mechanisms, which dynamically determine when an AI should act autonomously versus when it must defer to human expertise. However, while these state-of-the-art frameworks successfully delineate qualitative control structures and theoretical agency models, existing HITL architectures still lack an interval-scale mathematical mechanism based on pairwise comparisons to programmatically embed human preferences into low-level pipeline operations —a gap directly addressed by the proposed CPC-HCAD approach. 

2.4 Soft Computing for Human Role in AI&DS 

Soft computing is an interdisciplinary field focused on designing intelligent systems to process uncertain, imprecise, and incomplete information. Human-centricity is an inherent property of how people perceive a system, process, machine, or model, while information granularity reflects human uncertainty, playing a pivotal role in soft computing (Song & Wang, 2015). Kou et al. (2020) compared five multiple-criteria decision-making (MCDM) methods to evaluate and select feature-selection techniques for text classification. Similarly, Chen et al. (2008) applied the fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) to multidimensional scaling (MDS) to identify preference similarities among alternatives. 

Cognitive Pairwise Comparisons (CPC) (Yuen, 2009, 2012), a soft computing technique, serves as the foundational core of the Cognitive Network Process (CNP), outperforming AHP because CPC offers a structured elicitation methodology to encode tacit human preferences into an explicit format. Applications of CPC are widely demonstrated across diverse decision domains (Goldani & Ishizaka, 2025; Goldani et al., 2023; Paucar et al., 2019, 2020; Qi et al., 2017; Ri et al., 2024; C.-H. Wang, 2018, 2019; Yuen, 2014a, 2014b, 2021). To address these limitations, this study introduces key initiatives on how CPC can be systematically utilized for HCAD, as detailed in the subsequent sections. 2.5 Motivations

3. Framework of Human-Centric AI&DS with CPC 

3.1. Hybrid Human-AI decision-making framework 

The concept of Human-Centric AI and Data Science (HCAD) within this research is illustrated in Figure 1. Decision-making is typically achieved in one of three ways: utilizing human opinions through mental models, analyzing data via AI&DS algorithms, or employing a hybrid system that combines both approaches. Driven by the rapid expansion of data volume and variety, numerous statistical and machine learning algorithms have been adopted to analyze datasets of all scales, aiming to extract meaningful patterns that inform sound decision-making. 

Figure 1: Hybrid Human-AI decision-making 

Nevertheless, many decision problems lack historical data or reference cases; even when relevant data exists, it is often difficult or expensive to obtain. Human mental models formulate decision-making frameworks by utilizing human knowledge, judgments, preferences, and opinions as foundational inputs to construct primary solutions. Research in multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) is closely aligned with these human mental models. In many scenarios, complex decision problems require the simultaneous analysis of partial data and the integration of human knowledge. This necessity has driven research into HCAD —such as Hybrid Human & Machine Systems (HHMS) —which integrates human expertise with data analytics algorithms to offer promising solutions for complex problems. Within the HCAD framework, human participants may include data science professionals, domain experts, or end users. 3.2 CPC in HCAD 

Unlike prior studies that treat Cognitive Pairwise Comparisons (CPC) solely as a multi-criteria decision-making tool, this work introduces seven novel formulations that adapt CPC into programmatic data preprocessing and machine learning algorithms. The complementary strengths of humans and AI can offset each other's limitations to achieve more reliable and trustworthy decision outcomes. A primary research challenge in HCAD is determining how humans can effectively participate in conventional data science pipelines. • Data preprocessing and preparation: discretization, feature selection, feature weighting, and categorical encoding. 

• Model selection and evaluation: splitting datasets into subsets, such as training, validation, and testing sets. 

• Data transformation: weighted distances and data reduction. 

Finally, the preprocessed and transformed data can be utilized for supervised learning (regression and classification), unsupervised learning (clustering and association learning), and data reduction. 5.2. Feature Weighting with CPC

5.4 Discretisation (Binning or Categorisation) with CPC 

The scale of measurement includes nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio scales. Nominal and ordinal scale data represent categorical data, whereas interval and ratio data represent numerical data. Nominal data possess no intrinsic ordering, whereas ordinal data follow a defined sequence. Interval data feature an arbitrary zero point, whilst ratio data possess a natural zero point. Some machine learning methods —such as conventional association rule mining —require purely categorical data as inputs. Discretization (also termed binning or categorization) is the process of transforming continuous numerical data into discrete categorical variables. Cognitive Pairwise Comparisons are extended to numerical discretization to transform data formats to satisfy the input requirements of specialized machine learning algorithms.

Data reduction is used to transform feature variables into principal components, for which the top components are retained for downstream modeling. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a widely established data reduction technique. When importance weights are assigned to feature variables, features with higher weights contribute more significantly to the variance captured by the top components. Cognitive Pairwise Comparisons (CPC) are employed to determine these user preference weights. Algorithm 3 presents user-preference-weighted data reduction using PCA with CPC. In this workflow, feature variables are standardized to have zero mean, thereby unifying the scale across raw features. The standardized feature values are multiplied by their respective preference weights to construct a weighted correlation matrix, which is subsequently decomposed into eigenvalues ( 𝐿 ) and eigenvectors ( 𝑉 ) via eigensystem decomposition. These eigenvalues  𝐿  and eigenvectors  𝑉  are further utilized to define component loadings, standard deviations, variance proportions, and component scores, as defined in Table 8. 

Because top principal components are inherently sensitive to feature variances, variables exhibiting larger variances naturally dominate those with smaller variances in standard PCA. By assigning a higher preference weight to a feature, user-preference-weighted PCA amplifies that feature's variance, thereby increasing its relative contribution to the top principal components. These weighted components are subsequently utilized to build clustering models that uncover preference-aligned patterns. 6. Human-Centric Movies Evaluation and Recommendation with CPC 

6.1 Background and Motivations with CPC 

To illustrate the practical applicability and robustness of the proposed Cognitive Pairwise Comparisons for Human-Centric Artificial Intelligence and Data Science (CPC-HCAD) framework, the development of human-centric movie recommendation applications is discussed in detail. The benchmark open-source MovieLens 25M dataset (MovieLens, 2019), constructed as described by Harper and Konstan (2015), is utilized to build machine learning models guided by CPC. This approach facilitates continuous interaction between human decision-makers and algorithmic pipelines within human-in-the-loop (HITL) processes. All processed datasets, computational web modules for core algorithms, and 

supplementary materials are accessible via the author’s GitHub repository at 

https://github.com/kkfyuen/cpcHCADDemos  and permanently archived on Zenodo at 

https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23434993 . Live computational web module demos are hosted on GitHub Pages at 

https://kkfyuen.github.io/cpcHCADDemos/ . The key contributions of CPC across core AI&DS techniques are 

summarized as follows: 

• Data transformation: Raw metadata cannot be fed directly into machine learning algorithms without prior preprocessing to meet strict algorithmic input parameters. Within this framework, CPC is systematically applied to perform feature weighting, categorical encoding, and continuous data discretization. 

• Regression analysis: Variable preference ordering and representative data sampling represent critical prerequisites for effective regression modeling. Human-guided forward feature selection (Algorithm 2) and training-validation-test data partitioning (Algorithm 1) with CPC are deployed to address these requirements, systematically exploring feature spaces to predict continuous movie rating scores. 

• Classification model selection: Selecting the optimal classification algorithm among multiple candidate models is challenging, as competing algorithms yield disparate performance profiles across multiple evaluation metrics. Model selection with CPC (Eqs. 18 –20) is employed to evaluate, rank, and select the best predictive model to classify movie rating categories established via CPC discretization. • Weighted data reduction and clustering: Different user groups prioritize movie features differently. CPC encodes subjective user preferences into weighted Euclidean distance metrics (Eqs. 21 –24) and user-preference-weighted Principal Component Analysis (Algorithm 3), guiding K-Means clustering to discover preference-aligned viewer segments for personalized movie recommendations. 

• Association rule learning: Continuous numerical features cannot be natively processed by traditional association rule mining algorithms. CPC discretization transforms continuous movie attributes into discrete categorical bins, which are subsequently converted into binary dummy variables. Frequent itemsets and association rules generated via the Apriori algorithm are then prioritized and ranked according to CPC preference weights to deliver tailored recommendation lists. 

6.2 Data transformation with CPC 

The empirical evaluation of the Cognitive Pairwise Comparisons for Human-Centric Artificial Intelligence and Data Science (CPC-HCAD) framework utilizes two core relational files from the open-source MovieLens 25M benchmark collection (MovieLens, 2019): movies.csv (containing 62,423 raw film entries across movieId, title, and genres) and 

ratings.csv (comprising 25,000,095 user evaluations submitted by 162,541 unique evaluators across 59,047 movies). Representative data snapshots are provided in Table A1 and Table A2 in the Appendix. To prepare raw metadata for algorithmic modelling, a rigorous multi-stage cleaning and feature engineering pipeline was executed. First, release year patterns (𝑌𝑌𝑌𝑌)  were systematically extracted from movie titles using regular expressions. A total of 406 entries missing valid release years (a missing rate of 0.65%) were excluded, leaving 62,017 valid movies. Historical release year anomalies were individually verified and corrected —for example, rectifying 1013 Briar Lane (year 1013  → 2009) while validating legitimate early cinema records such as Passage de Vénus (1874). Raw title strings were subsequently cleaned by stripping parenthetical release years. Next, genre strings were parsed across 20 unique categories. Uninformative or invalid entries —specifically (no genres listed) (4,801 titles) and IMAX (1 title) —were removed (totalling 4,802 excluded titles), preserving 57,215 valid movies across 18 target genre categories. 

Simultaneously, ratings.csv was aligned with the cleaned movie table, filtering out 36,723 ratings associated with excluded movies and retaining 24,963,372 ratings across 54,351 shared movies. Evaluator timestamps were converted to calendar years, and user rating evaluations were aggregated per movie to compute summary statistics: rater volume ( raterSize ), mean rating score ( ratingScoreMean ), standard deviation ( ratingScoreSD ), and average rating year ( ratingYearMean ). Two key temporal metrics were engineered: movie age ( 𝑚𝑜𝑣𝑖𝑒𝐴𝑔𝑒 = 2019 − 𝑦𝑒𝑎𝑟 ) and average rating period duration ( 𝑟𝑎𝑡𝑖𝑛𝑔𝑃𝑒𝑟𝑖𝑜𝑑𝑀𝑒𝑎𝑛 = 𝑟𝑎𝑡𝑖𝑛𝑔𝑌𝑒𝑎𝑟𝑀𝑒𝑎𝑛 − 𝑦𝑒𝑎𝑟 ). Records exhibiting negative rating duration periods (85 movies where user ratings were recorded prior to official film release years) were removed, yielding a final, fully sanitized dataset (moviesDB ) containing exactly 54,266 unique movie records.

Figure 9: User preference involved Data Reduction for Clustering with CPC 

Figure 9 presents a conceptual overview of user-preference-guided data reduction and clustering within the Cognitive Pairwise Comparisons for Human-Centric Artificial Intelligence and Data Science (CPC-HCAD) framework. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a widely established data reduction technique, whilst K-Means clustering is a popular unsupervised learning method used to uncover latent patterns in multidimensional data. The integration of PCA and K-Means guided by CPC resolves a key limitation of conventional data reduction —namely, that standard PCA operates blindly to domain priorities. The technical procedures and computational modules for this section are hosted via the open-source web link:  https://kkfyuen.github.io/cpcHCADDemos/s5cpcClustering.html .

In this application, CPC models subjective evaluator preferences across movie features (Figure 4) to construct weighted principal components (Algorithm 3) and weighted Euclidean distance metrics (Theorem 1, Eqs. 21 –24). The input dataset comprises 22 rescaled features (comprising 4 continuous/scaled attributes —movieAge ( X1), raterSize ( X3), ratingPeriodMean ( X4), and ratingScoreMean ( X5)—alongside 18 CPC-weighted genre dummy variables ( X2,1 ,..., X2,18 )) across 54,266 sanitized MovieLens records (Table 9). Feature variables are rescaled into non-negative [0, 1] preference ranges using  ∇+(𝑋 𝑗 ) min-max scaling rather than z-score standardization, ensuring non-negative feature representations suitable for preference aggregation. 

Figure 10 illustrates the scree plot of eigenvalues alongside the cumulative variance proportion curve for the 22 user-preference-weighted principal components. Because standard PCA is sensitive to feature variances, assigning higher CPC weights amplifies target feature variances, ensuring that top principal components reflect user domain priorities. Applying an eigenvalue threshold of eigenvalue >= 0.9 selects the first 14 principal components (comp1 through comp14). These 14 components capture 80.0% (0.800) of total cumulative variance, effectively reducing feature dimensionality by 8 dimensions across 54,266 observation records (saving 434,128 values from downstream clustering computations) while preserving essential preference signals.

6.6. Associate rule analysis with CPC 

Association rule learning (or association rule mining) discovers underlying co-occurrence patterns and decision rules to predict the likelihood of specific item occurrences based on antecedent items in transaction databases. Figure 12 illustrates the conceptual workflow of Cognitive Pairwise Comparisons for association rule analysis in a human-centric movie recommender system. Because conventional association rule algorithms (such as Apriori) process discrete categorical inputs exclusively, continuous numerical feature variables cannot be directly utilized. Within the proposed CPC-HCAD framework, continuous variables are discretized into structured categorical intervals using CPC preference-proportional binning (Eqs. 12 –14), which are subsequently transformed into binary dummy variables via one-hot encoding (Figure 5, Table 9). Once feature data formats satisfy algorithmic input requirements, candidate rules generated by association mining are evaluated and ranked according to user preference weights, ensuring that recommendations reflect human domain priorities. 7. Conclusions and Discussion 

This study successfully advances and extends Cognitive Pairwise Comparisons (CPC) to directly program human domain knowledge and subjective priorities into AI&DS algorithmic pipelines. Unlike prior decision-making studies that treat CPC strictly as an isolated multi-criteria evaluation tool, this research introduces novel programmatic formulations (including Algorithms 1 –3) that adapt CPC into low-level data preprocessing, feature selection, and machine learning operations. Within the proposed framework, CPC operates as an interactive, user-facing interface that empowers data scientists, domain experts, and end users to shape machine learning workflows according to explicit preference constraints, thereby mitigating automation bias and capturing tacit domain knowledge. 

While existing literature in Human-Centric AI and Data Science (HCAD) predominantly focuses on qualitative design guidelines, conceptual surveys, or post-hoc interpretability tools, it lacks an interval-scale mathematical mechanism capable of translating subjective human judgments into programmatic pipeline operations. The proposed framework directly bridges this operational gap. Specifically, this study makes three core technical contributions to the HCAD literature: 

1.  Programmatic CPC Pipeline Extensions: Novel extensions of the CPC method were established to address critical preference-elicitation challenges across seven foundational data science pipeline stages: 

i.  Categorical encoding via interval preference matrices, replacing arbitrary one-hot or ordinal assignments (Eqs. 9 –11); 

ii.  Continuous data discretization using preference-proportional binning (Eqs. 12 –14); 

iii.  Training-validation-test data partitioning based on cognitive prioritization (Algorithm 1); 

iv.  Human-guided forward feature selection (Algorithm 2); 

v.  Multi-criteria learning model selection (Eqs. 18 –20); 

vi.  User-preference-weighted Principal Component Analysis (Algorithm 3); and 

vii.  Weighted Euclidean distance metric integration (Theorem 1, Eqs. 21 –24) for clustering and association rule learning. 

2.  Cross-Paradigm Algorithmic Integration: The proposed human-centered formulations were seamlessly integrated into established machine learning paradigms, encompassing linear regression, multi-class classification, weighted Principal Component Analysis, K-Means clustering, and Apriori association rule mining. 

3.  End-to-End Recommender System Validation: The practical applicability, validity, and robustness of the CPC-HCAD framework were empirically validated using the open-source MovieLens 25M dataset (54,266 sanitized records), demonstrating how human-in-the-loop interactions yield highly personalized, reliable, and preference-aligned cinematic recommendations. 

Despite its demonstrated efficacy in embedding tacit human judgment into machine learning pipelines, the proposed CPC-HCAD framework has several inherent limitations: 

1.  Cognitive Load and Pairwise Scalability: Complete pairwise comparison requires n(n-1)/2 evaluations. While hierarchical feature decomposition and criteria grouping effectively mitigate evaluator fatigue for moderate feature spaces, eliciting preferences for ultra-high-dimensional datasets (e.g., thousands of unstructured attributes) remains cognitively demanding for human experts. 

2.  Dependence on Evaluator Expertise: The quality of the derived pipeline weights depends directly on the domain 

knowledge and consistency of the human evaluator. Although the Accordance Index (AI ≤ 0.1) provides a strict 

mathematical gatekeeper for matrix consistency, biased or erroneous human judgment can still propagate into model parameters. 

3.  Static Preference Elicitation: Evaluator preferences are currently captured upfront via Pairwise Opposite Matrices (POM) as static weight configurations, rather than being continuously updated through real-time, interactive feedback loops. 

To address these limitations, future research will focus on: (i) developing sparse, incomplete pairwise comparison models to reduce evaluator fatigue in high-dimensional feature spaces; (ii) extending the CPC-HCAD framework to dynamic, real-time reinforcement learning settings where human preference weights update adaptively during system usage; and (iii) scaling the methodology to emerging state-of-the-art architectures, including deep neural networks, transformer-based language models, and multi-modal industrial applications.
