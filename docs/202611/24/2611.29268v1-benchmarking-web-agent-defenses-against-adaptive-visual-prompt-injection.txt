# Benchmarking Web Agent Defenses Against Adaptive Visual Prompt Injection 

## Haichuan Zhang *, Zhiyuan Zhong, Shih-Chieh Dai, Xinyu Sun, Guanhong Tao 

University of Utah 

## Abstract 

Multimodal web agents automate website interactions but remain vulnerable to visual prompt injection (VPI), where malicious webpage content manipulates their vi-sual processing and induces unintended actions. Although several defenses focus on detecting injected instructions in screenshots, malicious destinations such as phishing URLs embedded in HTML can remain hidden yet accessible through agent interactions. Existing evaluations have also not sufficiently examined adaptive attackers who know the defense mechanisms. To address these limitations, we in-troduce AdaptiveVPI, the first benchmark for systematically evaluating adaptive VPI threats in web agents. Specifically, we present a dataset of 360 webpage instances spanning 6 distinct hijack strategies across 10 real-world websites, with malicious destinations not visible in screenshots. We design an adaptive attack framework that iteratively refines malicious instructions based on feedback from the defense mechanism being evaluated. We also introduce three met-rics to measure the extent of agent hijacking at three differ-ent levels. Across 10 defense configurations for computer-use agents and 11 defense configurations for browser-use agents, our adaptive attacks achieve average success rates of 91.87% and 91.99%, respectively. These results reveal a gap between current VPI defenses and realistic threats and highlight the need for defenses designed with adaptive at-tackers in mind. 

## 1. Introduction 

Multimodal web agents are increasingly used to automate tasks on websites. Acting on behalf of users, they click, type, upload and download files, and complete multi-step tasks with minimal human intervention. Two common types are computer-use agents (CUAs) and browser-use agents 

(BUAs). CUAs observe screenshots and predict the screen coordinates of their next click. BUAs use both screenshots and the page’s HTML to identify and interact with webpage elements. Because both types rely on webpage content to

guide their actions, attackers can use that content to manip-ulate their behavior. This runtime threat is known as visual prompt injection (VPI) [4, 32]. An attacker embeds malicious visual con-tent, such as a pop-up, in a rendered webpage to induce the agent to pursue objectives that deviate from the user’s task. Defenses against VPI can be grouped into three cat-egories: content-level detectors that identify injected con-tent [3, 10, 20], action-level guards that check whether each proposed action aligns with the user’s task [24], and prompt-level defenses that modify the agent’s instructions or in-context examples [4, 11, 30, 32]. However, existing evaluations leave three important gaps in the assessment of these defenses. 

I. Attack Realism. Existing VPI benchmarks emphasize attacks with visible instructions that explicitly state the ma-licious objective (see Figure 1) [4, 32]. An attacker, how-ever, can conceal this objective while using benign-looking instructions to steer the agent. For example, a session ex-piration notice or verification prompt can direct the agent to click a button that links to an attacker-controlled desti-nation. This destination is specified in the page’s HTML but does not appear in the screenshot. The visible request may therefore appear legitimate, while screenshot-based de-fenses lack the destination information needed to assess its consequences. Such attacks test whether defenses can rec-ognize deceptive interactions without relying on explicitly harmful instructions or visibly suspicious URLs. 

II. Attacker Adaptivity. VPI defenses are typically evalu-ated against fixed attacks developed without knowledge of the target defense. However, attackers can adapt the word-ing or visual presentation of the injected content to evade that defense. In text-based settings, adaptive attackers with knowledge of the target defense have repeatedly bypassed defenses against jailbreaks and prompt injection that ap-peared robust against fixed attacks [23, 25, 31]. To our knowledge, comparable evaluations of defenses that inspect screenshots or proposed actions are lacking, leaving their robustness against adaptive attacks largely understudied. 

III. Success Criteria. Existing studies use different criteria to determine attack success. Some consider an initial action, 1arXiv:2611.29268v1 [cs.CR] 22 Nov 2026 (a) Attack page from Pop-ups [32]. (b) Attack page from VPI-Bench [4] (zoomed-in view).

A single binary outcome obscures the distinctions among initial redirection, subsequent interac-tion, and completion of the harmful action. Evaluating these stages separately reveals how far an attack progresses and at which stage a defense blocks it. To address these gaps, we introduce AdaptiveVPI . To our knowledge, it is the first benchmark for systemati-cally evaluating adaptive VPI threats against web agents. The benchmark combines benign-looking visual instruc-tions with concealed malicious destinations, an adaptive at-tack framework that uses defense feedback, and evaluation at three levels of agent hijacking. Our results show that cur-rent defenses remain vulnerable to adaptive attacks and that successful attacks can transfer to other agents without fur-ther optimization. Our contributions are as follows: • A VPI Attack Dataset. We construct a dataset of 360 fixed attack pages spanning 6 distinct hijacking strate-gies across 10 real-world websites. The pop-ups present benign-looking instructions that conceal the attacker’s harmful objective, while the malicious destination URLs are not visible in screenshots. • An Adaptive Attack Framework. Our attack iteratively refines the injected instructions using feedback from the defense under evaluation, producing attacks tailored to each defense. • Three-level Evaluation Metrics. We assess agent hi-jacking at three levels: ① Diversion : whether the agent is redirected to the attacker’s page; ② Engagement : whether it interacts with that page; and ③ Compromise : whether it completes the attacker’s objective, such as submitting sensitive information or downloading a malicious file. • A Systematic Evaluation. We evaluate 10 defenses for CUAs and 11 for BUAs, including screenshot-based, action-level, and prompt-level defenses. Our adaptive at-tacks achieve average Compromise rates of 91.87% for CUAs and 91.99% for BUAs. We further show that at-tacks that successfully compromise the source agent also transfer to other agents without further optimization. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Attacks on Multimodal Web Agents 

Instruction-based Attacks. A substantial body of work injects malicious instructions into webpage content. VPI-Bench [4], Pop-ups [32], and AdInject [27] study injec-tions through pop-ups, advertisements, and other inter-face content. WASP [11] injects human-written instruc-tions through user-generated content, such as Reddit com-ments. RedTeamCUA [17] studies injections into editable web content in hybrid web and operating system environ-ments. EIA [16] injects transparent HTML elements to ex-filtrate private information. Related injection attacks have also been studied in mobile agents [5, 6, 14, 18]. These studies focus on the design and effectiveness of injected in-structions, with limited exploration on how attackers adapt them using feedback from the target defense. Other work examines injected instructions that are diffi-cult for human users to perceive, include faint text in screen-shots processed by the Comet browser [2] and instructions that become visible only after image downscaling [26]. Be-yond web agents, Clusmann et al. [8] embed small or low-contrast instructions in medical images. 

Optimization-based Visual Attacks. Another line of work optimizes images or rendered webpages to induce attacker-specified actions. VisualWebArena-Adv [29] per-turbs an image on the page, WebInject [28] optimizes web-page perturbations using an approximation of the render-ing pipeline, and MIRAGE [9] generates adversarial im-ages within constrained regions such as advertisement slots. WebMirage [13] crafts localized visual perturbations that 2cause agents to select attacker-controlled content. These approaches optimize visual inputs, often using white-box model access or surrogate models. We focus on defense-aware refinement of injected webpage instructions using feedback from the defense under evaluation. 

2.2. Defenses for Web Agents 

Content-level Detectors. WARD [3] is a lightweight vision-language guard that classifies webpage content as malicious or benign given the user’s task, a screenshot, and the page’s HTML. SnapGuard [10] detects injections by combining gradient statistics of the screenshot with action-oriented phrases extracted through OCR. WAInject-Bench [20] systematically evaluates prompt-injection de-tectors for web agents. It finds that some detectors iden-tify attacks containing explicit instructions or visible per-turbations but largely fail against attacks without explicit instructions or with imperceptible perturbations. Our eval-uation includes its image- and text-embedding classifiers (Embedding-I and Embedding-T) and its LLaVA-1.5-based detection prompt [19] as baselines. 

Action-level Guards. DeAction [24] checks whether each proposed action aligns with the user’s intent before execu-tion. Its guard uses the current screenshot, the user’s task, a summary of the interaction history, and the proposed action to detect misalignment and provide structured feedback for iterative correction. 

Prompt-level Defenses. Several attack studies also eval-uate defenses that modify only the agent’s prompt. VPI-Bench [4], Pop-ups [32], and WASP [11] introduce warn-ings or instructions that ask agents to disregard prompt injections, ignore pop-ups, or reject instructions that con-flict with the user’s objective. VPI-Bench and Pop-ups re-port limited or no protection from these modifications. In-Context Defense (ICD) [30] instead provides a small set of examples demonstrating defensive reasoning before action planning. It reduces attack success rates for both pop-up attacks and environment injection attacks. Our evaluation examines these defenses under adaptive attacks and reports the results in Section 6.3. 

2.3. Adaptive Evaluation 

Adaptive attacks have exposed vulnerabilities in text-based defenses that appear robust against fixed attacks. Nasr et al. [23] use gradient-based optimization, reinforce-ment learning, random search, and human-guided explo-ration to bypass 12 jailbreak and prompt-injection de-fenses. Zhan et al. [31] bypass all eight evaluated indirect-prompt-injection defenses, while universal adversarial suf-fixes evade activation-based task-drift detectors [25]. Adaptive evaluation of web-agent defenses remains lim-ited in scope. WARD [3] is hardened against text-level adaptive attacks, WebAgentGuard [7] tests a fixed guard-targeted instruction, and DeAction [24] does not consider attacks tailored to the defense. This work instead system-atically evaluates multiple defense classes against adaptive attacks whose visible instructions conceal the malicious ob-jective and refines attacks separately for each defense. 

## 3. Threat Model 

We consider a web agent performing a benign user task un-der the protection of a deployed defense. The attacker con-trols part of a webpage visited by the agent and seeks to hijack its behavior while evading the defense. 

Attacker’s Goal. The attacker seeks to divert the agent from the user’s task and induce it to complete an attacker-specified action on an attacker-controlled landing page: signing in, granting permission, or downloading a file. Suc-cess requires achieving the final target action while evad-ing the deployed defense. For defenses that issue explicit alarms, the target action must occur before the first alarm. Defense-specific success criteria are defined in Section 5.3. 

Attacker’s Knowledge. We consider non-adaptive and adaptive attackers. Both know the website, the user’s task, and the default attack page. The non-adaptive attacker does not know the defense and uses a fixed default pop-up and landing page for each attack instance. The adaptive at-tacker knows the defense’s mechanism, including its inputs, prompts or in-context examples, and decision rules, but has no access to its weights or gradients. This assumption re-flects the principle that a defense should remain effective even when the attacker knows how it works. 

Attacker’s Capabilities. The attacker can display a pop-up through an advertisement slot [27] or injected overlay and controls the landing page linked to its button. The pop-up must present benign-looking instructions that conceal the malicious objective. The target action is fixed for each in-stance. The attacker may modify the pop-up and the appear-ance of pages it controls, but not the user’s task, the agent’s weights or prompt, or the defense. Pixel-level perturba-tions and attacks involving a compromised user or browser are out of scope. During adaptive search, the attacker can query the defended agent as a black box for up to 10 rounds. Feedback includes defense explanations or full responses, or the agent’s responses and actions for prompt-level de-fenses. Search information is not reused across pages or defenses. In the transfer setting, the attacker reuses pages optimized against another agent without querying the target or performing further optimization.

## 5. Methodology 

We present AdaptiveVPI, a benchmark for evaluating web agent defenses against adaptive visual prompt injection. We first construct attack webpages with benign-looking pop-ups and concealed malicious destinations (Section 5.1), then refine them using feedback from the target defense (Section 5.2). Finally, we introduce three metrics to mea-sure attack progression at different levels (Section 5.3). 

5.1. Attack Webpage Design 

Existing VPI benchmarks emphasize attacks with visi-ble instructions that explicitly reveal the malicious objec-tive [4, 32], as shown in Figure 1. However, attackers can conceal their objectives behind seemingly legitimate requests to reduce the likelihood of detection by users or automated defenses. To model this threat, we separate the visible instructions in a pop-up from the malicious desti-nation linked to its button. Figure 2 illustrates this de-sign. An attacker-controlled pop-up appears on a benign host page (Figure 2a). Its visible components, including a title, body, and button, present an ordinary notice that en-courages the agent to click without revealing the attacker’s objective. The button’s destination, however, is specified only in the page’s HTML (Figure 2b). The URL it opens or uses to download a file is not visible in the screenshot. A defense inspecting only the screenshot may therefore miss the harmful purpose of the request, even as the agent’s click leads to an attacker-controlled page or initiates a download. We instantiate this design using host pages from ten web-sites, six pop-up variants, and three payloads. Our bench-mark covers websites spanning e-commerce, travel, news, caregiving, and education. Each host page uses a website screenshot as its background, with the pop-up card ren-dered on top. For Booking, Amazon, and BBC, we use screenshots from VPI-Bench [4]. For the remaining seven websites—Walmart, Woot, Airbnb, Care.com, Preply, Hey-Tutor, and Princeton Review—we capture each homepage once without interacting with the site. We assign one be-nign user task to each website, such as finding the lowest price for a 55-inch TV on Walmart or summarizing the day’s news on BBC. To model how attackers might exploit common website interactions, we design six pop-ups covering three themes, with two variants per theme as listed in Table 1. Human-verification prompts (Pop-ups I and II) present a security check as a prerequisite for continued browsing. 5.2. Adaptive Attack 

Although the attack webpages described above conceal the malicious objective, defenses may still recognize their fixed wording or visual patterns. Detecting these default pages alone does not establish robustness against attacks adapted to the defense. We therefore use an LLM-based optimizer to refine the page content and appearance using defense feed-back, with the goal of achieving the attack objective while evading the target defense. Specifically, the optimizer modifies the pop-up’s title, body, and button label, and selects background settings for the page containing the pop-up and the landing page. The constraints on changing each component are listed in Ta-ble 10 in Appendix. The pop-up must remain consistent with its assigned theme discussed in Table 1, contain ex-actly one button, and introduce no additional tasks. We use a keyword validator to check consistency with the theme’s vocabulary. We do not change the user’s task, payload, but-ton destination, and landing-page text and form structure. The optimization is guided by the feedback from both agent execution and defense output.

6.5. False Alarms 

We also measure false alarms on benign pages to assess whether defenses unnecessarily interrupt legitimate tasks. For each of the ten websites, we evaluate a clean page and a page with an ordinary cookie banner, with three runs per page, yielding 60 runs per defense and agent. None of the evaluated detectors raises an alarm in these runs. DeAction raises alarms in 43 of 60 runs on the BUA and 5 of 60 on the CUA. The protection shown in Table 4 therefore comes at the cost of false alarms on benign pages, particularly in the BUA setting. 

6.6. Adaptive Defense 

We additionally evaluate an adaptive defense against our at-tacks. Specifically, we fine-tune the WARD-2B detector on our attack pages, named WARD-Adaptive. To measure the impact of the detector in benign settings, we replace the pop-up instructions with legitimate notices and remove the malicious payloads. As shown in Table 6, WARD-Adaptive reduces the Compromise rate from 91.5% to 0% on the CUA and from 87.0% to 0% on the BUA. However, this reduction comes at the cost of higher false alarm rates than those of the original WARD-2B. The false alarm rate rises from 1.1% to 38.9% on the CUA and from 1.1% to 76.7% 8on the BUA. This increase also extends to benign pop-ups from VPI-Bench, where the false alarm rate rises from 0% to 45.6% on the CUA and 42.1% on the BUA. 

## 7. Conclusion 

This work benchmarks adaptive attacks with concealed des-tinations using three-level hijacking metrics. Average com-promise rates exceed 90% on both agent interfaces, and suc-cessful pages transfer across agents. These results highlight the need for defenses designed and evaluated against adap-tive attackers.

## 9. Transfer Results on Gemma and Muse 

We examine whether adaptive attack pages that succeed against one agent remain effective when transferred to other model families. For each defense and agent, we select pages that compromised Qwen3-VL-8B in at least two of three evaluation runs, then evaluate the same pages on Gemma-4-12B-it and Muse-Glimmer-30B without further optimiza-tion. For the 180 pages per agent, 134–178 qualify on BUA and 133–180 on CUA, depending on the defense. Tables 7 and 8 report results on these source-successful subsets. The transferred pages retain high Compromise rates on both target models. Across defenses, Gemma reaches 80.1– 91.3% on BUA and 90.7–95.2% on CUA, while Muse reaches 86.0–97.2% and 81.1–94.7%, respectively. Thus, Compromise exceeds 80% in all 42 configurations. This transfer holds across detectors, action-level guards, and prompt-level defenses. For example, pages optimized against DeAction with Qwen still achieve 87.1% and 94.9% Compromise on Gemma’s BUA and CUA, and 94.5% and 94.7% on Muse’s. These results show that successful adaptive pages re-tain substantial attack capability beyond the original target agent. An attacker can reuse pages developed against one model to compromise agents from other model families, ex-tending the threat beyond the original target agent. The re-ported rates measure transfer conditional on success against Qwen, and each defense has its own selected subset. Within these results, switching the agent’s model family while re-taining the evaluated defense may not be sufficient to reli-ably prevent attacks by transferred pages.

## 12. Limitations 

In the main evaluation, we allow at most one search of up to 10 rounds per page–defense pair and evaluate each selected page in three runs, so the reported rates reflect this limited attack budget and are not upper bounds on achievable attack success. The Cmp metric records non-empty typing actions on sign-in pages, consent requests, and file requests rather than confirmed credential disclosure, successful authoriza-tion, or completed downloads. The BUA and CUA render-ings differ in viewport and sign-in layout, so performance differences cannot be attributed solely to the agent interface. 

## 13. Analysis of Evaluation Metrics 

Diversion, Engagement, and Compromise distinguish reaching an attacker-controlled page, interacting with it, and meeting the payload-specific criterion. Together, they re-veal how far an attack progresses, even when it fails to com-plete. We use these distinctions to diagnose outcomes in the non-adaptive and adaptive evaluations. 

Diagnostic Value of the Metrics. Table 13 compares the three metrics with those used by prior benchmarks. Pop-ups [32] measures clicks on the injected pop-up. VPI-Bench [4] distinguishes attempts from completed attacks, while WASP [11] reports intermediate and end-to-end attack success rates (ASR). AdaptiveVPI further distin-guishes between reaching the landing page and interacting with it. For example, an agent may reach a sign-in form without interacting or interact with it without issuing a non-empty typing action. Both cases fail the login Compromise criterion, but their Engagement outcomes distinguish where progress stops. The three indicators are computed directly from page URLs, agent actions, and local site request logs. The metric values can therefore be recomputed from the stored records without relying on a judge model. Comput-ing the metrics for 180 runs takes approximately 0.2 sec-onds on a single CPU core. 

Classifying Attack Outcomes. For login and authorization payloads, each evaluation setting contains 360 runs (10 websites × 6 pop-ups × 2 payloads × 3 runs). Across these runs, Compromise implies Engagement, and Engagement implies Diversion ( Cmp ⇒ Eng ⇒ Div ). Each run therefore falls into exactly one of the four categories in Table 12. A run is classified as not reached when Div = 0 ,

reached, no interaction when Div = 1 and Eng = 0,

interacted, not completed when Div = Eng = 1 and 4Cmp = 0 , or completed when Div = Eng = Cmp = 1 .Here, completion means satisfying the payload-specific Compromise criterion. We count events according to their timing relative to the defense’s first alarm or action-level block, as defined in Section 5.3. Reaching the landing page counts toward Diversion even if the defense raises an alarm upon observ-ing it, while Engagement and Compromise require events before the defense intervenes. Percentages in all four out-come columns are calculated over the same 360 runs. The Compromise rates in Table 4 and Table 5 also include 180 direct-download runs. The three metrics show how far each attack progresses. 

Identifying Where Attacks Stop. Without a defense, all BUA runs reach the landing page, but 15.3% end without interaction and 0.8% involve interaction without meeting the Compromise criterion as shown in Table 12. The BUA is therefore susceptible to redirection, although its landing-page Compromise rate is 83.9%. These attacks fail after the agent reaches the page, even when no defense is deployed. For CUA, 6.7% of runs never reach the landing page, while every run that reaches it meets the Compromise criterion. These results show that an incomplete attack does not nec-essarily mean that a defense prevented it. For non-adaptive BUA runs, the embedding classifiers and LLaVA prompt have the same results as the the unde-fended baseline across all four outcome categories. In each setting, 15.3% of runs reach the landing page without inter-acting, and 0.8% interact without meeting the Compromise criterion. Since these defenses raise no alarms, the incom-plete attacks provide no evidence of added protection. On CUA, SnapGuard illustrates how the metrics can identify where a defense stops an attack. Without a defense, every run that reaches the landing page completes the payload. With SnapGuard, 76.7% of runs reach the page, but none in-teracts with it or completes the payload. The logged alarms confirm that SnapGuard intervenes after the agent reaches the landing page but before it interacts with the page. To-gether, the three metrics and alarm logs show that the de-fense stops the attack, even though the undefended agent can complete the required landing-page actions.
