Embodied Interaction Policy:
A Unified Model for Full-Duplex
Human–Robot Interaction
Youqiang Gui1,2,∗, Yunfei Liu2,†,‡, Lijian Lin2, Ye Zhu2, Jianan Wang3, Cheng Peng1,‡, Yu Li2
1 Sichuan University 2 International Digital Economy Academy (IDEA) 3 Astribot
Real-time human-robot social interaction requires continuous coordination between speech and physical
behavior. Existing systems often separate interaction agents from motion generation, complicating coordination
as conversational states change. We propose Embodied Interaction Policy (EIP), a unified streaming framework
that couples an Audio-Interaction expert with a motion expert through layer-wise joint attention. This allows
motion generation to directly access the evolving interaction context. A chunk-wise causal mask prevents
access to future context, while a vocal token encodes speaking state and the semantic emotion of the response.
We construct Embodied Interaction Dataset (EI-Data) from co-speech and dyadic interaction data and train
our model in two stages to learn state-adaptive and emotion-conditioned behavior. Combined with the frozen
interaction expert, EIP supports continuous robot motion during proactive responses and interruption-aware
turn-taking. Quantitative evaluations demonstrate improved motion quality over representative gesture-
generation baselines. Compared with a representative cascaded system, EIP achieves higher interaction success
and reduces warm-start latency by 64%. Physical deployment and user studies further demonstrate improved
motion naturalness and interaction comfort.

1. Introduction
Natural human–robot social interaction requires a robot to continuously determine what to say, when to
speak, and how to move during interaction [28, 20]. Beyond generating appropriate verbal responses, the
robot must exhibit natural and smoothly transitioning physical behaviors that remain consistent with the
evolving conversational context. In particular, the robot should maintain smooth and expressive motion
as it switches between interaction states, such as listening, speaking, and responding to interruptions [18].
Such nonverbal behavior is closely coupled with conversational timing: decisions about when to respond,
remain silent, or yield to an interruption directly affect how physical movements should initiate, sustain,
and transition. Achieving this tight temporal coordination between interaction and motion remains a key
challenge in embodied social robotics.
Existing co-speech gesture generation methods have shown great success in generating realistic and diverse body,
hand, and facial motions aligned with speech [25, 9, 12]. However, these methods typically generate motion
in human-centric representation and in digital space (e.g.,, SMPL-X [14]). Using retargeting postprocessing
method [2] can be a solution for adapting to robot. However, such retargeting may introduce kinematic
discrepancies that compromise its expressiveness and temporal fidelity. To address this embodiment gap, recent
approaches propose to generate robot motion for physical deployment. ProAct [28] combines proactive reasoning
with continuous robot motion generation, while retaining turn-based verbal responses. RoboGesture [20]
integrates an Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR)–Large Language Model (LLM)–Text-to-Speech (TTS)
pipeline with a streaming speech-to-motion generator for humanoid deployment.
Although these systems enable embodied interaction to some extent, they largely follow a turn-based interaction
paradigm, where the robot waits for a user turn to complete before generating a verbal response and the
corresponding motion. This design couples interaction responsiveness with the latency of the upstream
ASR–LLM–TTS pipeline, limiting rapid responses to user utterances and interruptions (Fig. 2, left). More
recent duplex interaction models, such as Audio Interaction Model (AIM) [21], remove this constraint by
continuously processing the ongoing audio stream and supporting response generation without waiting for
a complete turn to finish. However, timely interaction responses alone do not guarantee natural physical
behavior. As the interaction state changes continuously, the robot must also smoothly initiate, sustain, and
transition its physical behavior accordingly, rather than switching abruptly between discrete motion states.
How to translate such evolving interaction context into continuous, expressive, and temporally coherent robot
motion in real time remains unexplored.
Our key observation is that the internal representations of a pretrained interaction model already encode
rich, continuously evolving cues about the ongoing interaction, and can therefore serve as a natural context
for robot motion generation. Building on this insight, we propose Embodied Interaction Policy (EIP), a
unified streaming framework that grounds such interaction representations into continuous physical motion.
EIP introduces a dedicated motion expert that directly consumes the evolving interaction context through
layer-wise interaction–motion coupling, allowing robot motion to be generated in lockstep with conversational

2. Related Work
2.1 Human–Robot Interaction
Social interaction has been increasingly explored through virtual humans and autonomous characters, where
multimodal social behaviors can be modeled without the physical constraints of robotic embodiment [5, 8].
Conventional approaches often learn motion in human representation and subsequently retarget it to the
robot [26]. Recent retargeting methods improve the fidelity of this transfer [2, 23]. However, transferring such
behaviors to physical robots introduces substantial embodiment gaps, including morphological differences,
kinematic feasibility, safety, expressiveness degradation, and real-time execution.
Recent robot-centric work has therefore shifted toward learning directly in the robot’s representation, moving
cross-embodiment alignment from inference-time post-processing to data preprocessing [28, 20]. Building
on this direction, ProAct [28] combines a streaming multimodal interaction model with a streaming motion
generator for real-time embodied social interaction. RoboGesture [20] learns streaming co-speech motion
directly in robot space and deploys it on a physical humanoid robot. Despite these advances, verbal interaction
3


and motion generation remain cascaded, introducing additional inter-module handoff and synchronization
overhead that can increase end-to-end latency. In contrast, EIP unifies interaction and motion generation
within a unified policy, enabling low-latency and synchronized embodied interaction.
2.2 Interaction Models
Interactive agents have evolved from text-based systems that interact through language and tools [24, 17],
to speech-based full-duplex models [6, 10], and multimodal models that continuously process audio and
visual inputs [22]. Recent work further treats interaction itself as a native modeling problem. Thinking
Machines’ Interaction Model [19] models audio, video, and text as time-aligned continuous streams, enabling
interruption, overlap, and responsive interaction. Audio Interaction Model [21] formulates a similar paradigm
for continuous audio through an always-on perceive–decide–respond loop. These works primarily model
interaction through digital modalities such as speech, text, and vision. We extend this interaction paradigm
to physical embodiment, incorporating robot motion into the continuous interaction loop.
2.3 Co-Speech Gesture Generation
Another related topic is co-speech gesture generation, which generates expressive motion in sync with
speech. Recent methods have advanced from holistic speech-driven motion generation [25, 9] to photorealistic
conversational motion [13] and causal online gesture generation [12]. While these methods primarily generate
digital motion conditioned on the speaker’s speech, EIP targets continuous embodied dialogue interaction,
jointly modeling robot behaviors across speaking, listening, and idle states.
3. Embodied Interaction Policy
3.1 Overview
As illustrated in Fig. 3, EIP is a unified streaming policy that couples a frozen interaction expert with a
compact motion expert. The interaction model continuously processes human audio, provides rich context,
and determines whether the robot should respond and how the robot responds, while the motion expert
generates the corresponding robot behavior. Following [21], both experts operate on a shared interaction clock
of ∆= 400 ms. At interaction step t, EIP takes the latest human-audio chunk xt, the robot state qt, and the
accumulated context ht−1, and produces synchronized speech and motion chunks for the next interval:
(ˆst, ˆat) = πEIP(xt, qt, ht−1).
(1)
Here, ˆst ∈R∆·rs denotes a 400-ms speech chunk, and ˆat ∈R∆·ra×N denotes the robot motion chunk. Here
rs, ra, N are the sample rate of audio, the motion frequency, and the dimension of action space, respectively.
EIP is a unified model through layer-wise joint attention between the interaction and motion experts, allowing
motion generation to access the evolving interaction context directly. To preserve causality, a chunk-wise
attention mask blocks future interaction context and motion information from other chunks. We further
introduce a vocal token that explicitly encodes the robot’s speaking state and the semantic emotion of its
response, thereby aligning physical behavior with verbal output. Next, we elaborate on the design and
implementation of our method.

3.3 Two-Stage Training
Jointly learning state adaptation and emotion-aware motion can lead to mode collapse. Therefore, we train
EIP in two stages. Stage I first learns motion patterns across speaking, listening, and idle states, using
only the speaking-state token of the vocal condition. Stage II then introduces emotion-conditioned motion
generation by conditioning the motion expert on the full vocal token (vt) defined in Eq. (8).

3.4 Playback-Synchronized Streaming Inference
During training, response tokens are teacher-forced using ground-truth speech timestamps. During inference,
we obtain this alignment through incremental speech synthesis. The interaction expert decodes a response
into punctuation-delimited phrases, each of which is submitted to TTS upon completion. Speech and motion
output can begin once the first playable audio chunk is available, while subsequent phrases are decoded and
synthesized in the background.
To preserve causality, we maintain separate decoding and committed KV caches sharing the frozen interaction-
expert parameters. The decoding cache supports response generation ahead of playback, whereas the committed
cache receives response tokens only as their corresponding audio intervals are played. The motion expert
accesses only the committed cache, preventing future response text from entering its interaction context. At
step t, the committed context, robot state, and vocal condition are used to generate the next motion chunk ˆat,
which is dispatched alongside the corresponding speech chunk ˆst on the shared 400-ms timeline. Algorithm 1
in Appendix B details incremental generation, temporal alignment, and interruption handling.

4. Experiments
We conduct empirical studies to justify three central claims: (i) EIP supports natural and expressive human–
robot interaction by coordinating speech with continuous, state- and emotion-aware motion; (ii) its streaming
motion generation improves motion realism, speech alignment, and diversity over representative gesture-
generation baselines; and (iii) compared with a cascaded variant, EIP achieves a higher observed success rate
for emotion-aware motion and lower warm-start latency, with similar observed success rates for proactive
response and interruption-aware turn-taking.
4.1 Experimental Setup
4.1.1 Dataset Processing
We construct EI-Data by combining co-speech data from BEAT2 [9], GUAVA [27], and SHOW [25] with dyadic
interaction data from Seamless Interaction [1]. Co-speech recordings are augmented with synthesized user
turns and fixed listening segments, while dyadic recordings preserve natural turn-taking and listening behavior.
We retarget motions to Astribot S1, filter motion artifacts, and annotate chunk-level interaction states to
provide supervision across speaking, listening and idle. The resulting training set contains about 60,000
interaction episodes of 20 seconds each. We select 20 high-quality test episodes based on clear distinctions
between speaking and listening behaviors, motion diversity, and emotion-motion consistency, while ensuring
that the training and test sets are strictly disjoint. Further details on data construction, selection, and
preprocessing are provided in Appendix A.
4.1.2 Implementation & Deployment Details
The interaction expert is initialized from the pre-trained Audio-Interaction model based on Qwen2.5-3B and
remains frozen throughout training. The motion expert consists of 36 Transformer blocks with 16 attention
heads, a hidden dimension of dm = 768, and an intermediate dimension of 3072, totaling approximately 380M
parameters. We use ∆= 0.4s and ra = 30Hz, giving 12 frames per motion chunk, with N = 20 for Astribot
S1 and N = 165 for SMPL-X skeleton. Input audio is sampled at 16 kHz, and output speech at rs = 24 kHz.
Stage I trains the motion expert for 15K optimization steps using AdamW with a global batch size of 32.
The interaction expert remains frozen, and the motion expert learns speaking, listening, and idle behaviors
using only speaking-state conditioning. The loss weights are set to λ1 = 1, λ2 = 2, and λ3 = 1. A sensitivity
analysis of these loss weights is provided in Appendix D. The learning rate is warmed up for 500 steps to
1 × 10−4 and then cosine-decayed to 1 × 10−5.
Stage II starts from the Stage-I checkpoint and introduces FiLM-based emotion conditioning in the final four
motion-expert blocks. The final eight motion-expert blocks and the motion head are fine-tuned for 1K steps
with a global batch size of 16. Newly introduced conditioning parameters use a peak learning rate of 1 × 10−4,
while the unfrozen pre-trained parameters use a 0.02× learning-rate scale. The learning rate is warmed up for
100 steps and then follows a cosine schedule. Both stages are trained on eight NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs.
We deploy the model on the Astribot S1, with online inference on an NVIDIA RTX 5090. The generated
30-Hz motions and synchronized speech are transmitted in 400-ms chunks to the safety gate. For collision
avoidance, we apply a filter based on model predictive control (MPC) to refine the generated trajectories. A

5. Conclusion
We present Embodied Interaction Policy (EIP), a unified streaming framework coupling interaction modeling
and motion generation on a shared 400-ms timeline. Building on a frozen pretrained interaction model,
EIP learns continuous robot motion that adapts to conversational state and response emotion through
two-stage motion training. We also introduced EI-Data to support policy learning and evaluate motion
quality. Quantitative evaluations, user studies, and deployment on the Astribot S1 demonstrate improved
motion quality, higher interaction success rates, reduced response latency, and greater interaction comfort and
10


engagement. Despite these promising results, EIP relies on audio to perceive interaction partners, limiting its
ability to interpret visual cues such as gaze, facial expressions, and body posture. Future work will incorporate
visual perception to capture these non-verbal cues and support more context-aware embodied interaction.

Conversion to a common SMPL-X representation.
Dataset-specific loaders first convert all motion sequences
into a common SMPL-X representation [14] consisting of the global root orientation, body pose, root translation,
and body-shape parameters. BEAT2 [9] stores each motion frame using a dataset-specific 215-dimensional
parameterization containing facial expression, jaw and eye poses, global orientation, body pose, and bilateral
hand poses. GUAVA [27], SHOW [25], and Seamless Interaction [1] are loaded from AMASS-style SMPL-X
files. We preserve the available hand, facial, and expression parameters, although the robot supervision
primarily uses body and arm motion.
13


Coordinate and temporal normalization.
We express the human motion in meters using a canonical Y-up
coordinate system. For sequences with a dataset-specific global orientation offset, we remove the sequence-level
mean root rotation and select the axis-aligned world rotation that makes the body upright. The same world
rotation is applied to both the global orientation and root translation. For GUAVA, we apply a five-frame
sliding rotation average on SO(3) to suppress high-frequency pose jitter.
To reduce the cost of fitting, the input sequence is temporally subsampled by a factor of two while its
frame-rate metadata is adjusted accordingly, preserving its physical duration. We then resample all normalized
sequences to 50 Hz. Rotations are interpolated with spherical linear interpolation, while translations and
other Euclidean quantities are interpolated linearly.
SMPL-X-to-SOMA fitting.
We first use the SMPL-X parameters to reconstruct the posed human mesh.
SOMA-X is then used to fit the intermediate SOMA skeleton to the mesh vertices [16]. Shape-dependent
identity parameters are prepared once for each body shape and reused across frames. Fitting is performed
in batches of 128 frames using two body-fitting iterations, one full-body iteration, and three Lie-algebra
refinement iterations. The resulting intermediate representation contains the SOMA joint rotations, root
translation, and per-vertex fitting error. We also export the fitted motion as a SOMA BVH sequence, which
serves as the common input to the robot-specific retargeters.
Robot-specific inverse kinematics.
The SOMA motion is retargeted independently to the Unitree G1 and
Astribot S1 embodiments. We first rescale the human skeleton using robot-specific segment-length factors and
transform the motion into the robot coordinate system. A Newton-based inverse-kinematics solver, accelerated
with NVIDIA Warp [11], then optimizes the robot configuration at every frame.
For G1, the IK objectives constrain the pelvis, torso, shoulders, elbows, wrists, knees, and feet while respecting
joint limits and encouraging temporal smoothness. Foot stabilization is applied as a post-processing step.
For S1, the planar chassis pose is obtained from the projected SOMA hip translation and heading, while
the upper-body IK objectives match the shoulder, elbow, and wrist trajectories. Head yaw and pitch are
recovered from the relative chest–neck orientation and constrained to the physical head-joint range. We use
24 IK iterations for G1 and 40 iterations for S1. Long sequences are processed in contiguous temporal blocks
while carrying the final joint state of one block into the next, preventing discontinuities at block boundaries.
Motion representation used for training.
The retargeter exports robot joint trajectories at 50 Hz. For S1,
the stored trajectory contains the planar chassis, torso, head, two arms, and grippers. Before training, we
convert angular quantities from degrees to radians, resample the trajectory to 30 Hz, and retain 20 actuated
upper-body degrees of freedom: four torso joints, two head joints, and seven joints for each arm. The planar
chassis and gripper joints are not included in the predicted motion vector.
Each 0.4-s interval consequently contains 12 robot frames. At streaming step t, the motion in the current
interval is used as the robot state, and the following 12-frame interval is used as the target motion:

A.2 Real-Interaction Episodes from Seamless Interaction
Seamless Interaction [1] provides recordings of two interacting participants. We retain the original speech
of the partner and the retargeted motion of the target, thereby preserving real turn-taking, interruptions,
pauses, and listening behavior. The processing is performed as follows.
Participant pairing.
We group recordings by their shared interaction identifier and match each retargeted
target sequence with the unique other participant in the same interaction.
A valid pair must contain the audio, transcript, and video streams of both participants, together with the
target robot motion. Because the construction is directional, either participant can serve as the target when a
valid retargeted motion sequence is available.
14


Common valid interval.
We compute the usable duration as the minimum duration among the two audio
streams, the two videos, and the target motion. The sequence is further truncated at the last detected speech
event so that long, inactive tails do not dominate training.
Chunk-level speech states.
Voice-activity intervals are read from the dataset annotations, and neighboring
intervals separated by at most 1 s are merged into a single utterance. The synchronized streams are divided
into non-overlapping 0.4-s chunks. For each participant, every chunk is assigned one of three states:
zk ∈{onset, speaking, non_speech}.
(14)
A chunk is labeled onset if it contains the beginning of an utterance, speaking if it overlaps an utterance
without its onset, and non_speech otherwise. Response-onset supervision applies to onset and non_speech
chunks; chunks in which the target is already speaking are excluded from this loss because the response has
already been initiated. Word-level transcript tokens are assigned to chunks according to their timestamps.
Partner-first episode extraction.
Candidate episodes are anchored at a partner-speech onset. Each episode
begins at the anchor and spans 20 s:

∆t = 0.4 s,

We retain only complete 50-chunk windows containing a subsequent target response and at least one valid
target transcript token. Accepted windows from the same interaction are made non-overlapping. This provides

Quality filtering and final subset.
We discard episodes with missing modalities, incomplete chunk sequences,
invalid transcripts, or unavailable robot motion. We additionally measure joint velocity, acceleration, and

A.5 Test Set Construction and Data Independence
Evaluation set construction.
For the quantitative motion evaluation in Table 3, we select 20 episodes
of 20 seconds each from EI-Data. The evaluation set is constructed to cover representative interaction
behaviors, including speaking, listening, and state transitions. Specifically, we consider three criteria: (1) clear
distinctions between speaking and listening states, (2) diverse motion patterns, and (3) consistency between
the expressed emotion and the corresponding motion. These criteria ensure that the evaluation includes
meaningful interaction dynamics rather than segments dominated by static or repetitive behaviors.
Conditioning protocol for Table 3.
During motion training and the teacher-forced evaluation in Table 3,
target-text tokens are assigned to their physical intervals. At decision step t, the motion expert can access
target-text history only through It, while the motion target lies in It+1. Target-text tokens from It+1 and later
intervals are masked. Online execution uses the same text-visibility cutoff, with generated, playback-confirmed
text replacing the reference text history. Reference speaking-state and emotion annotations provide the vocal
conditions during training and evaluation; online execution obtains these conditions from the response plan
and playback schedule.
Data split and independence.
To avoid potential overlap between training and evaluation, we partition
the data at the original recording or interaction level. For the Seamless Interaction dataset, all segments
originating from the same dyadic interaction are assigned exclusively to either the training or evaluation split,
including cases where the target and partner roles are exchanged. For the Co-Speech datasets, segments
derived from the same source recording are similarly restricted to a single split. This ensures that the training
and evaluation sets are disjoint at the original recording or interaction level.
Robustness to test-set selection.
To assess the influence of evaluation-set curation, we additionally evaluate
EIP on 20 randomly sampled 20-second episodes from held-out recordings, without applying the interaction-
diversity and emotion-consistency criteria used for the curated test set. All methods follow the same evaluation
protocol as in Table 3, using FGD, BC, and Div as evaluation metrics. As shown in Table 7, EIP maintains its
advantage over the evaluated baselines on the randomly sampled test set. Specifically, EIP achieves an FGD
of 53.07, compared with 86.42 for MIBURI and 109.31 for RoboGesture, while also achieving better BC and
diversity scores. Although FGD increases compared with the curated test set, the relative performance trend
remains consistent. These results suggest that the improvements of EIP are not solely attributable to test-set
curation, further supporting the robustness of the proposed method across different evaluation samples.

C. Rationale for the Attention Mask
The attention mask reflects the conditional generation process of EIP: the available interaction context, robot
state, and vocal condition guide the generation of the next motion chunk, while the noisy action tokens are
jointly updated through flow matching. The ordering [Qt; vt; Aτ
t ] separates the conditioning inputs from the
variables being denoised.
Interaction-context isolation and temporal visibility.
Interaction-context tokens retain autoregressive
attention and do not attend to motion-expert tokens. This preserves the frozen interaction expert’s information
dependencies and prevents its representations from changing with sampled motion. The interaction context
can therefore be computed and cached independently of action denoising. Each motion chunk accesses only
the context available at its decision step, preventing attention to later information when multiple chunks are
processed together during training.
Ordering of state, vocal, and action tokens.
Within each chunk, Qt represents the robot’s current physical
state, vt specifies the robot’s speaking state and the semantic emotion of its response, and Aτ
t represents
the action trajectory being generated. Allowing vt to attend to Qt lets the vocal-condition representation
incorporate the current physical state. Keeping the reverse connection masked leaves the state representation
independent of the vocal-token pathway. Action tokens attend to both blocks, combining the starting state
with the desired behavioral condition. Neither state nor vocal tokens attend to action tokens, so their
representations remain independent of the changing noisy trajectory throughout flow integration. This
ordering is an inductive bias that maintains a separation between fixed conditions and generated actions.
Bidirectional attention within blocks.
Tokens within the state block encode observations available at the
decision boundary and can attend to one another. Tokens within the action block represent a trajectory
generated jointly through flow matching. Bidirectional attention allows each action token to incorporate
information from the entire candidate trajectory and model dependencies among its frames. These tokens are
denoising variables available during the current computation, rather than future observations, so their mutual
visibility does not violate temporal causality.
Isolation between motion chunks.
Direct attention between motion chunks is masked. Blocking future
chunks prevents access to unavailable robot states and future motion information. Blocking past motion
chunks is an architectural choice that avoids dependence on their intermediate denoising representations:
temporal information is instead carried through the accumulated interaction context and the updated robot
state. Given the same conditioning inputs, each chunk has the same local attention dependencies whether
processed alongside other chunks during training or generated individually during streaming inference.
These restrictions are applied at every layer, preventing future context or other motion chunks from influencing
the current prediction through indirect attention paths. This argument assumes that the input features and
conditioning signals are available at the decision boundary.

E. Ablation of Stage-II Emotion Conditioning
We compare matched fine-tuning without explicit emotion conditioning (A), embedding-only conditioning (B),
and Full Stage II, using Stage I as a reference. Variant A disables both the additive emotion embedding and
FiLM, while B retains the emotion embedding in the vocal token without FiLM modulation. All Stage-II
variants start from the same Stage-I checkpoint and use identical training data, class-balanced sampling,
training budgets, and backbone fine-tuning settings. Emotion-aware motion success and FGD follow the
evaluation protocols and test sets of Tables 3 and 4, respectively.
As shown in Table 10, continuing fine-tuning under the Stage-II data and sampling recipe without explicit
emotion conditioning (A) reduces FGD from 69.33 to 55.20, while emotion-aware motion success remains at
40%. Thus, this continued fine-tuning improves the FGD score but does not improve the observed emotion-
success rate on the evaluated samples. The emotion-success gain of Full Stage II is therefore not reproduced
by the matched fine-tuning control.
Adding the emotion embedding to the vocal token (B) increases success from 40% to 75% and further reduces
FGD to 49.70. Since A and B share the training data, sampling strategy, and backbone optimization settings,
this comparison supports the benefit of explicit emotion injection through the vocal-token pathway. Full
Stage II further increases success from 75% to 95% and reduces FGD from 49.70 to 43.83, supporting an
incremental benefit from FiLM when the emotion embedding is already present.
Together, these results support gains from the additive emotion embedding and additional FiLM modulation
under the fixed Stage-II training recipe.
