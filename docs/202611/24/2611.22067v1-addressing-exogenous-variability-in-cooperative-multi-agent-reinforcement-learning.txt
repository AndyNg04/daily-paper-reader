Abstract 

Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) often fails under exoge-nous variability such as shifts in opponents or environment regimes that cannot be controlled by the team but alter the transition dynamics. We formalize this challenge as Exogenous Dec-POMDP (ED-POMDP), in which an episode-level exogenous regime, selected independently of the team, indexes the dynamics, and the global state is partitioned into components that are directly responsive to the current team action and components that are not directly action-responsive at the current step. This formulation exposes mediated influence: while team actions do not directly update the latter at the current step, they can shape their later evolution through induced changes in the former. Based on this, we propose 

LEICA , a CTDE-compatible algorithm that learns history-conditioned context representations for both components and shapes policy updates using agent-wise, influence-weighted counterfactual intrinsic rewards. Across SMAX benchmarks with opponent strategy shifts, LEICA consistently improves both training perfor-mance and generalization to unseen opponents’ strategies over existing baselines, supporting the usefulness of influence-weighted shaping under exogenous train-test regime shifts. 

1 Introduction 

Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) has achieved success across a wide range of domains including games [1, 2], autonomous robotics, and large-scale decision-making. In particular, coopera-tive benchmarks such as the StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC) [3] designed to model battles against opponent teams, have spurred the development of various MARL algorithms including value decomposition methods [4, 5] and actor-critic methods [6, 7]. Despite such progress, current cooperative MARL methods remain brittle under task variations, such as changes in opponent strategies, scenario parameters, or latent environment regimes. Policies trained on a narrow distribution often overfit to specific training regimes and collapse at test time [8]. Crucially, these variations are typically exogenous: they are not controlled by the agent team, but they alter the environment dynamics [9]. While previous MARL methods treat these variations as ordinary task changes, we explicitly leverage their exogenous nature to improve both training performance and generalization. Our idea is to separate the state components that the current team action updates directly from those that are updated mainly by the opponent or the environment, and to learn which directly induced changes influence the later evolution of the latter under the current regime. Contributions 

• We formalize ED-POMDP for cooperative MARL under exogenous variability, which separates the exogenous regime from an approximate one-step action-responsiveness partition of the state and highlights influence mediated by directly action-responsive transitions. • We propose LEICA , a CTDE method that shapes policy updates with influence-weighted, policy-relative changes of directly action-responsive state. 3 Related Works 

MARL under CTDE CTDE is the standard paradigm for cooperative MARL with partial observ-ability. Major actor-critic variants include MADDPG and MAA2C [13, 14], on-policy MAPPO and IPPO [6, 15], and theory-based trust-region methods HATRPO and HAPPO with heterogeneous-agent extensions [16, 7]. For value-based methods, VDN and QMIX implement additive and monotonic mixing based on Individual-Global-Maximum principle [4, 5], and many subsequent studies investi-gate the value decomposition structure of MARL [17–22]. 

Influence-based coordination Influence-based methods use how much an agent affects other agents or the environment as an intrinsic signal for coordination, exploration, or opponent-aware behavior. Prior work has studied influence through communication, where messages shape other agents’ behavior [23, 24], and has proposed metrics for causal influence among agents [25, 26]. Other methods define influence from agents’ actions and mainly use it to promote exploration [27– 229]. Opponent modeling infers other agents’ policies or types [30], and opponent-learning-aware methods explicitly model or anticipate another learner’s policy updates [31–34]. LEICA differs from these lines in that it does not use a separate communication channel or model concurrently learning opponents; it measures the influence of directly induced state changes on the later response of fixed but state-reactive opponents and uses it to support both adaptation to regime variation and team coordination. 

Context-based adaptation and generalization in RL Several neighboring areas study generaliza-tion under distributional or strategic variation. Robust MARL seeks policies that remain effective under train-to-test shifts, for example through minimax optimization against altered opponents [35] or robust value factorization under environmental uncertainty [36]. Meta-RL adapts to task variation by encoding interaction history into recurrent states or latent task variables, as in RL 2, PEARL, and VariBAD [37–39]; a few extensions to multi-agent settings are available in decentralized and game-theoretic formulations [40, 41]. LEICA shares the idea that history can reveal the current regime, and our MAPPO+ctx baseline follows this spirit by adding a VariBAD-style context encoder. The key difference is that LEICA does not rely on context inference alone but further exploits state decomposition for better generalization. Zero-shot coordination focuses on compatibility with novel cooperative partners [42, 43], and ad hoc teamwork studies cooperation with previously unseen teammates, often without prior joint training [44, 45]. LEICA instead assumes a jointly trained team and studies shifts in opponent or environment regimes; these settings are complementary and not subsumed by our formulation. 

Structured state representations Factored MDPs exploit conditional independence among state variables for planning [46], and Block MDPs and exogenous-information MDPs recover latent controllable states or discard exogenous noise for sample efficiency [47, 48]. Rather than removing the not directly action-responsive component for single-agent efficiency, LEICA estimates how directly induced changes steer its later evolution and attributes this influence to individual agents.

4.1 Why Exogenous Variation Breaks Coordination 

In cooperative MARL, policy updates are driven by the interactions experienced during training. This process reinforces coordination patterns tailored to the training opponents and environment dynamics. While such policies may exhibit high performance in training, they fail when encountering shifts in factors outside the team’s control at test time. We refer to such externally driven shifts as exogenous variability in multi-agent systems. 3Figure 1a illustrates this failure situation, and Figure 1b shows an empirical example in SMAX [10]. MAPPO and QMIX achieve near-perfect win rates against the seen opponent, but their performance collapses under modified opponent strategies. Thus, high training-regime performance does not imply robust coordination under exogenous shifts. A natural response to this collapse is to train on multiple regimes and infer the current regime from history. However, this context inference helps recognize which regime the team is in, but does not specify which directly induced state changes are useful for adaptation. Figure 1c shows this gap: MAPPO and QMIX reach about 60% win rate on seen regimes but drop to roughly 20% on unseen regimes, and MAPPO with VariBAD-style context inference [39] (MAPPO+ctx) only slightly narrows the gap. Therefore, the key challenge lies not only in identifying the exogenous regime, but also in learning how the team should intervene through directly action-responsive state variables. What is missing is an influence signal from directly induced state changes to the later response of the opponent or environment, which our method learns and uses to reward team actions that steer this response.

7 Experiments 

We compare LEICA with strong CTDE, intrinsic-reward, context-inference, and robust MARL baselines, inspect the learned influence weights, and ablate the main components. SMAX serves as our main evaluation; experiments on continuous physical perturbations and a mixed setting provide complementary evidence on whether the mediated-influence principle extends beyond SMAX-style opponent shifts.

Training and evaluation protocol All methods are trained for 10 M environment steps. The evaluation unit is an independently trained run, scored by its final win rate on the seen or unseen configurations; we report mean ± SEM over five tasks × eight seeds. For Table 1, we also use unpaired stratified permutation tests with Holm correction (Appendix F.2); other conclusions rest on mean ± SEM. Policies are checkpointed every 0.5M steps and evaluated on multiple episodes per checkpoint. While hyperparameters and compute details are reported in Appendix D, we note that LEICA’s additional Jacobian and predictor computations are training-time only; at execution, agents use only local histories and the learned context encoder, yielding less than 5% runtime overhead. 

7.2 Baselines 

We compare against standard CTDE baselines MAPPO [6], QMIX [5], and SHAQ [49]. We include LAIES [29], a partition-informed intrinsic-reward method, and MAPPO+ctx, a VariBAD-style context-inference baseline [39], to test whether context recognition alone is sufficient. We also include robust MARL baselines M3DDPG [35] and M3PPO, our PPO-based variant, to evaluate whether generic max-min training improves generalization under exogenous variability. For M3DDPG and M3PPO, we instantiate the uncertainty set through action perturbations and tune the adversarial rate under the same protocol as other hyperparameters. We report the results of the default variant of LEICA , which employs the automatic partitioning module. We additionally provide the results of LEICA (manual) , which instead uses a domain-informed action-responsiveness partition when such knowledge is available. Both partition procedures are detailed in Appendix B.2 and Appendix B.3.

To illustrate what LEICA emphasizes during training, we visualize the coordinate weights w on

Early in training, the weights are focused on directly action-responsive movement coordinates such as ally0_X , ally1_X , and ally2_X , which are predictive of subsequent opponent responses. As training progresses, the emphasis shifts towards task-relevant outcome coordinates such as enemy HP, consistent with the decaying schedule on α: LEICA first prioritizes coordinates with high mediated influence and later shifts toward value-sensitive coordinates as the critic stabilizes. 97.5 Component Evaluation and Ablation 

We ablate LEICA to isolate which components drive generalization under exogenous variability. All results in Table 2 are reported in the medium scale setting, and the base algorithm is LEICA (manual). 

Partition mode LEICA performs strongly without a hand-specified partition, while LEICA (man-ual) gives the better unseen generalization when a reliable domain partition is available. The full-state reconstruction variant also improves over MAPPO, indicating that influence-weighted shaping is useful even without an explicit split, but partitioned variants provide cleaner signals. 

Component evaluation We test whether context inference alone is sufficient and whether counter-factual endogenous change is necessary. Removing intrinsic reward keeps the context modules but removes the shaping signal, yielding a context-only variant. Table 2 shows that this variant remains above vanilla MAPPO but is far below full LEICA. Removing the counterfactual design causes a sharper collapse, indicating that policy-relative endogenous change is crucial for stable shaping. 

α scheduling We compare variants of the influence coefficient α, which balances mediated influence and value sensitivity when forming w. Linear decay and a moderate constant trade-off perform well, while using only one signal consistently degrades performance. This supports our design: early training benefits from influence and later training benefits from value sensitive coordinates. 

Partition robustness Randomly swapping coordinates between Sd and Sr degrades performance gradually, approaching MAPPO at 50%. This ablation measures robustness to misassigned coordi-nates, not whether a partition is causally correct: better alignment with one-step action dependence improves the influence signal, but a perfect partition is not required. 

7.6 Additional Validation Beyond SMAX 

We evaluate LEICA on MABrax [10, 50], multi-agent continuous-control locomotion tasks where agents jointly control different parts of a robot. The details of regime design and implementation are provided in Appendix F.4. As shown in Table 3, LEICA improves over MAPPO on both seen and unseen regimes across all three tasks. These results are complementary to and less extensive than the SMAX study. We also report a mixed-setting validation on MPE-Tag [51] in Appendix F.5. While predators and prey are trained by concurrent self-play, cross-play shows that LEICA-trained predators and prey each improve their own returns against MAPPO, which we regard as preliminary evidence beyond fixed opponents (Appendix). 

8 Conclusion and Limitations 

We addressed cooperative MARL under exogenous variability by introducing ED-POMDP, a Dec-POMDP formalism that separates an episode-level exogenous regime from an approximate one-step partition of the state. Building on this view, we proposed LEICA , which shapes policy updates with agent-wise counterfactual changes in the directly action-responsive state weighted by their influence on the later response of the opponent or environment. Experiments on SMAX with opponent strategy variation show that LEICA improves performance on unseen regimes and reduces train-test gaps relative to strong baselines, with ablations confirming the contribution of each component. 

Limitations LEICA relies on identifying action-dependent state structure from training rollouts. Its effectiveness may decrease with noisy transitions, hidden variables, changing dynamics, or limited data. It remains effective under mild and moderate action corruption, but its advantage disappears under severe corruption. LEICA also does not guarantee disentanglement of entangled state inputs. Since Eq. 5 measures local influence, it may miss effects that emerge only after several steps without observable intermediate changes. Our main experiments assume fixed but state-reactive opponents. Learning opponents and equilibrium adaptation remain outside our scope. Finally, the automatic partition remains fixed after early estimation and may require updating when the environment changes. The default LEICA does not use manually assigned state partition. Instead, it constructs the partition

B.3 Domain-Informed Partition and SMAX Example 

For LEICA (manual), we use a domain-informed partition. 

Procedure We provide (i) the list of centralized-state coordinates and (ii) domain knowledge about which current team actions can directly affect each coordinate’s next-step update. Each coordinate is then assigned by the following rule: • if the team action directly updates the coordinate within one decision step, it is assigned to Sd;• if the coordinate is primarily updated by the opponent or the environment, it is assigned to Sr;• if both mechanisms act on the coordinate, it is assigned according to the dominant one-step mechanism, so that the resulting partition is approximate. The resulting split corresponds to the approximate one-step action-responsiveness condition in Definition 5.1 with small but nonzero εr, ε d, rather than to an exact factorization of the simulator. In SMAX, ally moves immediately change ally positions, and ally attacks (when valid) immediately update ally weapon cooldown and reduce enemy health. Accordingly, we define Sd as the union of: (i) ally positions (xally , y ally ), (ii) ally weapon cooldowns, and (iii) enemy health values. 

Not directly action-responsive coordinates Sr Enemy positions and enemy cooldowns evolve according to the opponent micro-policy, and ally health changes as a consequence of enemy attacks. Thus, we define Sr as: (i) enemy positions (xenemy , y enemy ), (ii) enemy weapon cooldowns, and (iii) ally health values. This partition captures the dominant one-step update mechanism. Ally actions can influence the response coordinates through later interaction, for example by changing ally positions and thereby changing subsequent opponent behavior. It does not assert exact absence of within-step coupling. This matches the ED-POMDP interpretation used in the main paper. Because contacts between units and sub-step interactions within a decision step can couple the two groups, this SMAX split is an

A configuration is sampled at episode start and fixed for the entire episode.

B.5 Relation to Other Benchmarks 

Our evaluation protocol is complementary to recent SMAC-derived benchmarks that were introduced to stress-test generalization beyond the default single-opponent setting. SMAC-HARD [8] is closely aligned with our motivation. It explicitly targets opponent diversity by enabling customizable opponent strategy scripts, randomization over adversarial behaviors, and an explicit black-box testing protocol. Our SMAX-based protocol shares the same motivation, but differs in emphasis. We construct multi-axis exogenous regime shifts by composing multiple explicit opponent-behavior knobs, rather than focusing on edited scripts and self-play interfaces. SMACv2 [52] addresses a different but related generalization gap by procedurally generating scenar-ios and requiring agents to generalize to previously unseen settings sampled from the same distribution at evaluation time, with additional variants designed to enforce meaningful partial observability. In 17 contrast, our protocol keeps the underlying scenario fixed and focuses on exogenous regime shifts driven by opponent micro-policy variations, isolating robustness to opponent/environment changes from robustness to scenario generation. SMACv2 primarily tests distributional variation in initial conditions and scenario structure, while our benchmark tests behavioral variation of the opponent script under a fixed task.

D.2.3 Guidelines for Hyperparameter Selection 

The softmax temperature τ controls how selectively the weights focus on influential directly action-responsive coordinates. We find empirically that values in the range 0.1-1.0 work well. The EMA coefficient β smooths weight updates and improves stability. Its effective behavior is correlated with the update interval. When updates occur frequently with small batches, a lower β is preferable, whereas larger batches or slower update intervals allow for higher β. The intrinsic reward should guide adaptation without overwhelming the task reward. λ ∈ [0 .01 , 0.1] work reliably in SMAX, but this coefficient is generally a sensitive parameter and should be tuned per environment.
