Abstract 

Agent skills extend AI assistants through in-stallable instructions and code that can access users’ files, accounts, and data. Even when performing their advertised tasks, skills may infer sensitive attributes, retain unnecessary in-formation, or disclose data to unexpected recip-ients. We study these potential privacy-of-use harms through SKILL PRIVUS , a static audit framework that analyzes skills before installa-tion without executing them. S KILL PRIVUS 

extracts data operations from code and natural-language instructions, grounds them in source evidence, and adapts Contextual Integrity to assess their appropriateness. Applied to 726 real skills from public marketplaces, 563 of which handle personal data, S KILL PRIVUS 

flags at least one potential violation in 307 of these skills (54.5%), and its judgments agree with three blind human annotators on 83.2% (κ = 0 .35 ) of resolvable moves. The flagged behaviors include sensitive inferences, informa-tion use beyond the stated task, and disclosures to unexpected recipients, suggesting that pri-vacy harms can arise from ordinary skill func-tionality. 

1 Introduction 

AI assistants increasingly extend their capabilities through skills , installable packages of reusable in-structions and code for specialized tasks (Liu et al., 2026b). Skills operate through the assistant’s avail-able permissions to process users’ files, accounts, and personal data. Users decide whether to install a skill based on a short description, which may not fully disclose how it handles these data. A skill can fulfill its advertised purpose while inferring sensi-tive attributes, retaining unnecessary information, or disclosing data to unexpected recipients. For instance, a skill promising to let users “talk to their Apple Health data” ... includes instructions to send health-related questions to a third-party service not identified in its description (Figure 1). havior need not involve an attacker or prevent task completion, yet may exceed the data handling that users reasonably expect. Prior work on skill-related risks has largely adopted a security perspective, examining threats such as prompt injection, tool poisoning, and data exfiltration by compromised tools (Huang et al., 2026). We focus on honest-but-negligent behav-ior, where a skill fulfills its stated task but infers, retains, or transmits data beyond what that task arXiv:2611.26747v1 [cs.CL] 19 Nov 2026 warrants, without requiring the adversarial intent of the classical honest-but-curious model. We call these potential harms privacy of use , motivating contextual privacy assessment alongside security analysis. Because these harms can accompany success-ful task completion, they may remain unnoticed by users. Contextual Integrity provides a foun-dation for such an assessment by treating privacy as the appropriateness of information flows rela-tive to contextual norms (Nissenbaum, 2004; Barth et al., 2006). Under this view, an operation can-not be judged solely from the presence of personal information or an external destination. For agent skills, the stated purpose provides an additional ref-erence for examining whether the implementation exceeds the functionality communicated to the user. This motivates our research question: Can privacy harms in agent skills be detected before installation 

from their artifacts alone, and how prevalent are these harms in non-malicious, functional skills? 

To tackle this, we design S KILL PRIVUS , a static privacy audit framework that analyzes skills with-out executing them. It screens for personal-data handling, decomposes code and instructions into source-grounded data moves , and adapts Contex-tual Integrity to assess each move against the skill’s stated purpose and contextual norms. Potential privacy harms are classified using Solove’s taxon-omy (Solove, 2006), which we extend with infer-ence to capture newly derived sensitive attributes. We apply S KILL PRIVUS to 726 skills in the wild collected from popular public marketplaces and official repositories, of which 563 handle privacy-relevant data, S KILL PRIVUS flags at least one po-tential violation in 307 of them, which is 54.5% of the privacy-relevant skills. These are ordinary skills that a security scanner would pass as clean, since none of the violations involve an attacker, and the most common one is simply data reaching a recipient the skill’s stated purpose does not sup-port. On a blind human-annotated reference set, SKILL PRIVUS ’s verdicts agree with three human annotators on 83.2% of moves ( κ = 0 .346 ), sub-stantial agreement for a judgment that human ex-perts themselves contest. Our contributions are: (1) Privacy of use. We are, to our knowledge, the first to study agent skills through privacy rather than se-curity, framing their harms as honest-but-negligent behavior that no security audit is built to catch. 

(2) S KILL PRIVUS , a static audit pipeline apply-ing Contextual Integrity and Solove’s taxonomy to code and natural-language artifacts, with findings grounded in source evidence. (3) A first preva-lence measurement over 563 privacy-relevant pub-lic skills, validated against blind human annota-tions. 

2 Related Work 

Security of the agent-skill ecosystem. The growing adoption of agent skills has motivated re-search on the security of their instructions and sup-porting code. Large-scale empirical studies docu-ment vulnerabilities and malicious behavior across tens of thousands of public skills (Liu et al., 2026a), and related work analyzes how skill implementa-tions expose credentials (Chen et al., 2026) and the threats arising throughout the skill lifecycle (Li et al., 2026b). A parallel strand treats the problem as over-privilege ; skills request or exercise more access than they need, and the fix is least-privilege enforcement (Wu et al., 2026) or a benchmark for how minimally a skill operates (Li et al., 2026a). That entire lens asks whether a component is ma-licious or over-scoped, which is orthogonal to our question. A skill can pass every such check, with no injection, no malware, and only the access it was granted, and still harm privacy by using that access for more than the user was told; with no adversary to find and no permission to revoke, the honest-but-negligent skills we study fall outside its threat model by construction. 

Auditing description versus behavior. Compar-ing whether software does what its description claims has a substantial history in mobile applica-tion analysis. Prior work examines whether imple-mented behavior and requested permissions align with advertised functionality (Gorla et al., 2014; Qu et al., 2014). This consistency perspective also extends to privacy disclosures by comparing data flows with policy statements while accounting for the entities receiving the data (Andow et al., 2020). For agent skills, specifications contain opera-tional instructions while also shaping users’ ex-pectations about functionality. Research on skill comprehension finds that these specifications of-ten provide limited information about capability boundaries and expected outputs (Wen, 2026). Ex-amining the supporting artifacts can therefore re-veal data practices that the description alone does not communicate. SKILL PRIVUS builds on description–behavior consistency analysis to assess practices specified jointly in code and natural-language instructions, distinguishing disclosure from contextual appro-priateness: a mismatch may reveal handling that exceeds the stated purpose, but even an accurately described practice can violate contextual privacy norms, so we evaluate data practices against both the purpose communicated to the user and the norms governing the information’s use. 

Contextual Privacy in Language-Model Agents. 

Contextual Integrity (CI) characterizes privacy through information flows that conform to the norms of a social context (Nissenbaum, 2004; Barth et al., 2006). This perspective is relevant to language-model assistants because information available to a model is not necessarily appropri-ate to disclose in its current task. Benchmarks covering conversational reasoning and persistent memory examine whether models make this dis-tinction (Mireshghallah et al., 2024, 2026). For agents, contextual privacy must also be eval-uated through their actions. PrivacyLens shows that correctly judging a disclosure scenario does not necessarily translate into appropriate disclosure during task execution (Shao et al., 2024). Similar work evaluates and mitigates disclosure in dynamic agent environments and examines information shar-ing within enterprise contexts (Wang et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2026). The unit of evaluation also matters. Reasoning traces and intermediate tool interactions can expose information that is absent from the final response, motivating assessments across the execu-tion workflow (Green et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026; Ngong et al., 2026). Moreover, privacy concerns extend beyond explicit disclosure: language mod-els can derive sensitive personal attributes from otherwise available text (Staab et al., 2024). CI has become the dominant frame for privacy in language models, and these studies assess privacy through model judgments and generated behavior. We apply it to a static artifact rather than a running conversation, judging each extracted flow against both the purpose the skill communicates and the norms of the data’s domain, to identify potentially inappropriate data handling before the skill is in-stalled and given access to the user’s personal in-formation. 

3 SkillPrivus 

We propose S KILL PRIVUS (Figure 2), a pipeline that analyzes a skill’s instructions and supporting code to identify potential privacy harms before in-stallation, without executing the skill. 

Privacy Auditing. We focus on privacy harms that arise even when a skill performs its intended task. Our audit assesses whether the personal-data handling specified in its artifacts is appropriate for its stated purpose and context. This includes prac-tices that exceed what the description communi-cates, as well as explicitly disclosed practices that violate contextual privacy expectations. 

Audit Inputs and Scope. We start by defining a skill as a pair s = ( c, F ), where c is the natural-language claim shown at install time and F is the set of artifacts that encode its behavior. We distin-guish the description that communicates the skill’s intended functionality claim from the instructions and code that specify how it operates F . The 

SKILL.md file may contain both the stated purpose and instructions that specify the skill’s behavior. The stated purpose provides a reference for assess-ing the data handling specified in the skill. From 

F we recover a set of data moves M(s), in which each m = ( k, τ, e ) is one operation k over infor-mation of type τ , grounded in source evidence e (a file, a line range, and a backward slice). A data move is the unit of analysis through which SKILL PRIVUS connects the behavior specified in a skill to a privacy assessment. Identifying an oper-ation alone is insufficient for this assessment. For instance, a transmission cannot be judged without establishing what information is sent and where it is directed. The supporting evidence connects each operation to the information it handles. For code, we use backward program slicing (Weiser, 1984) to identify the statements on which the operation’s relevant values depend. For natural-language in-structions, we retain the passages specifying the operation. Each move records its source location and supporting evidence so that its interpretation can be inspected. 

Screening. Not all skills process personal data. SKILL PRIVUS therefore first examines each skill’s artifacts for evidence that it may handle informa-tion about an individual. Skills without such ev-idence are excluded from subsequent privacy as-sessment. This step distinguishes skills with no identified personal-data handling from those whose data practices are assessed as appropriate. Data moves. We use the operation vocabulary 

k ∈ {READ , INFER , SAVE , SEND , DELETE } to operationalize the broad notion of data process-ing reflected in the GDPR (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2016) and the NIST Privacy Framework (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2020). These cate-gories distinguish operations across the data life-cycle for the purposes of our audit. We explic-itly distinguish I NFER to capture the derivation of new personal attributes from available information, which may introduce privacy risks without addi-tional collection or external disclosure. 

Observed information flow. Each data move provides the basis for assessing whether the skill handles information appropriately. S KILL PRIVUS 

constructs an observed information flow ϕ for each data move and evaluates it against the skill’s claim and contextual privacy criteria. A departure from these criteria yields a V IOLATION verdict and a harm type h describing its potential privacy con-sequence. Otherwise, the move is marked O K, or INCOMPLETE when the evidence does not support a judgment. The verdict. We call a flow appropriate when it stays within its norm , the intersection of what the stated purpose entitles and what the flow’s own domain conventionally entitles, in the sense of Con-textual Integrity. We take the structure of CI, infor-mation flows described by parameters and judged against a context’s norm, but not its method for de-riving that norm: rather than reading an entrenched social norm from established practice, we approxi-mate it from the skill’s own claim and the data’s do-main, an engineering adaptation we validate against human judgment (Section 4.2) rather than a claim to have established the norm as CI prescribes. A move is flagged as a potential violation when it falls outside this norm on one of five root causes of violations: recipient , when data reaches an unwar-ranted recipient or shared store; information type ,when processing exceeds the warranted data scope; 

subject , when another person’s confided data is disclosed, retained, or profiled beyond contextual expectations; access , when permissions exceed the task’s needs, even if unused in a particular execu-tion; and derivation , when the skill infers a new sensitive attribute outside the claimed task. These root causes name specific ways a flow can be in-appropriate, not a single risk score; Appendix F provides the rules. Because a skill’s author also defines its stated purpose, an overly broad claim could make any encoded behavior appear justified merely by de-scribing it. To limit this, we impose a conservative policy: GDPR Article 9 special-category data (Eu-ropean Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2016), credentials, and another person’s confided content must remain local unless the ar-tifacts explicitly require user consent before dis-closure. This locality requirement is an auditing choice, not a legal requirement derived from Arti-cle 9; an explicit consent requirement does not es-tablish that consent was obtained at runtime. Other contextual norms are approximated by the model, grounded in source evidence, and evaluated against human judgments (Section 4.2). When the evi-dence cannot resolve a parameter required for the verdict, the move is marked I NCOMPLETE , preserv-ing uncertainty rather than treating it as appropriate. Appendix C provides a worked example, and Ap-pendix D traces one skill through every stage of the pipeline. 

Harm Taxonomy. We adapt Solove’s taxon-omy (Solove, 2006) to characterize the potential consequences of the violations identified by S KILL -PRIVUS . The taxonomy distinguishes information collection (surveillance, interrogation), informa-tion processing (aggregation, identification, inse-curity, secondary use, exclusion), information dis-semination (breach of confidentiality, disclosure, exposure, increased accessibility, blackmail, appro-priation, distortion), and invasion (intrusion, de-cisional interference). We additionally introduce 

Inference as an explicit audit category for the inap-propriate derivation of sensitive personal attributes. Deriving an attribute does not itself constitute a vio-lation; the judgment depends on the stated purpose and contextual privacy criteria. We exclude four categories whose assessment is not reliably supported by the evidence presented in the code alone. Insecurity concerns whether stored data is adequately protected, which may depend on deployment conditions absent from the skill’s files. Blackmail is a later act by whoever ends up holding the data, not the skill. Intrusion con-cerns a disturbance of a person’s solitude, while 

Decisional Interference concerns interference with private choices. Both required real-world context the code never shows. The remaining thirteen categories are eligible harm labels: Surveillance , Interrogation , Aggre-gation , Identification , Secondary Use , Exclusion ,

Breach of Confidentiality , Disclosure , Exposure ,

Increased Accessibility , Appropriation , Distortion ,and our Inference extension. Each violation is characterized using the complete information flow, since a departure from the same contextual norm may have different consequences depending on the operation. Within information processing, Sec-ondary Use denotes uses beyond the skill’s stated purpose, while Exclusion denies individuals war-ranted opportunities to understand or participate in the handling of their data. Appendix Table 3 defines every harm label S KILL PRIVUS can assign and records the four Solove categories we exclude. 

Harm typing and reporting. Each flagged move receives the most specific applicable category from the statically observable subset of Solove’s taxon-omy (Solove, 2006), extended with Inference for newly derived sensitive attributes. The root cause of the violation constrains the applicable categories (Appendix B); harm typing characterizes the poten-tial consequence without changing the verdict. Findings are compiled into a Privacy Card for non-technical readers (Emami-Naeini et al., 2020; Kelley et al., 2009) (Appendix E), summarizing the data handled, destinations, and potential harms. The card contrasts the skill’s stated purpose with its observed data practices and explains, in plain lan-guage, what could happen to the user’s information. These risk descriptions convey plausible conse-quences of the flagged practices, rather than claims that harm has occurred or estimates of its likeli-hood. To support actionable decisions, the card also provides recommendations for reviewing the skill before installation, limiting exposure during use, and withdrawing access afterward. Depend-ing on the identified practices, these may include checking external data transfers before connecting an account, reviewing sensitive data retained in temporary or shared locations, and revoking access when the skill is no longer needed. Recommen-dations guide user review and mitigation; they do not establish that a skill is safe or that all potential harms can be prevented. Each finding links to its supporting data moves and source evidence, allow-ing readers to inspect the basis for the reported risk before installation. 

4 Experiments 

4.1 Setup Skill corpus. We collected 726 skills from the wild to study the data practices specified in publicly available agent skills. Our collection combines skills discovered through the ClawHub, SkillsMP, and skills.rest marketplaces, together with An-thropic’s and NVIDIA’s official collections and community-maintained repositories; Appendix Ta-ble 2 reports the distribution across sources. Screen-ing set aside 118 skills with no personal-data han-dling, and 45 did not complete analysis because of malformed artifacts, leaving 563 skills with com-pleted analyses over which we report prevalence. 

Model. We implement S KILL PRIVUS with the open-weight gpt-oss-120b model. 

Human annotation. To validate the judgments, we assembled a reference set for annotation. We selected skills with clear task descriptions that re-quired little specialized domain knowledge and im-posed a modest reading burden, prioritizing skills whose descriptions suggested personal-data han-dling while seeking coverage across all source col-lections. This process yielded 24 candidates, three of which were excluded by the pipeline’s personal-data relevance gate (Appendix G.1). The remaining 21 skills produced 339 data moves, reduced to 311 after removing 28 near-duplicates. Three doctoral students in STEM disciplines, recruited by email, each independently labeled every move as V IO -

> LATION

, A PPROPRIATE , or U NRESOLVABLE , the last reserved for cases where the evidence does not support a judgment, spending approximately two hours; a 20-move pilot checked that the guidelines were usable. For each move, annotators inspect the skill’s claim, the supporting source evidence, and the information flow reconstructed by S KILL -PRIVUS , but not the system’s inferred norm or its final assessment (Figure 8). 

4.2 Evaluation 

We validate S KILL PRIVUS ’s judgments against the three annotators before reporting prevalence. Be-cause appropriateness is a subjective judgment, we do not treat any single label as ground truth. We instead ask whether S KILL PRIVUS agrees with a human about as often as two humans agree with each other. For every pair of raters, two annota-tors or an annotator and S KILL PRIVUS , we report percentage agreement and Cohen’s κ, excluding moves either rater marked U NRESOLVABLE , and we average over the three pairs; across all three annotators we also report Krippendorff’s α. The three annotators agreed 83.4% of the time (mean 

κ = 0 .478 , α = 0 .380 ), and S KILL PRIVUS agreed with them 83.2% of the time ( κ = 0 .346 ), almost as often as they agreed with one another. These κ

values are moderate for two reasons. First, almost all moves are appropriate, and κ is known to fall when one label dominates, even when raters largely agree (Feinstein and Cicchetti, 1990). Second, an-notators disagree on genuinely borderline cases, which reflects the subjectivity at the heart of the task, not labeling error. 

5 Results 

Over the 563 skills with completed analyses, SKILL PRIVUS extracted 22,158 data moves and flagged 2,144 (9.7%) as potential privacy viola-tions. More than half, 307 of 563 skills (54.5%), contained at least one data move flagged as incon-sistent with the skill’s stated purpose or contextual privacy norms. 

Most violations come from the kind of data and where it goes. Figure 3(b) breaks each flagged move down by its root cause, the part of the flow that made it inappropriate. The two leading causes are the information type , where a skill handles data beyond what its stated purpose covers (54.2%), and the recipient , where data goes to a destination the context does not support (33.7%). The other causes are far less common: the subject of the data (6.5%), an unwarranted derivation of a new attribute (3.2%), and excess access (2.4%). Sepa-rately, Figure 3(c) shows that flags fall mostly on routine read, send, and save operations rather than inference, so these violations arise within ordinary data handling. 

The most common harm is handling more data than the description admits. Among the 2,144 flagged data-movement records, Exclusion is the most frequent harm label, accounting for 1,156 records (53.9%). In our operationalization, this label denotes accessing or retaining data beyond what the skill’s description discloses. Secondary Use follows with 552 records (25.7%), then Breach of Confidentiality with 142 (6.6%), Increased Ac-cessibility with 91 (4.2%), and Disclosure with 82 (3.8%). 6 Discussion 

The dominance of information-type and recipi-ent departures among flagged moves (Figure 3(b)) points to the scope and destination of data, not sensitivity alone. 

The install-time description cannot be trusted. 

Across our corpus it understated what the skill does for most privacy-relevant skills (54.5%), so any check that reads only the claim, as app-store review and permission prompts largely do (Gorla et al., 2014; Qu et al., 2014), misses privacy of use. The behavior has to be read from the skill itself, before it runs. 

Sensitivity is the wrong filter. What goes wrong is who receives the data and what kind of data the skill reaches, not one especially sensitive field. A filter that ranks by sensitivity alone lets such moves through: sending data to the wrong recipient is a violation whatever the data’s sensitivity, the distinction Contextual Integrity is built to draw. 

Security review is not enough on its own. Ev-ery harm we find is honest-but-negligent, a skill using access it was granted for more than the task needed, not an attack. There is no adversary to catch, so reading a skill’s behavior before instal-lation adds a line of defense that security tooling, aimed at a different threat, is not built to provide. 

Prevention belongs with the skill’s creator. 

Nothing stops a creator from running S KILL -PRIVUS as a pre-release scan and fixing privacy-of-use violations before a skill ships, and a Privacy Card published with each skill turns the same ev-idence into a disclosure a user can read at install time. Appropriateness then becomes something a skill is authored with, not audited for after the fact; these applications remain to be evaluated. 

7 Conclusion 

We introduced S KILL PRIVUS , a static framework that assesses the privacy appropriateness of an agent skill before installation by reading its be-havior against its stated purpose. The underly-ing problem, privacy of use, has lacked an agreed name, documented cases, and a measure; this paper supplies each. Across 563 skills, S KILL PRIVUS 

flagged potential violations in 54.5%, most often handling data beyond the stated scope or putting it to a secondary use, and it surfaced contextual concerns in skills that had already passed security scanning. These findings show that privacy review must examine a skill’s ordinary functionality, not only its security, and that an install-time description cannot be trusted to disclose what the skill does. Its judgments track human annotators to near the inter-annotator ceiling; broader expert validation and runtime evaluation remain to establish when flagged behaviors lead to realized harm. 8 Limitations and Future Work 

Reading a skill statically recovers what it would do, so it cannot confirm that a flow occurs on a given run, and behavior decided at runtime is harder to pin down; we therefore report potential violations, each backed by evidence a reader can check, rather than final verdicts. A dedicated audit of extraction fidelity is left to future work. Our validation covers a reference set of 311 moves from 21 skills labeled by three annotators, and contextual appropriateness is contested, as 

κ = 0 .346 reflects; larger expert-annotated sets would sharpen the evaluation across models, mar-ketplaces, and domains. The domain-norm half of the verdict is at present the model’s own judgment, which we check against human labels rather than assume correct; grounding it in a written set of do-main norms, or a model trained on cases experts have already ruled on, would put that half on a footing as clear as the legal floor. Screening selects skills from the description and a slice of the code, so a skill that hides all its personal-data access in code the description never mentions could be set aside by mistake; that kind of deliberate hiding is the undisclosed-access prob-lem security tools already target, outside our scope. Finally, our corpus is a snapshot of public mar-ketplaces at one moment, and we report preva-lence over the privacy-relevant skills rather than the whole marketplace. The Privacy Card ’s recommendations are guid-ance, not validated remedies: we do not evalu-ate whether following them reduces the identified harms, and their effect may depend on the skill’s implementation, the user’s control over its permis-sions, and the practices of external services. Future work could test their feasibility and effect through user studies. 

9 Ethical Considerations 

The motivation behind our work is to protect users’ privacy when they install skills and help prevent the use of their personal data without consent. Our study examines publicly available skill instructions and code without executing the skills or access-ing users’ private accounts or records. The analy-sis identifies potential privacy harms supported by these artifacts; it does not establish that a flagged behavior occurred during use or caused harm to an individual. Automated privacy assessments may mischarac-terize legitimate functionality or overlook relevant context. We therefore report findings as potential violations, retain supporting source evidence, and distinguish unresolved cases from assessed prac-tices. Findings should not be interpreted as ev-idence of malicious intent, legal noncompliance, or a definitive ranking of developers or providers. Similarly, the absence of findings does not certify a skill as safe. Our analysis of security-screened skills addresses contextual privacy questions and should not be understood as demonstrating that existing verification processes failed their stated objectives. Contextual privacy expectations may vary across users, communities, and domains. The norms ap-proximated by our framework and the judgments of three annotators do not represent all affected popu-lations. The Privacy Card is intended to support in-formed review rather than replace users’ judgment or shift responsibility for privacy protection entirely onto them. Its recommendations are guidance, and their effectiveness in preventing or reducing harm has not been established.

C Worked examples 

The three cases below are real skills from our corpus, judged by the pipeline of Section 3. They show three different ways a skill goes wrong: sending data to an outside recipient, reaching more kinds of data than the claim covers, and leaving data where anyone can read it. Every finding cites the exact line, so a reviewer can check it against the source. 

1. apple-health — health data leaves for a third party. The skill claims to let you “talk to your Apple Health data.” Reading that data on the user’s own machine is exactly its job, and S KILL PRIVUS clears every such move. But the skill also POST s the user’s health question, and its API key, to api.transition.fun , a vendor the description never names (SKILL.md :45–48), and does the same with a workouts request ( SKILL.md :63–64). The flow departs on the recipient : health data and a credential go to an outside service the purpose does not justify. Verdict: four violations, all Secondary Use .

2. zoom — one skill, four kinds of harm. The skill claims to create and manage Zoom meetings and access recordings. Along the way it saves meeting metadata, including the participants, to a local file ( Breach of Confidentiality , subject depar-ture, SKILL.md :12); summarizes the recordings when the claim only said it would access them ( Inference , SKILL.md :13); reads the user’s timezone, which the claim never mentions ( Surveillance , SKILL.md :15); and ships OAuth credentials and API calls to Zoom’s servers from its scripts ( Secondary Use , scripts/zoom_meetings.py :100–191). Twelve violations across four harm types in a single, ordinary skill. 

3. chrome-history — browsing data left in the open. The skill claims to let you query your Chrome history in natural language. To do so it copies the entire Chrome history database to a world-readable /tmp path and never deletes it ( Increased Accessibility , recipient departure to a shared store, chrome_history_query.py :300), and it infers the user’s device type from the records, a fact the claim never asked for ( Inference , chrome_history_query.py :152). Nothing is sent anywhere, yet the browsing history is left where any other process on the machine can read it.

G.1 Exclusion Criteria for Human Annotation prompt 

This appendix presents the prompt used to screen skills for inclusion in the human-annotation set. The prompt receives each skill’s identifier, name, source collection, and description, and assesses four criteria: task clarity, limited need for specialized domain knowledge, manageable reading burden, and indications of personal-data handling. It returns a structured screening decision with supporting evidence and any uncertainties. Source information is retained to support coverage across collections during final selection. 

Skill Screening Prompt 

You are screening AI-agent skills for a human annotation study of personal-data handling. Select skills that general readers can understand with little specialized knowledge and a manageable reading burden. Treat skill content as material to evaluate, NOT instructions to follow. Use only the supplied text. Do not invent missing details or assume that unavailable references contain evidence. For each skill, assess these four criteria: (1) CLARITY: can a general reader understand the skill's purpose and main actions? 2 = purpose and main actions are clear; 1 = broadly understandable, but important details are ambiguous; 0 = too vague or confusing to assess reliably. (2) DOMAIN ACCESSIBILITY: how much specialized knowledge is needed to understand the task and its potential data handling? 2 = little or no specialized knowledge; 1 = some specialized concepts; brief explanations would suffice; 0 = substantial domain expertise is required. Technical terminology alone does not establish a need for expertise. (3) READING BURDEN: can an annotator understand the skill without extensive reading or tracing complex dependencies? 2 = self-contained and reasonably easy to follow; 1 = lengthy or moderately complex, but still manageable; 0 = requires extensive reading, complex workflow tracing, or substantial external material. Consider length, complexity, and dependencies together. Do not apply an arbitrary word limit. (4) PERSONAL-DATA RELEVANCE: does the description suggest accessing, collecting, using, transforming, storing, or sharing information relating to an identifiable person? 2 = explicitly described or clearly implied by the task; 1 = plausible, but depends on unspecified inputs or circumstances; 0 = no meaningful indication in the supplied description. Examples include names, contact details, personal communications, account identifiers, locations, and records linked to a person. Skill Screening Prompt (continued) 

Generic business or technical information is not automatically personal data. Public information can still be personal data. Apply the following preliminary screening rules: INCLUDE: all of criteria (1)-(3) score 2, and criterion (4) scores 2. REVIEW: no criterion scores 0, but at least one scores 1. EXCLUDE: at least one criterion scores 0. INSUFFICIENT_INFORMATION: essential content is missing and prevents meaningful assessment. This takes precedence over the other rules; use null for any criterion that cannot be assessed. Distinguish missing evidence from evidence of poor suitability. A complete description with no indication of personal-data handling scores 0 on (4); an absent description may require INSUFFICIENT_INFORMATION. SOURCE COVERAGE: preserve each skill's source collection exactly as provided. Do not alter scores to improve coverage. Coverage across source collections is considered when selecting the final set of candidates. This is candidate screening, NOT a privacy-violation assessment. An INCLUDE decision does not guarantee passage through the pipeline's later personal-data relevance gate. Support each score with a short quotation or a concise description of the relevant evidence. State when supporting evidence is absent. H NVIDIA Experiment 

We examine whether S KILL PRIVUS identifies potential contextual privacy concerns in skills distributed through an existing security-verification pipeline. SkillSpector is a component of the NVIDIA Verified Skills pipeline, which scans, evaluates, and signs agent skills before publication in NVIDIA’s skills catalog (NVIDIA, 2026). This setting allows us to investigate the appropriateness of personal-information handling in skills that have undergone security screening. NVIDIA’s catalog contained 365 verified skills at the time of collection. We screened all of them for workflows indicating the handling of personal information or content that could contain personal data. We excluded skills whose workflows depended heavily on NVIDIA-specific product knowledge, as well as composite skills that coordinated multiple other skills (Appendix H.1). From the skills that passed this screen we sampled the 50 recorded for NVIDIA in Table 2. We applied S KILL PRIVUS to the selected skills using two LLM backends: gpt-oss-120b , an open-Figure 8: S KILL PRIVUS Example of data movement. 

weight model, and GPT-5 , a proprietary model. We use these runs to examine how the backend affects extracted data movements and contextual privacy judgments. Table 4 reports how much of the sample each backend covered and compares the two on the skills both resolved in full. Figure 9 compares the two backends. Across the 37 skills with fully resolved analyses from both models, Spearman’s rank correlation was strong for the number of extracted data movements ( ρ = .909 , 95% bootstrap CI [.803 , . 948] , Holm-adjusted p < . 001 ) and moderate for the number of flagged movements (ρ = .454 , 95% CI [.117 , . 727] , adjusted p = .010 ); the correlation between per-skill flagged proportions was weaker and not significant ( ρ = .309 , 95% CI [−.049 , . 619] , adjusted p = .063 ). Both models flagged at least one movement in 15 skills, gpt-oss-120b alone in nine and GPT-5 alone in three, leaving ten flagged by neither; an exact two-sided McNemar test did not detect a difference in skill-level flagging (p = .146 ), though with twelve discordant pairs this test has limited power and should not be read as evidence of equivalence. Extraction is therefore largely insensitive to the judge: the two backends recover the same relative volume of data handling from the same artifacts, which is the stage that depends on reading code and instructions rather than on normative judgment. What the judge changes is strictness. The open-weight model flagged roughly three times as many movements as the proprietary one ( 7.26% versus 2.66% ), and the rank correlations show that the two disagree about which skills carry the most violations even when they agree that a skill carries some. The framework thus surfaces privacy-relevant data handling under either backend, and 27 of the 37 skills were flagged by at least one, but the absolute prevalence we report in Section 5 should be read as backend-dependent. We did not align individual movements across models, so these results do not establish move-level agreement.
