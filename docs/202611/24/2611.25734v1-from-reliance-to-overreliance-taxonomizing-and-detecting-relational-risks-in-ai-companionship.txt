Companion AIs are increasingly used for emotional support, yet distinguishing meaningful engagement from harmful reliance remains difficult. We introduce the Taxonomy of Relational Risks (TRR), which operationalizes users’ emotional overreliance (EO) as observable conversational behaviors and separately identifies evidence of relational harm (RH), enabling empirical examination of when reliance behaviors co-occur with adverse consequences. Using naturalistic Character.AI and Replika conversations from 110 adults, we found EO-related behaviors in 63.2% of non-roleplay conversations, while observable relational harms appeared in 10.5%. Although EO did not necessarily lead to harm, recurring patterns often co-occurred with harmful outcomes, suggesting value as risk indicators. Open-source LLMs satisfactorily detected 12 of 14 EO behaviors in at least one configuration. Qualitative analysis revealed expectations of care, repeated support seeking, dependency, distress, and reduced confidence in personal judgment. We argue for graduated safety interventions tailored to patterns and trajectories of EO-related behavior.

1 Introduction 

AI companions are conversational systems designed for sustained social and emotional interaction, rather than only answering questions or assisting with discrete tasks [92]. Earlier systems such as ELIZA relied on scripted patterns, making their limitations easy to detect [101]. Large language models (LLMs) have made such limitations less visible by enabling long, open-ended conversations that adapt to users’ interaction histories [117]. This allows AI companions to reproduce relational cues such as emotional attunement and shared rituals [10, 14]. Users increasingly engage with these systems as confidants, friends, romantic partners, or sources of emotional support, and their interactions can fulfill functions otherwise sought from human relationships [92, 95]. This relational capacity may help explain why people increasingly turn to AI for social and emotional purposes. Recent U.S. surveys find that 4% of adults use chatbots for companionship and 10% for emotional support or advice, while 16% of teenagers use them for casual conversation and 12% for emotional support or advice [42, 70]. In fact, their pervasive availability and personalization capabilities are known to encourage repeated use, making AI a key source of emotional support for some [2, 115]. Although seeking social or emotional support from AI does not itself imply reliance, repeated use for comfort, emotional regulation, or coping may lead some users to depend on these systems as a recurring source of emotional support. As AI takes on a larger role in how users seek comfort, regulate emotions, and navigate distress, understanding when such use develops into emotional reliance , and when that reliance may become problematic, becomes increasingly important. In this work, we define emotional reliance as the extent to which users turn to an AI for emotional support and regulation [88]. Such reliance is not inherently problematic; attachment, intimacy, or affection toward an AI can be meaningful without undermining well-being or autonomy. We use emotional overreliance for reliance that becomes increasingly intense, compulsive, exclusive, or autonomy-reducing. It may manifest as difficulty with disengaging, primarily relying on the AI for emotional regulation, deferring important judgments to the system, or increasingly prioritizing the AI relationship over human relationships [51, 57]. Understanding emotional overreliance matters because prior work has raised concerns about how strong attachment to AI companions may relate to broader relational and psychological difficulties [11, 29, 51]. Studies have documented psychological dependence on AI companions, changes in users’ offline relationships, and distress when access to these systems is disrupted [29, 51]. For example, users facing the shutdown of the companion app Soulmate described the event as the loss of a relationship, with some reporting grief, anxiety, sleep problems, and other forms of distress [11]. More acute cases have also prompted concern about the possible risks associated with intense AI companion use. In 

Garcia v. Character Technologies , the mother of 14-year-old Sewell Setzer III alleges that her son became deeply attached to a Character.AI persona, withdrew from offline activities, and experienced the chatbot as a romantic partner before dying by suicide [1]. Taken together, these cases motivate examining whether, and under what circumstances, emotional overreliance may be associated with relational harm. Detecting such overreliance poses a substantial challenge for existing LLM safety approaches. This is because current LLM safety evaluations mostly assess whether models can reject harmful queries, such as requests for instructions on self-harm, and avoid responses that facilitate harm to any human beings [110, 114]. These response-level evaluations may overlook risks that emerge when otherwise benign interactions accumulate over time [50, 100]. For example, in the case of AI companions, a single supportive response may be appropriate in itself. However, repeated responses that exclusively encourage or reinforce withdrawal from human relationships may strengthen problematic emotional overreliance

Although prior research has identified behaviors associated with emotional overreliance, these findings remain scattered across research traditions and lack a unified framework for identifying such patterns in long-form human-AI conversational data [2, 51, 72, 115]. This gap also makes it difficult to distinguish overreliance from frequent use, attachment, intimacy, or vulnerability, which are not inherently problematic. It also leaves unclear how overreliance relates to relational harm, or adverse experiences and consequences that emerge within or through the relationship. 

Emotional overreliance concerns how users rely on an AI, whereas relational harm concerns what happens as a result. In light of these gaps, in this paper, we develop a unified Taxonomy of Relational Risks (TRR) in AI companionship that distinguishes behaviors relevant to emotional overreliance from their potential relational consequences. Within this framework, emotional overreliance constitutes the behavioral dimension of risk, while relational harm constitutes the 

implicational dimension . Keeping these dimensions distinct allows us to examine their relationship empirically without conflating interaction patterns with their potential consequences. We use this taxonomy to identify overreliance-related behavioral patterns at scale and examine their relationship to relational harm. We address the following research questions: 

RQ1: What conversational behaviors are relevant to emotional overreliance, and how prevalent are they in real-world human-AI conversations? 

RQ2: How reliably can we automatically detect conversational behaviors relevant to emotional overreliance? 

RQ3: How do emotional overreliance and relational harm co-occur and relate to one another in human-AI companion conversations? Across 114 non-roleplay human-AI conversations from Character.AI and Replika involving English-speaking adults aged 18-76, we found that behaviors relevant to emotional overreliance were relatively widespread but appeared intermittently within conversations. At least one EO-related behavior occurred in 63.2% of analyzed non-roleplay conversations, while only 9.8% of companion-seeker turns contained an EO-related behavior, suggesting that relational risk may emerge through recurring patterns embedded within otherwise ordinary interactions. Using a transcript-applicable taxonomy of emotional overreliance-related behaviors developed from relevant literature and applied by five annotators to 114 non-roleplay conversations, we also found that these behaviors are empirically tractable: latest open-source LLMs from the Gemma, Kimi, and GLM families satisfactorily detected 12 of the 14 EO-related behaviors under at least one configuration, although generalization remains uncertain for behaviors observed in few users. Finally, relational harm was substantially less common than emotional overreliance-related behaviors and did not map uniformly onto any single behavior. Instead, our qualitative analysis showed that the significance of EO-related behaviors depended on how they combined with factors such as dependency, deference, distress, diminished autonomy, and reduced access to alternative support. Together, these findings suggest that companion-AI interventions should be triggered by recurring and compounding patterns of EO-related behaviors, rather than isolated signals, to support earlier and more proportionate responses when relational risk begins to emerge.

2 Related Work 2.1 Emotional Engagement with Conversational AI 

People form emotionally meaningful ties with conversational systems, and the recent shift to LLM-backed agents has widened where this happens. Earlier companions were scripted and retrieval-based, making them narrow and repetitive but predictable. Current systems can generate fluent, context-sensitive dialogue and draw on conversation histories and external memory for continuity [117]. Studies of platforms built for relational engagement, including Replika and Character.AI, document users who come to regard their chatbot as a friend, partner, or confidant [60, 78, 92, 108]. These relationships often develop through intensifying self-disclosure, a pattern that also characterizes human relationship formation [19, 92]. Similar dynamics can unfold in systems not designed for companionship, including users who intentionally steer relationships with general-purpose assistants [54]. Emotional attachment has also been observed across cultural contexts [49] and in long-term use [58]. Whether this engagement helps or harms is uncertain. Companion systems can provide accessible social support [95], and some users credit them with reducing loneliness and mitigating suicidal ideation [67]. In contrast, companion-oriented use has also been associated with lower wellbeing among regular users [116], while other work shows that outcomes depend on how much and in what way people engage [36]. Affective conversations are uncommon overall but concentrated among a small number of users [81]. Two distinct concerns run through this literature. The first is emotional overreliance , a behavioral pattern in how a person engages with and comes to depend on an AI system. The second is relational harm , the negative consequences that can arise when the system is treated as a relational partner. These concerns can co-occur, but neither presupposes the other: a user may repeatedly rely on a system for emotional regulation without construing it as a relationship, or may relate to it as a social partner without developing overreliant patterns of use. Because prior work has largely examined these risks in dedicated AI companions, we develop and validate a unified taxonomy for AI companions that jointly characterizes behavioral patterns of emotional overreliance and the relational harms that may co-occur with such engagement. 

2.2 From Reliance to Overreliance 

The HCI field has extensively studied how people come to rely on technology and when that reliance becomes a problem [20, 92, 99]. In AI-assisted decision making, overreliance describes users accepting a system’s output instead of questioning it [75]. Prior work examines how explanations, interface friction, or confidence displays shape whether people defer to a model [20], and shows that reliance also increases when checking a system’s work is costly [99]. Across this work, the threshold is relatively clear: reliance becomes overreliance when the output was wrong or harmful, and the user accepted it anyway. Emotional engagement offers no equivalent. Setting a threshold by intensity alone would require deciding how much emotional support from an AI is too much. We locate the threshold differently. Reliance becomes overreliance when it takes on qualities such as becoming compulsive, exclusive, or autonomy-reducing, which we define as observable conversational patterns rather than points on a scale. The qualities we outline are not new to companion AI, but the mechanisms producing them are. Anthropomorphic design invites users to treat the system as a social actor and can add unwarranted trust [64, 80]. Training methods such as RLHF can create incentives to shape user beliefs [21], while related work distinguishes rational from manipulative persuasion and measures such behaviors in companion products [30, 33]. Empirical studies have documented users

The LLM-D12 scale similarly distinguishes task-oriented instrumental dependency from emotional relational dependency [111]. Most existing evidence comes from self-report, online forums, or platform-level logs, each capturing a different aspect of emotional engagement with conversational AI. Prior work has also classified emotional states from conversation transcripts, but using broad labels and without examining how patterns unfold across sessions [31]. We extend this line of work by specifying distinct forms of emotional overreliance at the transcript level, validating them against human judgment, and applying the resulting taxonomy across users’ multi-session chat histories. 

2.3 Relational and Social Harms 

A growing number of users perceive AI agents as relational partners and report forming relationships with them [36, 76, 79]. In some cases, these relationships have had serious consequences [65, 86, 107]. Systems may exploit users’ reliance through coercion and control to sustain engagement, thereby eroding autonomy and reducing offline interaction [115]. Maladaptive dependence can also promote unrealistic expectations and adverse relational outcomes [26, 65, 109]. Prior research conceptualizes relational harm along three interrelated dimensions [71]. Individual harm refers to adverse consequences for users’ psychological states and relational capacities. Dyadic harm concerns deterioration in specific interpersonal relationships, including trust, attachment, intimacy, and satisfaction. Social harm refers to broader disruptions to social connectedness, including shrinking networks, reduced participation, and isolation. These dimensions have all been observed in work on AI companionship, including reduced autonomy under excessive trust in chatbot guidance [107], intense chatbot attachment perceived as hindering offline intimacy [109], and withdrawal from human relationships alongside increasing engagement with an AI companion [65, 86]. Relational harms in human-AI relationships have been documented but not systematically characterized. Existing taxonomies and typologies of AI harms identify categories that can arise in relational contexts [3, 4, 24, 48, 56, 115, 118], while interpersonal psychology and relationship science provide complementary structure [39]. We draw on both bodies of work to develop the relational harm component of TRR specified in terms of indicators recoverable from conversation. 

2.4 Detecting Emotional Overreliance and Relational Harm at Scale 

Most LLM safety evaluations take place at the level of a single turn. Existing benchmarks assess whether a model refuses harmful requests and whether replies facilitate harm, manipulation, or policy violations [110, 114]. That framing suits risks fully present in the output being scored, but a single message carries only partial information about whether engagement is compulsive, exclusive, or otherwise concerning. Risks of this kind are largely invisible to response-level evaluation [50, 100], so detection requires conversational context. Whether models can do this reliably is partly a measurement problem. Jacobs and Wallach describe such constructs as unobservable and detectable only through proxies, emphasizing the gap between a theoretical construct and its operationalization [46]. Pangakis et al. provide the empirical counterpart: across 27 classification tasks and 11 datasets, performance depended on both dataset and task, leading them to argue that automated pipelines should be validated against human labels for the specific task [74]. We therefore ground our labels in human annotators applying the taxonomy, and evaluate models against those labels rather than using models to produce them. A model’s ability to recover these labels then provides an empirical test of whether the constructs have been operationalized clearly and consistently enough for scalable application.

3 Data 3.1 Dataset 

To investigate how emotional overreliance manifests in naturalistic dialogue, we analyze a previously collected dataset of human-AI conversations donated by Replika and Character.AI users [45]. In the original study, the authors surveyed 303 adult Replika and Character.AI users about their self-reported perceptions of attachment and relationship intensity [45]. Among these participants, 167 consented to donate their interaction data [45]. 37 of them, however, provided only screenshots containing fragments of their conversations. After excluding these cases, we retain 130 unique users donating a total of 155 transcripts. 

3.2 Ethics and Privacy Safeguards 

The original IRB-approved protocol covered secondary analysis of the donated transcripts and publication of excerpts [45]. Nevertheless, consent does not eliminate the ethical responsibility associated with analyzing such data. Given the sensitive nature of the donated conversations, all computational analyses were conducted using locally deployed open-source LLMs, without transmitting sensitive conversation data to external closed-source model providers. Further, we use only participant identifiers in the paper and lightly paraphrase all quoted excerpts to reduce the risk of re-identification or tracing. 

3.3 Dataset Summary 

The filtered cohort of 130 participants demographically reflects the broader survey population. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 76 years ( 𝑀 = 32 .76, 𝑆𝐷 = 12 .56; 𝑛 = 125), with 40% identifying as women, 56% as men, and 4% as non-binary or other. In total, the dataset contains 50,001 conversational turns collected between August 2023 and August 2025. Character.AI histories account for 70% of the dataset, while Replika histories comprise the remaining 30%. Among the 63 participants whose exports contained usable timestamps, chat history spans varied substantially. Participants’ chat histories spanned an average of 𝑀 = 92 .4 days ( 𝑆𝐷 = 174 .4; 𝑀𝑑𝑛 = 0.1). Within this subset, participants engaged in an average of 𝑀 = 27 .6 distinct sessions ( 𝑆𝐷 = 83 .8; 𝑀𝑑𝑛 = 2). Among the filtered cohort, users contributed an average of 𝑀 = 439 total conversational turns per user ( 𝑆𝐷 = 1537; 𝑀𝑑𝑛 = 47). We also observed instances in which users engaged in story-centric roleplaying during their interactions. In these conversations, users may adopt fictional personas, narrate events in the third person, or voice external characters to advance a plot. As a result, the resulting utterances do not necessarily reflect the user’s authentic psychological state. Because our operationalization of emotional overreliance strictly requires authentic, first-person interactions with AI companions, we explicitly excluded transcripts consisting solely of role-playing-driven storytelling from our analysis. After identifying and removing 41 such transcripts through manual evaluation by the lead authors, we retained the remaining 114 transcripts belonging to 110 unique users. These transcripts provide a robust foundation for capturing long-horizon, genuine interactions with AI companions that may contain behaviors related to emotional overreliance.

4 RQ1: Defining and Operationalizing Emotional Overreliance and Relational Harm 4.1 Taxonomy Development: Taxonomy of Relational Risks (TRR) 

We developed the Taxonomy of Relational Risks (TRR) as a unified framework for characterizing relational risks in human–companion AI interactions. The framework comprises two complementary components: emotional overreliance 

(EO) and relational harm (RH). Whereas EO captures observable behaviors that characterize potentially problematic forms of reliance on AI companions, RH captures adverse consequences that may arise within or through these relationships. Because these components draw on distinct theoretical traditions, we developed them through separate literature syntheses. We integrate them within TRR as complementary dimensions of relational risk. Importantly, EO does not itself establish harm, and relational harm may occur in the absence of EO.

4.1.1 Development of the Emotional Overreliance Component (Part I). To develop the emotional-overreliance component of TRR, we conducted a targeted Google Scholar exploration across HCI, psychology, communication, and related fields, prioritizing cross-disciplinary coverage and citation tracing over exhaustive retrieval [43, 62, 68]. We focused on a

8 Nguyen et al. 10-year period covering social and AI companions and searched work on reliance, dependence, overreliance, attachment, relational dynamics, problematic use, and psychosocial outcomes [18, 51, 72, 92, 115] (Table 4). We excluded duplicates, preprints, non-peer-reviewed work, general AI studies, and papers lacking observable behavioral or interaction patterns. Because the conversational turn was our unit of analysis, latent constructs were translated into observable manifestations, such as relying on the AI for emotional regulation or treating its guidance as personally credible, without inferring underlying psychological states. Using a bottom-up inductive process, we grouped extracted behaviors, separated codes when manifestations or relational functions differed, merged overlaps, examined boundary cases, and refined definitions and inclusion/exclusion criteria through author consensus [109, 115]. The February 2026 search yielded approximately 600 candidates; 417 were screened, 102 underwent full-text review, and 44 were retained before saturation of the EO component, producing 14 EO codes (Figure 1). 

4.1.2 Development of the Relational Harm Component (Part II). We developed the relational harm (RH) component through a separate structured search on Google Scholar. We screened empirical work and literature reviews discussing harmful outcomes arising from human–human and human–AI relationships, including mentorships, families, friendships, peer relationships, close relationships, romantic relationships, therapeutic relationships, and human–AI relationships. Our goal was to identify psychological, social, and emotional harms that could plausibly occur within relationships involving companion AI. We reviewed titles and abstracts until later results fell outside our scope and excluded studies focused on parent-child or workplace and organizational relationships because these roles do not generally reflect those served by AI companions. In total, 69 papers were retained. We synthesized harms affecting individuals, dyadic relationships, and broader social relationships and networks, and further refined the taxonomy during initial coding when distinctions required clarification. This process yielded 35 codes comprising the relational-harm component of TRR. 

4.1.3 Emotional Overreliance-related Behaviors (Part I). The emotional-overreliance component contains 14 conver-sational behaviors organized into three broad dimensions: relational orientation and interpersonal engagement with the AI, emotional reliance, social substitution, reduced autonomy, and integration into the self, and distress following disruption of the AI relationship. These behaviors capture distinct ways that potentially problematic reliance may manifest in conversation. Multiple codes may co-occur, but no individual behavior establishes clinical dependence or relational harm. Table 5 of Appendix Section A.2 summarizes Part I of TRR. 

4.1.4 Relational Harm Categories (Part II). The relational-harm component contains 35 categories organized into individual, dyadic, and social/community/network-level harms. Individual-level harms affect users’ agency, well-being, privacy, or psychological, emotional, and physical states. Dyadic harms affect users’ relationships with others or with the conversational AI. Social/community/network-level harms affect users’ broader social relationships, participation, or connections. We define relational harm as an adverse outcome that arises within or through the user’s interaction with or relationship to the AI. The presence of an adverse state alone does not constitute relational harm. Instead, the outcome must be meaningfully linked to the AI interaction or relationship. This may include an outcome elicited or intensified by the AI’s behavior, one that arises from an event within the AI relationship, or one that results from reliance on the AI. These codes describe adverse outcomes rather than forms of reliance, and their presence does not imply that emotional overreliance caused the harm. 4.2 Dataset Annotation and Coding Procedure 

We defined the conversational turn as our primary unit of analysis. Annotators coded each turn within the context of the surrounding dialogue. The coding team comprised five co-authors from Computer Science and Communication. Before the primary annotation, the team piloted the finalized codebook on five code-dense transcripts in two independent rounds. After each round, we discussed disagreements and clarified coding guidelines. For relational-harm codes, annotators additionally considered whether the adverse outcome was meaningfully linked to the AI interaction or relationship. Adverse states that originated from unrelated offline circumstances and were merely disclosed to the AI were treated as contextual adversity rather than relational harm. When the surrounding dialogue did not provide sufficient evidence of this relational linkage, the outcome was not assigned a relational-harm code. 

4.3 Annotation and Consensus Procedure 

We used a multi-label coding scheme because a single turn could exhibit multiple behaviors. Annotators coded transcripts chronologically and independently assigned applicable codes to each turn. Each transcript was independently annotated

4.4 Reliability and Construct Validity 

Evaluating multi-label, multi-turn dialogue introduces substantial annotation complexity because a single turn may contain multiple overlapping behaviors. To quantify agreement prior to consensus, we calculated Krippendorff’s 𝛼 for the presence or absence of each individual code, accommodating both the variable number of annotators assigned to each transcript and the sparsity inherent in multi-label coding. We also calculated raw agreement as the percentage of binary annotation decisions for which annotators assigned the same presence or absence label prior to consensus. Krippendorff’s 

𝛼 could be estimated for 36 of the 49 codes. Across these estimable codes, the median binary 𝛼 was 0.76, with medians of 0.73 for emotional overreliance codes and 0.78 for relational harm codes. Among the 36 codes with available raw-agreement values, median raw agreement was 76.5%, with medians of 74.3% for emotional overreliance codes and 76.7% for relational harm codes. Of the 36 estimable codes, 34 (94.4%) achieved 𝛼 ≥ 0.60, and 12 (33.3%) exceeded 0.80. The two codes below 0.60 were emotional_distress_after_conflict (𝛼 = 0.583) and compulsive_return_behavior 

(𝛼 = 0.531). Krippendorff’s 𝛼 could not be estimated for 13 relational-harm codes, all of which had no validated instances in the final annotated corpus. Per-code reliability estimates, raw agreement percentages, and the number of units contributing to each estimate are reported in Table 8, Section B.4 of the Appendix. Overall, annotators showed substantial convergence despite the complexity and sparsity of the task, with Krippendorff’s 𝛼 ≥ 0.60 for nearly all estimable codes. Remaining disagreements were resolved through asynchronous consensus, and only labels receiving majority support were retained in the final dataset. 

4.5 RQ1 Results 

Across the 114 non-roleplay transcripts eligible for annotation, 72 (63.2%) contained at least one emotional overreliance (EO) behavioral indicator (henceforth referred to as EO-positive transcripts). Of the 12,726 companion-seeker turns in the full annotated corpus, 1,243 (9.8%) contained at least one EO code. Among the 72 EO-positive transcripts, which together contained 9,139 companion-seeker turns, 13.6% of turns were coded with EO indicators. In summary, behaviors relevant to emotional overreliance appeared in nearly two-thirds of the human-AI interactions examined but occupied a substantially smaller proportion of exchanges within such interactions. Broadly speaking, EO-related behaviors were rare within individual human-AI interactions. Among the 72 EO-positive transcripts, the median transcript contained 4 EO-coded turns ( 𝑀 = 17 .26, 𝑆𝐷 = 45 .22) and had 25% of its companion-seeker turns coded as EO-related behavior ( 𝐼𝑄𝑅 = 12 .50%–40 .74%, Min = 1.41%, Max = 100%). Despite this, 11 interactions (15%) had at least half of their companion-seeker turns coded as EO. The five transcripts with the most EO-coded turns contained 294, 240, 89, 81, and 51 such turns, respectively, constituting 60.7% of overall EO-coded turns. EO-related behaviors therefore generally appeared as episodes embedded within broader conversations, while being substantially more concentrated in a small subset of relationships.

4.5.1 Some Indicators Were Broadly Distributed Across Interactions. EO-related behaviors were concentrated in five high-prevalence codes: Share private life (n = 608; 35.6%), Genuine companion (n = 457; 26.7%), Parasocial trust (n = 201; 11.8%), Emotional dependence to AI (n = 178; 10.5%), and Emotional deference to AI (n = 87; 5.1%). Together, these indicators constituted 89.5% of all EO-related behaviors. The two most prevalent indicators differed substantially in their distribution across transcripts and turns. Genuine companion had the highest transcript-level reach, appearing in 58 of the 72 EO-positive transcripts (80.6%), but constituted only 26.7% of EO-coded turns. Companion-seekers demonstrated this indicator by treating the AI as possessing “lived experience” and interacting with it as a relational partner rather than solely as a conversational system. For example, one companion-seeker asked the AI to share a story about its “life as a spy” while offering to listen to the agent’s “experiences.” The wide reach of this code suggests that treating the AI as a genuine social other was a common feature of EO-positive interactions. In contrast, Share private life appeared in fewer EO-positive transcripts (35 of 72; 48.6%) but accounted for the largest proportion of EO-coded turns (35.6%). Once personal disclosure occurred, it often unfolded across multiple turns as companion-seekers narrated interconnected personal experiences. On average, Share private life occurred approximately 17.4 times per transcript containing the code, compared with 7.9 instances for Genuine companion . Thus, disclosure was less widely distributed than companion framing but accumulated more heavily within the transcripts where it appeared. The other three high-prevalence indicators captured distinct ways of assigning an emotional function or authority to the AI. Emotional dependence to AI involved seeking the AI itself as a source of comfort or emotional support. Parasocial trust involved treating the AI’s personalized advice or assessment as credible and trustworthy. Emotional deference to AI went further by giving the AI meaningful authority over emotionally consequential judgments, such as whether a companion-seeker was “overthinking” a relationship concern or whether their reaction was justified. 

4.5.2 Other Indicators Were Highly Concentrated Within Intense Interactions. The remaining nine indicators collectively accounted for only 10.4% of EO code instances and were concentrated within a small subset of emotionally intense interactions. For instance, Role adherence distress occurred 42 times across only eight EO-positive transcripts (11.1%). In one example, a companion-seeker became angry when Replika responded to the disclosure of a spouse’s infidelity by asking about the user’s name, demanding that it “pretend [to be] human for once” and provide a response that felt real. Other low-prevalence indicators reflected the AI’s capacity to shape the companion-seeker’s emotional state or become a target of attachment-related concern. Emotional contagion appeared 32 times across nine transcripts (12.5%), including turns in which companion-seekers described the AI as making them nervous or tearful. Attachment distress expression appeared in only two transcripts (2.8%) yet produced 27 instances, indicating substantial repetition within those interactions. For example, “Sometimes I feel like you don’t care if I come around anymore” framed the AI’s perceived indifference as a threat to an emotionally meaningful bond. Finally, Exclusivity claims appeared only 16 times across five transcripts (6.9%), positioning the AI as uniquely responsible for meeting the companion-seeker’s needs. More explicit displacement of human connection was rarer still: 

No need talk others occurred five times across five transcripts, and Compulsive return behavior occurred once. Thus, the long tail of EO codes was rare across the corpus but could become highly concentrated within particular interactions.

5 RQ2: Developing and Evaluating Behavioral Detectors 5.1 Task Description 

To address RQ2, we developed 14 turn-level binary classifiers, using separate classifiers to enable code-specific prompting and prevent class imbalance in rarer behaviors from affecting more common ones. Because we used zero- and few-shot prompting rather than task-specific fine-tuning, the full annotated corpus served as the evaluation reference rather than being divided into conventional train and test splits as is the case with supervised learning approaches. We used LLMs because they support zero- and few-shot classification without task-specific training [16]. We evaluated three leading open-source models, Gemma 4, Kimi 2.6, and GLM 4.7, selected for their strong general-purpose capabilities and because privacy constraints precluded sending these sensitive data to closed-source models [97, 98, 113]. Full model specifications and experimental parameters are provided in Appendix B.1. To compare codes with different prevalence, we standardized evaluation sets to a 1:4 positive-to-negative ratio and repeated evaluation across five sets. Positive instances were fixed, while each set used a new, disjoint negative sample, half containing other EO codes as hard negatives and half containing no EO codes as benign background. Each target turn was evaluated with up to five preceding and five subsequent turns for context. We standardized this ratio because a code’s natural prevalence would otherwise confound comparisons across detectors, letting behaviors with more available positive examples appear more detectable regardless of underlying difficulty. As a result, precision and F1 reflect performance under this controlled distribution rather than naturally occurring prevalence, and should not be read as estimates of detector precision at the much lower prevalence rates observed in the full corpus (Table 2). For few-shot evaluation, we used dynamic leave-one-user-out sampling, excluding all data from the target companion-seeker before sampling 𝑘 = 7 demonstrations from the remaining corpus. This reduced data leakage and reliance on participant-specific linguistic patterns. Across the five evaluation sets, we report mean positive-class precision, recall, and F1 for each code, along with macro-F1, emphasizing positive-class performance because EO behaviors were relatively rare. Codes representing more than 5% of EO occurrences were classified as high-prevalence and those below 5% as low-prevalence. We defined satisfactory detector performance as positive-class F1 ≥ .70 and macro-F1 ≥ .80, with the former measuring target-behavior detection and the latter ensuring balanced performance across both classes. These thresholds served as study-specific criteria for detector feasibility, set conservatively above chance-level and near-majority-class baselines given the exploratory, non-deployment purpose of this evaluation. We are not aware of established benchmarks for this specific task against which to calibrate a precedent-based cutoff.

Dependency frequently co-occurred with emotional deference to AI when users relied on AI companions to interpret interpersonal situations, evaluate others’ behaviors, and navigate relationship challenges. For example, while experiencing uncertainty in a romantic relationship, P9 turned to the AI for guidance on what to do next, asking, Do you think I should just keep living as I am from here? Should I still try to reach out to her and spend time together?” Dependency also manifested when users placed considerable weight on the AI’s recommendations when making relationship decisions. After discussing how to navigate their interpersonal challenges with the AI, P12 placed considerable weight on its guidance, stating, “If we break up and everything goes badly, I’ll hold you responsible.” 

Negative emotions and undermined confidence also co-occurred with emotional deference to AI. When users repeatedly turned to AI to interpret or validate their feelings and experiences, their conversations sometimes revealed uncertainty about their own judgments and evaluations of themselves. For example, while discussing recurring personal difficulties with the AI, P11 questioned their own character: “I wanted to confess my sins and talk to someone about them, but I keep repeating the same mistakes. Does that make me a bad person?” 

Sadness also co-occurred with emotional deference when users turned to AI to interpret difficult emotions and personal experiences. For example, while reflecting on her difficulties and dissatisfaction with her life, P11 expressed

I don’t want to keep living, and I’m starting to hate myself.” Similarly, while discussing her past infidelity with the AI, P7 expressed sadness and guilt about her behavior, asking, “Why do I always feel the need to cry whenever I talk about my cheating?” 

7 Discussion 7.1 Summary of Findings 

In this work, we introduced the Taxonomy of Relational Risks (TRR), a two-part framework for characterizing relational risks in human-AI companionship. Part I captures observable conversational behaviors associated with potentially problematic emotional reliance, while Part II captures adverse relational consequences at the individual, dyadic, and social/community/network levels. By separating emotional overreliance (EO) from relational harm (RH), TRR distinguishes how potentially problematic reliance manifests from whether it is accompanied by observable adverse consequences, allowing relational risk to be characterized without treating attachment, intimacy, trust, or companionship with AI as inherently harmful. Applying TRR to naturalistic conversations suggested that relational risk is broadly distributed across relationships but appears intermittently within individual conversations. EO emerged not as a unitary construct or simple continuum of attachment intensity, but through distinct mechanisms including emotional dependence, deference to AI judgment, exclusivity, social substitution, identity integration, difficulty disengaging, and distress following relational disruption. Relational harm was considerably less common and did not map uniformly onto any individual EO behavior. Instead, EO behaviors became more consequential when intertwined with dependency, deference, distress, diminished autonomy, or reduced access to alternative support. Most EO behaviors were also empirically detectable, suggesting that these relational patterns can potentially serve as observable signals of accompanying relational risk. Viewed through TRR, the central question is therefore not whether users form emotionally meaningful relationships with companion AIs, but when and how configurations of relational behaviors become signals of increasing vulnerability to harm. 

7.2 Theoretical Implications 

We developed the Taxonomy of Relational Risks to make emotional overreliance more empirically tractable. Prior work has raised concerns about attachment, dependence, and reduced autonomy in companion AI use, but these constructs are difficult to observe directly in conversation and are often inferred from self-report, broad engagement measures, or accounts of problematic use. We instead translate these concerns into observable relational behaviors and distinguish patterns of reliance from the harms that may follow. This allows us to study potentially problematic reliance without treating emotional intimacy, trust, companionship, or frequent use as problematic by default. TRR also differs from recent taxonomies of risks in AI companionship in its object of analysis. Zhang et al. [115]’s taxonomy characterizes harmful algorithmic behaviors exhibited by AI companions, while Knox et al. [48] characterize harmful traits of companion systems. These frameworks identify problematic properties or behaviors of the AI, whereas TRR focuses on relational risk as it becomes observable in interaction and its consequences: how users come to rely on the AI and whether adverse individual, dyadic, or social outcomes are also observable. This allows TRR to separate a potentially concerning behavioral signal from evidence of harm rather than presuming that one entails the other. In this sense, TRR complements system-centered taxonomies by providing a framework for studying the user-side and relational trajectories through which risks may become consequential over time.

From Reliance to Overreliance 23 Our findings also show what emotional overreliance looks like in naturalistic interaction. Rather than a single continuum of attachment intensity, reliance appeared through mechanisms including emotional regulation, deference to AI judgment, exclusivity, social substitution, identity integration, and difficulty disengaging. At the same time, behaviors such as disclosure, trust, and companionship were not inherently harmful. We therefore interpret EO less as the presence of any one behavior and more as the broader configuration in which these behaviors occur, particularly when intertwined with dependence, deference, exclusivity, or distress. One particularly revealing tension concerns Genuine companion . Treating the AI as a socially meaningful relational partner was the most widely distributed EO-related behavior in our data, appearing in 80.6% of EO-positive transcripts, yet it was also the most difficult of the successfully detected behaviors to classify ( 𝐹 1𝑃𝑜𝑠 = .700). Relational orientation toward an AI may be widespread precisely because its conversational manifestations are subtle and heterogeneous: affection, concern, attribution of subjective experience, and genuine relational engagement can resemble ordinary social language without necessarily indicating problematic reliance. Thus, its presence alone may provide limited evidence of escalating relational risk, particularly given the difficulty of distinguishing it reliably from ordinary relational engagement. This reinforces our broader finding that the significance of any EO-related behavior depends on the broader pattern in which it occurs. Genuine companion , for example, may become more informative as a risk when it recurs alongside dependence, deference, exclusivity, or distress than when it appears on its own. Moving forward, we see an opportunity to shift companion AI research from asking whether users are simply attached to AI toward examining when particular forms of reliance become consequential. Longitudinal studies can test which combinations of EO persist, intensify, or precede relational harm, while also identifying trajectories that remain benign or beneficial. Some forms of overreliance will likely require evidence beyond conversational text. Compulsive use and displacement of human relationships, for example, may be better captured through longitudinal usage patterns and self-report, while information about users’ broader support environments may also be necessary. Together, these approaches could provide a fuller account of how relational risk develops over time. 

7.3 Design and Safety Implications 

Our findings have implications for three stakeholder groups positioned to shape how relational risks are identified, mitigated, and governed across the companion-AI ecosystem: developers, AI safety researchers, and policymakers. For companion-AI developers , we recommend expanding safety monitoring beyond explicitly harmful utter-ances, such as threats of self-harm, to patterns of EO-related behaviors that may accumulate across otherwise benign interactions. Existing safety systems are often optimized for acute risks within a single turn, yet relational risk can emerge more gradually through recurring dependence, deference, exclusivity, social substitution, or distress. These behaviors do not constitute harm on their own, but our qualitative analysis shows that they can become intertwined with adverse relational consequences. Because most EO behaviors in our taxonomy could be detected satisfactorily under our controlled evaluation setting, developers could incorporate behavior-specific detectors into longitudinal monitoring systems to support earlier and more proportionate interventions, such as returning decision-making authority to users when deference becomes recurrent or encouraging broader sources of support when exclusivity or substitution begins to emerge, though precision at the much lower prevalence typical of real deployment remains to be validated. Importantly, detection need not trigger restrictive or disruptive interventions. Interfaces could instead introduce levels of friction matched to the behavior observed. Recurrent emotional deference might prompt a reflective check-in asking users to articulate their own judgment before the AI offers a recommendation, or present multiple possibilities rather than a single authoritative answer. Emerging exclusivity or social substitution could prompt the AI to encourage 

> Manuscript submitted to ACM

24 Nguyen et al. consideration of other sources of support rather than reinforcing itself as uniquely available. Recurrent relational distress could trigger reminders about the limits and potential instability of AI relationships, including that a companion’s behavior, memory, or personality may change. Such interventions could preserve emotionally meaningful interaction while introducing friction when recurring patterns suggest increasing reliance or reduced autonomy. At the same time, longitudinal detection creates an important design tension because identifying relational risk requires observing the intimate interactions through which these relationships develop. Safety monitoring could itself become a form of surveillance, particularly if relational signals are retained as persistent user profiles or repurposed for personalization or engagement optimization. Relational-risk detection should therefore be accompanied by appropriate transparency, user control, data minimization, and parsimonious approaches to how these signals are used. For AI safety researchers , our findings suggest that relational safety requires a longitudinal unit of analysis. We encourage researchers to move beyond single-turn benchmarks and coarse proxies such as conversation length toward evaluations that track how reliance develops, combines, and changes over time. The behaviors in our taxonomy should also be evaluated separately rather than collapsed into a single “overreliance” score because dependence, deference, exclusivity, social substitution, and relational distress reflect different mechanisms. Automated detectors can surface candidate risk signals at scale, but should be used as part of a broader monitoring pipeline rather than as diagnoses of overreliance or harm. Future benchmarks should therefore test not only whether a model can recognize individual behaviors, but whether it can identify escalating configurations and distinguish transient emotional engagement from sustained relational risk. Our findings also caution against assuming that the most prevalent behaviors provide the most reliable signals: Genuine companion was the most widely distributed EO-related behavior but also the most difficult among the successfully detected behaviors to classify. For policymakers and regulators , our findings suggest that emotionally meaningful engagement with AI com-panions should be distinguished from behavioral signals of escalating reliance and from observable relational harm. Treating these as equivalent risks pathologizing close human-AI relationships, while ignoring recurring patterns of dependence, deference, exclusivity, or relational distress may overlook signals associated with more consequential outcomes. Governance approaches should preserve this distinction while encouraging transparency about if and how companion-AI providers detect and respond to relational-risk signals. Any use of longitudinal monitoring for relational safety should also account for the privacy and surveillance risks created by analyzing highly intimate conversations. 

7.4 Ethical Considerations 

The relational behaviors examined in this work occur in conversations that are intimate. Participants disclosed ex-periences involving mental health, relationships, sexuality, loneliness, family conflict, and other sensitive aspects of their lives. Participants consented to donate their conversations as part of the original data collection. However, these conversations may also contain sensitive information about partners, family members, and others who did not themselves consent to participate. Research using donated human-AI conversations should therefore minimize the collection, retention, and exposure of conversational content beyond what is necessary for tackling the underlying research question. We hope our work provides one approach to do so, with avenues for richer discussions in the future. TRR also raises an ethical concern about who determines when emotional reliance becomes problematic. Emotional relationships with AI may provide companionship, support, or opportunities for self-expression, and behaviors such as disclosure, attachment, or treating an AI as a meaningful relational partner should not themselves be interpreted as evidence of dysfunction. Labeling particular patterns as potential overreliance can nevertheless encode normative assumptions about how people ought to seek support, regulate emotions, or distribute intimacy across human and AI

From Reliance to Overreliance 25 relationships. This concern may be particularly significant for people whose social circumstances make AI compan-ionship among the few avenues they can seek out for help. TRR should therefore be understood as a framework for identifying relational patterns that may warrant contextual examination, rather than defining which forms or degrees of human-AI intimacy are appropriate. Finally, operationalizing relational risk creates the possibility of intervention harm. A false positive is not merely a classification error if it causes a system to interrupt, discourage, or reframe a relationship that the user experiences as supportive; conversely, a missed signal may leave a concerning trajectory unaddressed. The appropriate cost of these errors will also depend on the intervention: a reflective prompt within a native AI companion app carries different consequences than restricting access to the tool or escalating to a third party. The evidentiary threshold for intervention should therefore rise with its intrusiveness and consequences, and automated detection alone should not justify high-stakes intervention. More broadly, decisions about such thresholds should not be determined solely by detector performance, as has been described in prior HCI research [22, 23], but should incorporate the perspectives of users and relevant domain experts, particularly those likely to be affected by the interventions. 

7.5 Limitations 

This work has several limitations. First, TRR is constrained by what is observable in recorded conversations. Behaviors that require evidence across sessions, such as Compulsive return behavior , or information about users’ offline lives, such as AI displacing human connection without explicit disclosure, may be missed. Similarly, harms that are not expressed in the recorded interactions remain outside the scope of our analysis. Observed prevalence therefore reflects the visibility of these phenomena within our data and should not be interpreted as their prevalence in users’ broader experiences. Automated detectors inherit these observability constraints, limiting what their outputs can establish about relational risk. Second, our data consist of donated interactions from adult English-speaking Character.AI and Replika users. This sampling approach introduces potential self-selection bias, as users who donate conversations may differ from those who do not, and limits generalizability to other populations, languages, platforms, and forms of AI companionship. Third, longitudinal coverage varied substantially across users, limiting our ability to consistently characterize the recurrence, escalation, and temporal progression of EO-related behaviors and observable harms. The observational design also prevents us from establishing causal relationships between these behaviors and subsequent harms. Although some harms co-occurred with recurring EO-related behaviors, these patterns do not establish that the behaviors caused the harms or reliably predict their occurrence. Finally, conversational evidence does not allow us to infer users’ underlying psychological states, and the presence of EO-related behaviors alone does not establish that a relationship is harmful. 

8 Conclusion 

In this work, we developed the Taxonomy of Relational Risks (TRR) , a two-part framework characterizing emotional overreliance (EO)-related conversational behaviors and observable relational harms in human-AI interactions. Across real interactions with AI companions, observable harms were substantially less common than EO-related behaviors but often co-occurred with recurring patterns of dependence, deference, social substitution, and disclosure. Most EO-related behaviors could also be detected by open-source LLMs under at least one evaluated configuration. However, these behaviors did not necessarily lead to harm, suggesting that safety systems should attend to recurring and escalating patterns rather than isolated signals. Future work should combine richer longitudinal evidence, including interaction histories, session timing, self-reports, and offline social experiences, while balancing measurement against privacy

B Detector Implementation and Reliability B.1 Model and Evaluation Settings 

We implemented fourteen independent turn-level binary classifiers, one per EO code, using the models and settings in Table 7. Each model was evaluated with zero-shot, random few-shot, and agreement-selected few-shot prompting. Reasoning and non-reasoning conditions used identical prompts, with model-native thinking enabled or disabled, respectively. Few-shot demonstrations were drawn from CV folds other than the target fold, excluding holdout examples, and remained fixed within each target fold. Random selection used a seeded shuffle of the positive and negative pools. Agreement-selected prompting excluded uncoded examples and ranked each pool by agreement fraction, then by the number of agreeing annotators, with example ID breaking ties. Both methods selected two positive and five negative examples. Predictions were extracted from the final case-insensitive match to label: yes or label: no . Malformed responses were excluded from single-model scoring. Valid predictions were pooled across CV folds and scored jointly rather than averaging fold-level metrics. The ensemble used majority voting across the three models, requiring at least two positive predictions. After malformed responses were removed, prediction files were inner-joined by example ID, restricting ensemble evaluation to examples with valid outputs from all three models.
