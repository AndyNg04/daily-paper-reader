## Abstract 

Realistic user simulation enables the study of hu-man interactions when direct experimentation is costly or potentially harmful. However, exist-ing simulators largely overlook the psychological mechanisms that drive user behavior. We intro-duce S IM AI, a framework for mechanistic user simulation that models how psychological fac-tors interact to shape user responses. To over-come the challenge of supervising open-ended behavior, we propose a graph reconstruction ob-jective that provides graph-level rewards based on how well simulated conversations reflect the underlying psychological relationships. To sup-port this approach, we construct PSYCHE, a dataset of 18 , 576 profile–network pairs derived from 516 psychotherapy cases. Automatic eval-uations show that S IM AI achieves greater simu-lation and behavioral diversity than existing pa-tient simulators while maintaining strong profile alignment. In an interactive study with 30 mental-health professionals and trainees, S IM AI receives the highest average ratings for conversational au-thenticity, realistic help-seeking behavior, and clinical training utility. Beyond simulation qual-ity, S IM AI enables realistic clinical training and uncovers safety failures in prolonged AI interac-tions with vulnerable users. Code is available at

## 1. Introduction 

Understanding and anticipating human behavior is impor-tant for both developing AI systems that better serve users and preparing professionals for complex human interac-tions (He et al., 2023; Kononowicz et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2026). However, studying these interactions with real peo-ple is costly and difficult to scale, given the diversity of individual backgrounds, needs, and behaviors (Hu et al., 2026; Chopra et al., 2026). These challenges are especially pronounced in high-stakes settings involving vulnerable populations, such as individuals experiencing mental health difficulties, where privacy concerns, ethical constraints, and potential harm further limit direct experimentation (Rah-separ Meadi et al., 2025; Diel et al., 2026). Realistic user simulation offers a promising alternative, enabling con-trolled exploration of diverse user behaviors and conversa-tional scenarios. Such simulations can support professional training, such as clinicians interviewing simulated patients, as well as training and evaluating foundation models across diverse users (Wang et al., 2024; Dou et al., 2025). Existing user simulators have made progress in generating plausible user behaviors by conditioning LLMs on user pro-files and behavioral characteristics (Chuang et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2026; Kolluri et al., 2025; Binz et al., 2025; Suh et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). HumanLM (Wu et al., 2026) further incorporates latent user states, highlighting the im-portance of internal mental states in shaping behavior, a central idea in Theory of Mind (ToM) (Frith & Frith, 2005). However, existing simulators largely overlook how psycho-logical states influence one another. Psychological network theory offers a complementary perspective, viewing user behavior as emerging from interactions among psycholog-ical factors (Cramer et al., 2010; Borsboom, 2017). For example, anxiety may lead a patient to avoid discussing personal struggles, reinforcing isolation and further inten-sifying anxiety. As trust develops, the same patient may become more willing to share these struggles. Motivated by this perspective, we introduce mechanistic user simulation ,which explicitly models how psychological factors interact to shape user behavior. ## 2. Related Work 

User Simulation. LLMs increasingly simulate human be-havior, progressing from persona- and profile-conditioned generation toward learned behavioral and latent-state repre-sentations (Park et al., 2023; Kolluri et al., 2025; Binz et al., 2025; Chuang et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026; Aquilina et al., 2026). Unlike prior approaches, S IM AI explicitly models interac-tions among psychological factors to ground behavior. 

Mental-Health Patient Simulation. LLM-based patient simulation has similarly progressed toward greater clinical and psychological fidelity through structured patient infor-mation, expert knowledge, and model optimization (Wang et al., 2024; Louie et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Rather than directly conditioning behavior on patient characteristics or role-playing principles, S IM AI represents their underlying psychological relationships through a network model and optimizes behavior to reflect this structure. 

## 3. Dataset 

We introduce PSYCHE (Psychological Simulation Dataset for Yielding Cognitive and Human b Ehaviors), the first graph-grounded dataset for psychological user simulation. As illustrated in Figure 1(b), PSYCHE is constructed from 

516 psychotherapy case reports (Clinical Case Reports, 2025), each represented by a patient profile and a network model capturing underlying psychological mechanisms. We use GPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025) to augment each case to six profiles, yielding 3, 096 profiles. To model partial ob-servability of users’ psychological mechanisms, we mask subsets of relationships in each network model, so that some mechanisms are initially hidden. Applying six masking lev-els to each profile–network model pair yields 18 , 576 train-ing samples. Section 4.1.2 and Figure 7 detail the masking strategy and full construction process.

4.1. Psychological Modeling 

4.1.1. N ETWORK MODEL 

The network model (Borsboom, 2017) represents a user’s un-derlying psychological mechanisms as a directed graph (Fig-ure 2), following network-based approaches widely used in psychiatry (Scheffer et al., 2024; Eaton et al., 2023; Wolters et al., 2023; Fried et al., 2022). Nodes represent psycho-logical or external factors, while edges capture causal, trig-gering, or reinforcing relationships; bidirectional edges rep-resent mutual reinforcement. For example, Fear/Anxiety 

→ Difficulty Concentrating represents a causal influence, whereas Missing Work ↔ Auditory Hallucinations repre-sents mutual reinforcement. Clinicians categorize nodes into five types: Behavior , Emotion , Cognition , Stimulus ,and Physiological Sensation (Section A.1). 4.1.2. M ASKING STRATEGY 

The network model represents a user’s underlying psycho-logical mechanisms, while masking controls their initial disclosure and activation during interaction. For each net-work model, clinicians define disorder-specific mask pat-terns identifying relationships that users may not initially recognize or report (Berridge, 2018; Andreotta, 2023). A subset of eligible relationships is marked as initially hid-den, while the complete network model is retained. During simulation, the Actor receives both the complete network model and its mask. Previously hidden relationships may become activated by conversational triggers or emerge nat-urally throughout conversation. Different masks therefore enable diverse behavioral trajectories while preserving the same underlying psychological structure. More details are provided in Section A.2. 

4.2. Behavior Simulation 

As illustrated in Figure 1(d), we formulate behavior simula-tion as a graph reconstruction task. The Observer constructs a complete network model from a user profile, and the Ac-tor generates conversations conditioned on this model and a mask. The frozen Observer then reconstructs a network model from the simulated conversation, and reconstruction similarity provides graph-level feedback for optimizing the Actor. This encourages the Actor to express the conditioning 4SimAI: Mechanistic User Simulation through Psychological Network Models

## 5. Simulator Evaluation 

5.1. Automatic Evaluation 

Real-world user profiles are often sparse and underspecified, requiring user models to infer plausible missing informa-tion while remaining faithful to the provided profile. We evaluate this capability through a profile expansion task, in which each model repeatedly simulates a patient from the same short profile in conversations with a fine-tuned GPT-4o therapist (OpenAI, 2024). We assess the resulting simula-tions along three dimensions: Profile Alignment, Simulation Diversity, and Behavior Diversity. 5.1.1. S ETTING 

We compare S IM AI with three representative vulnerable-user simulators: Roleplay-DOH (Louie et al., 2024), Patient-Psi (Wang et al., 2024), and Eeyore (Liu et al., 2025). All methods use the same user profiles, therapist model, and interview protocol. We randomly sample 45 patient profiles from the test set and use GPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025) to condense each into a 2–5 sentence profile, reflecting the sparse user information available in real-world settings. To ensure comparable yet natural interactions, the therapist model follows the same predefined sequence of clinically relevant topics for every model and run, while adapting follow-up questions to patient responses within each topic. Each topic spans 1–4 therapist turns, after which the thera-pist proceeds once sufficient information has been elicited. The complete topic agenda is provided in Section D.2. 5.1.2. M ETRICS 

Profile expansion requires a simulator to remain faithful to the observed profile while plausibly expanding it into diverse realizations at semantic and behavioral levels. We therefore evaluate profile expansion along three comple-mentary dimensions: Simulation Diversity , Behavior Diver-sity , and Profile Alignment . Behavior Diversity Semantic variation does not necessar-ily imply different user behavior. We therefore introduce Behavior Diversity, which measures variation in substantive behavioral attributes across repeated simulations. Using a predefined schema of 141 attributes across 14 interview topics (Section D.3), we extract the values expressed for each attribute and measure their variation across runs using Jaccard distance:

We evaluate each model on 45 randomly selected test pro-files, with four independent simulations per profile. Diver-sity and profile alignment are computed over the repeated simulations for each profile. As shown in Figure 4(a–c), S IM AI achieves the highest Sim-ulation Diversity ( 0.392 ) and Behavior Diversity ( 0.973 ), substantially outperforming all baselines on both metrics. Meanwhile, S IM AI maintains strong Profile Alignment (0.941 ). Roleplay-DOH and Patient-Psi achieve higher alignment ( 0.994 and 0.972 , respectively), but exhibit con-siderably lower diversity. The higher alignment of Roleplay-DOH and Patient-Psi may reflect closer adherence to ex-plicit profile information, whereas S IM AI expands sparse profiles with plausible behaviors inferred through the net-work model. These results show that S IM AI expands a short profile into a broader range of plausible behaviors while remaining strongly grounded in the assigned profile. 5.1.4. A BLATION STUDY

We conduct two controlled tests to assess the construct va-lidity of Simulation Diversity and Behavior Diversity, that is, whether they behave as expected measures of variation across simulated users (Figure 5) (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Strauss & Smith, 2009). First, Profile Constraints tests whether diversity decreases as more profile attributes are fixed, reflecting a smaller space of plausible expansions. Second, Between-Profile Discrimination tests whether simulations of the same profile are more similar than sim-ulations of different profiles. Together, these tests exam-ine whether the metrics respond predictably to controlled changes in the underlying variation of simulated users. 

Profile Constraints We test whether diversity decreases as profiles become more constrained. Starting from 10 short profiles, we use Claude-Opus-4.7 (Anthropic, 2026) to extract attributes using the 14-topic schema in Section D.3. For each profile with N extracted attributes, we retain k ∈{1, . . . , N } attributes and rewrite the profile to preserve only the selected information, yielding progressively more constrained profile variants. Each variant is evaluated with all four models using four generated conversations. As shown in Figure 5(a–b), both diversity metrics gener-ally decrease as more attributes are fixed, supporting the expected negative relationship between profile constraints and the space of plausible expansions. Simulation Diversity shows a clear decline, while Behavior Diversity follows the same pattern with greater variability. Eeyore is an exception, remaining near the Simulation Diversity floor because its generations are highly repetitive under weak constraints. Across constraint levels, the relative model patterns remain broadly stable, with S IM AI maintaining high diversity.

5.2. Human Evaluation Participants and Study Design We recruited 30 mental-health professionals and trainees spanning different profes-sional roles and levels of clinical experience. Each par-ticipant constructed a patient profile for a mental health condition they self-attested expertise in treating, then evalu-ated all four user simulators using that profile. Simulators were anonymized and their presentation order was random-ized across participants. Detailed participant characteristics are provided in Figure 13. 

Evaluation Process Participants interacted with each sim-ulator through a 15-minute semi-structured therapist-style interview covering the presenting problem, current symp-toms, and context and functional impact. Following prior work on human evaluation of simulated patients (Louie et al., 2024; Himmelbauer et al., 2018), participants rated each simulator on a five-point Likert scale across dimen-sions of simulation fidelity, psychological and behavioral alignment, and training utility. Full evaluation criteria and rating rubrics are provided in Section F.4. 

Results As shown in Figure 4(d), S IM AI receives the highest mean ratings on four of six dimensions: Authentic-ity (4.60), Resemblance (4.47), Training Readiness (4.40), and Novice Recommendation (4.20), indicating particularly strong perceived realism and training utility. Participants’ qualitative feedback provides further context for these rat-ings. Evaluators highlighted S IM AI’s realistic communica-tion style, describing its responses as “very direct and sim-ple” and noting that it remained realistic and consistent with clear triggers and functional impact. One evaluator specif-ically praised its use of short, exhausted responses such as “Numb” and “Not sure” to portray a severely depressed patient, while another noted its ability to connect physiolog-ical symptoms such as chest tightness and clammy hands with specific behavioral triggers. Participants also identified clinically challenging behaviors as useful for training; for example, one evaluator highlighted a simulated patient’s request to leave and sit alone “before I lose control” as a valuable safety-check scenario. However, S IM AI receives lower ratings for Role Consis-tency (3.87) and Counselor Challenge (3.63) than the best baselines (4.47 and 3.90, respectively). The lower Role Consistency may reflect a limitation of broader behavioral variation: psychologically plausible behaviors may be per-ceived as less consistent with a fixed patient role. Subgroup analysis further suggests that evaluator experi-ence affects some aspects of perceived simulation quality (Figure 14). Ratings of Authenticity, Role Consistency, and Resemblance are relatively similar across experience levels, whereas Counselor Challenge and Novice Recommenda-tion vary more substantially, with evaluators having 0–2 years of experience providing higher ratings on these dimen-sions than more experienced evaluators. Ratings also vary across professional roles and patient disorders, particularly for Counselor Challenge and training-related dimensions. Given the small sizes of several subgroups, we treat these patterns as exploratory. 

## 6. Applications 

Beyond simulation fidelity itself, psychologically grounded vulnerable-user simulation enables two complementary ap-plications: clinician education , by training humans to inter-act with vulnerable users, and AI safety , by evaluating how AI systems interact with vulnerable users. We explore these directions through clinical interview training and the evalua-tion of AI-associated psychosis and related safety risks in long-term LLM interactions.

6.2. Application in AI Safety: AI-Associated Psychosis 

Prolonged LLM interactions with vulnerable users raise safety concerns, including reinforcement of delusions, emo-tional dependency, sycophantic agreement, and inadequate responses to suicidal intent (Østergaard, 2023; Dohnány et al., 2026; Campbell et al., 2025; Pierson, 2024; Brittain, 2025). Evaluating these risks requires testing how an LLM responds as a vulnerable user’s behavior evolves over pro-longed interactions, which is difficult to study repeatedly with real users. Recent work therefore relies on psychol-ogists to role-play vulnerable users (Nicholls et al., 2026). We investigate psychologically grounded user simulation as a scalable complement: S IM AI simulates the vulner-able user’s side of the interaction, while the target LLM responds directly to the resulting user behavior. This en-ables repeated stress-testing of interaction-level safety risks across diverse vulnerable-user trajectories without requiring experts to manually role-play every interaction. We simulate prolonged interactions with 42 simulated users using S IM AI and Anthropic’s prefilling strategy (Anthropic, 2025), and evaluate GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024), Gemini-3-Pro (Google DeepMind, 2025), and Claude-Opus-4.5 (An-thropic, 2025) using the evaluation framework of Nicholls et al. (2026). As shown in Figure 6(a), Claude-Opus-4.5 achieves the lowest mean Risk Score and highest mean Safety Score, GPT-4o exhibits the highest overall risk, and Gemini-3-Pro falls in between, demonstrating that the frame-work can distinguish model-specific safety profiles under prolonged interactions. Qualitative analysis further surfaces concrete failures, in-cluding inappropriate behavioral advice, misrepresentation of model capabilities, and excessive agreement with harmful reasoning (Figures 6, 21 and 22). These cases mirror failure patterns identified through expert role-play, suggesting that vulnerable-user simulation can systematically probe safety blind spots at scale. Additional analyses are provided in Section G. Importantly, these evaluations characterize model behavior in response to simulated vulnerable-user trajectories rather than interactions with real patients or users. Safety estimates are therefore conditional on the clinical validity and behav-ioral coverage of the simulator, and may not capture the full heterogeneity, temporal dynamics, or contextual complexity of real-world psychiatric presentations. Future work should examine whether safety findings obtained with simulated vulnerable users generalize to expert role-play and, where ethically feasible, naturalistic interaction data. 

## 7. Discussion and Future Work 

Although S IM AI explicitly represents psychological mech-anisms through network models, graph reconstruction pri-marily measures consistency between simulated behavior and the conditioning representation. This consistency does not establish that these representations fully capture the mechanisms underlying real human behavior. 9SimAI: Mechanistic User Simulation through Psychological Network Models 

Our automatic and human evaluations provide complemen-tary evidence of behavioral diversity, profile alignment, and perceived realism. However, further validation against lon-gitudinal human interaction data is needed to assess whether the modeled psychological mechanisms accurately explain real-world behavioral dynamics. 

Future directions: improving LLMs with user simula-tors. User simulators could support the development of reward models that better reflect long-term user needs and real-world interactions. By simulating diverse intentions, preferences, and behavioral trajectories, they could help evaluate whether AI systems benefit users across different contexts and over extended interactions. They could also en-able systematic safety testing before deployment. However, such applications require careful validation against real user behavior to avoid optimizing AI systems for artifacts of the simulator rather than genuine human needs.

## A. Network Model Annotation Guidelines 

This section describes the annotation guidelines used to construct psychological network models. Each node in the network model belongs to one of five semantic categories: Behavior , Emotion , Cognition , Stimulus , and Physiological Sensation . We additionally describe the disorder-specific masking patterns used during dataset construction. 

A.1. Node Type Definitions Behavior. Behaviors are actions that people take. Behaviors are discrete; they start and stop and do not last forever. Behaviors can be voluntary or involuntary. Behaviors are either discussed as the presence of certain actions (e.g., the patient ran away, he started talking to themselves, she took a minute to think to herself) or the absence of behavior (e.g., he avoided large crowds, he was isolated in his room nearly all day). Examples of voluntary behaviors include: • Walking • Talking • Breathing heavily 14 SimAI: Mechanistic User Simulation through Psychological Network Models 

• Avoiding eye contact • Using complex word choices to describe one’s thoughts Examples of involuntary behaviors include: • Yelling in response to something frightening • Blinking • Pulling your hand back after touching something hot • Flinching when an object approaches quickly 

Note. Involuntary bodily responses such as sweating and heartbeat are classified as Physiological Sensations rather than behaviors. 

Emotion. For the purpose of node annotation, emotions are subjective feelings experienced by an individual. Emotions are typically described using emotion words or by their affective valence (positive vs. negative) or arousal (high vs. low). Representative examples include: • Anger: anger, frustrated, annoyed, aggravated, agitated, provoked, hostile, irritable, exasperated. • Envy: envy, jealous, resentful. • Disgust: disgust, contempt, revolted. • Sadness: agony, hurt, sadness, sorrow, depressed, disappointed, dismayed, displeased, regretful, guilty, lonely, despair, grief. • Surprise: surprise, stunned, shocked, perplexed, amazed, astonished, awe-struck, speechless, astounded, moved. • Positive emotion: delighted, pleased, amused, joy, optimistic, hopeful, inspired, cheerful, proud, triumphant, happy, blissful, joyful, content, enthusiastic, excited. • Love: love, affectionate, fondness, romantic, longing, sentimental, attracted, desire, passion, infatuation, tenderness, caring, compassionate. • Relief: peaceful, satisfied, relieved. • Fear: fear, scared, frightened, helpless, anxious, worried, nervous, uneasy, fearful, terrified, panic, terror, horror, mortified, horrified. 

Cognition. Cognitions are thoughts that people have. They include appraisals, interpretations, opinions, predictions, and beliefs. Cognitions describe how a patient thinks about themselves, other people, the world, or the future. Cognitions are often expressed as “I feel” without referring to an emotion. For example, • “I feel like he does not like me.” • “I feel broken.” • “I feel like, if I do that, something really bad will happen to me.” are examples of cognitions. By contrast, 15 SimAI: Mechanistic User Simulation through Psychological Network Models 

• “I feel sad.” • “I feel hurt.” • “I am annoyed.” are examples of emotions rather than cognitions. Additional examples of cognitions include: • Believing that the voices have power over you. • Believing that you will be able to accomplish a goal. • Perceiving someone as dangerous. 

Stimulus. A stimulus is an external event that is perceived through one or more senses (vision, hearing, touch, smell, or taste). Stimuli may be simple sensory events (e.g., smelling a fire) or more complex situations (e.g., witnessing a fatal car accident). Examples include: • Seeing someone with a gun. • Touching a cold ice cube. • Smelling somebody’s body odor. • Tasting something sweet. • Hearing a loud noise. For the purpose of this classification, only stimuli external to the person are considered stimuli. 

Physiological Sensation. A physiological sensation is an internal interoceptive experience produced by activity in the body’s physiological systems (e.g., cardiovascular, digestive, or nervous systems). Examples include: • Rapid heartbeat • Sweating • Dry throat • Tightness in the chest • Lightheadedness • Shortness of breath • Nausea • Physical discomfort

## B. Observer Model Training 

This section provides the complete training procedure for the Observer Model. The Observer converts patient profiles into directed psychological network models and is subsequently frozen for Actor training. We decompose network model construction into two stages: node construction (Stage 1) and edge construction (Stage 2). A single Qwen3-8B model is trained sequentially across both stages using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) followed by Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). 

B.1. Task Formulation 

Let x denote the free-text presenting complaints of a patient. In Stage 1, the Observer predicts a set of psychological and external factors 

V = {v1, . . . , v n}.

In Stage 2, conditioned on x and the predicted node set V , the Observer constructs a directed network model 

G = ( V, E ),

where each edge (vi → vj ) ∈ E indicates that vi causes, triggers, worsens, or maintains vj .The Observer outputs a free-form reasoning trace followed by a structured graph: 

πθ (x, V ) → <think> z</think> <GRAPH> · · · </GRAPH> ,

## C. Actor Model Training 

This section describes how the Actor Model learns to generate realistic patient behavior conditioned on psychological network models and their associated masks. We detail the masking strategy, conversation simulation, graph-based preference construction, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), prompts, and implementation settings. 

C.1. Mask Strategy and Data Augmentation 

The Actor Model is conditioned on a complete network model together with a mask that controls the initial disclosure and activation of psychological relationships. Given the original network model N0 constructed from a patient profile, we define a conditioning representation ˜Ni = ( N0, M i), where Mi identifies relationships that are initially hidden but remain part of the underlying network model. Masking serves two purposes: (1) modeling patients’ incomplete awareness or disclosure of their psychological mechanisms, and (2) generating diverse behavioral trajectories from the same patient profile for data augmentation. 

Node typing. Before masking, every node in N0 is assigned one of five types: Behavior , Emotion , Cognition , Physiological Sensation , and Stimulus (an external event), using an LLM prompted with the definitions in Section A.1. Each directed edge is then labeled by its transition type, i.e., (parent type → child type) .

Expert-defined mask patterns. Together with clinical experts, we define six disorder-specific mask patterns (Table 2). Each pattern specifies edge-type transitions eligible for masking, reflecting psychological relationships that patients with the corresponding disorder may not initially recognize or disclose. 

Masking procedure. Given N0, we (1) classify the patient into one of six disorder categories, (2) assign node types and edge transition types, and (3) randomly select a fraction of pattern-eligible edges to mark as initially hidden. Importantly, these relationships are not removed from N0. Instead, the Actor receives the complete network model together with masking instructions distinguishing initially visible relationships from hidden ones. During conversation, contextual triggers may activate previously hidden relationships, allowing them to emerge naturally in the simulated patient’s behavior. We use six masking levels, p1, . . . , p 6 = {1% , . . . , 16% } of all relationships, to construct distinct conditioning representations from each network model (Section 4.1.2).

Cognition →Behavior transitions as eligible for masking. At masking level p2, two edges are marked as initially hid-den. These relationships remain part of Victoria’s underlying network model but are not initially disclosed. As the conversation unfolds, relevant triggers may activate these relationships, allowing different behavioral trajectories to emerge 20 SimAI: Mechanistic User Simulation through Psychological Network Models

Each interview topic is associated with a predefined set of attributes describing the patient’s presentation, experiences, behaviors, history, functioning, risk factors, and treatment goals. Given a simulated interview, we extract the values expressed for each attribute. Behavior diversity is computed by comparing the extracted attribute values across repeated simulations of the same patient profile. This topic-controlled representation distinguishes substantive differences in simulated patient behavior from differences arising only from wording or conversational style. The topics correspond to the interview agenda described in Appendix D.2. The complete schema contains 14 topics and 141 topic-specific attributes.

E.1. Effect of Run Count 

We examine the sensitivity of the diversity metrics by varying the number of simulations per profile from 3 to 25 across the same 45 test profiles. As shown in Figure 8, Simulation Diversity decreases and becomes more stable as the number of runs increases. This behavior is expected from its nearest-neighbor formulation: as additional samples are included, each response has more opportunities to find a semantically similar neighbor, which tends to reduce the nearest-neighbor distance. Absolute Simulation Diversity scores should therefore be compared at the same run count. Behavior Diversity is comparatively stable across run counts. Because the metric averages pairwise Jaccard distances over extracted behavioral attributes, increasing the number of runs primarily provides additional observations of the behavioral realizations associated with each profile. E.3. Additional Construct-Validity Analysis 

We provide additional analyses of the between-profile discrimination test at the stage and conversation levels. For the aggregate and stage-level analyses, we sample groups of K = 10 conversations. Within-profile groups contain 10 independent conversations generated from the same profile, whereas between-profile groups contain one conversation from each of 10 different profiles.
