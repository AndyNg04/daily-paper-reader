Dynamic Human-Robot Role Allocation through
Entropy-Based Monitoring and LLM Reasoning
Elena Merlo, Marta Lagomarsino, Hamidreza Raei, and Arash Ajoudani
Abstract—Role allocation strategies in human-robot collabo-
ration typically assign tasks to both the human and the robot,
limiting the user’s ability to take initiative. Furthermore, allocation
decisions may conflict with the user’s intentions, and the rationale
behind such allocations may remain unclear, potentially reducing
both acceptance and collaboration fluency. The novelty of the
proposed framework is that the human is left free to contribute
to the task, while the robot is dynamically allocated activities
through a cost-driven Behavior Tree architecture according to
user-expressed preferences and behavior. Explicit preferences are
extracted from natural language instructions using a large lan-
guage model, whereas human activity is monitored online through
a Shannon entropy-based analysis of hand-object interactions.
Furthermore, the framework can recognize anomalies in human
execution and autonomously trigger corrective robot actions. The
approach was validated through a pilot study and a multi-subject
experiment involving 14 participants. Results demonstrate the
potential of such an allocation strategy for enabling intuitive and
engaging collaboration without increasing the perceived workload.
Index Terms—Human-robot collaboration, dynamic role allo-
cation, behavior trees, information theory, large language models.
I. INTRODUCTION
R
OLE allocation in human-robot collaboration (HRC)
originally relied on fixed, predefined strategies derived
from the inherent capabilities of humans and robots. In line with
Fitts’ List of “Men Are Better At/Machines Are Better At” [1],
humans were typically assigned tasks demanding reasoning,
adaptability, or creativity, whereas robots were responsible
for operations that were repetitive, precise, or hazardous. As
technology advances and increasingly powerful AI models
emerge, the traditional separation between human and robot
capacities is becoming less distinct, calling for adaptive and
context-sensitive role assignment strategies [2]. By monitoring
online the state of the human, the robot, and the surrounding
environment, roles can be dynamically adjusted during task
execution, optimizing the team performance and fostering fluid
collaboration [3], [4].
In industry and healthcare, repetitive tasks and heavy lifting
can lead to musculoskeletal injuries and reduced productivity.
Embedding physical condition and ergonomic assessments
within role allocation strategies facilitates optimized load
sharing, promoting both human well-being and system ef-
ficiency. Classical approaches rely on explicit ergonomic
thresholds or rule-based criteria. For example, Rapid Entire
Body Assessment (REBA)-based systems [5] reassigned tasks
to the robot when human posture becomes unsafe, while

online as human performance evolves. In [9], authors proposed

from Rapid Upper Limb Assessment (RULA) scores and

approaches can estimate fatigue through learned biomechanical

tasks cannot be decomposed, allocation shift to the control
level, with robots adapting assistance policies based on human
fatigue measured via EMG [11] or switching control modes
dynamically [12]: a strategy that is effective but often task-
specific and difficult to generalize. However, although these
strategies reduce musculoskeletal strain, when tasks have
similar ergonomic risk scores, the resulting role assignment may
become non-intuitive. While users readily accept not being
assigned to clearly high-risk tasks like heavy lifting, being
asked to perform a task with comparable risk but against their
intentions can also feel frustrating.
Other approaches exploit cognitive load measures or user
preferences to improve both performance and user satisfaction.
Early approaches relied on rule-based strategies: the robot
adjusts its autonomy level or reassigns subtasks when cognitive
load, stress, or trust estimates cross predefined thresholds [13],
[14]. A second family of methods formulated allocation as an
optimization problem, selecting roles that balanced productivity
with human well-being, keeping mental or physiological stress
within safe limits [15], [16]. More recent work has shifted
toward learning-based personalization. Robots learn human
capabilities or trustworthiness from task outcomes and allocate
roles accordingly [17], or model collaboration as a two-
player game in which both partners negotiate roles that satisfy
productivity and stress objectives, approaching equilibrium
through experience [18]. In this context, estimates of trust
or stress are not intentional inputs provided by the user, so
the resulting allocations may confuse an operator who still
wishes to retain some freedom in choosing what to do. More
recently, Large Language Models (LLM)s have been used to
process explicit user requests, which represents a step toward
more intuitive collaboration thanks to the naturalness of verbal
communication. For instance, [19] allowed users to express
states or preferences directly (e.g., “I have a back pain”),

While the above-mentioned approaches typically priori-
tized a single optimization objective, the following combine
multiple factors simultaneously. [20] introduced a capability-
based framework scoring agent-task pairs by task complexity,
dexterity, and physical effort. [21] formulated allocation as
a Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) problem that
accounts for makespan, workload, quality, preferences, and
spatiotemporal constraints, with dynamic re-allocation during
execution, but its computational burden limits online use. To
address this, [22] leveraged Behavior Trees (BT)s to decompose
jobs into smaller subproblems, solving a MILP only for
currently unlocked tasks. Allocation considered capabilities,
ergonomics, and availability. However, having many factors
influencing the allocation decision, it becomes even harder for
the user to understand why certain tasks are assigned to them.
In [23], an augmented reality interface was used for negotiation:
the user could explicitly accept or reject proposed allocations,
updating preference-based costs that influenced future algorithm
decisions. In this way, the user became part of the decision-
making process, recognizing that their intentions were taken
into account. However, continuously requiring the user to
confirm allocations can negatively affect the fluency of the
interaction, interrupting task execution and eventually becoming
tedious and unnatural [24]. Moreover, the human freedom of
action remains limited, as the user can only accept or reject
the proposed allocation without actively taking initiative within
the collaboration. Allowing users to choose actions on the fly,
rather than following imposed allocations, remains relatively
underexplored despite its relevance for tasks admitting multiple
valid execution sequences, such as lightweight assembly. To
include human decision-making authority in a role allocation
framework, [25] used wearable devices to recognize gestures,
informing an AOG-based task manager of the human’s ongoing
activity and triggering compatible robot actions. However, their
approach required task-specific gesture training and did not
assess activity completion and correctness.
This work proposes a dynamic role allocation framework that
aims to promote both intuitiveness and human agency in HRC
tasks. To foster intuitive interaction, users can communicate
with the robot through natural language, as they would with a
human teammate. By leveraging the commonsense reasoning
capabilities of an LLM, the robot adapts its behavior to the
provided instructions, making its actions easier to anticipate and
understand. To support human agency, our framework monitors
hand-object interactions through an RGB camera and evaluates
their information flow using Shannon entropy measures [26],
representing the evolution of human activity through dynamic
scene graphs [27] and allowing its identification without any
task-specific training phase. Moreover, the structural properties
of the generated scene graphs allow the system to assess
whether an activity has been completed correctly, providing a
basis for detecting execution anomalies and triggering appropri-
ate robot interventions. The main methodological contributions
are the design of: (i) a cost-driven role allocation framework in
which heterogeneous factors describing the collaboration status,
including spoken instructions, are encoded as cost vectors
and integrated within a dedicated BT-based decision-making
structure; (ii) a Shannon entropy-based algorithm for identifying
the activities the user spontaneously decides to perform and
evaluating the correctness of the execution, starting from the
online version of [27]. An overview of the proposed framework
is shown in Fig. 1.

Section II details the design of the BT-based role allocator
and the involved cost computation. Section III presents the

II. METHOD
The proposed framework is presented in three steps. First,
the BT-based role allocator is introduced as the central decision-
making layer that integrates the cost information describing
the current collaboration status and selects the activity to
be executed by the robot (Section II-A). Then, the modules
computing the different cost terms are detailed (Section II-B).
Finally, the entropy-based method designed for online human

A. Role Allocator
BTs are directed rooted trees widely adopted in robotics
for task execution and online decision-making due to their
modularity, reactivity, and flexibility. Execution starts from
the root node, which periodically propagates a signal, called
tick, to its children. Once ticked, each node returns one of
three possible statuses to its parent: RUNNING, SUCCESS, or
FAILURE. BT nodes can be divided into execution nodes and
control nodes. Execution nodes include action nodes, which
trigger robot primitives, and condition nodes, which evaluate
logical conditions online. Control nodes instead regulate the
execution flow among their children. Among the most common
control nodes, a Sequence node ticks its children in order
and returns SUCCESS only if all of them succeed, whereas a
Fallback node acts as an OR operator, sequentially ticking
its children until one returns SUCCESS. Another class of
control nodes is represented by decorator nodes, which modify
the execution policy or returned status of a single child node.
To keep the role allocation process continuously active
throughout the collaboration, the proposed BT integrates a
KeepRunningUntilFailure decorator connected to the
root node. This decorator repeatedly ticks its child subtree
until a FAILURE status is returned, allowing the robot to
continuously re-evaluate the allocation decision online. The
core of the role allocation mechanism is represented by
a modified version of the standard fallback node, named
Cost-Based Fallback. Differently from a standard fall-
back, where children are ticked according to a fixed order, the
proposed node dynamically reorders its N children at every
tick according to a varying cost vector c ∈RN. Each child
of the Cost-Based Fallback is an action node Ai, with
i ∈{1,...,N}, triggering the execution of a specific activity
included in the task model.

III. EXPERIMENTS
The experimental campaign aimed at evaluating the perfor-
mance of the proposed role allocation framework from both a
technical and a user-centered perspective. First, a pilot study
was conducted to demonstrate all the functionalities of the
framework, including activity recognition, preference handling,
and anomaly management1. Subsequently, some participants
were involved in a user study in which they collaborated with
the robot using the proposed allocation strategy to evaluate the
impact of the full framework on the intuitiveness and human
agency, rather than test each isolated component.
A. Role Allocation Framework Validation (Exp 1)
1) Experimental Setup: The experimental setup is shown
in Fig. 3. A human participant and a fixed-base Franka
Emika Panda manipulator, equipped with a Robotiq EPick
vacuum gripper, were positioned on opposite sides of a shared
workspace. A RealSense D435i camera, mounted above the
table on an L-shaped support, provided a top-down view of
the interaction area. Hand and object tracking was performed
from the RGB stream using SAM 2 [29]. To enable online
operation, we implemented a stateful tracker that processes
the live camera stream, initializes targets from user-provided
prompts in the first frame, and propagates segmentation masks
over subsequent frames. This provides temporally consistent
tracking without requiring object classification. The image-
plane centroid of each binary mask was used as the object
position. Under partial occlusions, positions were updated only
when the visible mask area exceeded a predefined fraction of its
reference area; otherwise, the last valid estimate was retained.
Tracking seven objects and one hand, the complete perception
pipeline operated at approximately 7 Hz. The participant wore
a microphone to communicate explicit preferences. The GPT-
5.1 model was used to process them. A monitor placed next to
the robot displayed a graphical user interface (GUI) reporting
both the target assembly and the activity currently assigned
to the robot. While the participant was free to perform any
activity, the GUI informed them about the robot allocation
decision, reducing the risk of conflicts during task execution.

The questionnaire results are reported in Fig. 6. The left
panel presents the scores of the custom questionnaire. Condition

had not paid attention to it, and 1% believed the allocation

larger difference between conditions. For both Ag1 and Ag2,

appreciated both collaboration modalities equally. Participant

because they believed it was optimizing the collaboration,
despite not understanding its rationale. The NASA-TLX results
are shown in the central panel of Fig. 6. The two conditions
produced comparable scores across most dimensions. Condition
A resulted in a slightly higher Mental Demand (MD), likely
reflecting the more active role of the participant. However,
the difference was not significant (p = 0.52), and a moderate

significantly between conditions (p < 0.001). In condition B,

pace was dictated by the robot and the task ended after a fixed
number of robot actions. In contrast, condition A allowed
participants to drive the collaboration, resulting in shorter
execution times and lower idle times, indicating reduced passive
waiting and greater user involvement.

V. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
In this paper, we presented a role allocation framework that
leaves the human free to contribute to HRC tasks, combining
LLM-based interpretation of user instructions with entropy-
based online monitoring of human activities to support intuitive
and flexible collaboration. The experimental results indicate
increased human agency: participants reported feeling more
involved, more in control of the collaboration, and less
constrained than under the baseline condition. Allowing users
to express verbal preferences and directly observe their impact
on the robot’s decisions also improved the intuitiveness of the
interaction. In particular, participants found the robot’s behavior
easier to predict, whereas in the baseline condition nearly 70%
either failed to understand the allocation strategy or did not
pay attention to it, passively following the assigned tasks. The
preference-based condition also resulted in significantly lower
frustration levels, suggesting that giving users greater initiative
does not increase the interaction burden but instead makes the
collaboration more natural and satisfying. The wide variability
in the execution strategies adopted by participants, together with
the absence of significant differences in perceived workload,


10
further supports the idea that, when no clear distinction exists
between activities better suited to the human or the robot,
users should be free to decide how to contribute rather than
being constrained by a rigid allocation strategy. Moreover, the
proposed framework significantly reduced both task execution
time and human idle time, indicating a more fluent collaboration
with less passive waiting. Finally, the framework automatically
detects execution anomalies and triggers corrective robot
interventions when needed. This closes the loop between
activity monitoring, role allocation, and task execution, enabling
the robot to recover from human errors while maintaining
task progress. The current LLM-based instruction handling
is tailored to the Tangram assembly task, where each piece
can be uniquely identified through simple semantic attributes.
Extending the approach to real-world assembly scenarios
involving less distinguishable components will make both
instruction interpretation and the mapping of user requests to
task model activities more challenging. Moreover, the adopted
task model assumes that each activity is executed at most
once and that dependencies are purely enabling. Supporting
richer task representations, including more complex dependency
structures, alternative execution paths, and reversible decisions,
will further challenge both the LLM reasoning process and
the design of effective prompting strategies. The entropy-based
monitoring also depends on the reliability of the perception
pipeline and the availability of representative activity references.
In more complex assembly scenarios, multiple execution
strategies may correctly accomplish the same task while
deviating from the reference interaction patterns, potentially
reducing recognition accuracy. Another limitation is that user
requests or actions performed while the robot is executing an
activity cannot influence its behavior immediately and are only
considered at the next allocation step. Similarly, the current
motion planner minimizes execution time without explicitly
accounting for human motion. Incorporating human-aware
planning, such as hand avoidance or trajectory generation
informed by the ongoing human activity, could further improve
collaboration fluency. Finally, integrating predictive models of
user behavior could enable the robot to anticipate likely human
actions and optimize role allocation over a longer planning
horizon while remaining responsive to unexpected behaviors
and changing user preferences.
