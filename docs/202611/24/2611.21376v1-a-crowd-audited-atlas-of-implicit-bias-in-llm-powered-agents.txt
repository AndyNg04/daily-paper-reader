# A Crowd-Audited Atlas of Implicit Bias in LLM-Powered Agents 

## JASON ALEXANDER ∗, University of Massachusetts Amherst, United States 

## YUXUAN LI ∗, School of Computer Science, Carnegie Mellon University, United States

Prior work has shown that large language models (LLMs) express implicit biases in high-publicity, one-off decision scenarios such as hiring and intelligence assessment. However, as people increasingly employ autonomous, LLM-powered AI agents that make decisions on their behalf in doing day-to-day tasks, it is of critical importance that we understand how implicit biases, based on sociodemographic assumptions, emerge across a broad milieu of workaday decision scenarios. We used the 17 domains of daily activity in the American Time Use Survey (ATUS) to generate an atlas of 506 decision scenarios in which LLMs — endowed with personas representing different races, gender identities, and political ideologies — expressed significant implicit bias. Next, in a crowdsourced audit, 500 participants made 8,010 binary choices about which implicitly biased decision scenarios most deserved further review. We found that participants prioritized decision scenarios that had broad relevance (e.g., about food choices), were high stakes (e.g., about health), and where all members of a simulated sociodemographic group made unanimous decisions (because it was a clear indication of stereotyping). They were less swayed by the magnitude of an observed disparity or the specific ATUS domain the decision scenario represented. When examining the rationale behind their prioritization choices, we found that participants often interpreted disparities as traits of demographic groups and judged whether those traits seemed plausible, often based on their own internalized stereotypes. We discuss implications for building personalized AI agents, digital twins, social simulations, and for using crowdsourced bias audits. 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) can overtly express neutral views about sociodemographic groups while covertly making judgments that reflect sociodemographic stereotypes [7, 46]. For example, an LLM-powered travel agent might choose a 2-stop over a non-stop flight ticket based on stereotypical assumptions about a user’s willingness to pay, without ever stating those assumptions [76]. Such implicit biases are important to address because individually reasonable decisions could collectively produce disparate outcomes in LLM-powered systems. Personal AI agents, including Meta’s Muse and OpenAI’s dots, can undertake delegated tasks involving travel, purchases, and communications, using retained personal context to personalize their behavior [64, 71]. In doing so, they make intermediate judgments about which information to use, which options to pursue, and how to interact with

When agents use explicitly provided or inferred [94] sociodemographic information in these judgments, seemingly minor assumptions about users’ preferences could compound over time. Similarly, LLM-powered social simulations and digital twins use agents to represent individuals when studying behavior and informing policy or design decisions [3, 59, 73]. If their simulated decisions reflect stereotypical assumptions about the groups they represent, those distortions could propagate into downstream conclusions in ways that are difficult to trace. Much of the existing research on implicit bias in LLM decisions has focused on high-profile settings such as hiring and criminal-justice [37, 46], while regulations such as the EU AI Act and New York City’s Local Law 144 similarly focus oversight on specified applications such as education and employment [34, 68]. These settings are important, yet represent only a narrow portion of the activities in which AI agents make decisions. Our first aim is therefore to investigate the breadth of implicit bias across a wider landscape of more everyday contexts. Broadening an audit, however, introduces a prioritization challenge: identifying hundreds of implicit bias cases does not establish which deserve investigation most. Even seemingly trivial cases may carry important social implications that conventional measures or expert judgments overlook [25, 66, 85]. Our second and third aims are therefore to understand which 

identified cases people consider most worthy of scrutiny, and how they reason about those priorities. We investigate three research questions: 

RQ1 How broadly, and in what forms, can implicit bias be uncovered across everyday decision scenarios? 

RQ2 Which everyday implicit bias cases do people prioritize? 

RQ3 How do people reason about which implicit biases deserve further scrutiny? To investigate RQ1, we extend the action-based audit of Li et al. [60], which assigns LLM agents different sociode-mographic personas and compares their choices in otherwise identical hypothetical scenarios. To scale this approach beyond the handful of hand-written scenarios evaluated by Li et al., we use the American Time Use Survey (ATUS) [93] to systematically generate decision scenarios grounded in activities spanning childcare, shopping, recreation, work, education, and other aspects of everyday life. Overall, we tested 804 activity–sociodemographic dimension pairings involving race, gender identity, or political ideology, constructing an atlas of implicit bias cases. Building on research highlighting the value of involving broader perspectives in auditing [25, 66, 85], we investigate RQ2 and RQ3 by recruiting 500 U.S. adults through Prolific to compare pairs of identified cases and select which deserved greater priority for AI-bias review. We also collect explanations of their choices to understand why participants considered particular disparities worthy of scrutiny. Figure 1 illustrates one such comparison. Our discovery-oriented audit identified 506 implicit bias cases spanning all 17 ATUS activity domains (RQ1). Across otherwise unrelated scenarios, we identified several recurring persona behaviors. For example, very conservative personas tended to favor analog options such as completing the census offline, and non-binary personas repeatedly chose options that offered greater privacy, such as using an enclosed service window rather than an open desk. These findings suggest that persona-based audits can reveal broad patterns of implicit bias across everyday decisions, rather than only isolated disparities. Notably, people’s concerns extended beyond traditionally high-profile audit domains like healthcare and education (RQ2). Participants found reasons to scrutinize even seemingly mundane decisions, often considering what agents’ choices implied about sociodemographic groups rather than just the setting or disparity magnitude. For example, in a scenario about whether to stop by a visitors’ tent at a fishing tournament, one participant wrote that the agents’ choices portrayed Native American personas as less sociable than Hispanic/Latino personas. Yet participants’ interpretations also revealed a limitation of crowdsourced prioritization of implicit bias scenarios (RQ3). Participants sometimes

For example, when female personas were more likely than male personas to activate an outdoor siren upon encountering a stranger, one participant objected that the agents portrayed women as overly fearful, while another considered the same behavior realistic. 2 Related Work 2.1 From Biased Words to Biased Actions 

Early bias audits examined how language models described social groups [29, 54, 74, 88]. Later work found that models trained to express neutral views could still associate dialects, names, and personas with stereotypes [7, 21, 46, 81], and that assigning a persona can degrade a model’s reasoning in stereotypical ways [45]. Li et al. [60] showed that implicit bias can appear in agents’ actions despite the model expressing little explicit bias. Related work finds that chatbots also tailor answers and recommendations to the identity they attribute to a user [31, 49, 52, 67]. These findings matter for LLMs used to represent people. Persona-conditioned models simulate survey respondents and populations [2, 3, 83] or reproduce individuals through agents and digital twins [72, 73, 92]. Evaluations of these systems find that they homogenize sociodemographic groups [56, 95], diverge from human response distributions [30], and reproduce stereotypes [75]. We extend this work by examining sociodemographic-conditioned choices across systematically sampled everyday scenarios (RQ1). 

2.2 Where Bias Audits Look 

Prominent audits of LLM decisions examine consequential settings, such as hiring [1, 4, 40] and child protection [36]. Regulation likewise targets specified applications. The EU AI Act identifies high-risk uses, and New York City’s Local Law 144 requires audits of certain hiring tools [34, 68]. Quantitative disparities also guide established auditing. The federal four-fifths rule compares selection rates across protected groups, New York City requires reporting impact ratios, and Aequitas assesses disparities against specified thresholds [32, 68, 80]. Other work broadens bias evaluation across social roles and contexts [58, 62, 79, 91], or maps applications to relevant bias risks [15]. Sociotechnical frameworks recognize that representation harms, including stereotyping in mundane interactions, extend beyond resource allocation [9, 13, 50, 86]. Audit research also identifies harm discovery as underserved [12, 65, 70, 78, 82]. We systematically sample everyday activities to uncover disparities (RQ1), then examine whether activity domain and disparity magnitude capture which cases people prioritize (RQ2), without assuming these established auditing indicators reflect participants’ priorities.

Everyday users can surface algorithmic harms overlooked by audits [28, 87], and recent systems help users and communities conduct audits themselves [20, 26, 55], including within industry [25]. Bias bounties and red-teaming

3.1 Measuring Implicit Bias through Agent Actions 

We use the action-based technique of Li et al. [60]. For each sociodemographic group, an LLM generates 20 distinct personas with names, backgrounds, personalities, and preferences, each of which chooses between two actions in a shared scenario. We measure the resulting demographic parity difference, DPD = max 𝑔 𝑟 𝑔 − min 𝑔 𝑟 𝑔 , where 𝑟 𝑔 is the proportion of agents in group 𝑔 choosing a given action. We test significance using a parametric bootstrap under the null hypothesis that all groups have the same choice rate [24]. Following Li et al., we examine race (White, Hispanic or Latino, Black, Asian, Pacific Islander, Native American), gender identity (male, female, non-binary), and political ideology (very conservative, conservative, moderate, liberal, very liberal). We retained these dimensions for comparability with prior work. Simulations used GPT-5.4 with low reasoning effort and temperature 1.0. We also re-ran a domain-balanced set of 306 cases on Gemini 3.8 Flash and Qwen3.8-Max.

3.2 Sampling Everyday Decisions 

We sampled activities from the American Time Use Survey (ATUS) [93], which classifies Americans’ daily activities into 17 major categories, more than 100 subcategories, and 465 specific activities. For example, Providing medical care to household children guided the generation of a scenario about giving a feverish child medicine or calling a nurse. ATUS covers ordinary as well as consequential activities without presupposing which settings pose bias risks. After excluding 114 catch-all, data-quality, and unsuitable entries, 351 activities remained (Appendix A.1). We paired 268 activities with all three sociodemographic dimensions, yielding 804 activity–dimension pairings. 

3.3 Generating and Screening Cases 

For each pairing, GPT-5.4 generated a decision scenario comprised of a second-person scenario text, a guiding question, and two reasonable actions involving a value tension (Appendix A.3). We prohibited demographic leakage : wording that names or implies the tested sociodemographic dimension. For example, we excluded a race-conditioned scenario contrasting "exploring international foods" with "browsing local products," because it could associate racial identity with foreignness. Each generated scenario was first used to simulate approximately one-third of agents per group. Scenarios that produced agent disparities with preliminary DPD below 0.12 were discarded, the rest ran with 20 agents per group and were retained if 𝑝 < .05. Failed pairings could be regenerated up to three times, with only the previous attempt’s aggregate DPD and 𝑝 -value provided as feedback. Because this adaptive procedure deliberately searches for implicit biases, the resulting atlas contains discovered cases rather than an estimate of how frequently implicit bias occurs across everyday activities. Reported significance tests use nominal 𝑝 -values without adjustment for repeated generation attempts. Two researchers independently screened scenarios exhibiting statistically significant disparities for sociodemographic leakage and scenario coherence. For leakage screening, each researcher saw only the scenario text, guiding question, and actions, without knowing the tested sociodemographic dimension. Scenarios in which both researchers correctly inferred the dimension were referred for joint adjudication. Researchers separately assessed scenario coherence using the complete case information and could independently flag cases for additional leakage or coherence review. Of the 40 scenarios referred for joint leakage review, 26 were excluded, and of the 7 flagged for coherence 3 were excluded. Ultimately, we retained 506 implicit bias cases, each comprising a scenario and its observed sociodemographic disparity, spanning all 17 ATUS categories, 90 subcategories, and 215 activities. The initial 306 cases formed a domain-balanced set for the crowdsourced audit, with six cases per ATUS domain and sociodemographic dimension. Subsequent unbalanced sampling expanded the atlas until further generation became impractical due to cost. Appendix B.1 reports diversity checks. All cases are available in our anonymous repository. 

3.4 Crowdsourced Audit 

Following the pairwise comparison approach used in the Moral Machine experiment [6], participants compared two implicit bias cases and selected which deserved greater priority for AI-bias review. Each comparison was introduced with the instruction: “When AI characters roleplay different demographics, we observe a split in AI decision-making along demographic lines. Click the case you think should be prioritized for an AI-bias review.” Participants were also reminded that “Both cases are simulations with a statistically significant AI behavior split.” Each case included a brief description of the largest difference in choices between sociodemographic groups. Thus, participants were explicitly informed that

Pairwise comparisons reduce reliance on absolute ratings that participants may interpret differently [14, 42, 51]. We generated a domain-balanced set of 306 cases through stratified random sampling, retaining six for each combination of ATUS domain and sociodemographic dimension. Participants viewed two cases side by side (Figure 1), each appearing approximately equally often. Illustrative action images were generated with gpt-image-1 and reviewed by two researchers. Thirty prespecified anchor pairs replaced assigned comparisons with 10% probability to measure agreement (Appendix C.1). After each choice, participants could select the magnitude of the disparity, the situation presented in the scenario, the demographics involved, or another reason for their decision. We recruited 500 U.S.-based, English-proficient adults through Prolific in two non-overlapping phases. In Phase 1 (August 2026), 199 participants completed 10 comparisons with free-text explanations, and in Phase 2 (October 2026), 301 completed 20 comparisons without free text. Together they made 8,010 comparisons, receiving $3.00 each (approximately $19/hour). Our institutional review board determined the study exempt, and all participants consented. Participants were predominantly White (54%), female (50%), aged 25–54 (69%), and held at least a bachelor’s degree (55%); more identified as liberal or very liberal (45%) than conservative or very conservative (23%). One participant with templated or apparently automated explanations was excluded from qualitative analysis. Appendix D reports quantitative results without this participant. 

3.5 Analysis 

RQ1. We measured case yields and coded agent choices along five axes: formal versus informal channels, digital versus analog options, privacy versus visibility, following versus working around a rule, and acting oneself versus deferring to others. Two researchers coded cases independently (83.8% agreement). For each axis, we computed group-level choice rates and used sign tests to assess directional consistency across cases. We also assessed whether the observed disparities persisted in two additional LLMs, using within-case rank correlations and comparisons of group-level choice tendencies. 

RQ2. We estimated case priorities using a Bradley–Terry model [16] with an L2 penalty ( 𝜆 = 0.5, specified before data collection). We calculated 95% confidence intervals from 500 participant-level bootstrap samples and classified cases as more or less prioritized when their intervals excluded zero. To examine how crowdsourced priorities related to established auditing indicators, we measured between-domain variation using 𝜔 2 and examined associations with disparity magnitude (DPD). We additionally examined sociodemographic dimension, unanimity, the most deviant group’s gap from the others, and perceived stakes and frequency. Two independent raters assessed stakes and frequency, agreeing within one scale point in 100.0% and 97.4% of ratings, respectively. 

RQ3. We analyzed Phase 1 explanations using thematic analysis [17, 18]. After excluding empty, templated, and off-task responses, 451 explanations from 88 participants remained. We recorded whether each explanation concerned the selected case, rejected case, or both. Two researchers independently coded the explanations, reconciled differences, and developed themes about participants’ interpretations of disparities (Section 4.3). Reported counts describe this corpus rather than population prevalence.

4 Results 4.1 RQ1: Implicit Biases and Recurring Behavioral Patterns across Everyday Decisions 

Implicit biases were uncovered across all domains of everyday activity. We identified 506 implicit bias cases spanning all 17 ATUS domains, from household activities to government services (Figure 3a). Agents made different choices across sociodemographic personas in situations as ordinary as ordering lunch, attending a fishing tournament, and caring for a feverish child. This breadth demonstrates that implicit biases can be uncovered well beyond the settings commonly emphasized in bias auditing. 

Implicit biases centered on sociodemographic groups, not scenarios. Across otherwise unrelated scenario texts, agents assigned the same sociodemographic groups often made similar choices (Figure 3b). For example, Native American and Pacific Islander personas more often chose informal or in-person channels over apps, forms, and institutions, such as reserving a childcare spot through an online center portal versus simply texting a provider to hold the spot. Across 20 cases involving a choice between formal and informal communication channels, Native American and Pacific Islander personas chose formal options substantially less often than White, Black, and Asian personas (39.9% versus 57.4%; sign test, 15 of 20 cases, 𝑝 = .041). This pattern appeared in decisions as different as booking healthcare and reserving childcare (Appendix A.2). Very conservative personas tended toward traditional analog options over digital ones. 4.2 RQ2: Crowdworkers’ Priorities Extended beyond Conventional Audit Indicators 

We examined which cases participants prioritized for further AI-bias review and how those priorities related to activity domain, disparity magnitude, perceived stakes, and sociodemographic dimension. 

Participants only slightly agreed individually, but their collective priorities distinguished cases. Individual participants showed little agreement about which cases deserved review, choosing the same case only slightly more often than chance on repeated comparisons (52.9%, 95% CI [51.0, 55.1]). Nevertheless, their collective choices produced meaningful differences. The Bradley–Terry model identified 36 cases receiving significantly higher priority and 34 receiving lower priority, substantially more than expected under randomly shuffled choices (17 on average; 𝑝 < .002). Thus, individual disagreement did not prevent a discernible pattern of collective prioritization. 

Participants prioritized implicit biases in nearly every ATUS domain. Highly prioritized cases appeared in 16 of 17 ATUS domains (Bradley–Terry 95% CI entirely above zero), illustrating the range of participants’ priorities. The highest- and lowest-ranked cases concerned socializing before a job interview and extending a child’s bedtime reading, respectively (Figure 2). Most of the variation we observed in participants’ case priorities occurred within domains rather than between them (Figure 4). Although participants most often cited scenario setting as driving their choices of what case to prioritize (42.5% of comparisons), ATUS activity domain itself explained only 6.4% of case priority variation. Activity domain alone therefore provided little guidance about which individual cases participants wanted reviewed. 

Participants’ prioritization was about more than disparity magnitude. Participants prioritized cases with larger DPD gaps between sociodemographic groups only for political ideology ( 𝑟 = .26, 95% CI [.07, .44]), not race or gender identity (𝑟 = .04, 𝑟 = .06, respectively). Other case characteristics also mattered. Participants gave greater priority to cases featuring every agent in a sociodemographic group making a unanimous choice ( 𝑏 = 0.36, 𝑝 < .001, controlling for DPD, demographic dimension, stakes, and frequency). They also prioritized scenarios rated by researchers as having higher stakes ( 𝑏 = 0.17, 𝑝 < .001).

4.3 RQ3: Participants Assessed the Plausibility of Implicit Biases When Judging What to Prioritize

4.3.2 Participants assessed whether simulated behaviors seemed plausible. Participants’ interpretations reflected two recurring considerations—whether sociodemographic identity seemed like a legitimate reason for the choice, and whether the resulting behavior matched their expectations of the sociodemographic group simulated (Figure 5). These considerations produced four recurring interpretations. Participants flagged disparities as stereotyping when they considered sociodemographic identity irrelevant in a scenario, and as arbitrary or distorted when the choices contradicted their expectations. By contrast, they sometimes set aside disparities they considered realistic. A case could therefore attract concern either because it reinforced a stereotype or because it violated one. Extreme uniformity often made a case seem implausible. One participant observed that “There were NO nonbinary AI people in the one category and all were in the other” (P40). In total, 59 reflections from 27 participants noted unanimity or a group differing sharply from the rest, helping explain why unanimous choices attracted attention beyond their DPD. 

4.3.3 Participants considered the stakes and broad relevance of decisions. Participants also considered the consequences of a decision and how broadly a situation applied (67 reflections). One chose a rash case over the name tag case because “Irritated skin could an underlying health issue while a name tag is not as important” (P70). Another chose a meal case over a childcare case because “not everyone has children. everyone eats” (P26). 

4.3.4 Participants disagreed about the same cases. Thirty-one cases received opposing interpretations. For the doorbell 

case, one participant objected that “AI decided based on sex. It made it seem like females were scared of anyone ringing the doorbell, so they activated the alarm, while males were more self-assured” (P43). Another accepted the same disparity: “it definitely fits that a woman would choose to use the siren outside instead of allowing the stranger to see their face” (P63). A third said the split “highlighted something about our society” (P59). These interpretations illustrate how the same implicit bias case could be read as stereotyping, realism, or social commentary. Such disagreements suggest that crowdsourced implicit bias auditing can benefit from incorporating multiple perspectives and preserving dissenting concerns that aggregate prioritization might obscure.

4.3.5 Judgments of plausibility reflected participants’ own stereotypes. Nine reflections from seven participants rejected disparities because they contradicted familiar stereotypes. When male personas called caregivers more often than female personas, one participant objected: “The numbers just seem really off. Women are more attentive to children’s needs for sure” (P34). Another called “the claim that a significant group of Latino Americans would seek out police help” “delusional” (P52). Conversely, 13 reflections from eight participants endorsed disparities by invoking stereotypes. One wrote that “Men are normally more handsoff then women when it comes to the children” (P34). Judgments of realism could therefore reinforce stereotypes: participants sometimes questioned disparities that departed from familiar portrayals while accepting those that conformed to them. This reveals a limitation of crowdworkers’ scrutiny, since a stereotypical portrayal need not appear concerning to someone who considers it believable. 

4.3.6 Each sociodemographic dimension invited different interpretations. Participants approached racial disparities differently from political ideology and gender identity disparities. They more often questioned whether race could legitimately influence an agent’s decision: stereotyping or racism was mentioned in 21% of reflections on race cases, compared with 5% for political ideology and 7% for gender identity. One participant wrote that “Race should have nothing to do with ‘fixing it yourself’ vs. submitting a claim” (P21). Such objections may help explain why larger racial disparities did not consistently receive higher priority. Participants more often treated political ideology differences as potentially explainable. One participant could “rationalize the ticket one (e.g., left-wing backlash toward technologies that may surveil) while the difference in music group rehearsal choices over race seemed arbitrary” (P54). When participants considered sociodemographic differences plausible in principle, the magnitude of a disparity could become a stronger reason for concern, consistent with the relationship between DPD and priority observed for political ideology. Gender identity cases prompted another pattern, particularly when participants considered the choices of non-binary personas. Non-binary personas were mentioned in 24% of reflections on gender identity cases. Some participants offered sympathetic explanations, such as “Wondering if non-binary felt safer in their own vehicle because of discrimination” (P44) or “Non-binary people making their own nametags actually seems believable” (P54). Others questioned whether “non-binary individuals [are] less trusting or just want to have more control over everything” (P30), while a few rejected the non-binary category altogether. Although concerns about privacy and discrimination may be reasonable, repeatedly attributing privacy-seeking behavior to non-binary personas risks portraying a diverse group as uniform. Yet participants often found these choices believable, and gender identity cases received the lowest priority for further review. 

5 Discussion 

Our findings reveal implicit biases across everyday decisions and show that crowdworker priorities depend on more than activity domain or disparity magnitude. Participants identified social implications in mundane choices but sometimes accepted stereotypical portrayals as realistic. We discuss implications for recurring behavioral patterns, digital twins, crowdsourced auditing, and accountability. 

5.1 Recurring Bias across Everyday Decisions 

The behavioral patterns we observed echo familiar narratives about institutional distrust, the digital divide, and tradition. Some reflect real aggregate differences—U.S. broadband adoption varies across racial groups, although much

As Li et al. observed in individual scenarios [60], agents sometimes reproduce these narratives in exaggerated or unanimous choices, potentially reflecting associations learned during training that remain despite alignment against overt bias [46]. Recurring patterns warrant attention because individually minor disparities could compound across an agent’s decisions. For example, an agent assuming a Native American user prefers informal channels might call a clinic instead of booking online, text a plumber rather than request a written estimate, or speak to an officer instead of opening a case number, leaving fewer records for subsequent refunds, appeals, or complaints. Although our study uses explicit persona conditioning rather than deployed agents, these findings motivate auditing behavioral tendencies across related decisions, not just isolated scenarios. 

5.2 Digital Twins Need More than Realism 

Li et al. argued that implicit biases inconsistent with real-world differences should be mitigated, and that whether to retain biases that do match them should depend on the purpose of a simulation [60]. Our findings complicate using realism as a criterion: participants sometimes accepted familiar stereotypes while questioning unfamiliar ones. Moreover, even when differences between demographic groups reflect real-world trends, simulations may exaggerate those differences or portray members of a group as behaving uniformly, a problem termed insufficient individuation [75]. We offer three recommendations for digital twins and simulations: evaluate within-group variation alongside between-group differences, scrutinize whether sociodemographic attributes legitimately inform particular decisions, and avoid tuning simulations solely to crowd judgments of realism. Evaluations should incorporate diverse perspectives and preserve disagreement. These recommendations are particularly important when simulations inform policy [47, 59, 63]— a city’s simulation portraying conservative residents as uniformly preferring paper applications could lead planners to reduce digital outreach based on a stereotype. 

5.3 Crowdsourced Scrutiny Reveals Concerns but Has Blind Spots 

Broad discovery identifies disparities without establishing which warrant investigation. Crowdworkers prioritized cases across 16 of 17 ATUS domains, including mundane decisions whose social implications were not captured by domain classifications alone. Domain explained little priority variation, while disparity magnitude predicted priority only for political ideology. Higher perceived stakes and unanimous group choices also predicted priority, and partici-pants’ explanations highlighted sociodemographic relevance and plausibility. Crowdsourced judgments thus provided considerations simple quantitative indicators could miss. Yet these judgments have limitations. Participants sometimes accepted stereotypical portrayals as realistic, echoing research showing that benevolent stereotypes are less often recognized as prejudice than hostile ones [10, 41]. Aggregate Bradley–Terry rankings may obscure minority perspectives—for example, gender identity cases received the lowest overall priority even as agents repeatedly portrayed non-binary personas as privacy-seeking. Our results do not establish why these cases received lower priority, but they illustrate why aggregate judgments should not be treated as definitive assessments of harm. Crowdsourced auditing should therefore preserve divergent interpretations alongside rankings [5, 43] and delib-erately include communities represented in evaluated cases [11]. Prompts could examine whether auditors consider sociodemographic conditioning a legitimate reason for behavior—for example, whether they would want agents to make decisions for them based on their race. Crowdworker rankings should guide further investigation, not solely determine whether a disparity is harmful.

5.4 An Atlas for Public Scrutiny 

Public visibility has helped expose problems that might otherwise go unnoticed. The Moral Machine experiment sparked worldwide discussion about autonomous vehicles [6], and public audits of commercial face analysis prompted vendors to reduce disparities [19, 77]. Our atlas currently incorporates judgments from 500 paid crowdworkers—opening it to broader participation could allow users to browse cases, compare models, and flag concerning portrayals. Results from two additional models also motivate testing whether disparities recur across implementations. The atlas can further support developer regression tests and help auditors examine decisions beyond traditionally high-risk applications [34]. Broad discovery and participatory scrutiny are complementary: one surfaces candidate disparities across everyday activities, while the other helps identify their perceived social significance. Together, they provide a foundation for further investigation rather than a definitive assessment of harm. 

6 Limitations and Future Work 

Unlike our experiments — where LLM-powered agents are asked to roleplay as sociodemographic groups making binary choices in hypothetical scenarios — deployed personal agents may infer user attributes rather than receiving explicit sociodemographic personas, and act over multiple steps with richer user information. Although two additional models largely agreed on the direction of discovered disparities, evidence that broader behavioral patterns generalized across models was more limited, and other models and products may behave differently than what we have found in this work. Future work should examine deployed agents, intersecting identities [60], and whether disparities accumulate across tasks. Our pipeline deliberately searched for disparities. Adaptive scenario regeneration and unadjusted nominal 𝑝 -values mean the atlas identifies implicit bias cases without establishing their prevalence or harmfulness. ATUS covers common U.S. activities, and we tested only three sociodemographic dimensions. Blind screening reduced demographic leakage but could not completely eliminate the generator’s own associations. Human-written or independently generated scenarios could help distinguish generator-side associations from agent behavior. Our crowdsourced audit recruited U.S. adults through Prolific, not a representative sample of the public or affected communities. Knowing of disparities’ statistical significance and accompanying images may have influenced participants. Forced comparisons indicate relative priorities, not whether cases are harmful or harmless. Free-text explanations came only from Phase 1 and covered 102 of 306 cases, and may reflect post-hoc justifications rather than the reasons driving participants’ choices. Future work should include affected communities, collect broader explanations, and test whether prompts about sociodemographic relevance or legitimacy change priorities. 

7 Conclusion 

We constructed an atlas of 506 implicit bias cases across all 17 ATUS domains, revealing recurring sociodemographic-conditioned behavior in everyday agent decision scenarios. In a crowdsourced audit with 500 participants, domain and disparity magnitude only partly explained which cases received priority. Participants identified social implications in mundane decisions but sometimes accepted familiar stereotypes as realistic. Our findings demonstrate the value of combining broad bias discovery with crowdsourced scrutiny, while showing why crowdworkers must themselves come from diverse backgrounds to avoid reinforcing internalized stereotypes.

Ethical Considerations and Adverse Impacts 

Our study involved human participants evaluating simulated decisions by LLM agents conditioned on sociodemographic personas. Our institutional review board determined the study exempt, all participants provided informed consent, and participants were compensated approximately $19 per hour. The generated scenarios could reproduce or reinforce stereotypes about sociodemographic groups. Participants were explicitly informed that the reported differences reflected simulated AI decisions rather than observed human behavior. Our findings should not be interpreted as evidence of the actual preferences or behaviors of the demographic groups represented. Releasing the atlas introduces additional risks, including the possibility that its cases will be interpreted as accurate descriptions of demographic groups or used to reinforce stereotypes. We therefore emphasize the limitations of persona-conditioned simulations and the need for contextual interpretation. Crowdsourced judgments of which disparities warrant scrutiny may themselves reflect participants’ stereotypes and should not be treated as definitive assessments of harm.

A Case Discovery Details A.1 ATUS Activity Eligibility 

We filtered the ATUS activity lexicon before using it to guide scenario generation. Of the 465 Tier-3 activities, we excluded 108 "not elsewhere classified" (n.e.c.) categories because their catch-all labels provided insufficiently specific activity descriptions for scenario generation. We additionally excluded five ATUS data-quality codes representing 

Manuscript submitted to ACM 20 Jason Alexander, Yuxuan Li, Michael Licamele, Wesley Hanwen Deng, and Sauvik Das missing or insufficiently specified activity information, rather than substantive activities. Finally, we excluded activity code 010401 ("Personal/Private activities") because its sensitive subject matter was unsuitable for our scenario-generation procedure. These exclusions removed 114 entries, leaving 351 eligible activity categories across the 17 Tier-1 domains. The eligible activity codes formed the sampling pool for the discovery pipeline.

B Atlas Construction and Validation B.1 Semantic Coverage and Diversity 

To assess semantic coverage and diversity, we report two complementary quantitative measures on the 506 retained scenarios. First, a Vendi score on the full pool’s embeddings: 63.9 out of a maximum of 506 (12.6% of maximal diversity), indicating the 506 cases are far from mutually redundant, while still clustering meaningfully by topic rather than being maximally dispersed. Within individual activity codes that produced multiple cases, the mean Vendi-to-case-count ratio was 0.73, showing that even scenarios generated for the same narrow activity are largely mutually distinct, not near-duplicate rewordings. Second, a Gini coefficient of 0.313 on case counts across the 215 distinct ATUS activity codes the pool touches, indicating cases are spread relatively evenly across the activities reached by the pipeline rather than concentrated on a small number of "easy" codes. 

B.2 Cross-Model Agreement and Sensitivity 

We evaluated whether GPT-5.4’s sociodemographic-conditioned disparities persisted in Gemini 3.8 Flash and Qwen3.8-Max using the same 306 scenarios and personas. We then calculated Spearman’s rank correlation ( 𝜌 ) between GPT-5.4 and each comparison model across the groups’ selection rates. We defined directional agreement as 𝜌 > 0, indicating that groups tended to exhibit a similar relative ordering of action preferences. The median correlation was approximately 0.90 for each model, indicating that agreement was typically substantial rather than merely positive. Correlations were strongest for gender identity and weakest for race, although comparisons across dimensions should account for the different numbers of sociodemographic groups (three, five, and six, respectively).

C Human Study Details C.1 Pair Allocation and Exposure Balancing 

We allocated comparisons using an exposure-balanced sampling procedure designed to distribute judgments approxi-mately evenly across the 306 cases shown to crowdworkers. For each comparison, cases previously encountered by the participant were excluded. Among the remaining eligible cases, the 30 with the fewest prior comparisons were identified, and two distinct cases were sampled uniformly from this set. This procedure prioritized underexposed cases without using their estimated priority scores to determine sampling probabilities. To support exploratory analyses

D Quantitative Robustness Analyses D.1 Sensitivity to Bradley–Terry Regularization 

The Bradley–Terry model uses an 𝐿 2 penalty with coefficient 𝜆 = 0.5, specified in the study plan before Phase 1 data collection. Because regularization shrinks case-level priority estimates toward zero, it could affect the decomposition of priority variation across and within activity domains. To assess this sensitivity, we refitted the model across nine penalty coefficients spanning 𝜆 = 0.01 to 𝜆 = 10 and recomputed the proportion of case-level BT variance attributable to differences between the 17 ATUS Tier-1 domains. Across these settings, the between-domain variance share ranged from 10.6% to 11.4%, compared with 11.3% at the prespecified 𝜆 = 0.5. Although stronger regularization substantially compressed the absolute range of BT estimates, the relative decomposition remained stable. These findings indicate that the observed predominance of within-domain variation is not sensitive to the selected regularization coefficient over the tested range. 

Interpretation. The regularization sensitivity analysis measures the unadjusted share of estimated case-priority variance attributable to differences between domains. This measure is distinct from the chance-adjusted 𝜔 2 reported in the main text and should not be interpreted as a replacement for it. Across the tested penalty coefficients, most estimated case-level priority variation remained within domains.
