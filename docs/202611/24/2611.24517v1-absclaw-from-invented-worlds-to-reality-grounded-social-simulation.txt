Abstract 

LLM-based artificial societies exhibit rich behavior but of-ten lack scientific grounding: personas and environments are assumed, interaction networks are synthetic, and simulators are generated as free-form code, making results difficult to tie to real populations and sensitive to the backbone model. We argue that simulated worlds should be sampled from ob-served data, while simulation logic should be implemented 

by composing operations from a shared, task-independent vo-cabulary. In this paper, we introduce ABSClaw, a zero-code agent that compiles natural-language requests into executable, reality-grounded social simulations. ABSClaw grounds who 

acts, where they act, and with whom they interact in sur-vey and census data, geospatial sources, and real networks. It expresses state transitions through 26 atomic operations. To support an open and extensible ecosystem, ABSClaw further allows capabilities to be added as lifecycle-scoped skills with-out modifying its core. Furthermore, we propose ABSBench, a benchmark of 193 real-world social-simulation tasks. On ABSBench, ABSClaw achieves the best overall performance among the evaluated social simulators. Three real-world case studies show that grounding personas, environments, and in-teraction networks each improves agreement with observed outcomes. With one runtime skill, ABSClaw executes 13 population-scale simulations, collectively involving 25 mil-lion agents. Together, these results demonstrate ABSClaw’s real-world fidelity, extensibility, and scalability. 

## Introduction 

Large language model (LLM) agents are transforming agent-based modeling into a flexible framework for constructing interactive artificial societies. This shift opens new opportu-nities for exploratory social science, policy prototyping, and the study of social intelligence (Bonabeau 2002; Park et al. 2023; Aher, Arriaga, and Kalai 2023). Yet behavioral richness is not scientific grounding. We identify three gaps that keep LLM-based simulations closer to toy societies than to reality-grounded ones. (i) Worlds are invented rather than grounded in observed data. Most systems prompt personas, assume generic environments, and construct random or fully connected networks. Even data-driven systems usually ground only one component, leaving people, places, and social ties unmatched to a real popula-

tion or setting. (ii) Mechanism implementation is entan-gled with simulator construction. To implement a specified social mechanism, existing systems often ask an LLM to gen-erate the entire simulator as free-form code, including both task-specific logic and basic state-transition machinery. Cod-ing errors and incidental implementation choices can there-fore change the simulated dynamics, making results sensitive to the backbone model and difficult to reproduce or compare. 

(iii) Capabilities are hard-wired rather than composable. 

Different studies require different data sources, mechanisms, and analyses: public-health models may need demographic profiles, mobility models may need roads and points of in-terest, and opinion models may need survey attitudes and empirical networks. A fixed simulator cannot cover this long tail without repeated redesign, motivating a general-purpose, pluggable framework for social simulation. We present ABSClaw, a zero-code agent for constructing reality-grounded social simulations. ABSClaw addresses the three gaps above by grounding personas, environments, and social networks in real-world sources; compiling simu-lation mechanisms into programs over a fixed vocabulary of 26 atomic operations rather than generating unconstrained code; and allowing new capabilities to be added as exten-sible skills without modifying the core simulator. Our main contributions are threefold: 

• A zero-code agent for reality-grounded social simula-tion. We introduce ABSClaw, which transforms natural-language requests into executable social simulations and jointly grounds simulated personas, environments, and social networks in real-world data. 

• A constrained and extensible mechanism-programming framework. We define a fixed vocabulary of 26 atomic operations over a shared simulation runtime. Given a natural-language description, the LLM imple-ments the requested simulation mechanism by composing these operations into an executable program, rather than generating the entire simulator as unconstrained code. We further enable new data-access, execution, and analysis capabilities to be added without modifying the core simulator.

## Related Work 

Generative social simulation. Agent-based modeling ex-plains macro-level phenomena through individual rules and interactions (Bonabeau 2002), while LLMs support language-mediated perception and decision-making without fully hand-coded behavior (Ghaffarzadegan et al. 2024). Sur-veys span individual-, scenario-, and society-level simula-tion, identifying persistent challenges in validation, scala-bility, reproducibility, and connecting individual behavior to collective outcomes (Mou et al. 2026; Orlando, La Gatta, and Moscato 2026). Generative Agents introduced memory, re-flection, and planning, while Concordia provided reusable abstractions for agents, environments, and game masters (Park et al. 2023; Vezhnevets et al. 2023). Later systems extend task-oriented interaction and team formation (Li, Sun, and Zhang 2025), general-purpose simulator genera-tion (Tang et al. 2025), realistic urban and socioeconomic environments (Piao et al. 2025), and large-scale social-media simulation (Yang et al. 2024). Other work models persistent affect, identity adaptation, real-time event-driven activity, and graph-optimized hybrid LLM–ABM execution (Fu et al. 2026; Fan et al. 2026; Allegrini et al. 2026; Zhou et al. 2026); Light Society combines full LLMs with distilled surrogates to exceed one billion agents (Guan et al. 2025). Despite ad-vances in cognition, interaction, coverage, and scale, these systems do not systematically combine real-world grounding with a stable, task-independent simulation representation. 

Empirical grounding and behavioral validity. Persona conditioning can reproduce demographic response patterns (Argyle et al. 2023; Aher, Arriaga, and Kalai 2023; Park et al. 2024). However, SocioBench reports only about 30–40% ac-curacy across more than 480,000 survey records, with sub-stantial variation across domains and demographic groups (Wang et al. 2025a); moreover, consistent self-reports may coexist with declining persona expression over long inter-actions (Gonnermann-Müller et al. 2026). Plausible role-playing therefore does not guarantee valid collective behav-ior, which also depends on environmental dynamics, ex-posure, scheduling, and initialization (Li and Tao 2026). System-level approaches ground simulations in observed net-works and environments: S 3 uses real social-network data (Gao et al. 2023); SocioVerse aligns users, environments, scenarios, and behaviors using ten million user records (Zhang et al. 2025). These systems demonstrate the value of empirical grounding but typically embed specific data sources and alignment procedures in domain-specific plat-forms. 

Automated simulator construction and research automa-tion. YuLan-OneSim generates executable social simula-tions from natural-language dialogue (Wang et al. 2025b); S-Researcher automates experiment design, participant simula-tion, analysis, and reporting (Wang et al. 2026); and SOCIA-EVO combines evidence blueprints with structural evolution and parameter calibration (Hua et al. 2026). Although these systems reduce manual effort, they largely generate or re-vise unconstrained code, making state mutation, scheduling, and interaction semantics difficult to inspect and compare across tasks or backbone models. Related work improves reliability through constrained generation or by separating adaptive reasoning from deterministic execution: PICARD rejects grammar-invalid decoding steps (Scholak, Schucher, and Bahdanau 2021); Synchromesh imposes syntactic and semantic completion constraints (Poesia et al. 2022). AB-SClaw applies the same principle to simulator construction by compiling requested mechanisms into bounded control flow over a fixed vocabulary of auditable state-transition op-erations, rather than regenerating infrastructure for each task. 

## Overview 

We formulate open-ended social simulation as a compilation problem. Given a natural-language request x, optional per-sona, environment, and network sources D = ( Dp, D e, D n),and a catalog of installed skills S, ABSClaw generates an ex-ecutable lifecycle program P = ( Psetup , P act , P tick , P term ).As shown in Figure 1, ABSClaw proceeds in four steps. First, it resolves the user’s multi-turn request into a struc-tured simulation specification s. Second, it materializes the required source data as run-scoped typed records and ex-poses only a compact manifest to the code compiler. Third, it compiles s, the manifest, and selected skill interfaces into a program over a fixed vocabulary of atomic operations O. Fi-nally, the executor advances the simulated world and records execution traces, metrics, and output artifacts.

Persona. Persona records contain sample keys and typed attributes. ABSClaw materializes them from individual-level surveys such as the World Values Survey (WVS) (Haerpfer et al. 2022) and census-derived demographic data. 

Network. Network records contain endpoint keys, rela-tion layers, and edge attributes parsed from SNAP topolo-gies (Leskovec and Krevl 2014). When network and per-sona sources are combined, a deterministic partial mapping projects the anonymous topology onto sampled persona keys. 

Environment. Environment records represent non-acting entities constructed from place, radius, and time con-straints. ABSClaw resolves locations through Nominatim and Esri, obtains road and point-of-interest context from OpenStreetMap, and retrieves time-specific weather condi-tions from Open-Meteo (Haklay and Weber 2008; Nomina-tim Contributors 2026; Esri 2026; Zippenfenig 2023). 

Deep Research. ABSClaw can conduct broad web re-search before formal source sampling. It decomposes the user request into a multi-angle search plan spanning con-ceptual background, theory, statistics, policy, stakeholders, behavioral mechanisms, and evaluation metrics.

## ABSBench 

We introduce ABSBench, a benchmark for evaluating the compilation of natural-language requests into executable agent-based social simulations. ABSBench was constructed independently of the ABSClaw implementation. The bench-mark contains 193 tasks curated to represent real-world social processes and practical modeling needs. Each task centers on interactions among heterogeneous actors, temporal changes in individual or collective states, and measurable system-level outcomes. The benchmark was constructed in four stages. First, we defined ten domains to cover diverse interaction mecha-nisms and application settings. Second, we collected can-didate modeling needs from academic literature and related resources. Third, domain experts removed tasks that were du-plicate, underspecified, infeasible to evaluate, or not mean-ingfully agent-based, while checking the remaining set for diversity and coverage. Fourth, an LLM standardized the retained seeds into a common task format, including ac-tors, interactions, state transitions, temporal requirements, and outcome measures. Experts then verified each standard-ized task against its original seed and corrected any semantic drift or unsupported additions. Every ABSBench task includes a public natural-language prompt, an evaluator-only behavioral specification, and struc-tured metadata. Candidate programs run through a common interface, and their outputs are normalized into a final sum-mary, per-round metric trajectories, and agent-indexed deci-sion records. Validity ( V ; 0–100) comprises base execution and required artifacts, task-constraint compliance, pipeline and semantic evidence, and artifact quality. Task correct-ness ( T ; 0–100) is assigned by an LLM judge across im-plementation fidelity (accuracy), code quality, and result plausibility. For validation, two domain experts indepen-dently and blindly scored a stratified subset of 59 runs; mean judge–expert quadratic-weighted Cohen’s κ = 0 .87 indi-cated strong agreement. 

Domain coverage. Figure 2 shows near-uniform represen-tation across ten domains. Every domain contributes between 9.8% and 10.4% of the benchmark. 

Prompt granularity. ABSBench contains 130 detailed prompts (67.4%) and 63 terse prompts (32.6%). Detailed prompts range from 298 to 545 whitespace-delimited words. They specify the agent population, individual states, interac-tion rules, interventions, and required artifacts. Terse prompts range from 13 to 20 words. They state the modeling prob-lem and intended outcome while leaving agent design and implementation choices to the evaluated system. 

Decision mechanisms and difficulty. Figure 2 shows that 174 tasks (90.2%) require live LLM calls for substantive agent decisions; the remaining 19 (9.8%) use mechanical or offline policies. The task-difficulty chart reports 18 Easy tasks (9.3%), 70 Medium tasks (36.3%), 88 Hard tasks (45.6%), and 17 Expert tasks (8.8%). These labels summarize com-bined technical demands from agent heterogeneity, persistent state, interaction topology, temporal feedback, counterfactual comparisons, scalability, and output verification. 

## Results 

External System Comparison 

We compared ABSClaw with SOCIA-EVO and OneSim be-cause all three support end-to-end construction of executable simulators from general natural-language requests. Other influential platforms, including SocioVerse and AgentSoci-ety, rely on dedicated environments, data infrastructures, or platform-specific workflows and are therefore not directly comparable under the ABSBench protocol. ABSClaw achieved 168.59 out of 200, exceeding SOCIA-EVO (148.70) by 19.89 points and OneSim (148.94) by 19.65 points (Figure 3). ABSClaw achieved the best performance across domains, prompt lengths, decision mechanisms, and task difficulties (Figure 4).

## Conclusion 

We introduced ABSClaw, a zero-code framework for com-piling natural-language requests into executable, reality-grounded social simulations. ABSClaw grounds personas, environments, and interaction networks in observed data, while expressing simulation logic through 26 shared atomic operations and extensible lifecycle-scoped skills. On the 193-task ABSBench, it achieved the best overall performance among evaluated systems and reduced cross-backbone vari-ation by 61.6% compared with unconstrained Spec-to-Code generation. Three real-world case studies showed that grounding each component improves agreement with ob-served outcomes, and a single APS runtime skill enabled 13 population-scale simulations totaling 25 million agents. These results demonstrate that shared, auditable simulation operations can improve reliability, composability, and scala-bility across diverse social-simulation tasks. References
