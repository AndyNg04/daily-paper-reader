## Abstract 

As symptom checkers based on artificial intelligence (AI) are becom-ing common entry points for health information, their influence on lay users’ diagnostic decisions raises questions about trust and bias. Of particular concern is confirmation bias, the tendency to favour information that supports one’s existing belief while dis-counting information that contradicts it. One of the central de-sign choices for such systems is the style of voice: whether they should present themselves with a human-like (anthropomorphic) or a clearly machine-like (xenomorphic) voice. This study investigates whether the voice design of an AI symptom checker modulates confirmation bias in self-diagnosis. In a within-subjects online ex-periment ( 𝑁 = 81 participants, 648 trials), participants selected a diagnosis and rated their confidence, then heard an AI-generated recommendation that either confirmed or contradicted their initial choice, delivered by an anthropomorphic or a machine-like voice, before deciding again. We observed a strong, direction-dependent influence of the AI: participants revised their diagnosis in 45.7% of trials when the AI contradicted them versus 1.2% when it con-firmed them (generalised linear mixed model, GLMM, 𝑝 < .001); confidence rose under confirmation but barely moved under con-tradiction (robust linear mixed model, 𝑝 < .001); and decisions took longer after contradiction (Gamma GLMM, 𝑝 < .001). This effect did not differ between the two voices (no voice or interaction term was significant, smallest 𝑝 = .282), even though a manipulation check confirmed the voices were perceived as markedly different. The findings suggest that the direction of an AI recommendation, rather than the anthropomorphism of its voice, drives confirmation-related bias in AI-assisted self-diagnosis.

## 1 Introduction 

Symptom checkers based on artificial intelligence (AI) are increas-ingly used as accessible tools for preliminary health assessment. By allowing users to enter symptoms and receive possible diagnoses or triage advice, these systems may support early orientation be-fore professional consultation. However, prior research has raised concerns about their diagnostic reliability, safety, and suitability for laymen [1, 2]. In medical decision contexts, the interaction between human judgement and AI recommendation is particularly sensitive. Users may not only benefit from AI support but also become vulnerable to automation bias, relying too strongly on automated recommen-dations even when they are wrong [3–5]. Such reliance is further shaped by cognitive biases, that is, sys-tematic deviations from rational judgement that arise from the way people process information. One of the most studied biases is con-firmation bias , the tendency to seek out, weigh more heavily (i.e., perceive as higher in quality), and more readily accept information that is consistent with one’s prior beliefs while discounting infor-mation that contradicts them [6]. In self-diagnosis contexts this bias is particularly relevant: users typically form an initial assumption about their symptoms, and they may therefore be more likely to accept AI feedback that supports that assumption than feedback that challenges it. This carries a concrete risk: when both the user and the AI are wrong, an agreeing recommendation can produce a false confirmation [7]. A layperson may, for instance, be falsely reassured that their symptoms do not require immediate medical attention and therefore fail to seek help in time. Voice-based AI assistants add another layer to this interaction: spoken recommendations are not perceived as neutral informa-tion, since vocal characteristics shape perceived credibility, social presence, and trust [8], and trust in healthcare voice assistants has been shown to depend on perceived usefulness, content credibility, service quality, and risk-related factors [9]. This raises a central design question: whether patient-facing AI should imitate human interaction or present itself as clearly non-human. Anthropomor-phic design is often pursued precisely to make a system feel familiar arXiv:2611.21379v1 [cs.HC] 16 Nov 2026 STUD SS2026, Summer Semester 2026, TH Ingolstadt, Germany Röthlingshöfer et al. 

and to engender trust; a machine-like (xenomorphic) design instead emphasises the system’s artificial nature and may reduce patient confusion by clearly distinguishing AI from human physicians [10]. While previous work has examined the accuracy of symptom checkers and trust in healthcare voice assistants, less is known about how the voice design of an AI symptom checker influences users’ diagnostic decisions after confirming or contradicting advice. This study addresses that gap, testing whether users exhibit a gen-eral confirmation bias toward the AI - across diagnostic choice, con-fidence, and response time - and, as its primary question, whether the strength of that bias is moderated by the AI’s voice design (anthropomorphic vs. machine-like). The formal hypotheses are stated in the Methods section. This paper makes three contributions: (i) a controlled within-subjects design that isolates the direction of an AI recommendation from its vocal presentation in a self-diagnosis task; (ii) mixed-effects evidence for a strong, direction-dependent confirmation bias across choice, confidence, and response time; and (iii) the finding that this bias is unaffected by whether the AI speaks with an anthropo-morphic or a machine-like voice, despite a successful perceptual manipulation. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Confirmation Bias in AI-Assisted Decision-Making 

In his comprehensive review, Nickerson [6] characterises confir-mation bias as a pervasive and largely unintentional feature of everyday reasoning rather than a deliberate strategy, spanning bi-ased search for information, biased interpretation of ambiguous evidence, and biased memory. The strength with which the bias operates is not fixed but mod-ulated by several factors. Four conditions in particular make it stronger: a firm prior commitment to the initial belief, which raises the psychological stakes of maintaining it; evidence that is ambigu-ous enough to be read as supportive; a source perceived as credible or trustworthy; and a personal motivation to reach a particular con-clusion [6]. These moderators matter directly for AI advice: an AI recommendation is an external piece of evidence that a user must integrate with an already-formed belief, and its perceived credibil-ity and the ambiguity of the underlying symptoms determine how readily it is accepted or resisted. In AI-assisted self-diagnosis this bias is particularly relevant, because users often enter the interaction with an initial assump-tion about their symptoms. When an AI recommendation agrees with that assumption, users may become more confident in their initial diagnosis even if alternative explanations remain plausible; a contradicting recommendation may instead be discounted because it conflicts with a prior belief. Confirmation bias thus provides a theoretical basis for studying how users react to confirming versus contradicting AI advice. Empirically, this bias has been observed in each of the three outcomes we examine. For choices , mental-health professionals and students who had first made a preliminary diagnosis of a clinical vignette rated a mock AI triage recommendation as more trust-worthy and were more likely to accept it when it matched that diagnosis [11]. For confidence , agreeing advice raises confidence even when it barely changes the judgement itself: in judge–advisor studies with numeric estimates, advice close to one’s initial estimate acts as social validation that increases confidence, an effect that shrinks and eventually vanishes as advice moves further away [12]. People place confidence even in a spurious consensus of redundant, agreeing opinions, whereas they discount independent opinions that conflict with one another, so that confidence and accuracy dissociate [13]; and when participants could adjust an incentivised confidence wager after seeing a partner’s judgement, agreement raised confidence more than disagreement lowered it, and parti-cipants took the strength of confirming but not of disconfirming opinions into account [14]. Comparable agreement effects occur with AI advice: advice that agreed with participants’ initial judge-ment led to larger increases in their certainty, and once heeded it was used similarly whether it was labelled as coming from an AI or from peers [15]. Decision time has been studied less directly. Con-flicting information can prolong decisions - decision makers spent more time per decision when a peer’s second opinion frequently disagreed with the AI’s recommendation [16] - and time constraints increase anchoring on AI advice: participants given 25 rather than 10 seconds disagreed more often with incorrect AI predictions [17], consistent with fast decisions reflecting less deliberate processing of the advice. Together, these findings lead us to expect that con-tradicting AI advice triggers more revisions, a weaker increase in confidence, and longer deliberation than confirming advice. 

## 2.2 AI-Based Self-Diagnosis and Healthcare Chatbots 

AI-based self-diagnosis tools and healthcare chatbots are increas-ingly used as accessible, immediate sources of health information and preliminary guidance. Yu and Chen [18], for example, examine the acceptance of healthcare chatbots among older adults with an extended UTAUT model and show that performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and social influence shape the intention to use such systems. At the same time, using AI for self-diagnosis intro-duces risks. Zada et al. [19] investigate medical misinformation in AI-assisted self-diagnosis and find that AI-generated medical advice is often unclear or inaccurate, so that relying solely on such systems for self-diagnosis increases the risk of being misinformed. This lack of expertise is what makes medical decision-making especially difficult for laypeople. Unlike clinicians, lay users have no formal training to interpret symptoms, to weigh competing dif-ferential diagnoses against one another, or to judge how reliable a given AI output is for their particular case [2, 20]. As a result they have few independent grounds on which to check a recommenda-tion, which makes them more dependent on - and correspondingly more swayable by - whatever the system suggests, and less able to detect when its advice is wrong - a concern given that such advice is often unclear or inaccurate [19].

## 2.4 Biases in AI-Assisted Medical Decision-Making 

Beyond accuracy, AI recommendations can shape human decisions. Automation bias refers to the tendency to rely too strongly on auto-mated recommendations even when they may be incorrect. Kücking et al. [3] studied automation bias in AI-based clinical decision sup-port and found that non-specialists were especially susceptible to accepting incorrect AI recommendations. We expect this suscepti-bility to be even more pronounced in the everyday use of symptom checkers by laypeople. Preliminary evidence from Qazi et al. [4] suggests that even medically trained, AI-literate users can be af-fected by erroneous large-language-model recommendations in diagnostic reasoning. Closely related to the present study, Rosbach et al. [7] show - in computational pathology - a direct confirmation-bias effect of AI advice: when an AI recommendation mirrored a pathologist’s own flawed tumour-percentage estimate, that erroneous estimate was reinforced rather than corrected, an effect the authors term false confirmation , and one that time pressure, counter-intuitively, weak-ened rather than worsened. This maps directly onto the situation we study, in a lay self-diagnosis rather than an expert pathology setting: an AI recommendation that agrees with a user’s initial diagnosis, whether right or wrong, may entrench it in the same way, which is exactly the mechanism our confirming condition isolates - underscoring, together with broader analyses of bias in medical AI [22], that the risks of AI-assisted diagnosis depend not just on system accuracy but on how users respond when an answer confirms or contradicts their own judgement. 

## 2.5 Voice Design, Anthropomorphism, and Machine-Like AI 

How AI advice is presented may further affect perception and decision-making. In voice-based systems the recommendation is processed as speech and not merely as text, and vocal characteristics can influence how confident, credible, or socially present a system appears. Kirkland et al. [23] show that speech features such as filled pauses, speech rate, and fundamental frequency affect perceived speaker confidence, suggesting that users read acoustic properties as cues to certainty or reliability. More broadly, a survey of voice in human–agent interaction reports that human-like voices are generally rated as more likeable and often as more trustworthy or credible than synthetic ones, whereas evidence for behavioural con-sequences such as persuasiveness is inconsistent [8]. Voice design may therefore affect decisions independently of content: Dubiel et al. [24] found that synthetic voice quality influenced participants’ choices even when the available options were held constant, and frame this as a potential dark pattern. A related question is whether medical AI should appear human-like or clearly non-human. Mil-ford et al. [10] argue against anthropomorphism in patient-facing medical AI and advocate a xenomorphic approach that emphasises non-human characteristics to distinguish AI from human physi-cians, arguing that human-like designs built on trust-engendering features fall into the uncanny valley, import cultural biases about who appears trustworthy, and risk competing with the human doctor–patient relationship. Beyond being a mere presentation choice, anthropomorphism may thus itself act as a bias: attributing human traits, credibility, or agency to a system can shape trust independently of the actual content of its advice. Such attributions are not neutral, either: anthropomorphic conversational assistants are frequently gendered: even where companies claim otherwise, system outputs are ambiguous about their humanness and users personify and gender them [25]. This distinction matters in health-care, where human-like systems may increase trust but also blur the boundary between automated advice and professional medi-cal judgement. In line with this literature we operationalise the non-human design as a machine-like voice and contrast it with an anthropomorphic one. These strands - confirmation and automation bias in AI-assisted diagnosis, the particular vulnerability of laypeople, and the percep-tual effects of voice design - have rarely been combined, leaving open whether the voice of an AI symptom checker modulates how strongly users maintain or revise an initial diagnosis after con-firming or contradicting advice: the question the present study addresses.

## 3.2 Participants 

The final sample consisted of 81 participants who completed the study (648 analysed trials); sixteen were recruited during a live event and received a slice of pizza as compensation, the remainder through convenience sampling within the researchers’ extended personal networks. Five sessions that were started but not com-pleted were excluded; the sample’s demographic composition is reported with the results (see Sample at the start of the Results). 

## 3.3 Materials and Apparatus 

The diagnostic cases were short patient vignettes constructed by the research team from the disease–symptom associations of a pub-licly available, artificially generated dataset covering 773 diseases and 377 symptoms [26]: each vignette describes the symptoms the dataset lists for one disease, written by the authors in everyday language so that laypeople could reason about them without spe-cialist knowledge. Each vignette was followed by three diagnostic options: the disease the symptoms were drawn from and two alter-native diagnoses that the authors judged plausible for the described symptoms. As the authors are not medical professionals, the cases were not clinically validated. Instead, the vignettes and options were piloted with peers to ensure that they were understandable and that none of the options appeared implausible. The study there-fore does not rely on the medical correctness of any option: the manipulation concerns whether the AI confirms or contradicts the participant’s own initial choice, and no option was scored as correct in the analysis. The pool comprised twelve vignettes; to broaden the stimulus set without lengthening the sessions, each participant received a seeded random subset of eight, and case was modelled as a random effect in all analyses. The participant-facing interface is shown in Figure 2. AI recommendations were pre-scripted by the authors rather than produced by a live model, so that the verbal content was held constant across participants and only the voice varied. For every option of every case, the authors wrote a short German explanation of two to three sentences that named the diagnosis and related it to the described symptoms (e.g., that the symptoms began directly after a large meal, which is typical of the recommended diagnosis); these texts were then synthesised into speech with the two voices described below. For each option the spoken content was identical across the two voices; the voices differed only in their acoustic delivery. The anthropomorphic voice was synthesised with ElevenLabs’ neural text-to-speech (TTS) service (model eleven_v3 , a custom female voice created with ElevenLabs Voice Design), configured for expressive delivery (stability 0 .35, similarity boost 0 .75, style 0.45, speaker boost enabled) and enriched with natural deliv-ery cues: audible breaths and hesitation fillers (e.g. “ähm”). The machine-like voice was synthesised with Microsoft Edge TTS (voice 

de-DE-KatjaNeural ), rendered with a lowered pitch ( −30 Hz) and slightly reduced rate ( −5%) to emphasise a flat, plainly synthetic character, and read the same content without any delivery cues. The two voices were thus intended to contrast human-like prosody and warmth against flat, mechanical delivery. To en-sure this was a contrast of character rather than level , all audio was loudness-normalised to −16 LUFS (EBU R128) with ffmpeg (loudnorm , 𝐼 = −16, TP = −1.5, LRA = 11). The study ran in the browser on both desktop and smartphone, and participants were advised to use headphones for consistent audio playback. 

## 3.4 Procedure 

Before the main experiment, participants provided demographic information (age and gender) and reported their prior experience with AI symptom checkers and AI voice assistants. A single practice trial with one additional vignette then familiarised them with the task and was excluded from analysis. For each case, participants first read the patient vignette, se-lected the diagnosis they considered most likely, and rated their confidence on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (very low) to 7 (very high). They then listened to the AI voice recommendation, after which they again selected one of the three options and re-rated their confidence. Each participant’s eight cases were split into two blocks of four, one block per voice, with a short break between the blocks. Each participant thus heard one voice throughout the first block and the other throughout the second, with no mixing of voices within a block. Trial assignment was generated deterministically from a per-session seed, which makes each session fully reproducible and exactly counterbalanced. A seeded pseudo-random generator (Mul-berry32, seeded via an FNV-1a hash of the session identifier) drove all randomisation. The participant’s eight cases were sorted into four pairs, each pair’s coin flip assigning its two cases to opposite AI conditions - yielding exactly four confirming and four contra-dicting trials per participant. Block order (anthropomorphic-first vs. machine-first) was likewise coin-flip balanced; each block received two confirming and two contradicting cases, shuffled within the block to reduce presentation-order effects. After completing all trials, participants heard a short sample of each voice again and rated it on eight 7-point semantic differentials, which served as a manipulation check. Human-likeness (machine-like–human-like), naturalness (artificial–natural), pleasantness (unpleasant–pleasant), and competence (incompetent–competent) were single items adapted from the anthropomorphism, likeability, and perceived-intelligence scales of the Godspeed question-naire [27], which was developed for robots and uses 5-point scales; the naturalness item combines its fake–natural and artificial–lifelike pairs. Trustworthiness and intelligibility followed subjective rating studies of synthetic voices [28], warmth (distant– warm) was added following the warmth–competence framework of social perception [29], and a final item captured whether the voice sounded male or female. All items were presented in German translations by the authors, in randomised order. As this set is an ad-hoc adaptation rather than a validated scale, we analysed the items individually. 

## 3.5 Measures and Analysis 

The three dependent variables were operationalised from the per-trial records. Diagnosis change was a binary indicator that the final diagnostic choice differed from the initial one. Confidence shift was the difference between the post- and pre-recommendation confi-dence ratings on the 1–7 scale. Final-decision response time was the time, recorded in milliseconds and reported in seconds, from the moment the answer options reappeared - i.e., after the recom-mendation had finished playing and the participant had continued - to the final diagnostic choice. Because the study ran unsupervised in the browser, these times also include any moments in which a participant was not attending to the task (see Limitations). We analysed the trial-level data (648 trials) with one mixed-effects model per outcome, each with fixed effects for AI condi-tion, voice, and their interaction and crossed random intercepts for participant and case. Both factors were coded with sum-to-zero contrasts (confirming AI condition and machine-like voice as the positively signed levels), so that each main effect is averaged over the levels of the other factor. Diagnosis change was modelled with a binomial generalised linear mixed model (GLMM, logit link) [30]. For the two continuous outcomes, the residuals of Gaussian linear mixed models (LMMs) deviated clearly from normality, with heavy tails for the confidence shift and a pronounced right skew for re-sponse time even after log transformation (Shapiro–Wilk 𝑊 = 0.975 and 𝑊 = 0.896, both 𝑝 < .001; Q–Q plots in Appendix A). We therefore modelled the confidence shift with a robust LMM, which down-weights outlying observations [31], and response time with a Gamma GLMM with log link on untransformed seconds [32, 33], which accommodates the right-skewed distribution of response times without transforming the outcome. For the confidence shift, the variance of the case intercept was estimated at zero (singular fit), i.e. the shift did not vary systematically between cases; this leaves the fixed effects unaffected. From each model we derived estimated marginal means for the four AI × voice cells and the contrasts of interest on the model’s natural scale: the odds ratio of a diagnosis change (contradict vs. confirm), the difference in confidence shift (confirm − contradict, in scale points), and the ratio of mean response times (contradict / confirm). Voice moderation (H2) was tested by the AI × voice in-teraction, expressed as the difference between the AI effects under the two voices. All tests and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) are Wald-type. As sensitivity checks, we refit the two continuous out-comes with the Gaussian LMMs used in an earlier version of this analysis (Appendix B) and ran participant-level nonparametric tests on aggregated data (Appendix C).

## 5 Discussion 

This study asked whether the voice design of an AI symptom checker influences confirmation bias in AI-assisted self-diagnosis. We found a strong, direction-dependent confirmation effect: partici-pants were substantially more likely to keep their initial diagnosis when the AI confirmed it, while contradicting advice produced more frequent changes and longer response times; confidence, in contrast, rose markedly after confirmation but barely moved after contradiction. This effect did not differ between the anthropomor-phic and the machine-like voice. 

## 5.1 A Confirmation Bias, Not a Between-Person Artefact 

Participants did not treat the AI recommendation as neutral infor-mation: its direction shaped both their choices and their subjective confidence. The clearest signature is the asymmetric confidence up-date : agreement raised confidence substantially (a shift of +0.96 scale points) while disagreement barely moved it ( −0.09), the two differing by 1 .05 points. This is precisely what confirmation bias predicts - confirming evidence is weighted more heavily than dis-confirming evidence, and people preferentially accommodate infor-mation that agrees with a belief they already hold [6]. The pattern mirrors advice studies in which a partner’s agreement raised confi-dence more than disagreement lowered it [14] and people trusted a spurious consensus of agreeing opinions while discounting conflict-ing ones [13]; in perceptual decisions, high confidence has moreover been shown to selectively attenuate the neural processing of discon-firming post-decision evidence [34]. An updater equally sensitive to both directions of evidence would lower confidence after contradic-tion about as much as it raises it after confirmation, while a sceptical updater would do neither; our participants raised their confidence after confirmation but barely lowered it after contradiction. The small average change after contradiction conceals two op-posite movements. Participants who kept their diagnosis despite the contradiction lowered their confidence (-0.36 points, 176 trials), whereas those who switched to the AI’s suggestion rated their con-fidence in the new diagnosis slightly higher than in their original one (+0.24 points, 148 trials). Contradiction therefore did affect par-ticipants: it either moderately shook their confidence or led them to adopt the AI’s view with about the confidence they had initially placed in their own. In neither case did it produce a confidence loss comparable to the gain after confirmation. Crucially, the design rules out the most obvious alternative: be-cause every participant responded to both confirming and con-tradicting recommendations, the asymmetry is a within-person contrast, not an artefact of stable differences in how readily people revise decisions. A generally reluctant participant would move little in either condition, yet the same people moved far more after con-tradiction than after confirmation, with the confidence gap holding within individuals once repeated measures were modelled - a prop-erty of how each person handled agreement versus disagreement, not between-person heterogeneity in trust or stubbornness. We are nonetheless careful about the scope of this claim: it char-acterises the bias at the level of the sample, not each individual as biased, since we did not separate participants by their general tendency to trust or distrust the AI (see Limitations). The high rate of diagnosis changes after contradiction shows that users were also readily willing to abandon their own judgement when the AI offered an alternative - a strong automation-bias signature that echoes con-cerns about over-reliance on AI advice [3, 4]. Taken on its own, the change rate could therefore be explained by automation bias alone: deferring to the AI produces many changes after contradic-tion and none after confirmation, regardless of any preference for confirming information. The confidence and response-time results go beyond this. Pure deference would predict comparable updating in both directions, yet confirmation raised confidence substantially while contradiction barely lowered it, and agreeing advice was pro-cessed markedly faster than disagreeing advice. Taken together, the three outcomes indicate that confirmation bias, and not only automation bias, shapes how users decide with AI advice. 

## 5.2 Why Voice Design Did Not Matter 

The absence of a voice effect is the more surprising result, given that our manipulation check confirmed the two voices were perceived very differently on human-likeness, warmth, naturalness, and trust. One explanation is that the content of the recommendation sim-ply dominated its delivery, with acoustic style playing at most a secondary role. This would be in line with findings that advice is used similarly once heeded, whether it is labelled as coming from an AI or from human peers [15], and that human-like voices, while generally better liked, have inconsistent effects on behaviour [8]. A second is that, although the voices differed relative to each other, both were rated as fairly artificial in absolute terms, so the con-trast may not have been strong or human-like enough to change susceptibility to the confirmation effect. Either way, the null carries a design lesson: prior work has warned that anthropomorphic design in medical AI may confuse patients about where automated advice ends and human medical care begins, motivating calls for deliberately machine-like (xeno-morphic) systems [10], but our findings show voice style alone did not measurably change susceptibility to confirmation-related bias, while the recommendation’s direction did. Designers should therefore not treat voice style as a primary lever for reducing biased decision-making - the leverage lies in what the system says, not in how human it sounds. 

## 5.3 Implications 

Our findings matter because symptom checkers are typically used outside professional supervision, where an inaccurate AI can steer laypeople in either direction: a confirming AI can entrench a wrong self-diagnosis through the very confidence boost we observed, while a contradicting AI can just as easily talk a correct user out of it, given the high revision rate we found. In both cases the recom-mendation’s direction, rather than its accuracy, drives the decision -exactly the failure mode that makes over-reliance on unsupervised medical AI consequential [3, 4, 7, 22]. Because the effect was carried by content rather than voice, mit-igation should target the content of the interaction: systems should 

communicate uncertainty by calibrating expressed confidence to the recommendation’s reliability, prompt users to consider alternative di-agnoses instead of presenting a single verdict, use reflective prompts

and carry explicit reminders that AI advice is not a substitute for pro-fessional care , especially before a confident-sounding confirmation. Given the null voice effect, these content-level choices matter more than acoustic or anthropomorphic styling: rather than sounding more (or deliberately less [10]) human, patient-facing AI should invest in how it frames uncertainty and invites users to question their own assumptions. These implications should be read against the profile of our sample, which skewed young (85% under 35). Younger, likely more technology-fluent users may relate to AI advice differently from older populations who make up a large share of real symptom-checker use, so the size - though not necessarily the existence - of the confirmation effect may vary across age groups; we discuss this generalisability question further in the Limitations. 

## 6 Limitations and Future Work 

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. The change rate under confirmation was near zero (4 of 324 trials), so the GLMM’s confirm-vs-refute contrast for diagnostic choice is estimated from a sparse cell and should be read qualita-tively rather than as a precise point estimate. The diagnostic cases were selected and adapted by the re-searchers, who are not medical experts, so their clinical accuracy and difficulty were not formally validated - less critical for our aim, since we tested whether decision and confidence changed after confirming or contradicting AI advice, not whether participants chose the medically correct diagnosis. Relatedly, participants reasoned about standardised vignettes rather than their own symptoms. Judging an adapted case is not the same as facing a personal health concern: real self-diagnosis carries emotional stakes that could either sharpen confirmation bias - people may cling harder to a feared or hoped-for interpretation - or weaken AI reliance, if users treat their own bodies as more authoritative than a generic recommendation. Our design therefore has limited ecological validity for how confirmation bias and AI reliance operate when the problem is one’s own. The study also used preconfigured AI responses rather than a live interactive system. This improved experimental control and held content constant across participants, but reduced ecological validity, as real symptom checkers respond dynamically to user input. The online format added further technical and environmen-tal variability - sessions ran on different devices and in different settings, and although headphones were recommended, listening conditions could not be controlled. A few participants restarted the study after technical issues; their incomplete first sessions were discarded, as were all other incomplete sessions. The unsupervised setting also affects response times: a small number of trials show very long latencies (7 of 648 trials above 30 s, maximum 172 s), most plausibly because participants were briefly distracted (e.g., looked away from the screen) before continuing. The app registered tab switches only during audio playback and cannot detect off-screen attention, so such interruptions could not be identified reliably and no trials were excluded; the response-time model was chosen to be robust to this right tail. Our analyses characterise the confirmation effect at the level of the sample rather than the individual. The mixed models and paired tests establish that, within participants, contradiction triggered substantially more revision and confidence loss than confirmation; but we did not classify participants by their general disposition to revise decisions or to trust the AI. A participant who rarely changes their mind, or who distrusted the AI throughout, can contribute to the aggregate pattern for reasons other than confirmation bias, and cleanly separating such dispositional differences from a within-person confirmation effect would require more trials per participant and explicit measures of baseline revision tendency and trust. The sample also skewed young (about 85% under 35) and inex-perienced with these technologies (median prior experience 3 on the 1–7 scale), both of which threaten generalisability in ways that could cut either direction: older adults may differ in their familiarity with AI tools, baseline trust, and self-diagnosis habits, so the effect’s size - though probably not its direction - may not generalise to older populations; and while inexperienced users have no calibrated basis for discounting a recommendation, more experienced ones might weight AI advice differently, whether by trusting or scrutinising it more. The magnitude of the effect may therefore vary across age and experience in ways our sample cannot reveal. Finally, and most relevant to our main question, the anthropo-morphism gap we induced may simply have been too small to move the bias: both voices were AI-generated and, though distinguishable from each other, were rated as fairly artificial in absolute terms. A genuinely human voice might produce a different result - a pos-sibility our design cannot rule out and that bears directly on our central research question. These limitations point to clear next steps: stronger, differenti-ated voice manipulations - ideally a genuinely human voice; med-ically validated cases with participants’ own health concerns, re-covering the ecological validity vignettes sacrifice and letting AI direction be crossed with correctness; and a real, dynamic AI chat-bot in place of the pre-scripted recommendations used here. Be-yond voice, content-level interventions - explanations, calibrated uncertainty, reflective prompts - may reduce confirmation bias and over-reliance, since direction seems to matter more than delivery. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We investigated whether the voice design of an AI symptom checker shapes confirmation bias in lay self-diagnosis. In a within-subjects online experiment ( 𝑁 = 81), AI recommendations exerted a strong, direction-dependent influence: participants changed their diagnosis far more often and deliberated longer when the AI contradicted rather than confirmed them, while their confidence rose substan-tially only after confirmation (all 𝑝 < .001). Contrary to our primary hypothesis, this confirmation effect was statistically indistinguish-able between an anthropomorphic and a machine-like voice, even though the two voices were reliably perceived as different. The presence and direction of an AI recommendation thus appear to matter more for confirmation bias than the specific voice style used to deliver it, with the design implications discussed above.

## C Participant-Level Tests 

To check that the trial-level results do not depend on the mixed-model assumptions, we also analysed data aggregated per par-ticipant. For diagnosis change, each participant’s proportion of changed diagnoses was computed in each of the four AI × voice cells (two trials per cell) and compared with a Friedman test with participant as the blocking factor; the cells differed significantly (𝜒 2 (3) = 123 .9, 𝑝 < .001, Kendall’s 𝑊 = 0.51). Additionally, a McNemar test compared, within participants, whether a person changed their diagnosis on at least one trial under contradiction and under confirmation: 79% of participants did so at least once under contradiction versus 4.9% under confirmation ( 𝜒 2 = 58 .0, 𝑝 < .001). For the confidence shift, a Friedman test on the per-participant cell means likewise showed a significant difference between the four cells ( 𝜒 2 (3) = 88 .2, 𝑝 < .001, Kendall’s 𝑊 = 0.36). Holm-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank tests trace both Friedman results to the AI condition: the confirm and contradict cells differed within each voice (all 𝑝 < .001), whereas the two voices did not differ within either AI condition (all 𝑝 = .847 or larger).
