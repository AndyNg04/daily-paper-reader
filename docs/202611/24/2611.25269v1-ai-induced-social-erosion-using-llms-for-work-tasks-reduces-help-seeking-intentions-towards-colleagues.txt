Generative AI is becoming embedded in knowledge work as an intelligent collaborator to assist in completing tasks. However, this
readily available assistance risks displacing key interactions through which colleague relationships are built and sustained at work.
This paper leverages the concept of help-seeking behaviour to investigate whether and how AI usage shapes colleague relationships.
We conducted an online experimental vignette study where 248 knowledge workers evaluated scenarios that systematically varied
whether and how AI was used across different work tasks. Using linear mixed-effects models, we found that AI-enabled work reduced
help-seeking intentions compared to non-AI work across tasks, and especially for brainstorming. Visible AI usage was associated with
greater help-seeking intentions towards colleagues, and individual contributors’ colleague relationships were more vulnerable than
managers. Our findings identify opportunities for workplace AI tools, practices, and policies to preserve colleague relationships in
increasingly AI-integrated workplaces.

Introduction
In 1986, Kelley and Caplan [69] sought to understand how Bell Labs, then one of the world’s most innovative research
laboratories, produced so many high-performing knowledge workers. Contrary to expectations that star performers
possessed greater cognitive capabilities, they instead found that these workers proactively built networks of colleagues
through which they exchanged ideas, sought help, shared expertise, and supported one another. Decades of subsequent
research have reinforced the importance of such strong colleague relationships at work, linking them to better job
performance [21], creativity [13, 101], job satisfaction [3, 109, 119], and overall well-being [17, 29].

Generative AI1 is becoming embedded in how the modern knowledge worker accomplishes their work tasks. As of
2026, Gallup reported that 30% of U.S. employees use AI in their role a few times a week or more [63]. While the potential
of such tools in the workplace has been recognized by many: they can help workers become more efficient [93, 96],
produce more [15, 35], and access information or assistance on demand [36]. At the same time that AI is increasingly
being embedded into work, workplaces continue to face a “loneliness epidemic” [43, 44, 87]. This raises important
questions about whether AI usage could have unintentional consequences related to workers’ relationships with their
colleagues, including both their sense of belonging and connection as well as their social capital [91]. By providing an
always accessible and non-judgmental source of help, AI may reduce workers’ reliance on their colleagues and displace
regular social interactions by which relationships are built [8]. Although exchanging help between colleagues at work
can be a slow, effortful, and vulnerable process [8, 73] these behaviours are characteristic of stronger, more trusting, and
psychologically safe teams [40]. However, little empirical research has directly examined whether using AI, compared
with completing work tasks without it, shapes relationships among colleagues.
In addition, AI usage is highly heterogeneous across individuals and organizations, with shared norms and best
practices remaining underdeveloped [5, 10] — meaning that the social effects of AI usage are unlikely to be uniform.
For example, using LLMs to address routine questions may reduce unnecessary interruptions and mitigate collaborative
overload [30], potentially preserving colleague relationships. Conversely, using AI in a manner that leads to passing on
“workslop” — poorly-vetted AI-generated output that requires significant additional effort from colleagues to interpret
or correct — may create frustration and undermine trust [75, 76]. These examples suggest that AI’s social consequences
among colleagues may depend on how it is being used at work, yet it remains unclear which aspects of AI use might
protect or strain colleague relationships.
Taken together, our overarching research question is: How does knowledge workers’ use of AI shape their
working relationships with their colleagues? We further examine two sub-questions:
• RQ1: How does the presence of AI-enabled work, compared to non-AI work, affect colleague relationships
across different work tasks?
• RQ2: How does the way AI is being used for work tasks relate to the quality of colleague relationships?
To answer these questions, we conducted an experimental vignette study [2] to systematically evaluate how different
factors around AI usage influence colleague relationships. We operationalized colleague relationships through help-
seeking behaviour, inspired by the foundational Team-Member Exchange, a measure of worker relationship quality [107].
On a custom-designed online portal, participants evaluated a set of vignettes around AI usage for work, before answering
survey questions on their subsequent help-seeking intentions towards their colleagues. We used linear mixed-effects
models to analyze 2444 vignette evaluations from 248 knowledge worker participants, finding that AI-enabled work
does lead to weaker help-seeking intentions and colleague connection, especially in brainstorming tasks. In AI-enabled
work, using LLMs in a shared workspace that is visible to colleagues protects against reductions in help-seeking.
We discuss how AI-enabled work might shift the cost-benefit calculus of help-seeking, the potential pitfalls of
simply making all AI use visible across teams, and present several organizational opportunities to facilitate appropriate
help-seeking even in an AI-embedded workspace. We make an empirical contribution: experimentally demonstrating
that AI-enabled work erodes behaviours that sustain colleague relationships (RQ1), and that these relational effects vary
primarily by what task AI is being used for and the visibility of AI use (RQ2). Overall, our paper informs efforts to design

Background and Related Work
We begin by reviewing help-seeking at work and describing how workers decide whether to seek help from their
colleagues. Next, we examine how technologies, such as generative AI, have shaped help-seeking at work. However,
with the limited and emerging research on AI and workplace help-seeking specifically, we round out our review by
considering prior literature on how AI has shaped social connections more broadly, including outside of the workplace.
2.1
What is Help-Seeking at Work?
Social connections at work are critical to both organizational success and worker flourishing. Strong colleague relation-
ships are associated with greater job performance, satisfaction, and personal well-being [3, 21, 29]. Such relationships
also offer resources that buffer workplace stress and contribute to psychological safety [26, 40, 65]. In this study, we
hone in on help-seeking as one such activity through which colleague connections can be developed over time [72, 115].
Help-seeking at work has been defined as an interpersonal, proactive, and discretionary process by which workers
approach colleagues to help address and solve work-related problems [6, 18, 72, 89]. It has been associated with a range
of positive individual and organizational outcomes, including improved job performance [49] and creativity [85, 117],
and is closely intertwined with workplace trust and supportive colleague relationships [42, 115]. Furthermore, the
exchange of help, information, and feedback is considered a key component of the aforementioned Team-Member
Exchange (TMX) [107, 108], a foundational construct in the organizational behaviour literature that measures the
quality of the working relationship between a worker and their teammates. However, seeking help, especially when
done in an overly dependent and reliant manner [6, 88], can create demands on colleagues’ time, attention, and energy,
and even a sense of indebtedness [52, 56, 124]. These demands can reflect concerns around “collaborative overload”
[30, 70], in which collaboration and helping demands lead to employee exhaustion and overwork. On the other hand,
routine everyday exchanges of help are also an important means and social touchpoint by which workers can build and
sustain colleague connections.
When deciding whether or not to seek help from their colleagues, workers face what has been coined as the help-
seeker’s dilemma [25, 89]: help-seekers must weigh the potential benefits of receiving help against the psychological
and social costs of asking for it. Costs particularly centre around self-presentation [50], as seeking help requires
acknowledging a need or inability to independently resolve a problem [18, 72]. When doing so, workers may risk
appearing less competent to their peers or supervisors, with these self-presentation concerns further complicated by
status and power dynamics [47]. In an integrative review of workplace help-seeking, Chen et al. [18] synthesized several
antecedents that shape help-seeking decision-making: characteristics of the help-seeker, the target helper, the task
itself, leadership and organizational culture, and broader environmental and temporal factors. Formal helping roles can
signal a potential target helper’s expertise and accessibility, which influences who workers approach for help [57, 115].
In addition, Cleavenger et al. [24] found that helping norms shaped help-seeking behaviours more strongly than task
characteristics or individual traits such as self-esteem. However, much of the foundational research was conducted in
face-to-face settings. In modern workplaces, technologies, including generative AI, are increasingly integrated into
both how work is accomplished and how workers communicate and collaborate with one another, potentially shaping
the costs and benefits workers navigate in making help-seeking decisions.

2.2
How Technology Has Shaped Help-Seeking at Work
Workplace technologies have long shaped how employees access information, communicate, and collaborate with one
another. Email, enterprise social media, and workplace messaging platforms have made colleagues easier to reach even
across different work arrangements, including hybrid and remote work, potentially reducing some of the practical costs
of help-seeking [6]. At the same time, technologies that provide easier access to broader information and organizational
knowledge may also reduce the need to seek help from colleagues in the first place [41, 94, 111]. Yet, empirical evidence
on technology’s effects on workplace help-seeking remains limited, with one early study finding that greater anonymity
encouraged employees to seek help more quickly [25], consistent with evidence from non-work contexts around
lowering help-seeking and disclosure-related barriers, such as social media [81] and mental health support systems [22]
— empirically supporting the impact of lowering social costs on facilitating help-seeking.
Generative AI makes the effects of technology on help-seeking especially salient as it may further alter both workers’
for assistance and the costs of seeking it. LLMs can provide a highly flexible and interactive form of help by synthesizing
knowledge, generating tailored responses, and offering task-specific guidance [15]. These capabilities may expand
what workers can accomplish independently and thereby reduce their need for additional assistance [36]. Moreover,
privately seeking help from AI chatbots neither requires exposing one’s own inability to another person nor does it
incur reciprocity expectations [90], potentially reducing the self-presentation costs that are key to the help-seeker’s
dilemma [89]. Consistent with this possibility, one recent study by Zhai and Qu [123] found that seeking help from AI
rather than human colleagues affords workers greater autonomy and reduces feelings of indebtedness, leading them
to seek more help from AI in the future. Implications of using AI for work assistance may thus extend beyond the
immediate helping exchange.
Beyond being an alternative source of assistance, AI usage could also lead to downstream effects — impacting workers’
subsequent help-seeking behaviour and sense of connection with their colleagues. This is especially relevant as AI
is increasingly embedded [15], and sometimes even organizationally mandated [9, 103], in how knowledge workers
execute daily work tasks. Yu et al. [122] surveyed service employees in China — although not knowledge workers,
they found that greater employee-AI collaboration predicted reduced help-seeking as a result of employees’ increased
psychological availability, or their perceived sufficiency of resources to carry out their work [67]. Emerging evidence
from educational settings also suggests that access to generative AI can reduce students’ subsequent help-seeking from
peers or instructors [58, 59]. Although these studies were conducted outside knowledge work settings, their findings
suggest that similar mechanisms could shape help-seeking in such settings.
Not only are the causal effects of working with AI on help-seeking unclear, but existing research has also largely
treated AI use as a binary condition — whether it is being used or not — but not how AI is being used, despite known
substantial variation in workers’ AI usage [5, 10] and widespread gaps in organizational guidelines around AI [1, 125]. In
our paper, we experimentally examine both whether working with AI shapes subsequent help-seeking when compared
to completing work without AI (RQ1), and whether this effect depends on key characteristics of how AI is being used
(RQ2). In doing so, we offer a more detailed account of when and how using AI for work impacts help-seeking, and,
consequently, workers’ colleague relationships.
2.3
AI and Social Connections
Much of the research on AI and social connections has been conducted outside the workplace, particularly in the context
of therapy and companion chatbots [4, 83, 110, 113]. Although these chatbots can provide immediate, non-judgmental

support and reduce loneliness in the short term [34, 83], their use has also been associated with greater dependence
and reduced human interaction [71, 121], especially exacerbated among individuals with social anxiety or smaller
social networks [60, 78]. Sycophantic AI may further undermine human connection by reducing willingness to repair
interpersonal conflicts [20, 62], and may encourage users to depend on frictionless AI interactions instead of more
socially demanding, but meaningful human relationships. Altogether, prior work suggests that AI may offer immediate
social support while simultaneously weakening long-term human connection.
Emerging workplace research around AI’s broader social implications appears to be similarly mixed: frequent
work-related AI use has been associated with employee loneliness and poorer well-being, including insomnia and
increased alcohol consumption [114]. Yet, the same research found that AI use heightened employees’ need for social
affiliation, with prompted greater helping towards colleagues [114]. Moreover, loneliness may encourage help-seeking in
workplace cultures characterized by high meaningfulness [74]. Taken together, AI use may heighten workers’ awareness
of unmet social needs and motivate compensatory behaviours, including seeking or providing help. Another growing
set of research focuses on how employees who use AI are being perceived by others. Academic studies and industry
reports suggest that workers who use AI — or disclose doing so — may be perceived as lazier [37, 102], less competent
[92], less trustworthy [106], and less desirable to work with [98, 118]. For example, Atlassian found that employees
were less willing to recommend a colleague for a project when that colleague disclosed using AI even when the quality
of their work is held constant, while also finding that organizational culture surrounding AI could mediate this social
penalty [37]. Thus, it is fairly established that workers generally exhibit negative perceptions of colleagues who use or
disclose using AI.
In our paper, we flip the research emphasis from how colleagues perceive AI-enabled workers to how these workers
behave toward their colleagues. Although perceptions can influence behaviour, limited research examines the social
behaviours that follow AI use in the workplace. Studying help-seeking behaviour provides us a more direct account of
potential social consequences as colleague relationships are shaped by workers’ decisions to interact, coordinate, and
engage — or not — with one another.
3
Methodology
We adopted the experimental vignette methodology [2] to enable us to causally establish how a worker’s AI usage for
work tasks might shape their subsequent intended help-seeking behaviour, and, consequently, their sense of connection,
towards their colleagues. Vignette studies have a long history of utilization in management literature [12, 39, 53],
including for help-seeking behaviours specifically [79, 116], and has also been successfully executed in workplace
HCI [16, 32, 104]. This methodology presents participants with carefully crafted, realistic, hypothetical scenarios (i.e.,
vignettes) that encapsulate various factors of interest, for participants to assess based on their behaviours, intentions,
and attitudes. The work context is especially suited to such an approach given the complexities of contemporary
workplaces, which can make controlled field experiments infeasible and prohibitively expensive. Vignettes also enable
researchers to examine plausible near-future technologies, including various conditions of AI use, before they are
widely deployed, by embedding hypothetical systems in recognizable and realistic work scenarios [16, 80].
3.1
Hypotheses
We developed five confirmatory hypotheses (H1—H5) that map to each of our key factors of interest around AI
usage, and how they impact colleague relationships. We start with ⋆AI Presence (H1), which distinguishes between
⋆AI-enabled and ⋆Non-AI vignettes to help us investigate the relational effects of using AI on work tasks. We then

■Visibility (H5).
To operationalize colleague relationships for our hypotheses, we formulated two outcome variables, Help-Seeking
and Colleague Connection, which were inspired and informed by the foundational Team-Member Exchange (TMX)
construct [19, 107, 108], designed to measure the quality of the working relationship between an individual and their
other team members. Seers’ [107] original TMX instrument included dimensions such as Exchange, which assesses
the extent to which team members give and receive feedback, ideas, knowledge, or assistance, as well as Cohesiveness,
which concerns members’ sense of connection or belonging to the team. We were loosely inspired by the dimensions
of Exchange and Cohesiveness to develop Help-Seeking and Colleague Connection, respectively. Help-Seeking
captures workers’ help-seeking intentions, and was conceptually included to represent a more proximal outcome.
Colleague Connection was included to capture a broader and more distal social outcome, concerning workers’
comfort, openness, and willingness to collaborate with a target colleague.
3.1.1
Factor Selection. We prioritized and selected factors based on if they (1) concerned AI usage decisions over which
individual workers or organizations have meaningful agency over, therefore having implications for practice or policy,
and (2) reflected relevant forms of AI usage in the current and emerging workplace, bolstered by relevant literature.
These factors were not intended to form a comprehensive taxonomy of workplace AI use. Other potentially important
aspects of AI usage fell outside this scope. For example, AI tone — while relevant to research around sycophantic AI
[20] — was deliberately excluded, as default conversational behaviour is largely shaped by model providers. While
users do have some level of agency indirectly through settings or prompting, we consider the range of AI prompting
strategies to be fine-grained interaction behaviours rather than a broader factor of workplace AI usage.
3.1.2
⋆AI Presence. We first examine whether ⋆AI-enabled work affects colleague relationships relative to
⋆Non-AI work, providing a baseline for evaluating the consequences on colleague relationships when introducing AI.
Prior research has often contrasted seeking help from AI with seeking help from other people [123]. We instead
examine whether using AI to complete a task at work affects workers’ subsequent help-seeking intentions towards their
colleagues. Workers who use AI may feel less need to seek human assistance as AI could feel sufficient to address the
problem at hand. Being readily available, AI also entails fewer social and psychological costs than approaching a colleague.
This could reduce interaction opportunities and, consequently, colleague connection. We therefore hypothesize:
• H1a: ⋆AI-enabled work is associated with lower Help-Seeking than ⋆Non-AI work.
• H1b: ⋆AI-enabled work is associated with lower Colleague Connection than ⋆Non-AI work.
3.1.3
• Task Type. The value of colleague assistance may depend on the task being performed. We developed four
task types — • Assurance, • Brainstorming, • Proofing, and • Search — that represent common workplace needs and
can be completed with and without AI support. McGrath’s circumplex model of group tasks, which organizes tasks
around generating, choosing, negotiating, and executing activities [82, 97], as well as research on social support that
distinguishes informational, instrumental, emotional, and esteem support [31, 105], informed our development of the
following task types.
• Assurance involves seeking emotional or esteem support that validates the worker’s perspective surrounding a
task. • Brainstorming is a creativity task involving the generation of ideas. • Proofing involves seeking feedback on
the work task itself rather than validation of the worker’s perspective. Finally, • Search involves acquiring information
relevant to the task and is a form of informational social support. AI tools may be able to more sufficiently address tasks

like • Proofing, and in particular, • Search, that are more objective and well-defined, potentially reducing the need
for subsequent help from colleagues. On the other hand, • Assurance tasks may be perceived by workers to uniquely
require a human touch. We thus expect task types to produce different levels of subsequent help-seeking and colleague
connection, leading to our hypotheses:
• H2a: • Task Type is associated with Help-Seeking.
• H2b: • Task Type is associated with Colleague Connection.
3.1.4
♦AI Role. Help-seeking research distinguishes between instrumental help, where assistance focuses on offering
ways to help solve problems, and executive help, which provides the solution itself [48, 68]. Organizational research also
draws a similar distinction between autonomous and dependent forms of help-seeking [88]. We apply this distinction
to the role workers have AI play in task completion. When AI is used in an ♦Assisting manner (akin to instrumental
or autonomous help), workers use AI in a supportive manner where they ultimately apply their own judgment and
reasoning to the task. When AI is used in a ♦Delegating manner (akin to executive or dependent help), workers use
AI to complete the task, offloading responsibility to the AI. Although prior research has examined how these different
approaches affect productivity or learning outcomes [46, 49], we examine their consequences on colleague relationships.
For example, using AI in a ♦Delegating manner may make workers’ feel like further human input is less necessary
as AI has already produced a solution that looks polished and complete, reducing subsequent help-seeking. In addition,
the social risks of delegation may be relevant to aforementioned concerns about “workslop” [75], as evidence has shown
that this kind of output can harm recipient’s perceptions of the sender. We therefore hypothesize:
• H3a: ♦AI Role is associated with Help-Seeking.
• H3b: ♦AI Role is associated with Colleague Connection.
3.1.5
▲AI Specialization. AI tools can vary in the knowledge available to them. Here, we compare ▲OpenAssist,
which draws primarily on generic knowledge and is meant to represent an off-the-shelf, but state-of-the-art AI
chatbot like ChatGPT — to ▲CorpAssist, which builds on ▲OpenAssist but also incorporates organization-specific
information (e.g., internal documents, past projects, team files, team communication) and tailors its responses accordingly.
Organizations are increasingly developing specialized AI tools such as ▲CorpAssist to improve output relevance,
leverage proprietary unstructured data, and protect sensitive information. A more specialized AI system may shape
social behaviour as ▲CorpAssist could be perceived as being able to provide information that workers might have
otherwise only been able to obtain from colleagues. However, workers may also view ▲CorpAssist, despite having
access to organization-specific information, as insufficiently capable of capturing the tacit and contextual knowledge
that is held by human colleagues. Examining AI specialization therefore reveals whether access to formal organizational
knowledge is sufficient to displace colleague interactions. We hypothesize:
• H4a: ▲AI Specialization is associated with Help-Seeking.
• H4b: ▲AI Specialization is associated with Colleague Connection.
3.1.6
■AI Visibility. A key element of the help-seeker’s dilemma is the threat to self-esteem and self-presentation
concerns. Because interactions with AI are typically private, workers can seek assistance without exposing their own
uncertainties, incurring indebtedness, or risking judgment from colleagues [89, 123]. However, AI interactions are
increasingly being embedded into shared workplace platforms (e.g., Claude Tag2) where conversations are both visible

Experimental Design
As seen in Figure 1, we conducted a within-subjects factorial vignette experiment [2] in which each participant evaluated
a subset, or deck, of the complete set of 36 vignettes: 32 ⋆AI-enabled conditions (4 levels of • Task Type × 2 of
♦Role × 2 of ▲Specialization × 2 of ■Visibility) and 4 ⋆Non-AI (1 per each level of • Task Type) conditions.
To limit participant fatigue, each participant evaluated 10 vignettes: eight drawn from the ⋆AI-enabled conditions and
two from the ⋆Non-AI conditions. Vignettes were assigned from a pre-computed counterbalanced schedule covering

the 32-condition full factorial (eight AI vignettes per participant), so that each condition received approximately
equal representation across the full sample. This design enabled within-participant comparisons of ⋆AI-enabled and
⋆Non-AI scenarios (RQ1) and of different configurations of AI usage (RQ2), while distributing coverage of the full

3.2.1
Designing Vignettes. We developed our vignettes iteratively to ensure that each clearly communicated the relevant
factors, were grounded in an organizational context and realistic, and presented a situation in which either seeking or
not seeking subsequent help were both plausible options. Each ⋆AI-enabled vignette followed a consistent sequence:
it first described • Task Type, followed by ▲Specialization and ■Visibility, and concluded with ♦Role. For
♦Role, the description of how AI was used (either ♦Assisting and ♦Delegating) was tailored to each of the specific
• Task Type. Each ⋆Non-AI vignette similarly combined the description of • Task Type with text specific to each
task explaining how “you” carried it out without AI assistance. Appendix Table 4 presents the complete set of textual
components used to construct the vignettes at each factor level. Each vignette also included visual iconography and
highlighting to direct participants’ attention to key information and help them recognize differences across vignette
evaluations. The icons are reproduced in Appendix Figure 5.
Before viewing the experimental vignettes, participants read a background vignette intended to establish and immerse
participants into the broader organizational scenario. Here, we provided information about the organization, “your” role,
the kinds of tasks “you” performed, “your” team and colleagues, and the organization’s general climate of collaboration.
To hold the nature of “your” relationship to “your” colleagues constant, we described the colleagues as peer workers
(e.g., held similar context about the team’s work and held similar tenure) with whom “you” have established professional
working relationships. The background vignette also introduced the organization’s AI tool, Assist, including its two
versions: ▲OpenAssist, a general-purpose version, and ▲CorpAssist, a company-specific version. It further explained
the distinction between using Assist in a ■Personal versus a ■Shared manner. Visuals (see Appendix Figures 6
and 7) were designed and partially generated with ChatGPT to illustrate the ▲Specialization and ■Visibility
factors, particularly for participants who may not have personally encountered comparable AI tools or modes of use.
3.2.2
Procedure. After providing informed consent, participants completed a brief orientation on the online custom
vignette portal (see Figure 2 for a screenshot of a vignette being evaluated) which presented the workplace background
across multiple illustrated pages, with comprehension checks inserted between each page. When evaluating vignettes,
participants were reminded to keep real-world workplace constraints in mind — such as an imminent deadline (e.g., the
tasks in the vignettes centred around a presentation due the same day), professional relationships, and what would be
practically feasible. Attention checks were inserted at randomly selected positions among response items.
Upon evaluating all ten vignettes in the portal, participants were redirected to complete the post-study survey in
Qualtrics, which collected additional demographic information and administered various validated scales to include
as covariates in our analyses, as we will discuss in Section 3.5. To contextualize why certain vignettes increased or
decreased help-seeking intentions, participants also responded to two open-ended questions about the vignettes they
just evaluated: “In the scenarios you just read, think of one scenario in which you were {MORE/LESS} likely to seek help
from your colleague. Please explain your rationale in a few sentences.”

added • Assurance as a new level of • Task Type. After our expert evaluations, we then also conducted three waves of
pilot testing (𝑛= 24 total) to ensure the robustness of data collection through our study portal.
3.3
Participants
We recruited participants via Prolific, filtering for English-fluent, full-time workers from the United States with a 95%+
study approval rate. Participants were paid $10 USD for completing the study, which took a median of 20 minutes. The
study was approved by the authors’ ethics review board. Participants first completed an eligibility survey on Qualtrics
that would then redirect them to our online vignette portal if found to be eligible. In addition to Prolific’s prescreening
filters, eligible participants needed to be knowledge workers who (1) used AI tools at work a few times a week or more,
(2) did not have a lack of trust in AI tools’ reliability, safety, and accuracy, (3) did not have supervisory responsibilities
for more than one individual, and (4) collaborated with at least two or more colleagues on a typical work day. In other
words, we wanted knowledge workers who could resonate most strongly with the workers in our vignettes — who

often collaborated with their colleagues, was comfortable with using AI for their work, and were mostly engaged in
executing tasks rather than supervisory duties.
Before finalizing recruitment, we conducted a power analysis for our planned linear mixed-effects models using
the simr package in R [51]. We specified a significance level of 𝛼= 0.05 and a target effect size of 𝑑= 0.25, which
we converted to a fixed-effect coefficient (𝑏= 0.182) by multiplying the residual standard deviation (𝜎𝑝𝑖𝑙𝑜𝑡= 0.729)
estimated from pilot data with the effect size. For a more conservative estimate, we based our simulation on the analyses

200 participants (or 1600 ⋆AI-enabled vignette evaluations, eight per participant) would provide at least 90% power to
detect the target effect.
In total, 248 knowledge worker participants completed our study (see Appendix Table 3 for full demographics
overview), resulting in 2444 vignette evaluations (1969 ⋆AI-enabled and 475 non-AI vignette evaluations3). These
totals reflect the exclusion of 16 vignette evaluations (15 ⋆AI-enabled, 1 ⋆Non-AI) completed in less than 10 seconds, a
threshold corresponding approximately to the first percentile of evaluation times and deemed unlikely to reflect sufficient
engagement with the vignette. The median evaluation time was 37 seconds, with 90% of the evaluations completed
within approximately 89 seconds. Most participants worked as analysts/researchers (44%), with others in software
development (17%), project management (15%), or writing/content creation (11%). Participants generally worked in
larger, enterprise-sized organizations, and more than 70% of our participants reported working in organizations that
provided AI training, as well as encouraged AI use.
3.4
Measuring Help-Seeking and Colleague Connection
To generate questions for measuring Help-Seeking and Colleague Connection, we first looked at TMX’s [107] Ex-
change and Cohesiveness dimensions for inspiration. Because the established TMX scale measures ongoing relationships
within existing teams, they could not be applied directly to our vignette study — questions needed to be connected to
the work task described in each vignette. As an example, the Help-Seeking items were inspired by TMX questions
concerning the exchange of feedback and assistance, such as “In busy situations, how often do other team members ask
you to help out?” and “Do other members of your team usually let you know when you do something that makes their jobs
easier (or harder)?” We translated these concepts into three questions around participants’ intended behaviour around
seeking help from their target helper colleague — in this case, Sam4:
• Q1: You would value Sam’s feedback on this task.
• Q2: You would seek Sam’s feedback on this task.
• Q3: You would actively incorporate Sam’s feedback on this task.
Together, Q1—3 capture aspects of participants’ anticipated engagement in the help-seeking process — valuing,
seeking, and finally incorporating feedback. Similar to Help-Seeking, Q4—6 were inspired by elements of trust and
connection in the Cohesiveness dimension of the original TMX instrument, as well as team psychological safety [40].
They also needed to be adapted to be centred around the task in our vignette study:
• Q4: You would feel comfortable with involving Sam in this task.
• Q5: You would express frustrations with the task to Sam.
• Q6: You would rather work on this task without Sam. (reverse-coded)

4.1.1
H1: AI-Enabled Work Leads to Weaker Colleague Relationships. With ⋆AI-enabled vignettes as a reference, we

able to complete most of the task for me. There was no additional help needed from my colleague. The AI provided enough
support.” In other words, if the nature of the task led participants to perceive AI as being capable of providing sufficient
assistance, this could reduce their perceived need to consult colleagues, thereby reducing Help-Seeking.

H5: Shared AI Workspaces Are Associated With Stronger Colleague Relationships. ■Visibility had the strongest
significant main effect out of the AI usage factors across both colleague relationship outcomes. Compared to using AI

with AI in a workspace that is visible to their colleagues, they report greater help-seeking intentions towards their
colleagues along with stronger connection to them than when the same AI interaction occurs privately. Notably, private
AI interactions remain the predominant mode through which workers interact with AI tools in many organizations.
Participants described how the ■shared workspace reduced both the practical and social costs of seeking help
through several pathways. With a ■shared workspace providing colleagues access to relevant context, participants felt
like they could ask for help “without me having to explain it from scratch.” It also made prior effort visible, making them

disclosing AI use because “...they already know that I’m using AI.” By contrast, seeking help when using AI ■personally

about burdening the helper, a well-established psychological cost of help-seeking, as well as a sense that using AI
personally made the work process feel more private and costly to subsequently share with a colleague. Together, these
quotes suggest plausible mechanisms through which a shared AI workspace may lower the psychological costs of
seeking help.

5
Discussion
We show that AI use reduces workers’ intentions to seek help from and feel connected to their colleagues, though
the magnitude of this effect varies by task type and is greatest for brainstorming (RQ1). Shared AI workspaces may
mitigate this decline, particularly relative to conventional, individual AI use (RQ2). Considered more broadly, our
findings have implications for social well-being at work. Work is an important source of meaning and social connection,
and relationships with colleagues can support worker well-being and buffer against stress [26, 65]. Yet, one in five
workers experience loneliness at work [64], and all the while AI is becoming increasingly embedded in workplaces. As
our findings suggest that AI use may continue to erode colleague relationships, it is important to consider how the
human relationships that support individual and organizational flourishing can be preserved.
This may require moving beyond a narrow, efficiency-centric view of work, in which help-seeking may appear
increasingly unnecessary as AI becomes more and more capable. Instead, more holistic perspectives of work [54] could

help us reframe help-seeking in the age of AI not solely as a means of completing tasks or improving productivity, but
as a relational practice through which social capital [91] is fostered and sustained. With appropriate practices, policies,
norms, and continued attention to AI design, reduced help-seeking need not necessarily lead to detrimental social
consequences if human help becomes more focused on situations where colleagues offer distinct value. We adopt this
broader perspective in the following sections as we interpret our findings and consider their implications. Specifically,
we discuss how AI may shift the expected perceived benefits of human assistance, introduce new self-presentation
costs, and complicate when and where human help is still needed. We conclude by reflecting on the value of shared AI
workspaces, including their potential drawbacks.
5.1
Shifting Expectations of the Benefits of Human Assistance
One plausible explanation of our observed help-seeking erosion (§ 4.1.1) was that AI reduced participants’ perceived
need for human help, thereby changing the relative benefit of turning to a colleague. Yet reduced need may be only one
way that AI impacts relative benefit. We speculate on how other qualities of AI use at work might further shift the
perceived benefits of seeking help from human colleagues.
“That’s a great question!” Many of us have encountered similarly enthusiastic responses from AI, even when the
question asked might have been ill-formed or simply mundane. AI chatbots are always capable of providing a polished,
pleasant response, no matter how you prompt it for help. Over time, our expectations around receiving help from
other human colleagues may plausibly shift as a result of our exposure to AI, and perhaps especially with sycophantic
AI, which has already been shown to reduce prosocial intentions [20]. We may come to expect the same kind of
agreeableness from our colleagues as we have become so used to with AI. As AI — the ultimate yes-man [86] — will
almost always attempt to cobble together an answer, we may even become dissatisfied when a human helper admits, “I
don’t know” — viewing this as a shortcoming of our colleague, rather than a possible feature: a signal that the question
requires additional resources, clarification, or deeper consideration. We may even come to expect the responsibility for
understanding our help-seeking requests to lie with the target helper, rather than with us — especially since AI will,
of course, answer any question we ask it, no matter how many grammatical errors, typos, or ambiguities it contains.
Future work could explore the plausibility of such a mechanism, focusing specifically on worker expectations of their
colleagues, and how it might shape subsequent help-seeking. Finally, as AI output quality continues to improve, the
relative benefit of seeking help from a human colleague may further diminish, potentially reducing help-seeking beyond
what we observed in our study and making this an ongoing challenge with evolving AI capabilities.
5.2
AI Adds New Self-Presentation Costs when Seeking Help
The costs associated with seeking help traditionally were psychological and social, and revolved around self-presentation
— our human desire to present ourselves favourably to others. In contemporary workplaces, where norms and attitudes
around AI use still vary considerably across individuals and organizational contexts, AI may introduce new self-
presentation costs to help-seeking, particularly around workers’ desire to appear competent to their colleagues. Our
open-ended responses (§ 4.2.3) suggest that one possible explanation may be the discomfort associated with disclosing
AI use when seeking help, which may compound the existing self-presentation costs of admitting the need for help in
the first place [6]. Shame, fear, and avoidance around disclosing AI have been documented in prior work [7, 61, 84],
particularly as disclosure can lead to reduced trust and other negative social evaluations [102, 106]. Workers may
therefore expend additional effort in masking AI-assisted work on a task before feeling comfortable with asking for help,


20
Chow et al.
especially as AI use raises questions about control and ownership over the work [66]. In this way, AI may introduce an
additional layer of self-presentation costs to the already socially-laden help-seeker’s dilemma.
Another plausible explanation is that workers desire to signal competence, independence, and effort (§ 4.2.3) to
their colleagues [6, 14], but AI may complicate how workers now approach signalling these qualities. In illuminating
why shared AI workspaces had higher help-seeking, open-ended responses suggested that having the AI conversation
be visible acts as a signal of the help-seeker’s effort in attempting to resolve the problem on their own first. Yet, the
utility of AI may change what an individual believes they should be able to accomplish on their own. When web search
became popular, Let Me Google That For You6, emerged out of a frustration that people were not being resourceful but
needlessly bothering others. AI drastically expands the range of what an individual could (or perceives they could)
accomplish on their own7 — and, as a result, may decrease human help-seeking out of a desire to be seen as competent.
An opportunity for organizational leaders is to reinforce that “AI could have answered that” is not, in itself, a reason
to avoid seeking help from colleagues, while remaining mindful of collaborative overload [30] — striking a balance
between independence and interdependence. The challenge may then lie in understanding when a human touchpoint is
required, which we only begin to touch on next.
5.3
The AI-Enabled Help-Seeker’s Dilemma: Discerning When Human Help Is Still Needed
The preceding sections discuss mechanisms by which AI-enabled work reshapes both the perceived benefits and costs
of seeking help from colleagues, central components of the classic help-seeker’s dilemma [89]. Revisiting and extending
this dilemma in light of AI-embedded knowledge work, we posit the AI-enabled help-seeker’s dilemma, which offers a
new challenge around discerning when additional human help is still needed, particularly when AI may seem to have
already resolved the need for help. This is best illustrated by one of our key findings: AI decreased help-seeking most for
brainstorming tasks (§ 4.1.2). Brainstorming is distinct from more objective, information-seeking tasks because it can be
difficult to judge whether an AI-generated response is sufficient [76]. The polish of AI output may pose additional risks
for novices, who may not yet have developed the ability to distinguish fluency from depth. AI-generated ideas may
appear complete while flattening information [38], lacking the divergent and unexpected contributions that human
colleagues can bring. Yet, this does not preclude a role for AI in augmenting human-human creativity, with prior HCI
work demonstrating its potential to act as a “social glue” [112]. This points to a design opportunity for AI tools that
scaffold brainstorming without displacing human help-seeking, instead creating opportunities to bring colleagues into
the creative process and combine both AI support with the divergent perspectives that colleagues can provide.
Our findings also suggest that tasks involving validation and perspective (categorized as • Assurance in the study,
§ 4.1.2) may more clearly benefit from colleagues’ tacit and organizational knowledge, which AI may not be able
to fully capture. By contrast, for other tasks, sufficiently convincing AI output might obscure what is lost by not
involving human others. Rather than seeking to preserve help-seeking uniformly across task types, there may be value
in redistributing help-seeking, or perhaps framed more broadly, support interactions, towards situations where people
offer distinctive value — with the human-human connection considered meaningful especially in AI-embedded work
[100]. This might mean a shift away from instrumental assistance towards emphasizing mentorship, perspective, and
human connection.
Altogether, a key implication for organizations is to move beyond broad AI training towards task-specific guidance
about when human input remains valuable, and how to ask for help in an AI-embedded workplace. For example, teams

5.4
Shared AI Workspaces: A Double-Edged Sword?
When considering how AI tools for work might be designed and used to preserve colleague relationships, one of our
key findings is that shared, visible AI use may help sustain human help-seeking (§ 4.2.3). The visibility of a shared
AI workspace could make work easier to discuss with colleagues, creating opportunities for others to contribute or
intervene and lowering the cost of help-seeking. It is worth acknowledging, however, that a shared AI workspace, as
described in our vignettes, bypasses the psychological cost of admitting that you need help by making the problem
and task already visible to colleagues. Future work could investigate workers’ willingness to engage with shared AI
workplaces in the first place, and how human-AI collaboration evolves in this emerging form of multi-user collaboration.
Yet, the ease of sharing in visible AI workspaces may also come at a cost. After a lengthy back-and-forth with AI about
a task, in such workspaces, a help-seeker can easily involve a colleague by tagging them into the conversation. However,
this shifts much of the cost of seeking help onto the prospective helper, who must sift through the conversation, identify
relevant context, before finally determining how to help. CSCW research has long emphasized the articulation work
involved in exchanging help [28]. Shared AI may bypass this articulation work by making it particularly easy for help-
seekers to offload that work solely onto colleague target helpers, which may simply result in another type of “workslop”
[75]. Naively introducing shared AI workspaces without appropriate guardrails may only produce collaborative overload
rather than meaningfully encouraging help-seeking and strengthening colleague work relationships. A possible design
opportunity could be for shared AI workspaces to help summarize relevant context and help workers formulate clear,
focused requests before involving a colleague — in other words, having AI assist us in becoming better help-seekers,
rather than solely focusing on having it solve our need for help in the first place. As an example, such a relational approach
to AI design could build upon emerging HCI work demonstrating that AI optimized and designed to account for
relationships between people can improve cooperation [23]. Working openly with AI may reduce the stigma associated
with speaking up and asking for help, especially with the aforementioned potential added costs when AI is being used.
Regardless, we argue that the extent to which these benefits can be realized do ultimately rest on psychological safety
and organizational culture — not simply on the introduction of a new technological system.
5.5
Limitations and Future Work
A common limitation of vignette studies is that they capture intended rather than actual behaviour, although much prior
work has supported their value as approximations of real-world decision-making [55]. We also used generic knowledge
work tasks in our vignettes to ensure evaluation validity across knowledge worker participants from various industries
and occupations, potentially overlooking domain-specific norms and constraints. Future studies could examine actual
help-seeking behaviour in simulated AI-enabled work environments, as well as target specialized groups of knowledge
workers — such as software developers — whose distinct help-seeking needs may be particularly impacted by AI.

We did not conduct mediation analyses because our research questions focused on whether — and under what factors

obligations. Future work could target cross-cultural replication.
6
Conclusion
Strong colleague relationships are key to individuals’ flourishing at work. The exchange of help is one way such
relationships are fostered. Yet, our experimental vignette study showed that in AI-enabled work, workers’ subsequent
help-seeking intentions decreased. This social erosion varied across different work tasks, but making AI usage visible
among colleagues may have helped retain help-seeking. As organizations continue to grapple with how to integrate AI
into the workplace while accounting for potential social consequences, our work empirically illuminates the extent
to which, and the conditions under which, AI use may weaken or preserve colleague relationships, while surfacing
opportunities for organizational action. In doing so, we optimistically envision an AI-enabled future of work that can
foster more focused and meaningful help-seeking around the kinds of support and relationship that is uniquely human.

• Assurance
You have been assigned to prepare a section of the presentation summarizing lessons learned from a
project that your team and peer colleagues at NextGen recently completed. As you work on the
section, you begin to second-guess whether your perspective on what happened during the project
is reasonable. Sam is working on another section of the same presentation.
• Brainstorming
You have been assigned to prepare a section of the presentation proposing ideas for new products or
features that NextGen could develop in response to recent industry trends. Sam is working on
another section of the same presentation.
• Proofing
You have been assigned to prepare a section of the presentation explaining the implications of
recent industry trends for NextGen. You have already prepared an initial draft of your section, but
think there may be ways to improve it. Sam is working on another section of the same presentation.
• Task Type
• Search
You have been assigned to prepare a section of the presentation where you need to search for recent
products launched by NextGen’s competitors. Sam is working on another section of the same
presentation.
• Assurance
For this task, you revisit the project materials and think through which takeaways are most
important. You use this review to consider which takeaways to include and continue preparing your
section of the presentation.
• Brainstorming
For this task, you brainstorm possible ideas for new products or features that NextGen could
develop. You consider the ideas you generate, consider which ones to develop, and continue
preparing your section of the presentation.
• Proofing
For this task, you review your initial draft, considering its clarity, organization, and completeness.
You identify potential revisions based on your review and continue preparing your section of the
presentation.
⋆Non-AI
• Search
For this task, you search company websites and other publicly available sources to identify recent
products launched by competing companies and other relevant competitors to examine. You review
the information you find and use it to continue preparing your section of the presentation.
• Assurance
You tell [OpenAssist/CorpAssist] that you are second-guessing your own perspective and ask it for
help to work through your uncertainty. It offers considerations and alternative perspectives to help
you think it through. You reflect on its response and decide for yourself whether your interpretation
is reasonable.
• Brainstorming
You ask [OpenAssist/CorpAssist] to generate possible ideas for new products or features that
NextGen could develop. You use its suggestions as a starting point, decide which ideas to develop,
and prepare your section of the presentation yourself.
• Proofing
You ask [OpenAssist/CorpAssist] to help review your draft and provide feedback on its clarity,
organization, and completeness. You review its feedback, decide which changes to make, and revise
your section of the presentation yourself accordingly.
