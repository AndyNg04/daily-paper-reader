# Digital Knockoffs 

Niuniu Zhang 

UCLA Anderson School of Management 

November 20, 2026 

Abstract 

Social simulations often use language model agents to represent people who differ on several measurable traits. However, describing a trait profile in a prompt does not guarantee the agent will express each trait at its intended level. We propose a method that calibrates agents to multidimensional profiles using activation steering: (i) extract steering directions for each trait, (ii) learn a shared mapping from steering strengths to measured trait scores, and (iii) search for a steering intervention for each target. We use Bayesian optimization for this search and evaluate the method on the Big Five personality scores of 500 OpenPsychometrics respondents. In each of five rounds, the assigned interventions yield lower mean absolute error across respondents than the prompting and steering baselines. Further, the model’s responses to a given intervention yield the same trait profile regardless of whose target motivated it, so a poor match for one respondent can be the best match for another. When we match each respondent to the closest profile found across all tested interventions, Bayesian optimization yields 16.6% lower mean absolute error than uniform random search at the same evaluation budget. Our method thus offers a way to build social agents whose measured traits dovetail with those of the people they seek to represent. Whether this closer fit translates to improved predictions of downstream behavior remains an open question. 

Keywords: large language models, agent calibration, Bayesian optimization, activation steering, social simulation.

1arXiv:2611.28482v1 [cs.CL] 21 Nov 2026 1 Introduction 

Social simulations ask how people with different traits behave and interact. Language models offer a flexible way to construct the agents in such simulations: a researcher can describe a person, assign a task, and observe the model’s response (Filippas et al., 2024). A difficulty arises before any interaction takes place: how closely does the resulting agent express the traits it was assigned? Personality makes this question concrete. A person who completes a Big Five questionnaire receives five scores for Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism (OCEAN). 1 An agent can answer the same items and receive the same type of score vector. Suppose the desired profile is (4 .5, 4.9, 3.5, 3.8, 4.6) on a 1 to 5 scale, in OCEAN order. A description in a prompt, like “organized and dependable,” suggests high conscientiousness but leaves open whether the intended score is 4.0 or 4.9. Listing the scores in the prompt specifies the target exactly, but the challenge is getting the agent’s own answers to yield scores close to all five values at once. How can we calibrate an LLM agent to such a profile with a limited budget of model evaluations? 

Activation steering supplies controls for this problem. It shifts internal representations during inference while leaving the model’s weights fixed (Turner et al., 2023). Concept directions extracted using existing methods provide a small set of controls in a high-dimensional model (Beaglehole et al., 2026). We extract directions for each OCEAN trait and set their steering strengths with five signed coefficients, β = ( βO, β C , β E , β A, β N ) ∈ R5.With the model, questionnaire prompt, and extracted directions held fixed, we learn a shared intervention–response map β 7 → y(β), where y(β) ∈ R5 gives the measured OCEAN scores. Given a person’s target profile yp, calibration seeks an intervention β whose measured response y(β)closely matches yp. A single control can affect several trait scores, so the controls must be chosen jointly.

We use Gaussian processes (GPs) to learn this shared map and Bayesian optimization (BO) to choose new doses. Every person retains an individual target and loss; the information used to choose interventions is shared. Our contribution fits within a broader effort to build empirical social agents. Manning and Horton (2026) combine theory-grounded instructions with human data to predict behavior in new games. Jackson et al. (2026) fit numerical type vectors expressed through prompts to observed human choices, then evaluate predictions in held-out games. Their type vector is an input fitted to explain behavior. Here, the human trait vector is an observed output target ; the fitted inputs are activation strengths that make the model’s questionnaire scores match it. Closely related work establishes numerical and multidimensional control. Fan et al. (2026) compare prompting, sparse-autoencoder features, and probe-based steering in economic and creative tasks. Their probe calibration tunes one steering strength to a scalar behavioral target. Hoppe et al. (2026) develop continuous personality controls that reduce interference between traits, evaluating simultaneous shifts toward selected high/low trait combinations. We ask a complementary question. How precisely can steering reproduce an individual’s numerical trait profile? We calibrate the model to the measured Big Five profiles of 500 OpenPsychometrics respondents (Open-Source Psychometrics Project, 2014). The GP predicts responses at unseen doses more accurately than the fitted linear map. In each of five passes, BO’s recommendations match their intended respondents more closely, on average, than descriptive profile prompting. When each respondent can select the closest response from the full intervention–response library, BO achieves 16.6% lower trait mean absolute error (MAE) across the five traits than uniform random search at the same evaluation budget. The results address three questions in turn: (i) Can we learn the response map (Section 3.1)? (ii) How well do recommended doses fit their intended targets (Section 3.2)? (iii) Does adaptive search build a more useful library than additional random sampling (Section 3.3)? Together they evaluate the fitting stage of social-agent construction. Whether the resulting calibration transfers to downstream behavior is a separate empirical question that this study leaves open. 

# 2 Methods and Experimental Design 

2.1 Human targets and five steering controls 

We randomly select a fixed cohort of 500 respondents from the OpenPsychometrics BIG5 dataset (Open-Source Psychometrics Project, 2014). Of its 19,719 records, 19,718 have complete responses to all 50 questionnaire items. Using observed profiles retains the combinations and correlations of traits present in these respondents, whereas synthetic profiles generated by independently sampling five scores would not. Respondent p’s five observed scores form the target vector yp ∈ [1 , 5] 5. All 3500 profiles are known to the optimization procedure. The instrument is the 50-item IPIP Big-Five Factor Markers questionnaire (International Personality Item Pool, n.d.), using the OpenPsychometrics wording and response scale. The International Personality Item Pool (IPIP) is the public-domain personality measurement resource described by Goldberg et al. (2006). Each OCEAN trait is assessed by ten questions, whose responses are scored and averaged. We report IPIP scores in OCEAN order to match our steering controls. Using common Big Five terminology (Soto and John, 2017), we label Intellect/Imagination as Openness and reverse the Emotional Stability scale so that higher scores indicate greater Neuroticism. Appendix E supplies the items, response scale, and scoring key. We use Llama 3.1 8B Instruct with fixed weights, 4-bit quantization, and greedy questionnaire responses. Following the recursive-feature-machine (RFM) approach of Beaglehole et al. (2026), we extract high- and low-pole controllers for each of the five traits. We then keep these controllers fixed. Their signed strengths form β = ( βO, β C , β E , β A, β N ): a positive coordinate selects the high controller, and a negative coordinate selects the low controller with the corresponding absolute strength. Holding the controllers fixed reduces the search over high-dimensional activation space to five steering strengths. A single control may change several measured traits, so we use questionnaire responses to learn how the five strengths jointly affect the measured scores. 2 Appendix C details extraction and the exact activation update. 62.5 Comparators and evaluation rules 

We compare three ways to choose steering doses: BO, linear PtO, and uniform random search. Uniform random search provides a budget-matched baseline for adaptive optimization (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012). It draws independently from B, assigning one dose to each of 500 people in each of five passes. BO receives the same budget: 5 × 500 = 2 ,500 additional interventions, each requiring 50 item calls, or 125,000 calls per method. Both may use the same initial library for final selection; their new responses remain separate. As a one-shot method, PtO contributes a single 500-dose batch. We also evaluate two prompting baselines. Blank prompting asks the model to answer as a generic respondent and produces the baseline trait vector y0. Profile prompting instead supplies a paragraph describing the target’s behavioral tendencies. The paragraph is generated from fixed score-to-behavior bands, with constraints against adding unsupported personal details. Its numerical target is not printed in the questionnaire prompt. These are direct prompting benchmarks, not prompt-search procedures matched to BO’s budget. Appendix F gives the exact templates and an example. We distinguish two calibration evaluations. # 4 Discussion 

The experiment identifies a useful role for statistical calibration in constructing social agents. Extracted activation directions provide controls, but their strengths need to be fitted to the desired joint response. A shared nonlinear surrogate predicts this response substantially better than the tested linear approximation. BO then produces useful individual recommendations and, in the main experiment, builds a more effective response library than equally many uniform evaluations. Reuse is central to this design. Once a response has been observed, every target can be compared with it at negligible additional generation cost. This makes calibration across a cohort different from running unrelated optimization problems for each person. It also makes the selection rule part of the method: an intervention that poorly matches its intended target can still improve the library for somebody else. The small-library experiment demonstrates that these two forms of usefulness can lead to different method rankings. The principal boundary is between calibration and behavioral transfer. Trait scores aggregate several 11 answers, so people with the same scores can differ on individual items. Matching those scores is a coherent fitting objective, but does not establish that an agent reproduces preferences, choices, or interactions beyond the questionnaire. Testing that next step requires freezing the calibrated intervention and evaluating it on an independently chosen instrument or economic task. We demonstrate joint calibration with one language model and one personality questionnaire, using descriptive profile prompting as the benchmark. The same question arises when a simulation calls for agents with specified attitudes toward risk or judgments of fairness. Extending the method to those settings would require human measurements and suitable steering controls. Our contribution is a way to check, before a simulation begins, how closely each agent matches the profile it was assigned.

15 C Controller Construction and Activation Intervention 

We use the RFM extraction pipeline of Beaglehole et al. (2026). The 15 BFI-2 facets provide a scaffold for OCEAN concept statements (Soto and John, 2017). For each of five domains, three facets, and two poles, the local model generates 40 first-person statements: 1,200 total. Mechanical checks reject malformed, repetitive, or unsuitable wording, and a same-model semantic judge checks the intended facet and pole. The statements describe generic behavioral tendencies; generation uses neither the human targets nor their IPIP answers. 6 Appendix G provides the facet scaffold and generation template. Each trait has a fixed facet-stratified split: 192 training statements and 48 validation statements, comprising 32 and eight per facet/pole, respectively. High and low controllers use the same split with reversed binary labels. RFM fitting uses up to eight iterations and validation AUC for selection. Five leading components of the average gradient outer product are extracted and oriented toward the labeled pole; execution uses the first component. # D Computation 

The experiments run locally on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Ti. We use Llama 3.1 8B Instruct with 4-bit NF4 double quantization and bfloat16 computation. Questionnaire calls are greedy, allow at most 32 generated tokens, and start a fresh conversation per item. All 50 items are attempted; malformed responses are recorded without identical retries. Complete conditions are required for trait scoring. GP regression uses standard Gaussian conditioning with constant means, Matérn-3 /2 kernels, and hyperparameters estimated by marginal likelihood (Rasmussen and Williams, 2005). EI is evaluated from posterior draws and maximized numerically over candidate interventions (Frazier, 2018). Each trait score maps to one of six fixed descriptions: low ( < 2), somewhat low ([2 , 2.75)), mixed ([2 .75 , 3.25)), somewhat elevated ([3 .25 , 4)), elevated ([4 , 4.5)), or very elevated ( ≥ 4.5). The local model joins the resulting five behavioral facts into five sentences, in OCEAN order, within a 90–130-word paragraph. Instructions prohibit invented biography, numerical scores, trait labels, and changes to the stated tendencies. Mechanical checks and a semantic judge assess fidelity; unsuccessful drafts receive revised correction prompts, with a fixed-template fallback after three attempts. These profile-generation checks are separate from the no-retry questionnaire protocol. 19 For the target (4 .5, 4.9, 3.5, 3.8, 4.6), the saved 106-word profile is:

In social situations, this person is generally engaged and comfortable initiating interaction, but also values quieter or more independent settings. They tend to be considerate and cooperative in their interpersonal interactions, while also being direct when necessary.
