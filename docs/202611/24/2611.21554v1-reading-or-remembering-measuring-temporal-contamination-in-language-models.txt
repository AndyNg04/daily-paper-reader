Abstract 

Language models are now used to read central-bank text for economic forecasting, but most were trained on text written after the docu-ments they read. A model reading a 2021 FOMC statement may already know that infla-tion rose in 2022, so part of its apparent skill can be memory of the outcome, a temporal form of test-set contamination. We measure this memory on FOMC statements and min-utes from 2000 to 2026 by comparing modern open models with chronologically consistent models, which are trained only on text from before a fixed date. Asked about past GDP outcomes given only a date, models that can-not know the answer still reach an AUC of up to 0.64 from priors on dates and eras, so chance is the wrong baseline. Against these memory-free models on the same questions, most modern models recall which years had strong growth. Membership-inference scores sort documents by era and document type rather than by whether a model saw them, as exact counts in an open pretraining corpus show. In real-time nowcasts, every lookahead premium we estimate on US GDP is smaller than the forecast comparison can detect at macroeconomic sample sizes, and none on GDP or inflation survives correction for multi-ple testing. Lookahead audits should therefore measure memory directly against memory-free models on the same items. 

1 Introduction 

Language models are now used to read central-bank communication for forecasters, and most of the models in use were trained on text written af-ter the period they are asked to read. A model that has seen the commentary of 2021 and 2022 does not need to infer from a 2021 FOMC statement that inflation is about to rise; it can remember that it did. In machine-learning terms this is tempo-ral contamination, a form of test-set contamina-tion that runs through time: the outcomes a model is evaluated on appear in text written after its in-puts. Lookahead of this kind has been documented in financial and economic prediction (Sarkar and Vafa, 2024; Glasserman and Lin, 2024; Lopez-Lira et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026; Eliseev and Seleznev, 2026), and contamination by release date has been found in benchmark results (Roberts et al., 2024). Chronologically consistent models, trained only on text available before a fixed date, now exist to remove it (He et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026). We want to measure, for a forecasting task, how much of a model’s apparent skill is mem-ory, and whether that memory reaches the forecast when the forecast is made as it would be in prac-tice: in real time, on the data available that day, and scored against the first published estimate. The usual checks are poorly suited to this. Scor-ing a model against chance assumes that a model which has not seen the outcome guesses at ran-dom. Membership-inference scores assume that the texts a model saw and the texts it did not see differ only in that respect, an assumption that fails when the two come from different periods (Duan et al., 2024). And a comparison of forecast ac-curacy with and without the suspect model needs more data than a macroeconomic series provides. We build such a measurement for the FOMC’s statements and minutes from 2000 to 2026 and for real-time nowcasts of US GDP and CPI growth from 2012 to 2024, and we score each part of it against a reference other than chance. Date-only probes give a model a date and ask about an out-come it could know only from later text. The ref-erence is a set of readers that cannot know the out-come, scored on the same items, and the inference allows for outcomes that cluster in time and for priors on calendar words. A ladder of fixed check-points from one chronologically consistent family asks whether a model does better on outcomes its training could have contained than on outcomes 1arXiv:2611.21554v1 [cs.LG] 16 Nov 2026 it could not. Membership scores ask whether a model saw a document; we check them against checkpoints with known cutoffs and against ex-act counts in one model’s indexed training corpus. Finally, a real-time nowcaster measures what the readings are worth, with a reader behind a tempo-ral gate as the control, two hard-data baselines, the professional nowcasts, and a power analysis. The first three of our six findings are positive re-sults. The other three mark what these data cannot resolve, and we report them with intervals and, for the forecast, the smallest effect the test could de-tect. 1. Readers that cannot know the outcome do not score 0.5. On the GDP probe they reach AUCs up to about 0.64 from priors on dates and eras, so every claim of memory below compares a model with these readers on the same items. 2. The modern models remember outcomes that the memory-free readers cannot know. Against the gated reader, six of the seven score higher after adjusting for multiple com-parisons. A date retrieves which years grew strongly. 3. Membership scores track era and document type rather than membership. Exact span counts in OLMo-2’s indexed corpus date the end of its FOMC text to 2 November 2022, a year before its model card’s cutoff, and within a year and a document type Min-K% does not separate the documents found from those not found. 4. The probes cannot resolve finer distinctions. Within a year there are too few mixed out-comes to tell whether a model remembers in-dividual quarters. The modern models’ mar-gins over ChronoGPT-2024, a small chrono-logically consistent model whose training also runs past every outcome, do not sur-vive adjustment for multiple comparisons, so whether the larger models remember more than the small one stays open. The check-point ladder’s intervals all include zero and are wider than that gap, which leaves open whether the small models remember at all. 5. The forecast comparison lacks the power to detect a lookahead premium. The leaky read-ers’ GDP premiums over the gated reader are a few percent of RMSE, below the 8.8 to 13.1 percent the test detects with 80% power, and their sign depends on whether 2020 or 2021 is left out. They are also measured on a GDP nowcaster that does not beat a running mean of past growth. 6. Text adds no skill that survives correction for multiple testing, either to the real-time now-caster or to the SPF, GDPNow and New York Fed nowcasts. A lookahead audit of a forecasting system should therefore rest neither on forecast accuracy, which at macroeconomic sample sizes cannot re-solve premiums of a few percent, nor on member-ship scores compared across eras, which confound membership with time, but on date-only probes scored against memory-free readers on the same items, and on exact corpus counts where a corpus is indexed. We release the corpus, every score and every script. 

2 Related work 

Memorisation and contamination detection 

Memorisation grows log-linearly with model ca-pacity, duplication and prompt length (Carlini et al., 2023). Detection methods ask whether a text was in the training corpus: Min-K% Prob averages the log-probabilities of a text’s least likely tokens (Shi et al., 2024), Min-K%++ normalises them by the next-token distribution (Zhang et al., 2025), and guided prompts flag contamination when a completion matches a reference instance (Golchin and Surdeanu, 2024). Contamination must be measured benchmark by benchmark (Sainz et al., 2023), and answers to Stack Overflow questions are often collages of memorised text whether or not the question predates training (Caspari et al., 2025); Xu et al. (2024) and Ravaut et al. (2025) survey the methods. Duan et al. (2024) find that membership inference barely beats chance on Pile-trained models and trace reported successes to members and non-members drawn from differ-ent periods. Our probes face the same confound, because answer priors track eras. We ask whether a model knows an outcome that postdates a docu-ment, and score each leaky reader against a gated reader and a persistence rule on the same items rather than against 0.5. 

Temporal generalisation and knowledge cutoffs 

Perplexity on text from after the training period 2worsens with distance from it (Lazaridou et al., 2021); conditioning on timestamps improves re-call of time-dependent facts (Dhingra et al., 2022); GPT pass rates on Codeforces and Project Eu-ler problems reveal contamination by release date (Roberts et al., 2024); and effective cutoffs often differ from reported ones (Cheng et al., 2024). We instead use known cutoffs as the treatment: within one chronologically consistent family, a ladder of fixed checkpoints shares architecture, size, tok-enizer and recipe, and each checkpoint’s cutoff di-vides the outcomes it could have seen from those it could not. 

Chronologically consistent language models 

StoriesLM continues each checkpoint on the fol-lowing year’s news from 1900 (Sarkar, 2024), and Time Machine GPT releases point-in-time models (Drinkall et al., 2024). ChronoBERT and ChronoGPT reach Sharpe ratios in next-day return prediction comparable to a much larger Llama model (He et al., 2026), with instruction-tuned versions following (He et al., 2025). Dat-edGPT trains twelve 1.3B-parameter models with annual cutoffs from 2013 to 2024 and finds a return-prediction premium of 26.4 basis points per standard deviation for models whose train-ing covers the outcome period (Yan et al., 2026). These papers evaluate on asset returns. We use ChronoGPT-Instruct as a gate inside a macroe-conomic nowcaster, use fixed checkpoints of ChronoGPT and DatedGPT as ungated controls, and measure the premium on 150 real-time ori-gins over 50 quarters together with the smallest premium the test can detect. 

Lookahead bias in economic and financial uses of language models Lookahead appears when models predict risk factors from earnings calls and election winners from biographies (Sarkar and Vafa, 2024); anonymising headlines improves GPT sentiment trading in-sample, so distraction can outweigh lookahead (Glasserman and Lin, 2024); models recall economic series from before their cutoff even when told to respect a histori-cal boundary (Lopez-Lira et al., 2025); and the probability of a non-unknown answer given only a firm, ticker and date collapses after the cutoff (Gao et al., 2026). Telling a model to ignore what it knows leaves a 52% gap to true ignorance (Li et al., 2026), no modern model passes the first fake-date test of Eliseev and Seleznev (2026) for macroeconomic forecasts, and the PaLM inflation forecasts of Faria-e Castro and Leibovici (2024) are in-sample for 2019–23. MACROCAST avoids leakage by pretraining on synthetic series (Car-riero et al., 2026), and Ludwig et al. (2026) state the general no-leakage condition. Most of this ev-idence concerns firm-level outcomes or a model forecasting a series directly. We study a model reading central-bank text as one input to a real-time nowcaster, price lookahead as a change in RMSE against first release, and find that the mem-ory a probe detects does not appear as resolvable forecast skill. 

Text in macroeconomic nowcasting News text adds information that hard indicators miss (Thorsrud, 2020; Kalamara et al., 2022; Ellingsen et al., 2022). Central-bank text has been read with topic models (Hansen and McMahon, 2016; Hansen et al., 2018), with machine learning on staff documents (Aruoba and Drechsel, forthcom-ing), and with encoders trained further on central-bank text (Gambacorta et al., 2026). Our bridge-factor nowcaster follows the real-time nowcast-ing literature (Giannone et al., 2008; Ba´ nbura and Modugno, 2014; Higgins, 2014), in which data vintages change results (Koenig et al., 2003; Croushore, 2011) and model nowcasts beat the SPF on headline inflation (Knotek and Zaman, 2017). We drop the two pandemic quarters, which is acceptable for estimation though it understates forecast uncertainty (Lenza and Primiceri, 2022). These text measures come from topic models, dic-tionaries or models trained on a chosen corpus; a general-purpose language model brings a corpus that extends past the forecast period, so we make the reader’s training cutoff the variable under test.

4 Measuring memory 

We measure memory three ways, each with its own null, before asking what memory is worth in a forecast. 

Date-only probes A reader is given a date and nothing else, a two-answer form of the date-only recall queries of Gao et al. (2026). In the decision probe it is told that the FOMC changed its target on that date and asked whether it raised or lowered it (74 changes, 2000–2025). In the GDP probe it is asked whether first-release real GDP growth in a given quarter was above or below 2 percent at an annual rate (100 quarters, 2000–2024). A reader that has not seen the outcome can only guess, but a guess need not score an AUC of 0.5: answer priors can line up with eras in which one out-come was common, and a gated reader’s check-point changes every January. The null is therefore set by two other readers scored on the same items: the gated reader and a persistence rule that uses only what was public before the item’s year began. 5Probe items cluster in time, so inference respects the clustering: p-values come from rotating the labels against the scores in date order (a circular shift, which keeps the dependence in both series), and intervals for AUC differences from resam-pling calendar years. We also compare quarters with the same quarter number across years, tested by rotating the years of the outcomes against the scores, and quarters within the same year, which removes most between-era comparisons (a fourth quarter is dated by its release in the following Jan-uary, so it is read by the next gated checkpoint). 

The checkpoint ladder The gated reader mixes 26 checkpoints, and its 2024 sibling has seen more text in total, so the gated-against-ungated contrast differs in more than lookahead. The ladder sep-arates the two. Each fixed checkpoint, ending in 2011, 2015, 2019 or 2024, reads every document and answers every probe. For each, an item is seen 

if its outcome was public by the checkpoint’s cut-off and unseen otherwise. Memory predicts that a checkpoint does better on what it could have seen than on what it could not; general capability, re-cency and checkpoint mixing do not predict that split within one checkpoint. 

Membership Probes ask whether a model knows an outcome; membership scores ask whether it saw a text. For every excerpt and reader we compute Min-K% Prob, the mean of the low-est k = 20 percent of token log-probabilities of the excerpt read as plain text (Shi et al., 2024), and Min-K%++, which standardises each token’s log-probability by the model’s own next-token distribution (Zhang et al., 2025). Membership scores are known to fail when members and non-members come from different periods (Duan et al., 2024), as ours do. For each fixed ChronoGPT checkpoint, documents released after its cutoff are certainly not members and documents released be-fore it possibly are; for OLMo-2, whose pretrain-ing corpus is indexed, we count exact spans of each excerpt in the corpus. 

4.1 Results: what the readers remember Memory-free readers do not score 0.5 Table 1 gives the GDP probe for every reader. The read-ers that cannot know an outcome are the right null, and they are not at chance: the gated ChronoGPT reader scores 0.48, the persistence rule 0.45, and checkpoints that cannot have seen most of their items score as high as 0.64 (ChronoGPT with a 2011 cutoff, which scores 0.60 on the 2012–2024 items it cannot know). AUCs up to about 0.64 therefore arise from priors on dates and eras, and a modern reader’s AUC is evidence of memory only where it exceeds these readers on the same items, with intervals that respect how outcomes cluster in years.

The ladder cannot resolve memory of this size 

For the five checkpoints with both kinds of item, the seen-minus-unseen difference in AUC runs from −0.19 to +0 .12 , two are positive, and all five intervals include zero (Table 2, Figure 4 in Ap-pendix B). The seen and unseen items come from different eras, so the cleaner contrast holds the items fixed and compares consecutive checkpoints on the outcomes released between their cutoffs: two of the five contrasts are positive, on 16 to 20 items each, with intervals from 0.61 to 1.08 wide (Table 8). The ladder cannot detect memory of the size the modern readers show over ChronoGPT-2024, so it neither confirms nor excludes memory in the small chronologically consistent models. 

The decision probe fails its null Readers that cannot know the outcome score well above 0.5 on 6the decision probe: the gated DatedGPT reader scores 0.79 on its 31 items, and ChronoGPT with a 2011 cutoff scores 0.65 on the 31 rate changes after its cutoff. We do not know which prior they exploit (the persistence rule, the direction of the last change before the year, scores only 0.54), so the probe cannot separate memory from such pri-ors. The modern readers score 0.64 to 0.95. We report the probe in Appendix B and draw no con-clusion from it. 

Membership Membership scores cannot be val-idated on the chronologically consistent check-points, because no one knows which documents released before a cutoff were in the training data. The comparison does show the era confound (Ta-ble 13): the mean log-probability ranks pre-cutoff documents as no more familiar than post-cutoff ones, usually less (AUC 0.16 to 0.49), because the Fed’s language changes over the decades. For OLMo-2 we count up to three novel 14-word spans of each excerpt in its indexed stage-1 pre-training corpus with infini-gram (Liu et al., 2024); a span is novel when no earlier FOMC document contains it, because statements repeat whole sen-tences. Of the 416 documents with a novel span, 68% of those released up to 2021 are found and 0% of those released from 2023; the last one found is the statement of 2 November 2022, a year before the model card’s cutoff. An absent span may have been seen in another form, and a statement quoted in the news may be found through the quotation, so the counts measure exposure to the text rather than to the document. Statements are almost all found (183/189 up to 2022) and minutes much less of-ten (69/183), and Min-K% separates the two doc-ument types (AUC 0.83 for OLMo-2-7B). Within a year and a document type, where neither era nor type can help, Min-K% does not separate the doc-uments found from those not found (AUC 0.54, permutation p = 0 .215 ). On these data, mem-bership scores track era and document type rather than membership. 

5 Measuring what memory is worth 

The real-time nowcaster We price memory by putting each reader’s signal into a real-time now-caster, which estimates growth in the current quar-ter before the official figure is published. At each origin the nowcaster takes one vintage of the eleven indicators. Indicators are released with dif-ferent delays, so the latest months are missing for some series; this uneven end of the panel is the ragged edge. The nowcaster fills it with forecasts from an autoregression of order two, AR(2), on each indicator’s own monthly changes, averages to the quarter, and forms growth. It then reduces the target’s own block of indicators (real activity for GDP, prices for CPI) to its first three princi-pal components, which we call block PCA, and regresses the target on these three factors with a ridge penalty. This ridge regression is the bridge, the usual name for a model that links monthly in-dicators to a quarterly target. It is estimated on the quarters from 2000 Q1 whose first release is out by the origin. A text model adds the five dimensions of the latest FOMC document published on or be-fore the origin; training quarters take the document available at the same position in their own quar-ter. Every regressor is standardised by its train-ing mean and standard deviation, so the penalty treats every reader alike. The fill and the indicator blocks were chosen among six specifications on a 2006–2011 hold-out; all six are reported on both windows (Appendix A.5), and the order in which they were tried is stated there. 

Baselines Text is only worth something if the hard-data model is competent, so we report two further benchmarks. The first is a mixed-frequency dynamic factor model in the style of Ba´ nbura and Modugno (2014), fit by expecta-tion maximisation on the same vintages with the ragged edge left as missing data, which this es-timator handles directly, and with factor number and order chosen on the hold-out. The second is the set of professional nowcasts practitioners use: the Survey of Professional Forecasters, GDP-Now (Higgins, 2014) and the New York Fed Staff Nowcast (Bok et al., 2018), each used only where its value was public on the date it carries: GDP-Now from July 2014, when it went online, and the New York Fed nowcast during its live publi-cation, April 2016 to September 2021 and from September 2023. We also ask directly whether a text signal corrects the professional nowcasts, by regressing their first-release error on the signal out of sample.

As checks, we compute the Harvey–Leybourne–Newbold test (Harvey et al., 1997), a small-sample correction of the standard test of equal forecast accuracy, run with a hori-zon of three origins, and a 95% interval from re-sampling quarters. The gain of a text model over the model without text is a nested comparison, be-cause the model without text is the text model with its text coefficients set to zero. We test it with the one-sided Clark–West test (Clark and West, 2007), which adjusts the loss difference for the noise the larger model adds by estimating coefficients that are zero under the null. Holm’s method, a step-down version of the Bonferroni correction, adjusts each family of tests for multiplicity: the premium tests within each target, the 26 text-against-no-text tests across both targets, and the 48 tests of text on top of the professional nowcasts. The mini-mum detectable effect is the premium the primary test would detect with 80% power, from a quarter-block bootstrap standard error of the mean loss difference and Student-t quantiles with 2m de-grees of freedom. We report the premium leav-ing out each year in turn, and a placebo for the gated and ChronoGPT-2024 readers: the sequence of document signals is rotated against the calen-dar, which keeps the signal’s distribution and per-sistence and breaks its alignment with outcomes, legitimate information included.

6 Discussion 

What the evidence supports The larger modern open models hold outcome memory that a date re-trieves, beyond what memory-free readers achieve on the same items. Whether the small chronologi-cally consistent models hold any is not resolvable with these probes. In a real-time nowcast, neither the memory nor the text shows up as resolvable skill. A probe can show that a model could use the future, but only a forecasting comparison can show that it does, and at macroeconomic sample sizes that comparison has little power. 

Why the forecast comparison has little power 

With 150 origins over 50 quarters, the primary test detects a premium of about 8.8 to 13.1 percent of GDP nowcast RMSE with 80% power, larger than every premium we estimate with or without 2020 and 2021. The swings of 2020 and 2021 move the sample RMSE more than all other quar-ters together, which is why the premium changes sign depending on which of the two years is left out. Audits of language-model forecasters that rest on forecast accuracy alone should report the min-imum detectable effect and the leave-one-period-out premium. 

Beyond central-bank text The design needs only dated inputs, outcomes with known release dates, and one family of checkpoints with known cutoffs, as in forecasting benchmarks of questions that resolve on known dates or problems sorted by release date (Roberts et al., 2024). 

Limitations The documents are one central bank’s, and the probes test two outcomes. A.3 Corpus construction and cleaning 

The corpus is built from the Federal Reserve’s own index of documents and release dates. Four cor-rections were applied before any result reported here. 1. Misdated minutes. Twenty-eight raw files held copies of minutes stored under the date of an intermeeting conference call, between three and sixty-six days before the minutes were published, which placed text before its public release for two test origins and thirteen training cells. Files with no entry in the Fed-eral Reserve’s index are not used. 2. Missing minutes. Twenty-four sets of min-utes from 2004–2006 were stored under non-standard file names. They are included, dated by their release. 3. Excerpt boundaries and dates. Twenty-six statements without a recorded vote run into the web page’s navigation text; their ex-cerpts stop at the page footer. Eight state-ments carried a date one day before their re-lease; statements are dated by their press-release URL. We added section markers for minutes whose outlook or policy discussion the standard markers miss; 6 sets of minutes still yield only one of the two sections. 4. Character encoding. Sixty-eight excerpts carried UTF-8 punctuation decoded as Latin-1. They are decoded as UTF-8. Labels for the decision probe come from the Fed-eral Reserve’s published table of target changes, 74 changes from 2000 to 2025, each dated by the statement that announced it. Every reader scored the corrected documents and probe items. 

A.4 Determinism 

Scores are deterministic up to floating-point noise. The ungated ChronoGPT-2024 reader was scored twice, on an Apple-silicon GPU and on a CUDA GPU, before the corpus cleaning; the 6,688 raw scores differ by at most 8 × 10 −5 and agree in sign in 100% of cases. Items changed by the cleaning were then scored again. 

A.5 Nowcaster specifications 

Six specifications were evaluated: three ways of filling the months not yet published (the mean growth of the last three months, no change, and an autoregression of order two on each indicator’s own monthly changes), each with all eleven in-dicators or with indicator blocks by target. The fills were run in the order listed, and once the au-toregressive fill was added the selection rule (the single specification with the lowest mean hold-out RMSE across the two targets, on origins 2006– 2011) was applied to all six. Four of the six had been seen on the test window before the rule was set. Table 6 reports all six on both windows and Table 10 the gated and ChronoGPT-2024 readers under each.
