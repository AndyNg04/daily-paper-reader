Shaping AI’s Beliefs Before It Generates:
Human Sense of Agency in Creative AI

Abstract
AI systems for creative media generation expand human capabilities yet risk
diminishing human agency. A core problem is anchoring, where the user’s thinking
becomes anchored to a polished, generated artifact, shifting the user’s role from
active authoring to reactive editing. We propose pre-generation belief controls
that can empower creators to inspect, revise, and steer the AI’s interpretation
before any media is generated, so that the initial anchor is set by the human.
We formalize four design principles and instantiate them in the Proactive Co-
Creator (PaCC), a system for early-stage conceptual ideation and prototyping
that proactively surfaces its assumptions and uncertainty through clarifications,
belief graphs, and media attributes, inviting users to shape the AI’s beliefs before
generating the final outcome. Using PaCC as a design probe in a small-scale, single-
arm exploratory user study, we observed that participants reported directionally
positive sense of agency and process control relative to their familiar prompt-based
AI tools, noting that surfaced assumptions helped them think through details they
had overlooked, whereas their feelings about ownership and authorship diverged.

Introduction
Human agency, the capacity to make deliberate choices and control one’s actions (Schlosser, 2019),
is central to well-being (Haggard, 2017; Hojman and Miranda, 2018; Welzel and Inglehart, 2010)
and paramount in creative work to ensure decisions reflect authentic vision and intent. Generative
AI expands human capabilities (UNDP, 2025), yet risks diminishing human sense of agency (SoA).
Typical human-AI interactions rely on post-generation critique, where users prompt a model, receive
a polished artifact, and then iteratively edit (or re-prompt) the system, positioning users as reactive
curators rather than active authors. These workflows can induce an anchoring effect on users (Furnham
and Boo, 2011). By autonomously resolving unspecified details and making choices, models can bias
human reasoning so that subsequent iterations orbit the AI’s initial generation rather than exploring
authentic intent (Fernandes et al., 2025; Shaw and Nave, 2026; Storey, 2026). This can contribute to
reduced ownership (He, 2026), destabilized SoA (Rafner et al., 2025), and homogenized creative
identity (Jiang et al., 2026), detailed in §A.
We posit that this erosion of agency stems in part from when intervention occurs. In contrast to
post-generation critique, we propose pre-generation belief control: empowering creators to inspect,
revise, and steer the AI’s underlying conceptual interpretation of human intent before media is
generated, so that the initial anchor is largely determined by the human rather than the machine.
We formalize this approach into four design principles adapted from Google PAIR (2024): intervening
before generation (DP1), making assumptions visible (DP2), preserving the possibility space (DP3),
and calibrating AI autonomy (DP4). We instantiate these principles in the Proactive Co-Creator

and (iii) scene-level media attributes that expose global stylistic defaults.
To explore how creators interact with these mechanisms, we deployed PaCC as a design probe in a
small-scale, single-arm exploratory user study, where observations are preliminary and hypothesis-
generating rather than confirmatory. While participants felt PaCC’s pre-generation controls surfaced
hidden assumptions and supported agency and process control, their views on ownership and author-
ship diverged based on their initial creative intent, stance on AI training data, and text-only style
limitations. These observations highlight key design tensions and motivate testable hypotheses for
future work on controlled evaluations, multimodal style steering, and agency-aware training.
In summary, our contributions are: (1) the concept of pre-generation belief control and four design
principles for supporting human agency in early-stage ideation; (2) the Proactive Co-Creator (PaCC)
system, instantiating these principles via clarification dialogues, editable belief graphs, and scene-
level attributes; and (3) exploratory insights from a design probe that surface key design tensions and
outline future research directions for agency, ownership, and authorship in human-AI co-creation.

The Proactive Co-Creator
The Proactive Co-Creator (PaCC) instantiates our design principles (§1) as an interactive, multi-modal
system for image, story, and video generation, extending designs in our prior work (Hahn et al., 2025).
PaCC is a React/TypeScript web application, built with Gemini assistance in Google AI Studio, that
orchestrates structured Gemini API calls for belief graph construction, clarification, and prompt
2


refinement (Appendix C). At its core, PaCC inserts an interactive belief control phase between prompt
entry and media generation (DP1: Intervene before generation), shifting human-AI iteration from
post-hoc editing to upstream conceptual guidance.
Figure 1 illustrates the PaCC interface. To support human agency across creative intent, expres-
sion, and process, PaCC operationalizes our design principles through three core pre-generation
interventions:
Clarification dialogues and delegation (Figure 1 C1–C2).
PaCC surfaces targeted, single-topic
clarifying questions to resolve scene ambiguities (Figure 1 C1–C2). Surfacing 3–5 diverse candidate
options per question preserves the creative possibility space (DP3), encouraging exploration rather
than collapsing decisions into singular AI defaults. To calibrate AI autonomy (DP4), each question
includes a “You Decide” response (letting the AI decide), allowing creators to delegate specific
tactical choices (e.g., color scheme) to the model while retaining manual control over core creative
decisions (e.g., mood and setting).
Belief graphs, relationship editing, and entity attributes (Figure 1 B, D1–D2).
To make as-
sumptions visible (DP2), PaCC parses the user’s prompt (including optional reference images) into
an editable graph distinguishing explicit entities directly stated by the user (solid borders; e.g., “cat,”
“bunny”) from implicit entities inferred by the model (dashed borders; e.g., “patio”), alongside editable
relationships (edge values; e.g., “hosting”). Each entity node carries fine-grained attributes with
multiple candidate values (Figure 1 D1–D2; e.g., cat breed, party theme), preserving the creative
possibility space (DP3) further inviting exploration. While the backend estimates model uncertainty
for those candidate values, raw numeric probabilities are omitted from the UI to prevent cognitive
clutter. Additionally, creators can calibrate AI autonomy (DP4) over these graph inferences via
three proactiveness tiers (low, medium, high; Figure 1 A1), which govern how aggressively the model
populates implicit entities, relationships, and candidate attributes.
Media attributes (Figure 1 E1–E2).
Beyond individual entities, PaCC surfaces macro, modality-
specific media attributes, such as lighting, art style, and camera angle for images; narrative tone for
stories; and camera motion, pacing, and duration for videos (Figure 1 E1–E2). Making these aesthetic
defaults visible (DP2) and providing alternative stylistic choices preserves the creative possibility
space (DP3), preventing hidden style biases. As with the belief graph, the proactiveness tier setting
dynamically modulates these aesthetic inferences, enabling creators to calibrate AI autonomy (DP4)
over global stylistic defaults.
Before triggering media generation (DP1), PaCC compiles all graph edits, clarification answers, and
attribute selections into an AI-refined prompt diff for optional verification. Creators can use these
pre-generation controls across iterative workflows spanning Image, Story, and Video modes. The full
technical implementation details, including LLM call pipelines, incremental JSON schemas, and a
step-by-step user journey walk-through, are provided in Appendix C alongside a principle-to-feature
mapping (Table 2).

Measuring Human Sense of Agency in AI-assisted Creative Practice
To evaluate whether PaCC’s pre-generation belief controls affect human agency, we measure a
subjective sense of agency (SoA) rather than objective metrics, because an individual’s felt agency
can neither reliably track individual contributions nor be calculated from the division of labor between
user and AI (He, 2026; Limerick et al., 2014; Rafner et al., 2025; Synofzik et al., 2008). Drawing on
the SoA literature (Gallagher, 2012; Moore, 2016; Polito et al., 2013), we define SoA as “the feeling
of being in control of one’s own actions and their outcomes,” and assess whether creators recognize
the final artifact as their own creations (Haggard, 2017; Moore, 2016).
We examine SoA alongside three interrelated experiential dimensions relevant to the context of our
study2: Control or the felt ability to direct the unfolding creative process, including exploration,
intervention, and iteration. Consistent with prior work (Louie et al., 2020; Pierce et al., 2003), we
treat control as a distinct measure from ownership, though a sense of control contributes to ownership.
Ownership, or the feeling that a work, including process and outcome, is one’s own and closely
connected to the self (Pierce et al., 2003). This is considered distinct from SoA because creators

Because automated tooling actively influences creative workflows (Heigl, 2025), creators need to
continuously negotiate and align AI contributions with their evaluative criteria and artistic values.
Extending Rhodes (1961), we evaluate these agency dimensions across four general aspects of
creative work: Intent (brainstorming and forming concepts for creative work), Expression (exploring
or refining unique artistic techniques, stylistic choices, voice or tone), Process (the non-linear creative
process of switching between exploration and production (Botella et al., 2018)), and Outcome (the
media artifacts produced during and as a final result of the process (Epstein et al., 2020)). Theoretical
definitions and evaluation criteria for these are detailed in Appendix B.

Exploratory User Study and Results
We deployed PaCC as a design probe to examine how creators (N=8 participants, 2 female, 6 male)
interact with pre-generation belief controls and to generate hypotheses for future controlled studies.
Participants (Table 3) were recruited from within our institution and had used generative AI for
creative ideation or artistic exploration within the past 6 months. 5 participants worked in AI research
and engineering (P1–P3, P6, P8), 2 were practicing creative interaction designers (P5, P7), and 1 in
UX research (P4). Following a 10-minute demo, participants spent 30 minutes on a self-directed
multi-modal generation task in PaCC across text, image, and video. They then completed a 20-
minute questionnaire rating PaCC overall and its features across sense of agency, control, ownership,
and authorship on a 5-point Likert scale (1=diminished, 3=preserved/no change, 5=augmented),
alongside retrospective comparisons against their go-to prompt-based AI tools (Appendices D.1–E).
Participants were provided with a broad definition of SoA for ratings. A semi-structured discussion
followed the survey.
Given the small sample (N=8) and single-arm design, we treat all findings as exploratory and direc-
tional rather than statistically conclusive. We focus on qualitative insights from participant feedback,
reporting quantitative Likert ratings as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise specified.
Defining sense of agency for AI co-creation.
Participants most frequently defined SoA in terms of
“process control” (6), “decision control” (4), and “ownership” (3), as well as “the ability to influence
the final output” (P1), spanning three recurring themes:
• Control and Ownership. Being in charge of the creative process, initiating actions, and maintain-
ing ownership of the final output even when collaborating with AI: "Feels like I’m in charge of
what I’m building out especially with AI tools - where I am in command and AI is collaborating
with me not taking my sense of ownership away. It’s still directed and owned by me. I feel like I’m
part of the creative process." –P8
• Intent and Execution. The ability to effectively communicate intent, have the AI reliably follow
instructions, and steer the creative process through iterations: "In the context of GenAI my sense of
agency would relate to how effectively I can communicate my intent to the AI and have it reliably
follow my instructions." –P2
• Empowerment and Expression. Having the freedom to express thoughts without friction and
possessing the power to make informed choices during creative work: "Freedom to express my
thoughts without friction and feeling heard. That being said not every creative thought is about
re-imagining the world, most of my creative thinking is quite tactical. I’ve already felt the problem
and have an idea what I want to make so I can iterate on it." –P7
AI alignment and past experience can influence SoA.
Participants described their current AI
tools as having an overall neutral effect on their SoA (diminish=3.13 ± 0.35, preserve=3.13 ± 0.64,
augment=3.00 ± 0.76), suggesting that current AI tools neither consistently preserve nor undermine
users’ SoA. Open-ended responses noted that SoA diminished when tools felt invasive or unaligned:
"If it’s not hijacking my sense of creativity, AI tools do augment me in the right way if they don’t feel
too invasive; they can diminish if they don’t understand what I want after multiple attempts" –P8, or
when outputs had evidence of the training data, "I fundamentally don’t feel any ownership over any
AI outputs, especially like in one of my explorations ... the [anonymized] logo appeared in the output
just as a constant reminder of the training data from real creatives used in the models." –P6.

More ways to control AI can enhance SoA.
Participants described how the modular and interactive
nature of PaCC expanded creative possibilities by offering a wider variety of choices, "I liked being
able to edit the belief graph / modify my prompt with clarifying questions. The modular nature was
also nice." –P1, "I was able to have more agency than usual because I had more options, and the
system felt more open to my input instead of doing its own thing regardless of what I say." –P3. One
designer noted that surfaced assumptions illuminated blind spots: "The clarifying steps illustrated for
me the things I didn’t think through, highlighted my assumptions" –P7. Descriptively, participants
rated PaCC positively on overall SoA (4.38 ± 1.06), with mean ratings for all features above
the “3. Preserved” midpoint (Prompt Refinement=4.25 ± 0.89, Clarifying Questions=4.00 ± 0.93,
Media Attributes=3.88 ± 0.83, and Belief Graph=3.75 ± 1.043).
Diverging views on creative ownership and authorship.
PaCC was rated most positively for
intent (3.67), followed by process (3.57) and outcome (3.50). Creative process received the highest
SoA ratings (4.25 ± 0.89). Participants highlighted the difficulties in mapping visual style using
language alone: “It was harder to map styles from my head into the photo, but relational concepts
were great” –P1, “Without providing an input of my own work, only using language, I feel like I’m
yielding most creative styling choices to the model.” –P5. While intent and outcome ratings were
identical for SoA (4.12), ownership was slightly higher for intent than for outcome (3.50 vs. 3.12),
suggesting that some participants felt greater ownership over their ideas than over the final artifact.
"I started to think more about the process of creation - what am I missing, is this prompt right, the
product notifications took me out of creative mindset - than the artifact. It inserted itself into my
creativity. Maybe this is good, maybe this is bad. I don’t know." –P7. Views on ownership and
authorship were polarized across participants. Those who felt little-to-no ownership over the final
artifact attributed this either to open-ended exploration without a pre-formed concept or to feeling
steered by AI-generated multiple-choice options: "...as far as ownership, like, do I feel like I made
this? Definitely not, but especially um I think I might have felt more ownership if I had the idea
fully myself and we’re using the tool to try to arrive at it as opposed to exploring." –P5, "...the
final product, I definitely felt no ownership over because like at every step of the way, I was like
prompted with some options I can choose between. But then by the end I like don’t know like if
this was what I wanted in the first place." –P4. In contrast, half of the participants (P1, P2, P3, P8)
reported high felt ownership (M ∈[4.00, 5.00]) and preserved or augmented authorship (P1 did not
rate authorship): "(Feeling of SoA, Ownership, Control) seem similar, perhaps with the exception
of authorship, which feels more like it doesn’t change because I am still the author regardless."
–P3, "I felt ownership over the end-product and the idea, but there were some style elements where I
feel like reference images could have helped me confirm that I should update a particular attribute."
–P1. Overall, PaCC received positive mean ratings for sense of agency (4.09 ± 0.84) and sense
of control (3.89 ± 0.69, N=7) across intent, expression, process, and outcome, whereas ratings
for sense of ownership (3.22 ± 1.49) and sense of authorship (2.75 ± 1.13, N=7) exhibited the
widest variance across individuals. Together with qualitative feedback, this suggests that participants
generally perceived pre-generation controls as supporting agency and process control, whereas felt
ownership and authorship varied with participants’ task goals and attitudes toward AI.
Pre-generation controls with interleaved interactions may enhance felt authorship.
Although
the Belief Graph earned high SoA ratings (3.75 ± 0.95; Table 1), opinions were split between it
being “useful”: “It gives me confidence that the complex visual concepts in the scene are exactly
how I picture them” –P1, and confusing: “I love data visualization, but it was difficult to interact
with and the structure of it didn’t reflect how I was thinking” –P7. Prompt Refinement scored highest
across agency and control, whereas Attributes received moderate scores partly from lower interaction.
Clarifying Questions rated uniformly high across agency, control, and ownership, and was the only
feature all 8 participants rated as providing greater agency than their current tools (Table 1). The
majority of participants rated PaCC above their current tools for sense of agency (5/7), control (6/8),
and ownership (4/7) (Table 1). Authorship was the most varied dimension (3/7 greater, 3/7 equal, 1/7
lesser). Interestingly, individual features showed a more differentiated pattern: clarifying questions
and prompt review individually received (5/7) “greater” authorship ratings, while the belief graph
and media attributes were more evenly split. This variation suggests that pre-generation interventions
that involve active dialogue may support felt authorship more effectively than others, though this
proposition would require verification with larger samples.

Discussion
We discuss the implications of our empirical observations, nuances around agency and authorship,
hypotheses for future investigation, and study limitations (with extended analyses in Appendix D.9).
Proactive artificial agency stemming from pre-generation belief control.
Rather than au-
tonomously resolving unspecified details, PaCC is designed for proactive self-transparency: it
identifies ambiguity, surfaces assumptions for critique, and invites creators to reshape beliefs before
media is generated. This mirrors professional workflows where intermediate low-fidelity artifacts
elicit critique without prematurely anchoring on a final output (Dow et al., 2010). Artificial agency is
thus exercised by asking rather than answering, allocating autonomy to process-level structure while
leaving content-level creative decisions to users.
Diverging perspectives on ownership and authorship.
While participants generally reported
positive agency and process control with PaCC, their views on ownership and authorship diverged.
Half of our participants reported high ownership, whereas the other half reported low ownership
due to open-ended exploration without a pre-formed concept, feeling steered by AI multiple-choice
options, skepticism toward AI training data provenance, or text-only style limitations. This motivates
testable hypotheses for future work: (1) pre-generation belief controls may yield stronger ownership
in goal-directed tasks than in open-ended exploration, and (2) pairing conceptual controls with
multimodal style inputs (e.g., personal sketches or style references) may strengthen felt authorship
over outcomes. Moreover, because aggregate agency scores can mask polarization in ownership and
authorship, evaluations should measure these dimensions separately across creative aspects.
Incentivizing human agency in AI training and agent objectives.
Models optimized solely for
output quality and preference alignment can generate polished artifacts without improving SoA. To
preserve human agency, future reward models and multi-turn training should incentivize collaborative
negotiation, such as asking clarifying questions and surfacing implicit assumptions before generation.
Study limitations and future work.
While our sample (N=8, primarily technical professionals)
served as an initial probe into conceptual framing, longitudinal deployments with professional artists
and writers are needed to examine sustained workflows, creative identity, and domain-specific agency
thresholds. Furthermore, whereas our evaluation used retrospective self-assessments against familiar
tools, future work should conduct controlled A/B experiments against post-generation critique
baselines (further details in Appendix D.10).
6
Conclusion
We proposed pre-generation belief control to mitigate anchoring in creative workflows, instantiated
in the Proactive Co-Creator (PaCC), an interactive system for inspecting, clarifying, and steering the
AI’s assumptions before generating media. By surfacing model beliefs through clarification dialogues,
belief graphs, and media attributes, PaCC directs artificial agency toward eliciting user intent rather
than autonomously resolving unspecified details. Using PaCC as a design probe in an exploratory
study, we observed that participants generally reported positive perceived agency and process control
while expressing diverging perspectives on ownership and authorship, motivating testable hypotheses
for future research. Ultimately, we argue that designing generative AI around the human creative
process, rather than single-turn output generation alone, is important for supporting human agency.

A
Background
Agency and Sense of Agency.
In the philosophy of action, agency denotes the capacity of an
individual to make deliberate choices and exercise control over their actions (Anscombe, 1956;
Davidson, 1963; Schlosser, 2019). In sociology, anthropology, and cognitive science, agency is
recognized not as a static property or the unilateral imposition of a pre-formed mental plan onto
passive matter, but as a dynamic process of engagement with one’s environment (Alexander, 1988;
Barandiaran et al., 2009; Emirbayer and Mische, 1998). Theories of craft and art-making emphasize
that creativity emerges through an ongoing, dialogic correspondence between creator, tool, and
material (Ingold, 2013; Sennett, 2008). In human–computer interaction (HCI) and creativity support
tools (CSTs), researchers examine how interface design distributes power and control between human
and system (Li et al., 2023), focusing on the subjective experience of agency, termed Sense of Agency
(SoA)—the feeling of being in control of one’s actions and their resulting outcomes (David et al.,
2015; Friston et al., 2013; Moore, 2016; Polito et al., 2013). Gallagher (2012) noted that SoA involves
consciously attributing an action to oneself and experiencing self-authorship when outcomes align
with personal intentions, beliefs, and narrative goals. Interactive systems sustain SoA when their state
transitions remain legible and responsive to user mental models (Limerick et al., 2014; Tchemeube
et al., 2023).
AI’s Impact on Creative Agency, Cognitive Surrender, and Intent Debt.
Recent empirical
studies demonstrate that increasing AI automation can inadvertently degrade human SoA and psycho-
logical ownership across creative workflows, with novices and practicing creatives often experiencing
steering and ownership differently (Brade et al., 2026; Krol et al., 2026, 2025; Louie et al., 2020). He
(2026) showed that automated assistance in creative tasks lowers creators’ perceived ownership and
belonging, an effect particularly pronounced among experienced practitioners. Rafner et al. (2025)
observed that SoA fluctuates throughout AI-assisted generation depending on how effectively users
can steer AI outputs to match their original intent. At a foundational level, Shaw and Nave (2026)
proposed Tri-System Theory, extending dual-process models by introducing System 3 (Artificial
Reasoning). Across preregistered experiments, they documented cognitive surrender—the tendency
for users to uncritically accept polished AI outputs, bypassing both intuitive evaluation (System 1) and
9


deliberative reflection (System 2). Complementing this perspective, Storey (2026) highlighted how
automated AI generation accumulates intent debt and cognitive debt, where the creator’s underlying
vision is progressively diluted by unexamined machine defaults, creating a disconnect between human
intent and synthetic output.
A principal driver of cognitive surrender and intent debt is anchoring (Furnham and Boo, 2011):
once a user is presented with a high-fidelity rendered artifact, their cognitive search space contracts
around the AI’s generated choices, constraining subsequent iteration to reactive curation rather than
open-ended correspondence (Fernandes et al., 2025; Ingold, 2013). To mitigate anchoring and prevent
intent debt, Shaw and Nave (2026) and Storey (2026) advocate for design interventions that surface
assumptions, introduce productive conceptual reflection, and expose uncertainty indicators prior to
final artifact generation.
Shared Agency and Pre-Generation Control.
As AI systems become more proactive, interaction
design must evolve from opaque one-shot generation toward models of shared agency (Legaspi et al.,
2024; Moruzzi, 2022; Rezwana and Maher, 2023), where human and artificial agency are dynamically
negotiated. However, most contemporary generative AI tools operate primarily on a post-generation
critique loop, positioning users to evaluate and edit finished outputs only after the model has silently
resolved creative ambiguities. We argue that supporting shared agency in conceptual ideation and
multi-modal prototyping requires shifting intervention upstream through pre-generation belief control.
By externalizing the AI’s internal interpretation into an editable conceptual representation before
rendering, systems can restore dialogic correspondence at the ideation stage and empower creators to
establish the initial anchor.
B
Detailed definitions and evaluation criteria for creative aspects
To evaluate where in the workflow pre-generation belief control augments or diminishes human
agency, we assess four core aspects of creative work, extending the classic analysis of creativity
by Rhodes (1961)4:
• Intent: Creators often approach initial subject matter to brainstorm, explore different ideas, and
form their conceptual intent. We evaluate the extent to which an AI system augments or preserves
the creator’s original idea or concept, versus deflecting them from it or redirecting them toward
repetitive, default realizations (Doshi and Hauser, 2024).
• Expression: Creators use creative expression to explore stylistic trajectories suitable for their
concept, applying personal artistic techniques, voice, and tone. We evaluate the extent to which
the system encourages creators to articulate their stylistic choices and apply their own creative
techniques, versus steering them toward homogeneous defaults (Anderson et al., 2024; Doshi and
Hauser, 2024).
• Process: The non-linear progression in which creators move fluidly between exploration (where
ideas and stylistic trajectories are incubated and rejected) and production (where ideas are finalized
and concrete artifacts are refined) (Botella et al., 2018). We evaluate the extent to which the system
allows creators to control how and when they explore alternatives, set priorities, and iterate on
intermediate outcomes, versus curtailing their workflow (Rezwana and Maher, 2023; Yannakakis
et al., 2014).
• Outcome: Concrete media artifacts produced during and at the conclusion of the creative process
provide artistic direction and feedback until creators are satisfied with the final outcome. We
evaluate the extent to which the system promotes a genuine sense of authorship over the final
artifacts produced (Epstein et al., 2020).

Implementation details
PaCC is implemented as a single-page web application built with React 19, TypeScript, and Vite,
using the @google/genai JavaScript SDK to communicate with Google’s Gemini APIs. The front-
end comprises a main application controller, interactive components for the belief graph, clarification

10


cards, attribute inspector, and prompt review modal, and a centralized service module that orchestrates
all LLM calls. The source code is publicly available.5
LLM call pipeline.
Each analysis cycle in PaCC issues three LLM calls, each with a distinct
system prompt and a typed JSON response schema enforced via the Gemini API’s responseSchema
parameter (ensuring outputs conform to the expected entity–attribute–relationship structure without
post-hoc parsing):
1. Belief graph and media attributes (parsePromptToBeliefGraph). Given the user’s
prompt, optional reference images, the selected modality (image/story/video), and the
proactiveness tier, this call produces a structured JSON object containing: (a) entities with
names, descriptions, explicit/implicit provenance flags, and per-entity attributes each with
2–3 candidate values; and (b) relationships between entities (spatial, emotional, functional),
also marked as explicit or inferred. Mode-specific instructions inject mandatory scene-level
attributes (e.g., camera angle and lighting for images; pacing and duration for videos).
2. Clarification generation (generateClarifications). Issued in parallel with the belief
graph call, this produces exactly three atomic clarifying questions, each with 3–5 selectable
options. The system prompt conditions on the proactiveness tier, the current prompt, any
reference images, and previously asked questions to avoid repetition.
3. Prompt refinement (refinePromptWithAllUpdates). After the user answers clarifi-
cation questions and/or edits the belief graph, this call integrates all updates, including
question-answer pairs and graph edits (attribute changes, entity additions/removals, relation-
ship modifications), into a single refined prompt. The model is instructed to preserve all
existing details not explicitly changed, remove entities marked absent, and respect “You
Decide” (AI delegation) selections (temperature = 0.2).
Incremental graph updates.
When the user modifies the prompt after the initial belief graph
has been constructed, PaCC issues an incremental updateBeliefGraph call that produces a
JSON delta patch rather than regenerating the full graph. The delta schema supports six op-
erations: add/remove/update entities (including entity renaming and attribute add/remove) and
add/remove/update relationships. The client applies these patches locally, preserving entity identity
and relationship continuity across edits. If the incremental update fails, the system falls back to full
graph regeneration.
Proactiveness tiers.
PaCC supports three proactiveness levels (low, medium, and high) that modu-
late both the belief graph and clarification system prompts. At low, the model extracts only entities
and attributes explicitly mentioned in the prompt. At medium, it infers reasonable defaults for
missing attributes and may add 1–2 implicit entities. At high, it proactively invents creative attributes,
generates 3+ implicit entities, and suggests novel stylistic or narrative elements. Users can also
delegate individual clarification questions to the AI via the “You Decide” button.
Reliability.
All API calls use exponential-backoff retry (up to 5 attempts with jittered delays) for
transient errors (HTTP 429, 500, 502, 503, 504). If the user-selected model fails after retries, the
system falls back to gemini-3-flash-preview.
Models.
PaCC was built using the app building functionality in Google AI Studio (https://
aistudio.google.com/apps), with the application code itself authored with the assistance of
Gemini models within the same environment. PaCC is configured to use Gemini APIs (https:
//ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/models). Text models (belief graph, clarifications, prompt
refinement, story generation): gemini-3.1-pro-preview, gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.1-flash-lite-
preview, gemini-flash-latest. Image generation: gemini-2.5-flash-image, gemini-3.1-flash-image-
preview, gemini-3-pro-image-preview, imagen-4.0-generate-001. Video generation: veo-3.1-fast-
generate-preview, veo-3.1-generate-preview. Participants used the free tier for text and image
generation and tier 1 for video generation.

D.1
Extended study design, participant demographics, and procedure
Participants.
We recruited 8 participants (2 female, 6 male) from our institution, working in AI
or AI-related professions spanning research, design, and engineering roles from locations including
New York, Boston, Maryland, San Francisco, Mountain View, and Seattle (see Table 3). We excluded
AI artists (individuals who use AI as their primary medium for commercial art creation), as that
population would require a different study setup. Participants received a $40 gift card for completing
the study. 5 of 8 participants worked in foundational or applied AI development and research, 2 in
creative roles, and 1 in a non-creative professional role. All participants reported using AI in the past
6 months, during which 7 participants used AI to complete work projects, 5 to explore ideas for work,
5 to complete personal art projects, and 5 to explore ideas for personal projects.
Procedure.
Following a 10-minute demonstration of PaCC’s features (Clarifying Questions, Belief
Graph, Attribute Choices) for interactive prompt refinement, participants spent 30 minutes on any
unconstrained media generation task that they considered artistic or creative with no completion
requirements. Participants were encouraged to use all pre-generation controls across writing, image
generation, and video generation with no further instructions. Authors assisted with technical
issues, of which three arose: participants required API keys, model rate limits prompted participants
to switch models or modality during the study, and safety filters occasionally required prompt
adjustments. Participants were then introduced to the study goals ("evaluating how PaCC affects
an individual’s SoA") and completed a 20-minute Google Form survey (Appendix E). The survey
captured participants’ job titles, job descriptions, and use of generative AI in the last 6 months. The
next section asked for a definition of SoA and ratings of whether the participants’ current AI tools
augmented, preserved, or diminished their SoA. The third section captured whether PaCC decreased
or increased their SoA on a 5-point Likert scale (1=Diminished, 3=Preserved, 5=Augmented).
Further sections for individual PaCC features used similar five-point items for rating sense of agency,
ownership, control and authorship across creative intent, expression, process, and outcomes. The last
section asked participants to rate whether the sense of agency, ownership, control, and authorship
experienced with PaCC was greater than, equal to, or less than that of their current tools. Each
survey section closed with an optional written explanation and all survey questions were optional.
The total study time was 60 minutes, and participants who completed the survey early engaged in a
semi-structured discussion. Participant risks were minimal and consistent with using an interface and

D.9
Extended discussion: Ownership and authorship variation, expressive modality, and
role-based hypotheses
To maintain a concise directional focus in Section 5, we provide here an extended discussion of
three empirical nuances observed in our study: diverging participant perspectives on ownership
and authorship, the contrast between conceptual and expressive control, and directional hypotheses
regarding user background and role-based needs.
Diverging perspectives on ownership and authorship in generative AI.
In traditional frameworks
of action, sense of agency (SoA), control, ownership, and authorship are closely entwined: feeling in
control of an action typically produces a sense of authorship over its outcome (Gallagher, 2012; Moore,
2016). In our exploratory study, however, participants generally reported high perceived agency
(4.09±0.84) and process control (3.89±0.69), yet rated authorship lower on average (2.75±1.13) and
expressed polarized views on ownership (3.22±1.49; Table 9). Of the six participants who rated both
control and authorship, five rated PaCC authorship 0.75–1.25 points below their control rating. Half
of our participants (P1, P2, P3, P8) reported high ownership and preserved or augmented authorship
(P1 did not rate authorship), whereas the other half (P4–P7) reported low ownership and authorship
due to exploring without a pre-formed idea (P5), feeling steered by AI-generated multiple-choice
options (P4), skepticism toward AI training data provenance (P6), or text-only style and workflow
friction (P5, P7). Notably, individual features also differed: dialogue-based interventions (clarifying
questions and prompt refinement) received higher comparative authorship ratings (5/7 “greater”) than
visualization-based features (belief graph and media attributes). This raises the hypothesis that felt
authorship depends on both the creator’s initial framing and the interaction modality of pre-generation
control.

Hypotheses on user background and role-based needs.
Rather than drawing definitive conclu-
sions from our exploratory sample (N=8), our findings suggest two directional hypotheses regarding
individual differences that warrant verification in future work:
• Baseline constraint hypothesis: Only one participant (P1) reported that current AI tools very
often diminish their agency (all others answered “sometimes”), and this participant gave
PaCC among the highest agency and control ratings. Though a single case cannot support
a correlational claim, it suggests the hypothesis that pre-generation control provides the
greatest benefit to creators who feel constrained by reactive post-generation critique loops,
whereas users who are comfortable curating AI defaults experience less differentiation.
Verifying this hypothesis is important for designing adaptive interfaces that tailor pre-
generation scaffolding to individual user workflows.
• Role-based ownership hypothesis: Averaged across features, AI professionals (P1–P3, P6,
P8) and creative professionals (P5, P7) both reported positive agency (M=3.85 vs. 3.72)
and control (3.80 vs. 3.42), but the creative professionals rated ownership lower (2.75
vs. 3.78), citing the lack of personal visual inputs (P5) or interruptions to their creative
flow (P7); skepticism toward AI training data came from an AI professional (P6). This
motivates the hypothesis that creative professionals need more direct expressive input and
less workflow interruption than technical users before claiming ownership. If validated,
designers of creative AI tools should not assume that conceptual steering controls alone
confer felt ownership; supporting creative professionals may require transparent provenance,
multimodal style inputs from the artist’s own work, or hybrid workflows where the AI serves
strictly as an ideation partner while the human executes the final artifact.

NeurIPS Paper Checklist
1. Claims
Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the
paper’s contributions and scope?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: Claims are justified by references and human studies.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the abstract and introduction do not include the claims
made in the paper.
• The abstract and/or introduction should clearly state the claims made, including the
contributions made in the paper and important assumptions and limitations. A [No] or
[N/A] answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers.
• The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how
much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings.
• It is fine to include aspirational goals as motivation as long as it is clear that these goals
are not attained by the paper.
2. Limitations
Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: They are included in discussions.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper has no limitation while the answer [No] means
that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.
• The authors are encouraged to create a separate “Limitations” section in their paper.
• The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to
violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings,
model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors
should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the
implications would be.
• The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was
only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often
depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.
• The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach.
For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution
is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be
used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle
technical jargon.
• The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms
and how they scale with dataset size.
• If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to
address problems of privacy and fairness.
• While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by
reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover
limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best
judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an impor-
tant role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community. Reviewers
will be specifically instructed to not penalize honesty concerning limitations.

a complete (and correct) proof?
Answer: [N/A]

Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include theoretical results.
• All the theorems, formulas, and proofs in the paper should be numbered and cross-
referenced.
• All assumptions should be clearly stated or referenced in the statement of any theorems.
• The proofs can either appear in the main paper or the supplemental material, but if

• Inversely, any informal proof provided in the core of the paper should be complemented

4. Experimental result reproducibility

Answer: [Yes]
Justification: Our human participants are fully anonymized but there is enough information
to setup the same human study for other participants.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
• If the paper includes experiments, a [No] answer to this question will not be perceived
well by the reviewers: Making the paper reproducible is important, regardless of
whether the code and data are provided or not.
• If the contribution is a dataset and/or model, the authors should describe the steps taken
to make their results reproducible or verifiable.
• Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways.
For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully
might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may
be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same
dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often

instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case
of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are
appropriate to the research performed.
• While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submis-

nature of the contribution. For example
(a) If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how
to reproduce that algorithm.
(b) If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe

(c) If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should

the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct
the dataset).
(d) We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case
authors are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility.
In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in
some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers
to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results.

10. Broader impacts
Question: Does the paper discuss both potential positive societal impacts and negative
societal impacts of the work performed?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: This paper is about the societal impacts related to human agency.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that there is no societal impact of the work performed.
• If the authors answer [N/A] or [No], they should explain why their work has no societal
impact or why the paper does not address societal impact.
• Examples of negative societal impacts include potential malicious or unintended uses
(e.g., disinformation, generating fake profiles, surveillance), fairness considerations
(e.g., deployment of technologies that could make decisions that unfairly impact specific
groups), privacy considerations, and security considerations.
31


• The conference expects that many papers will be foundational research and not tied
to particular applications, let alone deployments. However, if there is a direct path to
any negative applications, the authors should point it out. For example, it is legitimate
to point out that an improvement in the quality of generative models could be used to
generate Deepfakes for disinformation. On the other hand, it is not needed to point out
that a generic algorithm for optimizing neural networks could enable people to train
models that generate Deepfakes faster.
• The authors should consider possible harms that could arise when the technology is
being used as intended and functioning correctly, harms that could arise when the
technology is being used as intended but gives incorrect results, and harms following
from (intentional or unintentional) misuse of the technology.
• If there are negative societal impacts, the authors could also discuss possible mitigation
strategies (e.g., gated release of models, providing defenses in addition to attacks,
mechanisms for monitoring misuse, mechanisms to monitor how a system learns from
feedback over time, improving the efficiency and accessibility of ML).
11. Safeguards
Question: Does the paper describe safeguards that have been put in place for responsible
release of data or models that have a high risk for misuse (e.g., pre-trained language models,
image generators, or scraped datasets)?
Answer: [N/A]
Justification: Safeguards are enforced at the model level and inherited by PaCC.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper poses no such risks.
• Released models that have a high risk for misuse or dual-use should be released with
necessary safeguards to allow for controlled use of the model, for example by requiring
that users adhere to usage guidelines or restrictions to access the model or implementing
safety filters.
• Datasets that have been scraped from the Internet could pose safety risks. The authors

• We recognize that providing effective safeguards is challenging, and many papers do
not require this, but we encourage authors to take this into account and make a best
faith effort.
