Abstract 

Real-time task guidance is inherently flexible: when an agent guides a user through a task, multiple responses and timings can be appropri-ate, and sometimes the best action is to remain silent. Existing benchmarks often miss this flexibility by treating synthesized dialogues as fixed targets for what to say and when to say it. We introduce FLUID (Fl exible, Evidence-Grounded Situated Task G uid ance), a unified framework for modeling, evaluating, and en-abling flexible interaction in multimodal task guidance. Using naturally occurring human-human guidance from WTaG and HoloAssist, FLUID grounds interventions in their support-ing evidence, necessity, and timing flexibil-ity, and uses this representation both to eval-uate flexible guidance and to build a real-time agent for task guidance. Our agent achieves the strongest overall balance of response correct-ness, intervention precision, timely guidance, and response latency among evaluated systems, while still leaving room for improvement in avoiding speech overlap. Together, FLUID moves situated task guidance beyond rigid reference matching toward more natural and adaptive human-AI interaction. Project page: 

https://fluid-guidance.github.io .

1 Introduction 

With the growing adoption of smart glasses and other wearable devices, multimodal AI assistants are becoming increasingly accessible for providing real-time, situated assistance in everyday activi-ties (Cui et al., 2026; OpenAI, 2026; Google Deep-Mind, 2026). An important emerging application is perceptual task guidance, where an AI assistant ob-serves the user’s environment and ongoing actions through an egocentric camera and provides context-aware guidance through natural language as shown in Figure 1. To evaluate these capabilities, existing work commonly relies on human-human interaction data or synthetically generated dialogues as references for expected guidance behaviors, measuring how closely the AI instructor’s responses align with the reference responses in both semantic content and temporal placement (Bhattacharyya et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; Shi et al., 2026). Although a good starting point, these approaches treat refer-ence interactions as fixed targets, overlooking the inherent flexibility of situated guidance. In prac-tice, human-human guidance is fluid and adaptive. Certain situations, such as incorrect actions or im-minent safety risks, require immediate intervention, while others allow a wider range of appropriate tim-ing for proactive assistance. Similarly, backchan-nels and acknowledgments help maintain shared understanding, but their timing and frequency can vary considerably. This creates a fundamental mis-match between rigid reference-based evaluation and the flexible nature of real-world task guidance, calling for evaluation criteria that can take into account both the correctness of guidance and the flexibility of communication. Addressing this challenge requires a principled framework for characterizing the flexibility of sit-uated guidance. Importantly, flexibility does not imply unconstrained behavior. Effective guidance 1arXiv:2611.26491v1 [cs.AI] 19 Nov 2026 must remain grounded in perceptual and interaction evidence, with different communicative behaviors subject to different requirements. When AI as-sistants decide to intervene or provide guidance, some interventions (e.g., correcting a user mistake or giving a warning about a potential hazard) are necessary within narrow temporal windows, while others (e.g., backchannel) are optional and allow greater flexibility in content and timing. Human references remain valuable for identifying effective guidance behaviors, but should not prescribe a sin-gle correct response at a fixed moment. Instead, the key is to characterize the underlying evidence that warrants communication and the conditions under which different responses are appropriate. This evidence-grounded perspective provides a founda-tion for both evaluating and generating flexible guidance behaviors. In this paper, we introduce FLUID ( Fl exible, Evidence-Grounded Situated Task G uid ance), a unified framework for characterizing, evaluat-ing, and enabling flexible interaction dynamics in multimodal task guidance. First, we develop an evidence-grounded representation that char-acterizes communicative behaviors in terms of their necessity, supporting evidence, and tem-poral flexibility ( FLUID-Modeling ). Second, we introduce an evaluation framework that lever-ages human references while moving beyond strict response matching to assess the appropri-ateness of guidance content and timing ( FLUID-Evaluation ). Third, we propose an agent architec-ture that explicitly models and adapts to evolving interaction dynamics, enabling flexible, evidence-grounded decisions about when and how to com-municate, including reactive responses, proac-tive guidance, and appropriate silence ( FLUID-Agent ). Together, these contributions provide a systematic approach to understanding, evaluat-ing, and generating flexible, evidence-grounded guidance, advancing multimodal AI assistants toward more natural and adaptive human–AI interaction. 

2 Related Work 

Real-Time Interaction. Recent advances in mul-timodal systems have rapidly expanded real-time human-AI interaction, from streaming audio-visual perception (OpenAI, 2026; Qwen Team, 2026a) to low-latency, full-duplex spoken interaction (Cui et al., 2026; Thinking Machines Lab, 2026; Group, 2026; ByteDance Seed, 2026; Google DeepMind, 2026). Scaling such interactive capabilities has increasingly relied on synthetic or automatically constructed interaction data, often by repurposing existing egocentric videos (Grauman et al., 2022; Peddi et al., 2024; Damen et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2024) and generating intervention traces at scale (Shi et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025a; Kundu et al., 2026). In contrast, naturally oc-curring human-human interactions in which one person continuously observes and guides another through a physical task remain scarce. Existing efforts include WTaG (Bao et al., 2023), Holo-Assist (Wang et al., 2023), EgoInstruct (Sakai et al., 2025), Ego-EXTRA (Ragusa et al., 2026), and Ego-MC-Bench (Bhattacharyya et al., 2026), but some of them remain limited in released modalities. In this work, we primarily build on WTaG and HoloAssist, both of which provide audio alongside egocentric video, to study how interactions naturally unfold during human guid-ance. 

Proactive Agent Modeling. Existing proactive video agents span query-conditioned event re-sponse, continuous commentary, and situated pro-cedural guidance (Wang et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2025; Qian et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a). Representative systems typically pro-duce textual responses or discrete decisions about whether to intervene, with timing supervision de-rived from annotated event boundaries, task tran-sitions, mistakes, or synthesized utterances (Pan-chal et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023, 2025b; Bhat-tacharyya et al., 2025; Kundu et al., 2026). Recent methods move beyond purely event-triggered poli-cies: Pro²Assist additionally annotates assistance need, while Vinci2 generates visually grounded service opportunities and reasons about whether in-tervention is warranted (Xu et al., 2026; Gong et al., 2026). They provide increasingly rich supervision; however, their assistance targets are constructed from task states or annotated events rather than nat-urally occurring intervention decisions. In contrast, we study human-human guidance. This preserves a broader range of communicative behaviors and enables intervention-specific annotations of neces-sity and valid timing, characterizing not only when assistance may occur but how much flexibility each interaction permits. 23 FLUID-Modeling: Grounded Evidence in Task Guidance 

3.1 Problem Definition 

In real-time task guidance, an agent continuously observes a user performing a task and must decide how to respond appropriately as the task and inter-action evolve. We characterize each intervention decision along four dimensions. WHY : what ev-idence warrants the intervention, namely the user utterance, action, or state that the guide reacts to; we refer to it as the grounded evidence of the intervention. WHETHER : whether the interven-tion is necessary, optional, or not needed. WHEN :the period during which the intervention is use-ful. WHAT : the interaction act of the intervention and its content. Interventions triggered by a user utterance are reactive; all others are proactive. 

3.2 Source Data 

We study WTaG (Bao et al., 2023) and Holo-Assist (Wang et al., 2023), two egocentric datasets of naturally occurring human-human task guid-ance in which a remote instructor observes the user’s view and provides verbal guidance. The two datasets exhibit complementary interaction regimes. WTaG is relatively self-paced: users receive a high-level task recipe in advance and can often proceed independently, which produces mixed-initiative interaction. HoloAssist has a stronger knowledge asymmetry and is predomi-nantly instructor-led, with users relying on ongoing guidance for each step. In total, we select 43 WTaG sessions and 72 HoloAssist sessions with substan-tive interactions between users and instructors. 

3.3 Multi-Layer Annotation 

To reveal the interaction semantics underlying nat-urally occurring human interventions, we augment WTaG and HoloAssist with four linked annotation layers, which together cover the four dimensions of Section 3.1 (see Figure 2 for an example). 

Layer 1: Utterance Taxonomy. We catego-rize every user and instructor utterance using an utterance-level taxonomy with 11 user categories and 13 instructor categories. 

Layer 2: Action and State. We annotate user ac-tions and states that are closely related to instructor interventions, including hand actions, visual loss, stalling, and searching. Each action is further la-beled by whether it is valid or deviating and by its effect on task execution, such as task progress, step completion, or recovery. 

Layer 3: Trigger Link. Using the annotated ut-terances and actions, we link each instructor inter-vention to the preceding utterance, action, or state that triggered it, forming a grounded evidence link that explains why the human instructor de-cided to intervene. Each link receives a trigger type derived from the linked events, such as Step Done , Error , or User Query . We further catego-rize each intervention as necessary if, without it, the user would make a mistake, get stuck, or lack needed information; optional if the user would pro-ceed correctly on their own, so that speaking and remaining silent are both acceptable; and excluded 

if it is irrelevant to the task, such as self-repairs, or relies on knowledge that cannot be obtained from the shared egocentric view, the dialogue, or the task knowledge. 

Layer 4: Evidence Onset and Intervention Win-dow. We annotate temporal anchor points for in-terventions, which define the time window within which an intervention should be initiated. For reac-tive interventions, we annotate (1) the time at which the user finishes their most recent utterance, tusr ,and (2) the latest time at which the intervention remains useful, tlatest . For proactive interventions, there is no corresponding tusr ; instead, we annotate (1) the earliest time at which initial signs suggest that an intervention may be necessary, tearly , and (2) the time at which enough evidence becomes available to determine that the intervention is nec-essary, tevid , together with tlatest . Together, these anchors define an evidence-derived window for evaluating intervention timing. 

Annotation Workflow. For WTaG, we first use majority voting across multiple language-model annotators to produce initial annotations, followed by careful manual review of all instances. Based on the errors and practical observations identified during this process, we refine the machine annota-tion pipeline and apply it to the selected HoloAssist sessions, and manually review about 10% of the generated annotations to ensure the synthetic data quality.

Similar situations may or may not require in-tervention. Among step completions on the self-paced WTaG, only 12.9% are followed by a nec-essary intervention (e.g., providing the next-step instruction), while 17.5% are optional (e.g., “Nice” or “uh-huh”) and 69.6% receive no intervention (n = 418 ). In contrast, existing approaches often use a more rigid event-to-response mapping, such as triggering a next-step instruction whenever a step is completed. These observations motivate evaluating guidance against the grounded evidence that warrants an in-tervention, whether intervention is necessary, and when it remains useful. 

4 FLUID-Evaluation on Task Guidance 

Existing reference-based evaluations assess guid-ance primarily through content similarity and tem-poral proximity to human reference utterances, of-ten using a fixed timing window (Zhang et al., 2025a; Bhattacharyya et al., 2025, 2026). Such cri-teria overlook the flexibility of natural interaction, where appropriate guidance depends on task evi-dence and may vary in necessity and timing. We in-troduce an evidence-derived evaluation framework that leverages our annotations to evaluate interven-tion appropriateness based on supporting evidence, necessity, and valid timing windows. Appendix C specifies the protocol and metrics in full. 

4.1 Intervention Matching 

Real-time interaction does not follow fixed turn boundaries: within any time interval, a system may produce zero, one, or multiple utterances, whereas evaluation requires a one-to-one correspondence between system utterances and annotated human interventions. We therefore first match them based on content and supporting evidence. For each nec-essary or optional intervention, we consider candi-date system utterances within a matching window that covers its annotated evidence window and the surrounding intervals of adjacent reference utter-ances. An LLM judge then assesses content equiv-alence based on the dialogue history and triggering evidence. Since a system may generate multiple equivalent utterances within the same time interval, we select the earliest qualifying utterance as the match. 

4.2 Timing Appropriateness 

Evaluating the timing of matched interventions is essential, yet common approaches (Zhang et al., 2025a; Bhattacharyya et al., 2025, 2026) have two limitations: (1) fixed timing windows ignore when an intervention becomes possible and when it stops being useful, admitting invalid onsets and exclud-ing valid ones; and (2) binary in-window scoring treats all onsets within a window as equally appro-priate, although their usefulness varies with timing. 4For the first limitation , we derive intervention-specific valid windows from the temporal anchors annotated in Section 3.3: [tusr , t latest ) for reactive guidance and [tearly , t latest ) for proactive guidance. Unlike a fixed timing window around the reference utterance, each window opens after the user’s turn ends or at the first sign of need, and closes when the intervention is no longer useful. An onset is valid if it falls anywhere within the window of the corresponding intervention. We validate these windows with a human study that isolates timing from content. For each of 79 WTaG instructor utterances, we keep the utter-ance fixed and sample six candidate onsets around its annotated anchors, spanning early, in-window, and late times and including the instructor’s ac-tual onset. Three researchers rate how appropriate it would be to deliver the same utterance at each onset on a 0-10 scale (See Appendix G for more details). We treat an onset as acceptable when its mean human rating is at least 6, and compare this label with whether the onset falls within the pro-posed intervention window. We report Macro-F1 to measure how well the window classification agrees with human judgments across acceptable and un-acceptable timings. Overall, our evidence-derived windows outperform the fixed [−6, +3] s window around reference utterance onsets adopted from ProAssist (Zhang et al., 2025a) (Macro-F1: 0.788 

vs. 0.713 ). The improvement is more pronounced for reactive guidance, where our windows exclude inappropriate onsets that would interrupt the user, while for proactive guidance, our windows achieve comparable performance while admitting earlier evidence-supported onsets missed by the fixed win-dow. For the second limitation, we introduce a contin-uous timing score that captures varying degrees of timing appropriateness. The score peaks at a pre-ferred response time tpref and decreases for earlier or later onsets. For reactive guidance, tpref = tusr ,since responding immediately after the user fin-ishes speaking is preferred; for proactive guidance, 

tpref = tevid , when sufficient evidence becomes available. An earlier utterance lacks supporting evidence, and a later one becomes less useful as the task progresses. We start with a simple linear score that decreases from tpref toward the window bound-aries. For each utterance, we compute Spearman’s correlation between the scores assigned to the six candidate onsets and their human ratings. Aver-aged across utterances, the linear score achieves a correlation of 0.83 , compared with 0.65 for binary in-window scoring. We further refine the shape of the timing penalty. Intervention Precision and Turn-Taking. 

Timely Correctness measures whether necessary guidance is delivered correctly and on time, but frequent interventions may improve coverage at the cost of unnecessary or disruptive speech. We therefore introduce Intervention Precision , the proportion of all played system utterances that cor-rectly match a necessary or optional intervention within its valid window. Optional interventions are counted as correct when produced, while remaining silent for an optional intervention is not penalized. Unmatched, repeated, incorrect, or mistimed utterances lower this score. Additionally, we report Speech Overlap Ratio , the proportion of user-speaking time overlapping with system speech, to measure potential disruptions to natural turn-taking. 

5 FLUID-Agent: From Interaction Dynamics to Real-Time Guidance 

Motivated by the interaction dynamics character-ized in Section 3, FLUID-Agent is a real-time sys-tem for flexible, evidence-grounded task guidance. As shown in Figure 3, it uses an asynchronous agen-tic architecture built around a single Qwen3.6-35B-A3B vision-language model (Qwen Team, 2026b), which serves perception, utterance understanding, and intervention reasoning through task-specific prompting. The components communicate through a shared task memory that tracks task-relevant ev-idence and interaction context over time (see Ap-pendix D for implementation details). 

Perception and Task Memory. The perception module continuously analyzes keyframes sampled from short temporal windows of the live video stream. Guided by task knowledge and the cur-rent task state, it tracks relevant user actions, states, and task progress. These observations, together with user and system utterances and step-level com-pletion status, are maintained in a continuously up-dated shared task memory, providing grounded task and interaction context for subsequent intervention decisions. 

User Utterance Understanding. Not every user utterance calls for a response. The user utterance understanding module uses the utterance taxon-omy and task context to distinguish utterances that require a response from those that do not, such as task narration, progress reports, and acknowl-edgments. This enables FLUID-Agent to respond when needed while avoiding unnecessary interrup-tions. 

Evidence-Conditioned Intervention Decision. 

Each potential intervention is grounded in evidence from the ongoing task and interaction, such as an observed error, action progress, or user request. The intervention module uses this evidence and the interaction history to reason about whether, when, and how to provide guidance. As the task evolves, FLUID-Agent reassesses pending inter-ventions, providing evidence-grounded guidance when appropriate or remaining silent when it is no longer warranted. 

Asynchronous Execution. FLUID-Agent coor-dinates perception, utterance understanding, and intervention reasoning asynchronously, allowing each component to process new information as it becomes available. The system continues moni-toring the task while speaking, yields to new user requests, and cancels pending guidance when it is no longer relevant.

6.2 Main Results 

We evaluate all models alongside the original hu-man instructor guidance from both datasets under the FLUID-Evaluation framework. As shown in Ta-ble 1, human instructors substantially outperform all evaluated models, establishing a human per-formance baseline. By incorporating interaction dynamics, FLUID-Agent generally achieves more timely and precise interventions than other models. However, Gemini exhibits less speech overlap on both datasets, suggesting that effective turn-taking remains a challenge for FLUID-Agent. Substan-tial gaps remain between FLUID-Agent and hu-man instructors, particularly in proactive Timely Correctness (below 0.07 vs. above 0.88) and In-tervention Precision (below 0.17 vs. above 0.83). These results highlight the remaining challenges in providing timely and appropriate guidance. 

Evidence-grounded evaluation captures inter-vention quality beyond reference matching. 

Systems with similar LLM-Judged F1 can exhibit substantially different interaction behavior. On WTaG, Gemini and VideoLLM achieve compara-ble F1 scores (0.127 vs. 0.138), yet Gemini has much higher Intervention Precision and substan-tially less speech overlap. Similarly, on HoloAssist, FLUID-Agent and JoyAI achieve nearly identical F1 (0.099 vs. 0.098), while FLUID-Agent achieves higher proactive Timely Correctness and Interven-tion Precision. By accounting for intervention ne-cessity and evidence-derived timing windows, our evaluation reveals differences in intervention qual-ity that reference-matching metrics cannot capture. 

Fine-grained evaluation reveals interaction-specific strengths and failures. Figure 4 reports correctness by trigger type and instructor utterance type. FLUID-Agent leads across multiple cate-gories on both datasets. Across systems, correct-ness is generally higher for explicit user queries, but remains low for proactive interventions requir-ing an understanding of task progress and user be-havior. Correctness remains low for Error and 

Stuck triggers, as well as Question to User utter-ances that require seeking missing information. These results highlight the challenge of ground-ing proactive interventions in task evidence and deciding when and what to say, motivating our di-agnostic analysis in Section 6.3.

7 Discussion and Conclusion 

Recent advances in vision and language foundation models as well as agentic architecture have made it feasible to develop perceptual agents that can pro-vide real-time task guidance to help people acquire new skills and complete physical tasks. However, evaluating perceptual guidance agents remains a significant challenge due to the inherent flexibility of instructional dialogue, where appropriate guid-ance can vary in content and timing. To address this issue, we developed FLUID, a framework that evaluates not only the accuracy of generated guidance but also the flexibility of AI agents in providing different types of guidance based on users’ needs and task contexts. FLUID grounds its evaluation metrics in human instruc-tor guidance, focusing on the evidence supporting the guidance, its necessity, and the appropriate-ness of its timing. Our current FLUID framework contains instructional interaction data, fine-grained annotation and evaluation protocols. It can be used to support systematic and comparative evaluation of AI agents’ guidance capabilities. Our experi-mental results show that, while human instructors achieve high performance under FLUID metrics, state-of-the-art AI models still lag significantly be-hind. These findings highlight the substantial gap between perceptual agents’ capabilities and profi-cient human instruction. For tasks that require specialized procedures, such as special daily tasks relevant to different peo-ple, FLUID provides a pipeline to establish evalu-ation metrics based on a few examples of natural interaction sessions between an instructor and a follower. FLUID can then provide an initial as-sessment of agent behavior and reliability before deploying agents in real-world user tasks. Evaluating real-time guidance systems is an ex-tremely challenging problem. Ultimately, these agents must be evaluated through real-time interac-tions with actual users. However, such evaluations are prohibitively expensive and difficult to scale. FLUID provides a way to bootstrap the develop-ment and evaluation of agents’ guidance capabili-ties before moving to real-world user studies. We hope FLUID will offer new perspectives on how to evaluate real-time guidance systems and moti-vate the development of perceptual guidance agents capable of providing flexible, contextually appro-priate, and timely guidance comparable to that of skilled human instructors. 

Limitations 

Our annotations primarily recover the grounded evi-dence underlying observed instructor interventions, which provides a strong reference for evaluation but does not exhaust all potentially valid interac-tion behaviors. In particular, the absence of an intervention in the demonstration does not imply that no intervention would have been appropriate at that moment. We also do not explicitly model silent moments, partly because WTaG and Holo-Assist contain few cases where speaking is clearly prohibited; many additional utterances could be non-harmful as long as they do not disrupt task progress. Identifying such unobserved but poten-tially valid intervention opportunities, as well as moments where silence is truly required, involves more subjective judgments and is left for future work. 9Our framework supports a broad range of proac-tive and reactive assistance behaviors in complex task interactions. However, more fluid and dy-namic interaction scenarios, such as full-duplex perception and generation, remain challenging, as overlapping perception and response generation are difficult to support naturally within a modu-lar system. In the datasets, we do observe several dynamic interactive behaviors, including backchan-neling, overlapping speech, and visual barge-ins, and some of these are partially reflected in our eval-uation. However, a comprehensive characteriza-tion and evaluation of richer full-duplex interaction is beyond the scope of this work. Future work could study these behaviors more systematically, supported by interaction data with denser and more diverse back-and-forth dynamics.

A.2 Multimodal Preprocessing and Synchronization 

Video, audio, and annotation timestamps are mea-sured in seconds from the start of each recording. Video is sampled at 8 Hz using presentation times-tamps, taking the latest frame available at each sampling time. For the transcripts, we manually revise the WTaG transcripts to correct speech boundaries and restore missing utterances, and keep HoloAssist’s released transcripts and timing. These speaker-labeled transcripts are used for annotation and eval-uation; at runtime, user text comes from streaming automatic speech recognition (ASR) instead. For the audio, instructor speech is muted, except where it overlaps user speech. There it is left in place to preserve the user’s signal, so the instructor may remain audible in overlap regions. Recordings are replayed continuously. Streaming ASR pro-duces partial transcripts during an utterance and a final transcript once the utterance ends. These out-puts become available only after the corresponding audio has been received and processed. 

A.3 Dataset Statistics and Interaction Characteristics 

HoloAssist sessions are shorter than WTaG ses-sions but contain denser instructor guidance. The median session duration is 265.8 s in HoloAssist and 594.0 s in WTaG, while instructors speak 8.25 and 3.21 turns per minute on average per session, with a median interval between adjacent instructor utterances of 1.99 s and 7.19 s, respectively. 12 The two datasets also differ in the type of guid-ance (Figure 5). HoloAssist has a higher proportion of instructions, while WTaG has higher proportions of answers, approvals, and corrections. This pat-tern is consistent with denser ongoing instruction in HoloAssist and more frequent answers and cor-rections in WTaG. Correction labels describe the instructor’s guidance, not the frequency of actual task errors. The necessary interventions in the released an-notations show a corresponding difference in how guidance is initiated. In WTaG, 66.1% are reac-tive and 33.9% proactive; in HoloAssist, 31.4% and 68.6%. WTaG therefore puts more weight on responding to users, while HoloAssist more often requires guidance that is not prompted by a user request. 

B Evidence-Grounded Annotation Protocol 

The annotations connect each instructor interven-tion to the user speech, visible actions, and task states that support it. This appendix defines the four annotation layers, including the rules for in-tervention necessity and timing, then describes the annotation workflow and the review procedures for WTaG and HoloAssist, and closes with boundary cases and a worked example. 

B.1 Annotation Overview and Label Schema 

We annotate 43 WTaG and 72 HoloAssist test ses-sions with the four linked layers of Section 3.3. 

Layer 1: Utterance Taxonomy assigns each turn a communicative-function label and at most one attribute. Layer 2: Action and State locates visible task evidence in time and records its validity and task effect. Layer 3: Trigger Link connects each instructor intervention to its supporting evidence and records its trigger type and intervention status. 

Layer 4: Evidence Onset and Intervention Window 

records the temporal anchors that define when the intervention should begin. Three additional WTaG sessions provide task knowledge only and are not annotated or evaluated. The unit of annotation for speech is the utter-ance unit , one speaker contribution in the cleaned transcript. Fragments of one contribution that were split by speech recognition or transcript cleaning are merged before labeling. A contribution is split into several units only when its functions are clearly separable, for example There is already water in it. 

(U7) and Should I pour it out? (U2); otherwise it receives the label of its main communicative func-tion. 

B.2 Layer 1: Utterance Taxonomy 

This layer labels the communicative function of each utterance, that is, what the speaker is asking, reporting, or responding to. Users and instructors have separate label sets, and labels are assigned from the transcript and the conversational context. For brief responses, the position of the turn in the interaction also matters, since it separates acknowl-edgments from backchannels (U5 vs. U10; I10 vs. I11). 

B.2.1 User Utterance Taxonomy 

Eleven labels describe user turns: requests, re-sponses, task reports, and speech with little or no task-related content (Table 5). When more than one label could apply, annotators check the label-ing rules in the order U10, U9, U11, U8, U3, U2, U1, U4, U5, U7, U6 and use the first one that fits. An incomplete utterance that clearly asks a ques-tion is not labeled U9 but receives one of the three question labels: U3 asks about something the in-structor recently said, U2 proposes an answer about the task or the world and asks whether it is correct, and U1 asks for information without proposing an answer. Appendix B.7.1 explains how these labels are separated when the interpretation depends on context. 

B.2.2 Instructor Utterance Taxonomy 

Thirteen labels describe instructor turns (Table 6). They record what the instructor is doing in the conversation, regardless of whether the content is correct. When more than one label could apply, annotators check the labeling rules in the order I12, I13, I8, I5, I9, I6, I2, I1, I4, I3, I7, I11, I10 and use the first one that fits. A correction is therefore labeled as a correction even if it also expresses approval or answers a question, and information given in response to a user request is labeled as an answer rather than as an instruction offered without a request. For turns with little substantive content, the label depends on timing: I11 while the user is still speaking or performing an action, and I10 after the user has finished.

B.3.1 Action Vocabulary 

WTaG action descriptions use task-specific vocab-ularies developed from the recipes and checked against the videos. Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct 15 generated initial verb, noun, and adjective lists from the task steps, their execution variants, and the 48 HoloAssist verbs as a reference, and then checked sampled frames from 506 video segments for missing words. Annotators reviewed the words supported by at least two segments and merged synonyms. Compound objects are represented by a noun and an adjective, such as jar with nut_ butter ; locations and hand use are recorded sepa-rately. A final video check with Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct added remove and shake and found no missing nouns. The resulting vocabulary contains 83 verbs, 120 nouns, and 107 adjectives. Observa-tions outside the vocabulary are retained as out-of-vocabulary descriptions rather than forced into an unsuitable category. 

B.3.2 Action-Candidate Generation and Human Review 

WTaG action annotations combine model propos-als with a human scan of the full task timeline. Four models independently propose intervals and descriptions: Qwen3.5-27B, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Qwen3.8-27B, and Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct. Tem-porally clustered proposals supported by at least two models enter review, including incompatible proposals that meet this threshold; the votes decide only which candidates are reviewed, not their valid-ity or boundaries. Reviewers resolve the candidates using the video and task context and add manual 

pins for missed events, which follow the same re-view procedure. Any candidate may be accepted, edited, merged, split, or rejected: of 2,611 model proposals, 665 were kept unchanged, 1,423 were kept after editing, and 523 were rejected. After this review, a separate model-based pass assigns valid-ity and task effect to the reviewed actions using the task context and the annotation guidelines.

is the earliest frame showing a specific sign that the intervention may be needed, even if the condition is not yet certain; entering a task step alone does not establish ES. Evidence Onset (EO) is the earliest frame that makes the condition unambiguous and complete enough to justify the intervention; the whole object need not be visible, but its identity and task relation must be supported. Latest Useful Time (LU) is the exclusive boundary after which the target intervention is no longer useful on the recorded trajectory. The valid window is [ES , LU) ,with the timing score peaking at EO (Section 4.2). 

Utterance-Triggered Interventions. For a reac-tive intervention, including one with MIXED evi-dence, the first eligible time is the Response-Ready Time (R) , the end of the completed user turn in the trigger evidence; ES and EO are not separately an-notated for utterance-triggered interventions. Par-tial speech does not set R unless the utterance is already semantically complete and only a delayed transcription endpoint remains. The reactive win-dow is [R , LU) .

Operational Definition of LU. LU is the earlier of task expiry and dialogue cutoff on the recorded trajectory: 

LU = min(task -expiry , dialogue -cutoff) .

Task expiry occurs when the user completes the relevant action, starts self-correction or recovery, or reaches a state in which the intervention would have to be reformulated. Dialogue cutoff is the start of the first acknowledgment, answer, or down-stream action that presupposes the target interven-tion, since inserting the intervention later would reverse the recorded conversational order. Unre-lated speech does not set LU. For self-correction, LU marks its start rather than its completion. 

Frame Selection and Precision. Annotators scrub coarsely, then step frame by frame around each boundary and select the earliest frame that satisfies the definition. Stored timestamps are in seconds at the source video’s frame resolution and are not rounded to whole seconds. 

B.6 Annotation Workflow and Quality Control 

Annotation proceeds layer by layer. Annotators first review segmentation, transcripts, and utterance labels, then action candidates and manual pins, and finally trigger links, intervention statuses, and tem-poral anchors. Completed upstream layers stay visible during review, and errors found in them are resolved with the annotator responsible. The three annotators are authors and received no addi-tional compensation. They developed the rubrics together, annotated a shared session, and discussed disagreements before annotation began, and guide-line changes led to re-review of the affected anno-tations. B.6.2 Release and Reproducibility Notes 

Every utterance, action/state, trigger link, and tim-ing record has a stable ID, and records refer to one another by these IDs rather than by matching tran-script text. To reproduce or extend the annotations, follow the controlled values and boundary rules above, and leave uncertain cases explicitly marked, for example with AMBIGUOUS validity or an audit flag, rather than assigning a confident label.

B.7.1 Utterance-Label Boundary Cases 

A turn’s role in the exchange determines its label, even when its wording is the same. The rules below separate requests, uptake, and task feedback. 

U1 versus U2. Use U2 only when the user pro-poses a concrete candidate that can be affirmed or rejected; otherwise use U1. Thus Which spoon should I use? is U1, whereas Should I use this spoon? is U2. A declarative such as That should be enough is U2 when it functions as a bid for validation. 18 U2 versus U3. Use U3 when the object of the request is the instructor’s recent words themselves; a question about the task world is U2 even if it fol-lows an instruction. What do you mean by pinch? 

is U3 with repair_target=meaning , and Thirty seconds, you mean? is U3 with task_detail ,whereas a delayed check of remembered content, Ithink you said it was a teaspoon earlier, right? , is U2. 

U4, U5, U9, and U10. Okay or yeah is U4 when it answers an instructor question, U5 when it takes up a completed instructor turn, U10 when it occurs while the instructor is still speaking, and U9 when it is self-directed. An echo is U5 when the user closes it ( Twelve ounces? Okay. ) and U3 when the user waits for confirmation ( Twelve ounces? ). 

I1, I2, I3, and I9. I1 supplies content requested explicitly or implicitly by the user; the same con-tent is I3 when volunteered as a required step. For a proposed candidate, I2 accepts it and I9 rejects or replaces it. Thus The teaspoon answers Which spoon? as I1, while Yes after This teaspoon? is I2 and No, use a teaspoon after Should I use a spoon? 

is I9. 

I3 versus I4 versus I9. I3 is required guidance that presupposes no error; I4 is optional or caution-ary guidance with no existing error ( Be careful not to add too much salt ); I9 repairs an actual, pro-posed, imminent, or omitted error ( You added too much salt; remove some ). C.5 Reference Fidelity Metrics 

Similarity F1 and LLM-Judged F1 follow Zhang et al. (2025a) and are computed over all instructor utterances of the test set. For Similarity F1, texts are encoded with the all-mpnet-base-v2 sen-tence encoder, pairs are assigned one-to-one by a cost that combines the time offset with the cosine distance, and an assigned pair is correct when the cosine similarity exceeds 0.5. For LLM-Judged F1, 20 pairs are pre-filtered to each reference’s and each candidate’s four most similar partners, graded by the content judge (Appendix C.8), and assigned one-to-one at grade ≥ 4 with credit 1 (grade 5) or 0.75 (grade 4) so as to maximize total credit. Precision divides the correct pairs (or credit) by the number of played utterances and recall by the number of references.

C.9 Aggregation and Statistical Analysis 

Every metric is pooled over the test sessions of a dataset: event metrics over events, Intervention Precision over played utterances, the Speech Over-lap Ratio over summed user speaking time, the F1 scores from pooled precision and recall, and Response Latency as the pooled median. Every reported value carries a 95% session-cluster boot-strap interval (10,000 resamples of sessions; tables print the half-width of the 2.5–97.5 percentile in-terval). ProAssist, VideoLLM, JoyAI, and FLUID-Agent were each run twice under the same con-figuration and the reported values are the mean of the two runs; Gemini and the two ablation variants of Table 3 were run once. A difference between two systems or variants is computed on identical session sets as the pooled difference with a paired session-cluster bootstrap interval. 

D FLUID-Agent Implementation 

This appendix describes FLUID-Agent (Section 5, Figure 3) as run in every experiment, with one configuration on both datasets. A single frozen Qwen3.6-35B-A3B vision-language model (FP8 weights, vLLM, temperature 0) serves every on-line role through task-specific prompting: it per-ceives the scene, judges the task state, interprets and answers user turns, and generates spoken inter-vention utterances. Deterministic code around the model maintains the shared task memory, sched-ules the calls, evaluates the evidence conditions under which an intervention is opened, and man-ages turn-taking. The model therefore supplies the evidence, and the agentic structure decides what that evidence licenses. 22 Session Loop. When a visual change is detected, the perception module analyzes the video and adds an observation report to the task memory. The Sit-uation Tracker (the task-state tracking part of the Task Memory block in Figure 3) reassesses the task state when the observed activity or the dialogue has changed, and the intervention conditions are re-evaluated after each judgment. In parallel, each completed user turn is handled by one call that in-terprets it and, when a reply is due, answers in the same model call. Under the teacher-forced protocol (Appendix C.2), released instructor utterances are visible to perception, to the Tracker as testimony of what the user was told, and to utterance under-standing; the system’s own outputs do not enter its prompts. 

D.1 Perception and Task-State Tracking Task Knowledge Compilation. An offline com-piler (Qwen3.8-27B, distinct from the online back-bone) reads the same task knowledge that every baseline receives (Appendix E.1) and produces an action-level plan: each step becomes a few impera-tive actions with the tools and destinations the step names, its stated quantities, an observable comple-tion check where the knowledge states one, and the step’s prerequisite relations. Task-specific pro-cedural knowledge reaches the online model only through this plan, which supplies the checks the perception module answers, the reference for as-sessing the user’s progress through the task plan and detecting deviations, and the content of inter-vention utterances. The dialogue and the visual observations supply the task context of the mo-ment. 

Perception Calls. A novelty signal computed from DINOv2 features of the incoming frames trig-gers a perception call at a novelty peak, during sus-tained activity, or after a fixed interval. The percep-tion call receives recent keyframes together with the dialogue associated with the current step and its completion checks, and reports the user’s hand activity, the current action, visible state changes, the check values, and per-step progress, marking whether each value was seen or inferred from the dialogue. 

Task Memory. Perception reports are determinis-tically integrated into a belief state that tracks task progress, committed completion checks, and recent evidence for each step. A check value is committed when consecutive perception calls agree, a commit-ted completion is retained while the check is out of view, and the memory records per step how often the action associated with that step was observed. The Tracker, the intervention conditions, and utter-ance understanding read this belief state rather than individual reports. 

Situation Tracker. The Tracker is a text-only call. It runs after a perception update has been in-tegrated into the belief state when the observed activity has changed, when a user turn or a re-leased instructor utterance has arrived since its last judgment, when a pending intervention candidate awaits confirmation, or otherwise after a bounded interval, with a minimum spacing between calls. From the plan, its previous judgment, the step ev-idence of the belief state, and the recent reports interleaved with the dialogue, it returns the cur-rent step and action, each step’s state with its basis (observed, moved past, or the user’s testimony), at most one deviation with whether it matters, and wait and stuck states. It weighs evidence conser-vatively: the user’s behavior rather than the plan order defines the user’s position in the task plan, a deviation requires agreeing consecutive reports or one report plus the user’s own words, and in-structor utterances count as testimony of what the user was told, not as evidence of what was done. A consequential deviation is accepted only when consecutive judgments agree, and a stuck state only when it persists; the resulting position in the task plan sets the checks of the next perception call. 

D.2 Proactive and Reactive Intervention Decisions Evidence-Conditioned Proactive Decision. Af-ter each accepted judgment, a fixed set of interven-tion conditions is checked against the current belief state. When a condition is satisfied, the agent cre-ates a typed intervention candidate (typed by the trigger types of Section 3.3) grounded in the cur-rent evidence, which may subsequently be spoken, held, or discarded as that evidence develops. Cor-rection opens on an accepted consequential devia-tion and states the contrast between what the user is doing and what the action calls for. Progress coaching opens at the onset of an action with a stated amount, manner, or critical parameter while the user is manipulating task objects and no devia-tion is open. Completion and hand-off opens when a step is reliably done: the final step receives a clos-ing confirmation, and a completed non-final step is 23 followed by the next step’s instruction unless that step has already been given, by the system or by a released instructor utterance, or has already started or passed. Stall help opens on a persisting stuck judgment. A ledger makes sure that the same evi-dence is voiced only once per visit to a step, and a pending candidate is discarded when its evidence is withdrawn or its window closes. The content of an intervention utterance is assembled from the plan (and, for corrections, from the Tracker’s deviation description, which states what the user is doing and what the action calls for), and one short model call rephrases it as a spoken sentence. 

Taxonomy-Guided Reactive Decision. Each fi-nal ASR transcript with speech content is handled by one model call; final transcripts without words and very short acknowledgment or filler transcripts are filtered without invoking the model. The call labels the turn with one of the user-utterance cat-egories of Table 5 and, for labels that call for a reply, continues in the same model call with the answer. The label places the turn in one of three re-sponse tiers: requests for information, verification, or clarification and trouble reports are answered. Narration and answers to a pending question are voiced only when the user’s turn states something that can be checked against the task context and the model produces a brief confirmation or correction. Plain narration receives no reply, and the remaining labels are silent. A reply is one or two short spoken sentences grounded in the plan, the latest situation judgment, and the recent dialogue, and a failed call produces no response. 

Turn-Taking and Speech. While the system speaks, incoming partial ASR transcripts are checked: a filler-only partial transcript lets it con-tinue, and a question cue or a label requiring a reply causes the system to defer to the user. Deferring stops the current sentence, holds queued proac-tive responses until the user has finished (dropping those whose step is no longer current), and can-cels unstarted answers. Sentences are synthesized by the shared speech server (Appendix E.1) and played one after another while perception contin-ues. 

D.3 Ablation Implementation 

The two variants of Section 6.4 run the final sys-tem with one switch each. Each replaces one deci-sion mechanism, together with the control logic at-tached to it, by a direct decision of the same model on the evidence that mechanism used, while the backbone, perception, task memory, and Situation Tracker stay those of the final system. The contrasts therefore show what the taxonomy-guided reactive decision and the evidence-conditioned proactive de-cision contribute to the system’s selectivity. They do not isolate the contribution of any single rule, since each variant also differs from the final system in the control logic noted below. 

Direct Reactive. This variant replaces the taxonomy-guided utterance understanding (the cat-egory label, the response tiers, and the filler guard) by one direct decision of the model on the same turn context. The prompt keeps the reply rules with-out reference to the taxonomy, states no preference for silence, and asks the model to decide whether the turn needs a spoken reply now and, if so, to write it in the same call. Turn-taking on partial hypotheses and the proactive side are unchanged. 

Direct Proactive. This variant replaces the evidence-conditioned proactive decision (the in-tervention conditions, the candidate ledger, and the templated content with its phrasing step) by one di-rect decision of the model every 2 s of session time, skipped while a previous decision is still being computed. The decision reads the same evidence products (the plan, the latest Tracker judgment, the step evidence, and the recent reports with the di-alogue) together with the utterances the user has recently heard, and returns either a response text, spoken as written, or nothing. Proactive utterances are thus generated on a fixed schedule rather than on evidence-triggered candidates, and a newer ut-terance supersedes an older one that has not started playing; replies to user turns and speech already playing are unaffected. The reactive side is un-changed.

G Human Timing Study 

This study tests the annotated timing windows and calibrates the graded credit used by Timely Cor-rectness. Three raters judged the timing of WTaG interventions whose content was held fixed while only the speech start time was varied, so that the rat-ings reflect timing rather than wording. We first de-scribe the study events, the candidate moments, and the rating procedure, then compare the annotated windows with the human judgments of acceptable timing, and finally compare temporal scoring func-tions and select their parameters with leave-one-session-out (LOSO) cross-validation. Throughout, the anchors are written as in Appendix B.5: ES, EO, LU, and R correspond to tearly , tevid , tlatest ,and tusr of the main text and Appendix C.6. 

G.1 Study Events 

The formal study covers 79 WTaG events from 31 sessions, and a separate set of 12 events was used for calibration. Formal events required complete source media, a reference instructor utterance, and the anchors needed for the reactive or proactive window. We included the 12 non-Other instructor categories (Table 6) and excluded reference utter-ances that overlap user speech, since these would confound the speech start time with simultaneous speech. Events were sampled with category quotas to cover the intervention types, with at most three events per session and none from the calibration sessions; the quotas are not intended to reflect cor-pus prevalence. The formal set contains 26 coffee, 28 mug-cake, and 25 pinwheel events: 43 reactive and 36 proactive, 46 necessary and 33 optional. The calibration set contains one event from each of 12 distinct sessions, four per task. It is bal-27 anced between reactive and proactive, necessary and optional, and three anticipated difficulty lev-els, covers 11 of the 12 non-Other categories, and uses the same candidate-moment construction as the formal study. 

G.2 Candidate-Moment Construction 

Each event has six candidate moments spanning early, valid, and late responses. Two are fixed: the preferred time, which is the end of the triggering user turn (R) for reactive events and the evidence onset (EO) for proactive events, and the speech start time of the original instructor utterance. The other four are sampled relative to the event’s anchors. The early moment precedes EO for proactive events and falls within the triggering user turn for reactive events. Two moments cover the first and second halves of the interval from the preferred time to the latest useful time (LU). The last slot is sampled after LU with probability 0.5, from a heavy-tailed distribution, and otherwise uniformly between the earliest candidate position (ES for proactive events, the start of the triggering user turn for reactive events) and LU. Moments must be at least 0.1 s apart; near-duplicate fixed moments are merged and the open slot is resampled. Sampling therefore covers positions relative to the anchors rather than uniformly across the video. 

G.3 Rating Protocol and Calibration 

Raters viewed the source context up to each can-didate moment and then judged the timing of the same synthesized reference utterance on an inte-ger scale from 0 (definitely should not intervene now) to 10 (exactly the desired moment); they were asked to judge timing rather than wording. The rubric marked 5 as borderline and 6 as acceptable but improvable, and Cannot judge responses were treated as missing. All three raters were authors. The roles of candidate moments (early, preferred, or late), annotated timing boundaries, and original reference start-time labels were hidden from raters. All raters completed calibration before the for-mal study. The 12 calibration events produced 216 records, including one Cannot judge . Calibration familiarized the raters with the interface and the rubric; its ratings were excluded from the primary reference, MAE, and parameter selection, and were not used to exclude or reweight raters. The formal phase produced 1,422 rating rows over 474 candidate moments, of which 1,415 are valid ratings and seven are Cannot judge . The hu-man reference for each moment is the mean avail-able rating divided by ten.

G.7 Results 

The LOSO-selected cosine has the lowest MAE among the compared functions (Figure 9). Thirty of the 31 folds selected (λE , p L) = (0 .7, 0.5) and one selected (0 .6, 0.5) . The parameters used by Timely Correctness, (0 .7, 0.5) , are those selected on all 31 formal sessions; the MAE reported for the selected cosine is its out-of-fold value. The selected cosine achieves MAE 0.182 , com-pared with 0.225 for linear scoring and 0.262 for constant valid-window credit. Its paired difference from linear is −0.043 with a 95% confidence in-terval (CI) of [−0.064 , −0.023] , its improvement over constant credit is 0.080 ([0 .069 , 0.091] ), and it also improves over the (0 .4, 1) cosine setting by 0.015 ([0 .005 , 0.025] ). These comparisons sup-port graded credit within the window. The selected 

pL = 0 .5 lies inside the search grid rather than at its lower boundary, and it gives a shallower decline through moderate delay than pL = 4 while still assigning zero at LU. Linear and selected cosine scores show similar within-event ranking agree-ment ( τb = 0 .740 and 0.738 ), but the selected co-sine achieves better calibration to the human rating scale, as reflected by its lower MAE. 

Spearman Correlation. The comparison with binary scoring in Section 4.2 uses Spearman’s rank correlation computed within each event between a function’s scores and the mean human ratings of its six candidate moments, averaged over events. Linear and selected cosine scores rank the six mo-ments identically in 78 of the 79 events and reach mean correlations of 0.833 and 0.832. Constant valid-window credit is identical across all six mo-ments of 12 events, where the correlation is un-defined; over the remaining 67 events it averages 0.645 (0.547 when undefined events count as zero), and the fixed [−6, +3] s step averages 0.616 over its 50 defined events. Pooling all 474 moments in-stead gives 0.787 for both graded scores and 0.586 for constant valid-window credit. Selection using only the 46 necessary events from 27 sessions gives the same parameters in all 27 LOSO folds, with out-of-fold MAE 0.178 

versus 0.219 for linear scoring, so including the optional events does not change the selected set-ting. We use one shared function rather than sepa-rate scoring functions for individual instructor cat-egories, whose small groups are partly confounded with the reactive/proactive composition. 

G.8 Calibration and Rater Sensitivity Analyses 

The selected cosine keeps its advantage after adjust-ing for rater differences and when any one rater is omitted. For the calibration-informed analysis, we jointly modeled the valid calibration and formal rat-ings with a random intercept for candidate moment and fixed effects for study phase, rater, timing re-gion (before the preferred time, within the window, or after LU), intervention side, and the correspond-ing rater interactions; the calibration ratings serve here only to estimate rater severity and timing tol-erance. The adjusted formal reference correlates 

0.993 with the raw rater mean. Against it, cosine MAE is 0.190 and linear MAE is 0.236 , a paired difference of −0.046 (95% CI [−0.063 , −0.029] ). This agrees with the primary analysis. Given the small number of raters and calibration events, we 30 treat this adjustment as an exploratory sensitivity analysis rather than independent validation. The two-way mixed-effects absolute-agreement intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) is 0.663 for one rater (ICC(A,1)) and 0.855 for the three-rater mean (ICC(A,3)). Omitting each rater in turn pre-serves the cosine advantage, with MAE improve-ments of 0.031 –0.058 . Raters differed in strictness and preferred timing, which supports using their mean rather than a single rater’s curve. 31
