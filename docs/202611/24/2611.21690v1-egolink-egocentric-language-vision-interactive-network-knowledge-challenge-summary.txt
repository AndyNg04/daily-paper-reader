Abstract 

The EgoLink Challenge at ACM Multimedia 2026 benchmarks ego-centric multimodal intelligence linking first-person perception, so-cial reasoning, and interactive action. Unlike benchmarks centered on visible objects and activities, EgoLink evaluates whether models can interpret emotional and social cues, resolve visually grounded references, and convert user requests into verified tool-mediated ac-tions. Its two tracks cover social reasoning in egocentric video and interactive agents that complete service tasks through dialogue, tool use, and closed-loop execution. Together, they test whether percep-tion, reasoning, and decision-making remain coupled under both offline question answering and state-changing interaction. We high-light a multi-stage Track 1 data pipeline for multiple-choice ques-tion (MCQ) generation, chain-of-thought generation, automatic quality grading, and video-contribution filtering. We summarize the task design, evaluation metrics, rules, leaderboards, and representa-tive solutions. 1 Introduction 

Egocentric multimedia intelligence requires models to interpret the world from the user’s situated point of view [3]. This setting is central to AI glasses, augmented-reality assistants, social robots, and embodied service agents, where a system must reason over camera motion, partial observations, speech, gestures, objects, and ongoing human interactions [8, 9]. In such environments, a correct response often depends on more than recognizing visible entities: the system must identify emotional states, explain social causes, and infer future intents [7, 8], while deciding when a visually grounded request should become an executable action [9]. Existing egocentric benchmarks have advanced first-person per-ception [3], whereas EgoLink targets integrated social and interac-tive intelligence beyond perception. Two recurring failure modes motivate this focus. First, social answers can be linguistically plau-sible but unsupported by the observed video or audio [4, 5]. Second, interactive agents can produce fluent final responses while invok-ing the wrong tool, using an ungrounded entity, or changing the database state before the task preconditions are verified [9, 15]. Both failures reflect weak evidence–decision coupling. The EgoLink Challenge 1 addresses these issues through two complementary tracks (Figure 1). Track 1, Social Reasoning in Ego-centric Video, uses a unified multiple-choice question (MCQ) proto-col to evaluate emotional perception and localization, social causal reasoning, behavioral intent prediction, and egocentric semantic summarization. Track 2, Interactive Agent Challenge, evaluates whether a service agent can combine first-person visual streams, user instructions, and external tools to complete real-world tasks in retail, kitchen, restaurant, and order scenarios [9]. Together, the tracks test whether perception, reasoning, and decision-making can remain coupled under both offline question answering and closed-loop interaction. The paper makes three contributions. First, we present EgoLink as a benchmark that jointly studies egocentric social cognition and interactive task execution. Second, we detail the Track 1 construc-tion pipeline—question and choice generation, template-guided chain-of-thought generation, five-dimensional quality grading, and video-contribution filtering—together with the official data, rules, evaluation protocol, and leaderboards for both tracks. Third, we an-alyze representative submissions and identify a shared emphasis on explicit evidence construction before final prediction or execution. 

2 Related Work 2.1 Egocentric Social Reasoning 

Egocentric video understanding has expanded from activity recog-nition to social and affective interpretation. The 𝐸 3 dataset provides first-person videos with embodied emotion annotations and forms the basis of EgoLink Track 1 [8]. EgoLink reformulates these videos into multiple-choice questions for social reasoning. Interpreting gestures, tone, and facial expressions requires temporal and inter-personal context, as similar observations may carry different social meanings [7]. DeSIQ further shows that social-interaction MCQs can contain shortcuts exploitable without the intended multimodal context [4]. These findings motivate EgoLink’s emphasis on social answers grounded in observable multimodal evidence. 

Figure 1: EgoLink challenge overview. 

2.2 Interactive Multimodal Agents 

Interactive multimodal agents extend visual understanding to tool-mediated task execution. Prior benchmarks study multi-step mul-timodal planning, visually grounded execution, and stateful tool-agent-user interaction [6, 12, 15]. EgoBench formalizes the egocen-tric setting through a user-agent-tool sandbox with simulated users, official tools, and database-state validation [9]. EgoLink Track 2 adapts this setting to interactive service tasks. Compared with text-only tool-use benchmarks, task entities are often specified visually or deictically. A user may refer to a prod-uct or previously observed object without providing its canonical database name. Agents must map these references to valid tool parameters and use tool feedback to guide subsequent actions, with execution evaluated against the resulting database state. 

3 Challenge Description 3.1 Tasks 

3.1.1 Track 1: Social Reasoning in Egocentric Video. Track 1 evalu-ates social reasoning in egocentric video through a unified multiple-choice question (MCQ) protocol. It covers four capability groups: emotional perception and temporal localization, social causal rea-soning, behavioral intent prediction, and egocentric semantic sum-marization. Answering requires interpreting temporal, affective, and interpersonal cues beyond object and action recognition. 

3.1.2 Track 2: Interactive Agent Challenge. Track 2 evaluates inter-active service-task execution using first-person visual streams, user instructions, scenario databases, and official tools/APIs. The agent must ground user references, decide whether to answer, request clarification, or invoke tools, and use returned observations to guide subsequent actions. Database updates must be supported by visual evidence and tool observations [9]. 

3.2 Datasets and Data Construction 

The Track 1 dataset pairs 𝐸 3 videos [8] with EgoLink-specific labels and MCQs. The release provides training and validation annota-tions, together with an evaluation benchmark of 4,030 MCQs paired with 1,055 egocentric videos. The challenge annotations must be used in place of the original 𝐸 3 labels.

MCQs and answer choices are generated from multimodal clips, followed by template-guided chain-of-thought rationales [13]. Qual-ity grading follows the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm [16], using Qwen3-235B-A22B [14]. Each candidate receives a score on a five-point scale for answer consistency, distractor quality, language fluency, reasoning logic, and simplified reasoning quality. These scores guide quality-based filtering. Following the contribution-aware principle of Audio-Contribution Filtering [5], Video-Contribution Filtering screens candidates for dependence on visual evidence (Figure 2). This step aims to reduce modality shortcuts [1] by filtering questions answerable without viewing the video. Track 2 uses the EgoBench user-agent-tool sandbox [9] across retail, kitchen, restaurant, and order scenarios. The final release con-tains 309 tasks across retail6 , retail10 , kitchen4 , restaurant5 ,and order2 . The official final scoring list identifies a common sub-set of 96 tasks selected to avoid ground-truth ambiguities: 20 each from retail6 , retail10 , and order2 , and 18 each from kitchen4 

and restaurant5 .

3.3 Evaluation Metrics 

Track 1 uses overall top-1 MCQ accuracy as the primary metric, with additional scores for emotion, causality, intent, and summarization. Track 2 ranks systems by joint_success_rate : a task achieves joint success only when it passes both tool-call-based process eval-uation and database-result-based evaluation [9]. Micro accuracy measures call-level tool accuracy, while separate tool-based and result-based success scores diagnose process–result mismatches. Table 1 summarizes these evaluation signals.

3.4 Rules 

The EgoLink Challenge follows rules designed to make leaderboard differences reflect reasoning and execution methods rather than hidden data advantages. 

• For Track 1, participants must pair EgoLink labels with the 

𝐸 3 video data rather than using the original 𝐸 3 labels. 

• Manual annotation of the evaluation set is prohibited. 

• External labeled or unlabeled video data cannot be used for training, fine-tuning, distillation, calibration, or pseudo-label construction in Track 1. 

• Track 2 submissions must follow the official interaction pro-tocol and output valid natural-language responses or JSON tool-call arrays. 

• The Track 2 challenge does not allow participants to directly expose the test-set database or user instructions in their prompts. 

• The challenge materials are released for non-commercial research and education use under the stated license and data-governance policy.

4.1 Track 1: Social Reasoning in Egocentric Video 

The leading Track 1 approaches address complementary risks: un-supported social interpretations and sensitivity to answer-option positions. GXU-LIPE frames answer selection as evidence verification. It builds a question-conditioned social evidence graph and evalu-ates each candidate for supporting, contradictory, and missing ev-idence. The system combines these assessments with risk-aware re-verification and all-option evidence calibration to resolve uncer-tainty among socially plausible candidates. Lai focuses on the distinction between semantic candidate iden-tity and option position. Its structured cold-start data and EgoAf-fect reward incorporate question type, emotional tone, and candi-date elimination. Option-Perturbed DAPO reorders answer candi-dates and assigns rewards by semantic identity to reduce reliance on displayed letters. The reported ablation reaches 91.98% with process-aware reward and 92.75% validation accuracy with the full option-perturbed setting. These results support further evaluation of candidate discrimination under option permutations. 

4.2 Track 2: Interactive Agent Challenge 

Track 2 highlights the distinction between execution outcomes and tool-process correctness. For example, BigVisionAgentGoGoGo achieves 0.896 result-based success and 0.802 tool-based success. This gap motivates reporting process correctness alongside final-state success. MediaLab couples a scenario-aware service agent with a correc-tion agent through a shared evidence ledger. Visual hypotheses are canonicalized through official tools before execution, while the cor-rection agent audits high-risk database mutations and final replies against dialogue context, tool observations, and current database state. Removing correction reduces Joint from 0.844 to 0.781; re-moving scenario rules lowers it to 0.646. The larger drop without scenario rules indicates reliance on scenario-specific guidance in the reported setting. GML-MM Group emphasizes explicit execution-state tracking and evidence compression. It represents user requests as objectives with dependencies, constraints, and completion status. Reporter summaries retain verified facts, state changes, intermediate com-putations, and unresolved requirements, while paired grounding across adjacent or cropped frames helps recover occluded object references. This design links progress tracking with visual evidence recovery during multi-step execution. 

5 Conclusion and Future Directions 

EgoLink benchmarks egocentric social reasoning and interactive service-task execution. Across the submissions summarized in Ta-ble 4, candidate verification, option-robust reasoning, tool canoni-calization, and execution-state tracking highlight the role of explicit intermediate evidence. The reported ablations suggest the value of option-robust training and execution correction in the evaluated settings, while generalization beyond the released scenarios is not established by the results summarized here. Future work should strengthen evidence grounding and robust-ness. For Track 1, priorities include modality-dependence tests, option-order perturbations, and temporal evidence evaluation to distinguish grounded reasoning from shortcut-based success [2, 4]. For Track 2, evaluation should extend to unseen scenarios, longer interactions, and recoverable tool failures, using step-level progress and database-state checks to assess execution reliability [10, 11]. These directions aim to strengthen the assessment of auditable reasoning and reliable execution across scenarios.
