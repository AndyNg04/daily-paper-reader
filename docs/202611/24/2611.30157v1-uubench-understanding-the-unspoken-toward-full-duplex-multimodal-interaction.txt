Abstract
Effective real-time, full-duplex multimodal interaction requires systems to understand not only what
users say, but also the communicative needs conveyed through prosody, facial expressions, gestures,
and context, and to generate audiovisual responses that appropriately address those needs. Existing
evaluations offer limited insight into whether systems translate an understanding of such unspoken
needs into effective responses. We introduce UUBench, a benchmark for assessing how well multi-
modal interactive systems Understand the Unspoken intent and address users’ implicit communicative
needs. The inherent subjectivity of implicit intent inference poses a key challenge for evaluation. To ad-
dress this, we introduce Process-Oriented Scoring, which grounds the assessment of intent fulfillment
in observable auditory and visual cues, such as prosody and gestures. Rather than relying solely on a
single overall score, it evaluates how well a response addresses the communicative needs conveyed
by each applicable primitive dimension. We aggregate the dimension-level scores according to each di-
mension’s relevance to the annotated evidence. Beyond intent fulfillment, Outcome-Oriented Scoring
evaluates the naturalness, appropriateness, and human-likeness of the reply itself under audio-only,
visual-only, and audiovisual conditions. To make these judgments traceable to observable evidence,
we propose a Fact-Grounded Scoring JEV module, which uses structured, timestamped audiovisual
observations of the user and the digital human’s reply to assess one dimension or criterion at a time.
UUBench provides a foundation for assessing whether multimodal interactive systems respond effec-
tively to meaning beyond literal wording, supporting progress toward context-aware, real-time, full-
duplex interaction.

To address this gap, we introduce UUBench, a benchmark for evaluating how
well digital humans respond to users’ implicit communicative intentions. Im-
plicit intent is not directly observable, and inferring it from an interaction in-
volves subjective judgment. A single rating of intent fulfillment can obscure
which aspects of the user’s need a reply addresses. We therefore introduce
Process-Oriented Scoring, which grounds this assessment in observable au-
ditory and visual cues. As shown in Figure 2, the speaker describes how dance
sustained her. The annotated evidence links her words, soft voice, and low-
ered gaze to an inferred need for support and understanding. We score the
reply separately for each applicable primitive dimension based on how well it
addresses the need conveyed by the corresponding cues. We then combine
these scores using weights derived from the relevance of each primitive’s la-

We selected 28,548 candidate user clips from four video sources—
TalkVerse (Wang et al., 2025), TalkCuts (Chen et al., 2025a), TalkVid (Chen
et al., 2025b), and SpeakerVid-5M (Zhang et al., 2025c). Human reviewers
then selected 216 high-quality clips from this pool for evaluation. For each se-
lected clip, annotators labeled the speaker’s intent and the auditory and visual
primitives, then recorded the words and observable behaviors that supported
their interpretation. We evaluated five closed-source and four open-source
digital human systems on these same 216 inputs. All nine systems received
positive overall audiovisual quality scores (Q = 1.01–2.03), yet each had a neg-
ative mean score on every visual primitive dimension (Table 4). These results
suggest a shared difficulty among the evaluated systems in addressing com-
municative needs conveyed through facial behavior, gaze, head movements,
and gestures.
Our main contributions are:
1. A benchmark for implicit intent fulfillment. We introduce UUBench to eval-
uate whether digital human responses address users’ implicit communica-
tive intent. From a pool of 28,548 user clips, we selected 216 for evaluation.
For each selected clip, annotators identified the speaker’s intent, labeled
the auditory and visual primitives, and recorded the words and behaviors
supporting their interpretation.
2. Scoring response quality and intent fulfillment. We introduce two com-
plementary scoring perspectives (Figure 2): Process-Oriented Scoring as-
sesses how well the reply addresses the need conveyed by each applica-
ble primitive. Outcome-Oriented Scoring measures audiovisual response
quality (Q). Our Fact-Grounded Scoring JEV module grounds both assess-
ments in structured audio-visual observations. We report primitive-level
scores and aggregate them using evidence-based weights within each re-
ply, then average across clips to obtain the system-level intent-fulfillment
score (S).
3. Comparative evaluation of responses to implicit intent. We evaluated five
closed-source and four open-source digital human systems with UUBench.
All nine achieve positive overall audiovisual quality scores, yet each re-
ceives negative mean intent-fulfillment scores across all four visual primi-
tive dimensions: facial behavior, gaze, head movements, and gestures (Ta-
ble 4). These findings show that positive overall audiovisual quality scores
can coexist with systematic shortcomings in addressing visually conveyed
intent, motivating further research into how digital humans can translate
multimodal intent understanding into appropriate responses.

SECTION 02
Related Work
2.1 Multimodal Intent Recognition and Pragmatic Understanding
Multimodal intent benchmarks examine how language, nonverbal behavior,
and context inform communicative interpretation. MIntRec provides utterance-
level intent classification (Zhang et al., 2022), while MIntRec2.0 extends this
setting to multi-party conversations and out-of-scope detection (Zhang et al.,
2024). VAGUE pairs an ambiguous expression with an image and asks what
action the speaker is requesting (Nam et al., 2025). READI tests whether mod-
els can use an image to infer a speaker’s directive intent across three levels
of indirectness in the utterance (Kim et al., 2026). GesBench examines how
interpretations change as an utterance unfolds. For 590 of its 6,208 clips, an-
notators describe the speaker’s intent, suggest how a listener might respond,
and explain their judgments (Li et al., 2026). Table 1 summarizes the refer-
ences these tasks provide. Predicting what a listener might do and evaluating
how well an actual reply addresses the speaker’s need are distinct tasks.
2.2 Interactive Digital Human Generation
Interactive digital human generation combines conversational behavior with
audiovisual synthesis.

Avatar Forcing conditions causal generation on user audio and motion to-

tained interaction also requires temporal consistency and behavioral control.
SoulX-LiveAct enables hour-scale, real-time animation with bounded histori-

the conversational-system level, LiveTalk connects an audio language model
with streaming video generation for multi-turn interaction (Chern et al., 2025),
while InteractiveAvatar uses transcribed user speech and the avatar’s current
state to plan a spoken reply and accompanying actions (Song et al., 2026).
These developments make coordinated speech and visible feedback central
to digital human interaction. Evaluating such systems therefore requires as-
sessing both the quality of audiovisual realization and whether the generated
behavior appropriately responds to the user’s communicative goal.
2.3 Evaluation of Digital Human Responses
Existing evaluations address several aspects of this problem. THEval mea-
sures talking-head quality, naturalness, and synchronization (Quignon et al.,
2025). ViCo evaluates responsive listening behavior using paired speaker–
listener recordings (Zhou et al., 2022); MARS, introduced for REACT 2025,
supports multiple acceptable facial reactions and evaluates appropriateness,
diversity, synchrony, and realism (Song et al., 2025). AvaMERG annotates
emotion causes and response goals to support empathetic reasoning.
Its
human evaluation rates empathy in textual replies and assesses the consis-
tency of content and style across text, speech, and avatar video (Zhang et al.,
2025a). VidChatBench evaluates dialogue coherence alongside visual qual-
ity, identity preservation, audiovisual synchronization, and the alignment of
facial expressions in generated and reference videos (Zhang et al., 2025c).
LiveTalk evaluates the semantic relevance of replies, nonverbal engagement,
and visual and behavioral consistency across turns (Chern et al., 2025).
UUBench links each annotated intent to observable cues in the user’s clip. For
each applicable auditory or visual primitive, it scores how well the generated
reply addresses the need those cues convey. For example, a user describing
a friend’s move abroad as “a great opportunity for her” may speak at a lower
pitch and volume, shift their gaze downward, turn down the corners of their
mouth, and bow their head. A digital human should interpret these cues in
context and recognize that the user may be seeking acknowledgment of their
reluctance to part and disappointment about the separation.

3.2 Data Collection and Curation
We sought exchanges in which a digital human could respond to a speaker’s
communicative needs. We screened clips from four video sources—
TalkVerse (Wang et al., 2025), TalkCuts (Chen et al., 2025a), TalkVid (Chen
et al., 2025b), and SpeakerVid-5M (Zhang et al., 2025c). In the initial text-
based screening, Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) rated five aspects
of each clip. The model assessed whether the speaker addressed an inter-
locutor, conveyed an implicit intent, and called for a reply. It also assessed
how narrowly the utterance constrained an appropriate reply and whether the
context was complete. Each criterion received 0, 1, or 2 points. A total of 8–
10 across the five criteria qualified a clip for human review, yielding 28,548
candidates.

3.3 Annotating Intent, Primitives, and Supporting Evidence
For each of the 216 evaluation clips, human annotators identified the speaker’s
communicative intent (I) and labeled the applicable auditory and visual prim-
itives (P). Table 2 groups the cues into four auditory and four visual dimen-
sions. For example, the prosody dimension captures pauses and changes
in pitch, while the speaker-emotion dimension describes the affect or stance
conveyed by the voice.
Annotators interpreted the speaker’s words alongside the vocal and visual
cues they identified in the clip. After labeling the intent and primitives, they
documented the supporting evidence (E). In the evidence annotation, annota-
tors identified relevant words, vocal cues, or visible behaviors in the clip and
explained how these supported their interpretation of the speaker’s intent.
For clip j, the primitive labels determine the set of dimensions Kj used to
evaluate every system’s reply to that clip. On each dimension, we assess how
well the reply addresses the need conveyed by the user’s cues. In Figure 2,
for example, the speaker’s soft voice and lowered gaze give context to her
account of how dance sustained her. A digital human can address her implied
need for understanding by acknowledging in words what dance has meant to
her.

3.4 Perception and Evidence Preparation
The annotated intent Ij records the annotators’ interpretation of the speaker’s
goal. Our Fact-Grounded Scoring JEV module uses timestamped observa-
tions of the user clip and the avatar’s response to assign the scores in Fig-
ure 2(c). For each outcome-oriented score qj,m,c, Jev (TypeSafe AI, 2026) re-
tains the full user context and rates one quality criterion using the audio, visual,
or combined audiovisual observations of the response. For each process-
oriented score xjk, Jev uses the full audiovisual observations of both partic-
ipants and the user’s labels for primitive k to judge how well the reply ad-
dresses the need conveyed by those cues (Figure 5). We use the relevance of
those labels to the annotators’ supporting evidence Ej to weight the resulting
primitive scores (Section 3.6).

3.6 Process-Oriented Evaluation: Evidence Perspective

the user and the reply, together with the user’s labels for dimension k, to rate
how fully the reply addresses what the cue conveys in context. The resulting

local misreading to a reply that reverses the cue’s meaning or entirely misses
an essential response. The zero anchor describes an ordinary continuation
that fits the cue when no special response is needed. The positive anchors

proportionately. Appendix B.3.2 gives the seven-point rubric and examples for

4.1 Experimental Setup
We evaluated nine digital human systems on the same 216 user clips. The
five closed-source systems were Emma (Nuance Labs, 2026), Tavus (n.d.),
Vidu (n.d.), D-ID (n.d.), and bitHuman (n.d.). The four open-source systems
were CyberVerse (Lynpoint, n.d.)
with SoulX-LiveAct (Zhen et al., 2026),
OpenAvatarChat (Cheng et al., 2025) with LiteAvatar (Zhuang et al., 2022),
Open-LLM-VTuber (n.d.) with Live2D (Live2D Inc., n.d.), and EmpaAva (Yang
et al., 2026) with GaussianAvatars (Qian et al., 2024) and DEEPTalk (Kim et al.,
2025). We used the same response-generation prompt for all nine systems
(Appendix B.1) and scored all replies using the protocol in Section 3.
4.2 Overall Results
All nine systems received positive overall audiovisual quality scores (Q = 1.01
to 2.03). For every system, however, the mean scores for responding to the
user’s facial behavior, gaze, head movements, and gestures were all negative
(Table 4). Across systems and dimensions, these means ranged from −0.35 to
−0.02. On this evaluation set, replies could earn positive overall quality ratings
while still falling short on needs conveyed through visible behavior.
The two perspectives also ranked the systems differently (Figure 1). Emma had
the highest Q (2.03), while Tavus had the highest evidence-weighted intent-
fulfillment score S (0.47). Among the four open-source systems, CyberVerse
with SoulX-LiveAct had the highest Q (1.64) and S (0.18).

4.4 Process-Oriented Results: Responding to User Cues
The primitive scores evaluate how well the reply addresses the need conveyed
by a cue in the user clip. For example, although Emma received the highest
score for the human-likeness of its own visible behavior, its mean scores for
responding to the user’s facial behavior, gaze, head movements, and gestures
were −0.08, −0.15, −0.13, and −0.15, respectively. A spoken acknowledgment
can address a need conveyed by a user’s expression or movement.
The auditory primitives showed a different pattern. Eight of the nine systems
had positive means for speech function (A3), which measures how well a reply
addresses the communicative purpose of the user’s utterance. Tavus had the
highest mean on A3 (1.24, 214 applicable clips). Vidu led on nonverbal vocal-
izations (A4, 0.68). All nine systems, however, had negative means on each of
the four visual primitives. The contrast with speech function suggests that, on
this subset, most systems responded more successfully to the purpose of an
utterance, such as asking a question or sharing an experience, than to needs
conveyed through the speaker’s expression, gaze, or movements.

SECTION 05
Conclusion
We introduce UUBench to evaluate how well digital humans respond to what
users leave unsaid. From a collection of 28,548 user clips, we curated 216
evaluation clips and annotated each speaker’s intent, the auditory and vi-
sual primitives present in the clip, and the evidence linking those cues to
the intent. These annotations make it possible to ask which aspects of an
inferred need a reply addresses. Our Fact-Grounded Scoring JEV module
uses structured observations of the user and the digital human for both as-
sessments. Outcome-oriented scoring evaluates the audiovisual quality of
the reply. Process-oriented scoring evaluates how well the reply addresses
the need conveyed by each applicable primitive, then combines the scores
using weights derived from the annotated evidence.
Evaluating five closed-source and four open-source systems on the same
clips revealed a consistent gap between audiovisual quality and responsive-
ness to visual cues. All nine systems earned positive overall audiovisual qual-
ity scores, yet each had negative mean scores for responding to needs con-
veyed by facial behavior, gaze, head movements, and gestures (Table 4). A
digital human may produce a natural facial expression yet give a reply that
overlooks what the user’s own expression conveys. By scoring how a reply
addresses the needs supported by specific cues, UUBench identifies which
aspects of the user’s intent the reply may have missed. This allows future eval-

uations to test whether improvements in how digital humans look and sound
are accompanied by better responses to what users leave unsaid.
Limitations
The current evaluation set comprises 216 user clips, with limited scale and
coverage of communicative intents, interaction contexts, and multimodal cues.
Some cues are applicable to relatively few clips, limiting detailed comparisons
across contexts and cue combinations. The reported results therefore charac-
terize the evaluated systems on the current dataset; larger-scale evaluations
are needed to assess whether these findings generalize to broader interaction
settings.
The automated judge Jev and the evidence-weighting mechanism have not yet
been systematically validated against independent human ratings. Although
the framework organizes scoring around concrete audiovisual cues, agree-
ment with human judgments at the dimension level and in overall intent ful-
fillment remains to be quantified. Whether evidence weighting better reflects
human judgments of responses than alternative aggregation schemes also re-
quires validation.
The current study also lacks systematic ablations that isolate the contribu-
tions of different observation types and the evidence-weighting mechanism.
While we evaluate nine digital human systems, further tests across different
data sources and additional systems are needed to assess the stability of the
scoring framework and establish its scope of applicability.
SECTION 06
Future Plans
This preview release establishes an initial evaluation framework for under-
standing and responding to implicit communicative intent. We plan to develop
UUBench further along five directions.
Scaling toward UUBench 1.0.
We plan to expand the evaluation set beyond
the current 216 clips, increasing coverage of communicative intents, interac-
tion contexts, and auditory and visual cues. We also plan to evaluate addi-
tional open-source and closed-source digital human systems. We used the
same response-generation model, Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct (Xu et al.,
2025), across the four open-source systems to control for differences in how
replies were generated. We plan to compare additional response-generation
models using the same user clips and avatar renderer to assess how model
choice affects responses to implicit intent.
Human validation, ablations, and generalization.
We plan to recruit inde-
pendent raters to assess the audiovisual quality and intent fulfillment of re-
sponses, report inter-rater agreement and agreement between human and
18


inSIght Labs
UUBench Preview
automated ratings, and analyze cases of disagreement. To validate evidence
weighting, we will hold dimension-level scores fixed and compare evidence-
weighted aggregation with equal weighting over applicable dimensions, as-
sessing how well each scheme matches independent human judgments of
overall intent fulfillment. We will also conduct ablations that remove different
types of observational information to isolate their contributions to the corre-
sponding scoring tasks. We will repeat these validations across different data
sources and additional digital human systems to assess whether agreement
with human judgments remains stable. Supplementary materials will docu-
ment the validation results, qualitative examples, and visualizations of system
behavior.
Training intent-aware digital humans.
Beyond evaluation, we aim to explore
how intent annotations, supporting evidence, and primitive-level feedback
can support model training. A central research question is how digital humans
can translate multimodal intent understanding into responses that appropri-
ately address users’ communicative needs through language, vocal expres-
sion, and visible behavior.
Extending to multi-turn interaction.
The current evaluation focuses on in-
dividual responses to user clips. Future benchmarks will extend this setting
to multi-turn conversations, where intent may emerge gradually, change over
time, or require clarification. We aim to examine whether digital humans track
these developments, use conversational history, and repair misunderstand-
ings across turns. We also plan to investigate how response timing and turn-
taking interact with intent fulfillment in full-duplex settings.
Releasing evaluation resources.
We plan to progressively release evalua-
tion protocols, scoring rubrics, and supporting code to facilitate reproducible
comparisons. Subject to source-data permissions and privacy considerations,
we also aim to provide annotations and data preparation instructions. The re-
lease scope and schedule will be documented as the benchmark develops.
