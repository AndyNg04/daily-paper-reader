AI supporters for mental well-being are becoming more prevalent. When these AI supporters start acting in ways beyond their expected function it leads to relational harm. Standard approaches to evaluating such AI supporters are limited to inspecting their efficacy in narrow task-specific settings, which do not explain these discrepancies in expected and actual behavior. To address this gap, we propose the AROMA (A Role-Oriented Model of Analysis) framework to provide a common role taxonomy for different support contexts. We developed AROMA using a theory-informed annotation of over 7,515 conversations from 13 datasets and propose eight "Role States" based on observed—not prescribed—behavior. We show these Role States vary according to the context of support; depict unique structural and temporal characteristics of AI supporters vis-a-vis Humans; and inform novel AI support prompting strategy for better role alignment. This framework provides a new lens to understand AI support failures. 1 Introduction 

LLM-based AI systems now routinely provide emotional support, counselling, and companionship. Millions of users turn to chatbots for mental health support, peer coaching, and romantic relationships [29, 57]. This expansion from task assistance to relational care has redefined what users expect from conversational AI. Now, consider Tessa , a wellness chatbot deployed by the National Eating Disorders Association in 2023 [17]. Tessa was designed to play the role of a supportive companion that helps individuals cope with eating disorders. In practice, however, Tessa was found to be directing users into caloric restriction and weight monitoring—performing the role of a weight-loss coach. Several users found this behavior harmful and eventually Tessa was taken offline [17]. We know in traditional human support settings, to reduce harms it is critical that the role a seeker expects is aligned to the role a supporter performs [15]. Moreover, regardless of context, humans express roles in flexible and interchangeable ways [1]. As we witness in Tessa’s case, this collapse in roles does not only result in conversational error but in relational harm. Although prior works have suggested approaches to evaluate AI chatbots in support settings, these methods have some key barriers that prevent it from understanding misalignment in roles. The dominant method of engineering the function of an AI supporter is through persona injection , which is the practice of specifying a system’s intended role via an initial system prompt [64]. Several evaluation methods benchmark how AI perform their prescribed roles to measure if it can effectively meet its goals [50, 64]. These are useful to study the fidelity of support within a role, but does not account for scenarios that require a multitude of roles. Another set of methods focus on how AI behavior drifts from intended role [27], but does not concern itself with describing what roles the AI embodies instead. Thus, the current frameworks to assess AI support provides only narrow goal-specific understanding that is limited to specific domains. We posit an alternative approach to assessing AI support by drawing from literature on Role Theory [1] that distinguishes prescribed role —what an actor is assigned to do—from the enacted role —what an actor actually does. This theory has been salient to understand interpersonal dynamics across a variety of domains [1]. Despite its popularity, so far the literature does not describe roles through canonical labels (e.g., mentor) and thus does not provide clear lens to understand support behaviors in a transferrable way. Defining roles has always been challenging. Recently Lindgren stated that roles have shared properties across situations and can be “partly overlapping and fluent” depending on these situations and called for AI techniques that allows for "explainability, negotiation and adaptation of the enacted roles." [23] In response to this call, this paper presents AROMA (A Role-Oriented Model of Analysis), a context-agnostic framework for understanding enacted roles in support conversations. We developed AROMA by supplementing the theory in Role Theory with empirical evidence from a corpora of human-human, human-AI, and AI-AI support conversations sourced from three different supper contexts peer support [4, 25, 46, 65] , counselling [28, 43–45] and motivational interviewing [32, 63]. Using the AROMA framework we answer the following questions: [RQ1] What features distinguish roles support-seeking contexts?; [RQ2] How does AI support roles differ from that of human actors?; and [RQ3] How can AI supporters be designed for better role alignment? We report how we developed AROMA ’s taxonomy of Role States by annotating 7 , 515 conversations from 13 datasets using different dimensions of support— orientation and support type —that we derive from Hill’s helping-skills model [16] and Cutrona and Russell’s optimal-matching theory [8]. We validate the taxonomy with an LLM-based classifier that achieved 𝜅 = 0.734 for orientation and AC1 = 0.889 for support type (section 3). Then our paper shows that support roles can be described through a shared taxonomy (section 4). Affirming in peer support is the most prominent context-distinguishing state ( 𝑂𝑅 = 5.35), alongside elevated Reflecting in MI ( 𝑂𝑅 = 2.25) and Directing in peer support (𝑂𝑅 = 3.18). This shows how different context exhibited quantifiable different ways of support (RQ1). Next we present AROMA: Understanding AI Support For Mental Well-being through Role Theory 3which can help assess the divergence in human and AI roles (section 5) by observing changing in how frequently the role states change ( H-H conversations switch roles on 62% of consecutive turn pairs, compared with 36% for H-AI and 52% for AI-AI ) . And finally, this informs how we might align them for different contexts . Steering reduces the distributional gap between AI and human role profiles by 70–85% (section 6). Together our paper contributes the following: 

• A descriptive taxonomy of Role States operationalised by how supporters respond to seekers ( orientation ) and what kind of support they provide ( type ). These Role States are uniquely distributed across contexts, reinforcing how varying situations require different ways of support (RQ1). 

• Empirical evidence of how AI support roles differs from humans in terms of AI’s affinity towards certain roles as well as its probability of transitioning to different roles (RQ2). 

• A Markov-informed steering mechanism that relies on role transition probabilities to provide per-turn scaffolding to prompt AI support (RQ3). Our paper concludes with a discussion on the importance of being able to measure how roles are enacted, the dangers of mislabeled roles creating impossible expectations on human supporters, and a need for new labels to describe new, emergent AI-native roles. 

2 Related Work 2.1 Role Theory and Role Conflict 

Role theory offers a vocabulary for how people organize behavior around social positions [1]. Linton [24] distinguished a status , the position a person occupies, from a role , the dynamic aspect of that status: what the person does by virtue of occupying it. Later work separated the expectations attached to a position from the behavior actually performed in it [1]. We call what a person is expected to do in a given position the prescribed role (e.g., a teacher is expected to provide instruction), and what the person actually does the enacted role (e.g., a teacher may also act as a parental figure). Common approaches to designing AI supporters, rely on persona injection which focus on defining a prescribed role ,but we lack methods to evaluate enacted role .Roles are not fixed traits. They are enacted over time and shift with the interaction partner and the conversational context. Turner [62] framed role-taking as an ongoing process rather than conformity to a fixed script, with participants continually making and remaking their roles in relation to one another. When a person faces two or more incompatible expectations at once, the result is role conflict , which studies have associated with stress, poorer job performance, and higher turnover [1]. AI role prompting prescribes a role once at the start but provides no turn-by-turn guidance. Thus, unlike human support, it is unclear how rigid or transient enacted roles in AI support are. 

2.2 AI in Support-Seeking 

AI chatbot functions have expanded beyond task assistance into companionship, emotional support, and romantic relationships [29]. AI systems today are simultaneously addressed as therapist, coach, friend, and informant, and the role they enact may blend within a conversation or drift across it. People increasingly turn to conversational systems for emotional and psychological support because of their accessibility, anonymity, and perceived non-judgment [57]. Researchers have explored LLM support in various forms, from motivational interviewing coaches that use non-directive questioning to elicit change talk [19, 32] to emotionally supportive coworkers for front-desk workers [9]. Users report that AI coworkers help them avoid negative thinking and recenter their thoughts, but the same study also surfaces limitations of such support. For instance, an AI coworker cannot share lived experience, something that is indispensable 4 Ting et al. to peer support [9]. AI is increasingly cast in supportive roles whose relational obligations, such as sharing lived experience, it cannot truly fulfill. The role an AI system occupies is not fixed at deployment. People have long been known to treat computers as social actors, projecting onto them human-like qualities that might not have been programmed into the AI system [36]. These qualities accrue through repeated interaction, sometimes leading to the user developing reliance or even emotional attachment, taking on romantic roles when that role was not prescribed [39]. AI systems that take on the role of a companion can also introduce a distinct class of harms, including relational transgression, harassment, and privacy violations [67]. Furthermore, vulnerable populations can be manipulated through LLM interactions [54]. Harmful responses may go unnoticed due to unquestioning user trust [7]. Users’ tendency to treat AI as a social actor can lead them to form attachments and cast AI into roles that was not prescribed, exposing them to dependence, relational harm, manipulation, and unnoticed harmful responses. Role instability also originates in the model itself. LLMs can also drift from their prescribed role [13] and display sycophancy, a tendency to give socially desirable responses that causes further deviation [5]. Over time, LLMs’ behavior deviates from the prescribed personas over extended interactions [11, 27]. Purohit and Heuer show that LLM role adherence is conditional : models maintain prescribed roles only when conditions remain stable, and deviate when context shifts [42]. Unlike humans, LLMs do not experience role conflict as stress, but prompts and interaction contexts can still impose conflicting expectations, and models may produce behavior consistent with role conflict [55, 66]. Recent work shows that LLMs can internalize role conflict [66], and their decisions in role-conflict scenarios are shaped by social-role preferences [55]. This matters because a single AI application could be repurposed for multiple needs, which can lead to it attempting to perform a conflicting positions where it takes up multiple roles or deviates from the prescribed one. 

2.3 Evaluating AI Behavior in Support-Seeking 

The current research landscape is well aware of these challenges and dangers, and many studies and evaluation methods have been developed to detect when an AI’s behavior does not align with its prescribed role or ethical guidelines. Some approaches focus on domain-specific contexts. For example, the Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity (MITI) coding system scores fidelity to motivational interviewing (MI) practice at the session level [35] and has been used to evaluate LLM coaches using MI [18, 38]. BOLT [6], a computational framework for assessing LLM therapist behavior, finds that LLM responses often resemble low-quality therapy rather than high-quality therapy. These evaluation methods are invaluable for understanding how well an AI system performs a specific prescribed role. However, they do not specify how to describe, distinguish, or measure adherence across the multiple roles that an LLM may enact during support-seeking. Our proposed approach, AROMA aims to address these gaps in our understanding of AI support by looking at how we understand roles from a Role Theory perspective. 

3 Analytical Framework 

Existing evaluation methods assess agent behavior based on the values and goals they are committed to, these approaches do not account for general-purpose AI agents in multi-use settings and fail to provide domain-agnostic insights on AI behavior [21, 55, 56]. This approach does not account for the multi-faceted and fluid nature of human role adaptation in a conversation. To improve our understanding of AI support in naturalistic settings, we focus on inspection of enacted roles across different domains. We propose a theory-grounded data-driven method to evaluate role dynamics, that we AROMA: Understanding AI Support For Mental Well-being through Role Theory 5call AROMA (A Role-Oriented Model of Analysis). AROMA establishes what role an AI system actually performs, without knowing what role it was assigned. It provides a taxonomy grounded in established theory, applied across different datasets spanning peer support, motivational interviewing, and counselling. For each context, we establish human baselines (RQ1), compare AI enactment against those baselines (RQ2), and test whether the resulting divergence can be reduced through steering (RQ3). This section describes the shared framework; the method and results for each research question follow in Sections 4–6. 

3.1 Corpora Composition 

To ensure our framework is representative of actual AI support behavior, we set out to compile a corpus of publicly available datasets of real support conversation . Throughout the paper, support conversation will refer to any dialogue in which one party (the supporter ) intentionally assists another (the seeker ) in managing emotional distress, exploring a problem, or changing behavior. A support role is the pattern of behaviors a supporter enacts over the course of such a conversation: not the label they are given but what they observably do, turn by turn. Each corpus, that we compiled, included datasets from a fixed support context. For our study, we needed each corpus to contain at least one dataset where support conversations involve a human seeker conversing with human support ( H-H ) [28, 63] and a human seeker conversing with an AI supporter ( H-AI ). [32, 65] These different pairings of seeker and supporter, or as we call them dyads , were important for us to be able to learn how AI behavior might deviate from human behavior in the same setting. Additionally, we expanded any corpus with AI seeker and AI supporter dyads ( AI-AI ) as these are commonly used in research for simulation and inform many of the AI support agents within the scope of our work [4, 22]. 3.2 Theory-Guided Bottom-Up Role Annotation 

The rich corpora of data we established helped us develop a taxonomy for support roles from the bottom-up. To encode different types of roles, we referred to the literature on social role theory to derive fundamental definitions of roles. 

Dimensions of a Support Role : In general, we found that the literature does not describe canonical roles such as “friend”, “mentor” or “parent”. Instead, prior work in this domain defines roles through different dimensions. Our framework predominantly relies on two such dimensions. The first is Orientation , which was developed using Hill’s helping-skills model [16], which identifies 12 verbal response modes that supporters use, including asking, reflecting, restating, and advising. These responses are further organised along the temporal evolution of the support conversation (exploration, insight, action). This model was particularly appropriate for our framework because it accounts for a variety of transitioning response modes within a single conversation. The second is Support Type which was based on Cutrona and Russell’s optimal-matching theory [8], which distinguishes five support types along a nurturant—action-facilitating axis, providing the vocabulary for what kind of support a given response provides. 

Initial Coding Process to Refine Dimensions : We operationally defined a conversational segment as a sequence of three turns that begins with a seeker’s message, a supporters response, and then the seeker’s next message. Two co-authors independently coded the same segments for the Orientation and Support Type . This initial pool was a stratified sample of 57 segments from our corpora. For the first phase of coding we relied on the definitions of from Hill’s helping-skills [16] and Cutrona and Russell [8]. The coders inspected each segment and assigned labels for each dimensions separately. For Orientation , in particular, the coders found that a supporter might demonstrate multiple different helping skills within the same segment. In fact, human supporters had exhibited simpler turns, [16] “So have your feelings about marijuana changed since we last talked?”[28]. However, a typical AI turn from our counselling data below[22] illustrates a bundling reflection, validation, and a question. 3.3 AROMA’s Role Taxonomy 

With LLM-assistance, we coded support type and orientation on our support seeking corpora. Crossing the two dimensions yields eight Role States, such as Exploring (Ask×Nur ) and Reframing (Adv×Nur ). Each capturing both the manner of delivery and the kind of support provided. Table 4 lists out the 8 Role States. Although these dimensions are theoretically independent, we found the two role dimensions, to be coupled with each other at different degrees across datasets ( Fig. 2). We quantified this coupling with Cramér’s 𝑉 , a measure of association strength between two categorical variables (0 = independent, 1 = perfectly associated). When coupling is high, knowing the orientation largely determines the support type; when coupling is low, the same orientation appears with both nurturant and action-facilitating support. 4 RQ1: What are the fundamental features and structures of supporters’ roles in different support-seeking context? 

We know from role theory that distinct social context produces distinct patterns of conduct [1]. We developed AROMA to be able to provide a unified vocabulary of social roles across different support contexts. Therefore, to answer RQ1, we set out to analyze if the role dimensions described by AROMA can uniquely distinguish the support characteristics of different contexts (motivational interviewing, counseling and peer support).

5 RQ2: How do AI support roles differ from that of human actors? 

Role theory holds that social roles are enacted dynamically, shifting in response to the interaction partner and the conversational context [1]. LLMs, by contrast, are given a single prescribed role via a system prompt [61] and have no social identity to maintain or adhere to beyond that prescription [26, 41] . Therefore, we hypothesize that AI supporters will exhibit less variability in their role states, producing a narrower and more rigid profile of roles. We test this hypothesis through two analyses to compare differences between human and AI actors: first, we model the static distribution of role states as a function of supporter and seeker identity; second, we analyze role states as a dynamic transitioning property of different H-H , H-AI , and AI-AI conversations.

7 Discussion 

We presented a descriptive, validated taxonomy of eight Role States that uniquely distinguished supporter characteristics across motivational interviewing, counseling, and peer support, finding that specific roles were more prominently featured in supporters of a particular context ( RQ1 ). We also found that AI supporters deviate both in the structure of their roles and in how they transition through roles compared to humans, and were often less dynamic ( RQ2 ). Motivated by this deviation, we developed and evaluated a novel Markov-steering prompting approach that shifted AI’s enacted role distributions more closely to humans. When assessed against human linguistics, prompting approaches 18 Ting et al. that leveraged our taxonomy of Role States approximated human supporters’ language better, but the best approach was nuanced and varied across contexts ( RQ3 ). Reflecting together on our development of AROMA and on the implications of our findings, we offer two key points for discussion: (1) how Role States surface potential challenges of AI-driven support conversations, and (2) how different levels of abstraction shape our ability to analyze and steer AI support. 

7.1 Role States Surface Challenges in AI Support Systems 

Our findings suggest that examining support conversations through the lens of Role States could surface limitations of AI support systems. We found that AI roles differ from human roles, both in terms of role distribution and turn transition dynamics, even when comparing within a specific support context. AI roles tend to be more static, with less flexibility. This was notable as generative AI systems are often referenced by their flexibility to respond to a wide range of needs and adapt to user responses turn to turn. Yet, at the level of conversational roles, we observed the opposite. Thus, while an AI’s language may appear adaptive at individual turns, its broader supportive role may remain comparatively static. Role States can offer a vocabulary for making these higher-level conversational patterns visible. This rigidity matters because effective support may depend not only on performing an appropriate role, but also on recognizing when that role is no longer appropriate during the conversation and shifting accordingly. Human therapists are explicitly trained to attend to such dynamics, and even informal human peer supporters can draw on social and relational cues to adjust how they respond. Our findings suggest that AI systems may not make these shifts in the same ways. This distinction may become especially consequential when AI constitutes a person’s primary or only accessible source of emotional support. Repeated exposure to a comparatively narrow set of Role States could shape expectations about what receiving support looks like, including expectations carried into future interactions with human supporters. This provides another reason to be cautious about framing AI mental health systems as therapists: doing so risks misrepresenting not simply the system’s capabilities, but the nature and dynamics of the therapeutic relationship itself [20]. Related work has similarly raised ethical risks surrounding the use of language models in automated mental healthcare [14]. Sycophancy may compound these concerns: systems that default toward agreeable or socially desirable responses can decrease prosocial behavior and foster dependence [5]. For people who are particularly vulnerable or in need of human connection, role-level rigidity could risk orienting support increasingly inward toward continued AI interaction rather than outward toward other people. Our analysis of both sides of the support interaction — supporters and seekers — surfaces a further concern: AI may shape not only how support is provided , but also how support conversations unfold . We found that AI seekers tended to shift conversations toward more action- and solution-oriented Role States. This pattern has implications for the growing use of AI–AI interactions to generate synthetic support conversations. If AI-generated seekers systematically steer conversations toward actionability, synthetic datasets produced through interactions between AI seekers and AI supporters may over-represent solution-oriented conversations relative to human ones. When such datasets are subsequently used to train or evaluate AI support systems, this bias could become self-reinforcing: AI seekers elicit more action-oriented support, those interactions enter training data, and future systems learn that effective support should itself be more action-oriented. Yet, it is common knowledge that people do not always want to hear solutions when engaging in support conversations. Effective support requires moving around a broader set of roles. Role States thus provides an opportunity for auditing the behavior of supportive AI systems, as well as the conversational assumptions that are embedded in the synthetic AI–AI datasets that are increasingly used to develop them. 7.2 Choosing the Right Level of Abstraction for AI Support Conversations 

Our findings also raise a broader methodological question: At what level of abstraction, or what granularity, should we be looking to analyze and steer effective AI support? This question becomes particularly relevant when situating our Role States and steering prompts alongside other approaches to analyzing and structuring AI support conversations. Compared to our bottom-up characterization of enacted roles, existing approaches often operate top-down, prescribing values, identities, or roles that an AI should follow. Yet, what a model is prescribed to be may differ from how those prescriptions manifest in conversation. Work on value alignment illustrates this distinction: ValueActionLens finds that what models say about values can differ from how they act in value-relevant situations [52]. Moreover, abstractions such as values can be inherently subjective and context-dependent: value alignment has been shown to vary across geographical contexts and models [52], while ValueCompass demonstrates substantial variability across 56 human values [53]. A bottom-up approach that instead is derived from observable characteristics of support conversations may offer a more concrete basis for analyzing what models actually do, rather than relying solely on whether their behavior conforms to broader prescribed abstractions. Moving from values to prescribed roles reduces the level of abstraction, but still highlights important differences from our approach of enacted roles. Goel et al., for example, prescribe four roles drawn from social support theory—Inform, Coach, Relate, and Listen—and show that role assignment meaningfully shapes both perceived support quality and interactional risks [12]. Such top-down roles require strong theoretical grounding and allow designers to deliberately configure how an AI should provide support. However, specifying roles in advance may obscure distinctions or conversational dynamics that fall outside the chosen theoretical framework. Doing so also requires researchers to identify an appropriate theory and set of roles a priori . Our finding that different support contexts exhibit distinct Role State distributions further highlights a limitation of prescribed roles: roles defined a priori may not align with the dynamics of a particular support context, and may therefore overlook meaningful variation in how support is actually enacted. Conversely, a bottom-up approach could also be more context- or dataset- dependent, especially if derived from observed conversational patterns. The tradeoff may be centered around theoretical grounding for sensitivity to enacted, observable conversational behaviour. Another option is to move to an even lower level of abstraction. Linguistic features can provide precise descriptions of how language is used, but may be difficult to translate into meaningful supportive actions or into prompts that can effectively steer AI support. Our work positions Role States as a potentially useful middle ground: high-level enough to represent meaningful supportive functions, yet low-level enough to observe and steer turn-by-turn. Rather than suggesting that roles are necessarily the “right” level of abstraction, our findings highlight a tradeoff among theoretical grounding, contextual sensitivity, and actionability . One opportunity for future systems is therefore to analyze and steer support across multiple levels: values can guide what good support should accomplish, roles can characterize what a supporter should do at a particular moment, and linguistic features can shape how that role is enacted. 

7.3 Limitations and Future Work 

Our work has several limitations that simultaneously open opportunities for future research. First, we do not claim that the Role States identified by AROMA are exhaustive. Our analysis was limited to the support contexts represented in publicly available datasets, which also predominantly captured text-based and single-session interactions. Other therapeutic approaches, support contexts, multi-session conversations, or even modalities such as voice, may surface Role 20 Ting et al. States not captured here. Future work can apply and extend AROMA to these settings, both to assess the generalizability of our approach, the roles we identified, and to potentially surface new support roles. Second, our evaluation of Role State steering focused on the linguistic properties of the steered conversations as an initial investigation, leaving an assessment of their effectiveness for human support seekers for future work. Producing conversations with desired Role State dynamics does not necessarily mean that those conversations provide better support. Future work should evaluate our Markov-based steering and prompting approaches with human support seekers and domain experts, including trained therapists or counselors, to examine whether steered Role States do indeed improve the perceived quality, appropriateness, and effectiveness of support. Finally, although we used human support dynamics and roles as a benchmark for AI support in our analysis, human dynamics may not always be the optimal goal across contexts. Just as peers and therapists provide complementary forms of support, future work could examine how AI might similarly complement human support by exhibiting different Role States, rather than simply reproducing human patterns. 

8 Conclusion 

We presented AROMA, a role-oriented framework for understanding the enacted social roles of AI supporters in mental well-being contexts. AROMA asks what roles an AI enacts, how those roles transition, and how they compare to human supporters across contexts. We found that support contexts have distinct Role State profiles, that AI supporters systematically underuse Reflecting and Affirming while switching roles less often than humans. We experimented with a Markov-informed steering mechanism that reduced divergence from human role profiles by 70–85%. These findings position role alignment as a measurable design and evaluation target: not a replacement for user perception or clinical safety, but a structural complement that can reveal misalignment before it reshapes user expectations. Designers of AI support systems should consider not only what role a system is prompted to play, but what roles it enacts over time, how it transitions between them, and whether those transitions respect seeker autonomy. Future work should pursue three directions. First, steering should adapt dynamically to conversation-level variables such as seeker distress and topic sensitivity. Second, user-centric validation should establish whether structural alignment correlates with perceived empathy, therapeutic alliance, or well-being. Thirdly, expand beyond the context of support by trying to ground other types of roles.

A Codebook Quick Reference 

This appendix condenses the AROMA codebook into a quick reference for the D1, D2, and D3 coding dimensions. The full codebook, with worked examples and boundary cases, is included in the replication package. Each supporter turn is coded within its three-turn triad: seeker turn ( 𝑆 𝑁 ), supporter turn ( 𝑃 𝑁 +1), seeker response (𝑆 𝑁 +2). All three dimensions are coded independently. 

> Table 8. AROMA coding dimensions.

Code Dimension Type Values Source D1 Spans Multi-label Eight move labels Hill (2010) D2 Orientation Five-class Ask / RB / Ack / Adv / Other Hill (2010), collapsed D3 Support type Binary Nurturant / Action-Facilitating Cutrona & Russell (1990) 

D1 span labels. Tag every functionally distinct segment of the supporter’s turn: Asking (questions that open space), 

Restatement (rephrases content), Reflection of Feelings (mirrors emotion), Interpreting (offers a new frame), Challenging 

(points out discrepancies), Approval & Reassurance (validates or normalises), Advising (suggests actions or provides information), and Personal Experience (shares the supporter’s own experience). D1 grounds D2. 

D2 orientation decision pipeline.                     

> Step 1. Not a helping skill? -> OTHER. Stop. Step 2. Tag D1 spans. Step 3. Trace details from S_N. Step 4. Contains a question? Exploratory (past/perception) -> ASKING Directive (future action) -> ADVISING Confirmation tag -> continue Step 5. Restatement + question? S_N+2 answers question -> ASKING S_N+2 confirms mirrored -> REPEATING BACK Step 6. Verbatim echo or direct feeling reflection? -> REPEATING BACK Step 7. 2+ traced details? Substitution passes (backdrop) -> ACKNOWLEDGEMENT Substitution fails (load-bearing) -> REPEATING BACK Step 8. Affirmation of strength/ progress/coping? -> ACKNOWLEDGEMENT Step 9. Content delivery? Remove advice -> nothing left -> ADVISING Validation carries the turn -> ACKNOWLEDGEMENT Extended advice (lists, plans) -> ADVISING Encouragement imperative -> ACKNOWLEDGEMENT Step 10. D1<->D2 consistency check. Step 11. S_N+2 tie-breaker if needed.

Key distinctions. Asking opens the seeker’s material further. Repeating Back gives it back, processed: mirrors, rephrases, names. Acknowledgement normalises, validates, or affirms without mirroring specifics. Advising sets the seeker’s material aside and delivers something new. 

Substitution test (RB vs. Ack). Replace the 𝑆 𝑁 details with unrelated details. If the turn still serves the same function, the details are backdrop → Acknowledgement . If the turn collapses, the details are load-bearing → Repeating Back .

D3 support type decision pipeline. Does the supporter introduce a new strategy, resource, or fact the seeker could use to act upon the stressor? If no, Nurturant . If yes, remove the fact. If the turn still works relationally (fact is backdrop), 

Nurturant . If the turn collapses (fact IS the turn), Action-Facilitating .
