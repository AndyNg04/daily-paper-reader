## Abstract 

Integrating gesture sensing into furniture offers a way to interact with digital applications and physical devices through familiar sur-faces. We present TableControl, an interactive demonstration of continuous surface-vibration-based gesture recognition with the complete processing pipeline running on a microcontroller. Using four concealed piezoelectric sensors, the system combines signal acquisition and preprocessing with neural event detection and ges-ture classification. The resulting labels for knock, swipe-up, and swipe-down gestures are transmitted to a host computer without streaming the underlying sensor signals. Visitors explore two exam-ple applications through hands-on interaction: an endless runner game and a physical table lamp with on/off and brightness control. A dashboard displays recognized gestures, class probabilities, and processing times.

## 1 Introduction 

Smart-home technologies offer new ways for people to interact with their living environments. This type of interaction is commonly based on smartphone applications, dedicated control panels, or voice assistants. A complementary and unobtrusive approach is smart furniture, where input capabilities are integrated into existing objects. Therefore, familiar surfaces can become interfaces while retaining their appearance and primary function. Surface-vibration sensing provides one way to realize such in-terfaces. Knocks and swipes generate mechanical vibrations that can be captured by sensors mounted underneath the surface and analyzed for gesture recognition. Smatable [8] demonstrated this approach by recognizing swipe gestures on ordinary tables using concealed vibration sensors. Building on this approach, Hettstedt et al. Extending this work, we present TableControl, an interactive demonstration of surface-vibration-based gesture recognition in which the complete sensor data processing pipeline is executed on a microcontroller. Signal acquisition and preprocessing, including filtering and windowing, are combined with a two-stage neural recognition pipeline consisting of an event detector and a gesture classifier. ## 2 Related Work 

Previous research has explored ways to turn everyday objects and surfaces into input interfaces, each with specific requirements for integrating the interface into its environment. HIC [7] provides a projected graphical interface for controlling smart-home appli-ances, requiring projection hardware in the interaction space. Sen-surfaces [6] integrates modular capacitive sensing into surfaces made from materials such as wood and concrete while preserv-ing their appearance. However, it requires an underlying electrode array, and its sensitivity depends on the material’s thickness and di-electric properties. Acustico [1] leaves the surface uninstrumented by using wrist-worn microphones and accelerometers to detect and localize taps, but requires users to wear a sensing device. For TableControl, we pursue gesture input through an existing tabletop using concealed sensors attached to the furniture by placing the sensor elements and the hardware beneath the tabletop. Surface-vibration sensing provides a basis for this approach by capturing mechanical signals generated during interaction. SAWSense [4] demonstrates how voice pickup units attached to objects can rec-ognize touch gestures and activities from surface acoustic waves. 

VibSense [5] uses piezoelectric sensors to localize touches and, with active vibration excitation, identify objects. Although TableControl also uses piezoelectric sensing, its interaction model relies on distin-guishing knock and swipe gestures rather than identifying contact locations or objects. More directly aligned with this goal, Smat-able [8] combines concealed piezoelectric sensors beneath ordinary tables with a spectrogram-based two-dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN) to classify swipe directions. arXiv:2611.24218v1 [cs.HC] 18 Nov 2026 ISS Companion ’26, November 23–26, 2026, Turin, Italy Florian Hettstedt, Andreas Erbslöh, and Gregor Schiele

## 3 System Overview 

TableControl consists of a concealed sensing setup, a continuous on-device recognition pipeline, and host software for game and lamp control. 

## 3.1 Sensing Hardware 

The microcontroller enables the local inference of the whole recog-nition pipeline, transmitting recognition results to the host com-puter for application control. TableControl uses the custom sensing platform introduced by Hettstedt et al. [3]. Figure 1 shows (a) a close-up of a sensor unit, (b) the concealed sensor placement on the underside of the tabletop, and (c) the custom acquisition hardware combining the analog front-end to record the continuous sensor data stream and the computing unit. Four sensor units are mounted underneath the tabletop to capture mechanical vibrations generated by gestures on its surface. Their concealed placement preserves the appearance of the tabletop and leaves the interaction area unob-structed. Each sensor unit combines a piezoelectric element with two small neodymium magnets and is mounted beneath the table-top using a self-adhesive metal disk. Shielded cables connect the sensors to the analog front-end of the custom acquisition board. The board provides analog signal processing and 18-bit analog-to-digital conversion for up to nine channels and incorporates an RP2350 microcontroller. The signals from the four sensors are acquired at 1 kHz per channel and passed to the embedded processing pipeline running on the microcontroller. 

## 3.2 On-Device Gesture Recognition 

Figure 2 provides an overview of the recognition pipeline, which runs continuously on the microcontroller. It follows a two-stage design: an event detector identifies potential gestures, and a ges-ture classifier determines their class. Shared signal acquisition and preprocessing provide the input data for both stages. The microcontroller acquires digitized sensor signals via SPI using a timer interrupt and stores the samples in two alternating buffers, each holding 200 ms of data. When one buffer is full, acqui-sition switches to the other while the completed block is processed. The duration of the block was selected to accommodate the mea-sured classifier inference time while leaving additional time for preprocessing, event detection, and result transmission. To avoid buffer overruns, processing of the completed block must finish be-fore the other buffer fills. Samples from each completed block are filtered independently for each channel using a second-order But-terworth IIR-bandpass filter within the range of [225 Hz, 375 Hz]. Filter states are preserved across buffer boundaries to maintain continuous filtering. The filtered samples are written to a separate ring buffer that retains signal history across successive blocks. This history remains available after the corresponding acquisition buffer has been processed and released, providing the input windows for both recognition stages. In the first stage, the event detector evaluates successive 100 ms windows of filtered samples from the ring buffer with a stride of 100 ms and distinguishes between gesture events and background activity. These windows are evaluated sequentially as the completed acquisition block is processed. An accepted detection triggers the second stage, with the midpoint of the detector window serving as the estimated event timestamp. The second stage uses a 500 ms classification window, covering 50 ms before and 450 ms after the estimated event time. Acquisition and filtering continue until the complete window is available in the ring buffer. The window is then extracted, normalized using channel-wise Z-score normalization, and passed to the gesture classifier, which distinguishes between three gestures: knock, swipe-up, and swipe-down. While an event is awaiting classification, additional detections are not accepted. The larger classifier therefore runs only for segments associated with an accepted event. Both deep neural networks use depthwise separa-ble one-dimensional convolutions, combining depthwise temporal filtering with pointwise convolutions across feature channels. The event detector comprises three convolutional blocks with a kernel size of 13, while the gesture classifier uses six blocks with a kernel size of 21. Each block includes batch normalization, ReLU activa-tion, and max pooling with a factor of 2. The detector contains 2,198 and the classifier 8,311 trainable parameters. The event detector and gesture classifier were trained on the knock, swipe-up, and swipe-down subset of the dataset introduced by Hettstedt et al. [2]. While the original recordings were collected in a single laboratory environment, everyday use can involve ad-ditional background vibrations induced by environmental sounds. These vibrations are captured by the sensors and can interfere with both event detection and gesture classification. To expose the mod-els to disturbances beyond the original recording conditions, train-ing included clean recordings and versions additively augmented with separately recorded sensor signals from music, conversations, and podcasts at nominal signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) of [20, 10, 5, 0, −5, −10] dB. Noise scaling was based on mean power over unfiltered 1 s event windows and complete noise recordings. For event detection, 100 ms band-pass filtered windows were extracted around annotated ges-ture onsets, with uniformly sampled temporal shifts within ±50 ms during training. Each condition included an equal number of nega-tive windows from separate quiet or noise-only recordings, using the same noise scaling as the corresponding positive recordings for augmented conditions. Each of the 19 training conditions contained 4,500 gesture windows. Including the additional background win-dows for event detection, this yielded 85,500 training samples per epoch for the gesture classifier and 171,000 for the event detector. Both networks execute locally using TensorFlow Lite Micro with 32-bit floating-point operations. The preprocessing methods are generated with the elasticAI.preprocessor framework 1. In an offline evaluation using leave-one-subject-out cross-validation with clean and noise-augmented samples, the event detector and gesture classifier achieved mean accuracies of 82.5% and 85.7%, respectively. In each fold, all samples from one participant were reserved for testing, while the models were trained on samples from the remaining participants. Across ten inference calls per neural network, mean inference times were 14.7 ms for the event detector and 107.1 ms for the gesture classifier. The mean latency from the estimated event timestamp to the completion of gesture classification was 598.2 ms. This interval includes buffering and classification-window collection, but excludes transmission to the laptop and application rendering. 

## 3.3 Application Interface 

The acquisition board transmits recognition results to the host com-puter via USB serial or TCP over Wi-Fi, depending on the firmware configuration. Each recognized gesture produces a newline-terminated JSON message containing the gesture identifier, class probabilities, the estimated event timestamp, the inference times of both neural networks, and the event-to-classification latency. A Python bridge running on the host receives and validates these messages and forwards the recognition results to the application via local UDP messages. The bridge provides a common interface for both trans-port variants, allowing the application to consume gesture events independently of the connection to the acquisition board. 

## 4 Interactive Demonstration 

TableControl allows visitors to control either an endless runner game or a physical table lamp by performing gestures on an instru-mented tabletop. The demonstration setup includes a laptop, the lamp and everyday objects placed on the table. The central area remains clear for gesture interaction, as the sensors are mounted directly beneath it. In the endless runner game, implemented in Python using pygame-ce , the character runs automatically. Visitors use swipe-up gestures to jump over low obstacles (Figure 3a) and swipe-down gestures to duck beneath elevated obstacles. A knock starts a short tutorial that introduces the controls through two jumps and two ducking actions without obstacles. Once these actions have been completed, the game begins automatically. A collision ends the run, and another knock resets the game and score for a new attempt. The second application uses the same gestures to control a physi-cal table lamp. A knock switches the lamp on or off (Figure 3b), while swipe-up gestures increase its brightness and swipe-down gestures decrease it. The host computer sends the corresponding control commands to the lamp over Wi-Fi, with the resulting changes directly visible at the lamp. This application demonstrates how ges-tures performed on the tabletop can serve as input for an everyday physical device. The game and lamp provide visible feedback on the triggered actions, while a recognition dashboard provides additional infor-mation about how the system interprets the gestures. It displays the most recent recognized gesture and the probabilities assigned to the gesture classes, alongside the inference times of both neural networks and the event-to-classification latency. 

## 5 Discussion and Conclusion 

TableControl demonstrates how tabletop gestures can control digi-tal applications and physical devices, with recognition performed entirely on a microcontroller. The game and lamp scenarios illus-trate how the same gestures can support playful interaction and everyday device control, highlighting the potential of familiar fur-niture surfaces as input interfaces. Future work will investigate shorter classification windows and model quantization to reduce latency and resource usage while assessing their effects on recognition performance. Further eval-uation will examine recognition across users, tabletop materials, object arrangements, and environmental vibrations, with particular attention to unintended activations during everyday table use. We will also investigate gesture mappings and application feedback to guide the design of further applications for smart furniture.
