Stress is an increasingly prevalent concern, creating a need for accessible and personalized support. Stress-management-chatbots primarily rely on what users report, which may not capture their physiological changes. Wearables capture these changes, but chatbots rarely use them as memory across conversations. We therefore present MEM-STRESS, a stress-support chatbot that turns wearable ring’s physiological-data into a layered memory of daily records, baseline deviations, LLM-generated summaries, and uses it to drive multi-session conversations grounded in Dialectical Behavior Therapy(DBT). In a four-week, non-randomized study with 60 adults, we compared MEM-STRESS(Treatment) with a chatbot remembering only past conversations(Control). 

The memory shaped conversations: The Treatment chatbot referenced participants’ physiological-features (e.g., activity in 71.8% of sessions) and suggested more body-related DBT-skills than Control (14.5% vs. 5.7%), with Treatment participants also using slightly more body-related words (1.76% vs. 1.45%). Treatment participants praised personalization three-times as often (44.4% vs. 14.3%), more frequently reported reflecting differently on stress (69.0% vs. 46.4%, not significant), and described gaining insight into their stress signals. Their perceived stress fell 14.2% from baseline versus 3.6% for Control, though the group difference was not significant. Grounding stress-support chatbots in layered physiological memory is thus feasible and may improve perceived personalization, motivating larger randomized studies. 1 Introduction 

Stress is among the most common everyday health concerns [5]: academic, financial, and social pressures accumulate over weeks, and persistent stress erodes sleep, cardiovascular and immune function, and perfor-mance [20, 65, 82]. Yet most stressed people never receive support: professional care is limited by cost, stigma, and provider shortages [19, 50], and many prefer to cope alone [26, 27]. The UbiComp (Ubiquitous Computing) and HCI (Human-Computer Interaction) communities have therefore designed low-barrier, self-guided everyday tools [71, 112]. Chatbots are a promising form of such support: from early cognitive-behavioral agents [32, 33, 45] to empathic ones built on LLMs (Large Language Models) [96, 103], AI (Artificial Intelligence) chatbots reduce distress [39, 57]. Yet users trust them only with some topics [85], report generic or inconsistent answers and forgetfulness [61, 100,

102], and LLMs risk misinterpreting and mirroring users [78, 98]. At root, such chatbots know only what the user types, and only while the conversation lasts . Memory-augmented agents retain information across sessions [17, 79, 86, 122], and long-term memory increases health-related self-disclosure [47], but they recall what users said , not how their bodies have been responding : a chatbot cannot know its user slept only four hours last night unless the user notices and mentions it. Consumer wearables such as smart rings continuously record this missing context: sleep, heart rate, HRV (Heart Rate Variability), physical activity, and recovery. These signals are tied to stress: stress and sleep influence each other [4, 49], heart rate rises under acute stress [15], and reduced HRV marks stress and diminished emotion regulation [52, 107]. UbiComp research has mostly used such signals to detect stress [3, 42, 63, 92, 94, 101]. Detection alone, however, does not tell a person what to do next [68]. LLMs have begun using wearable data for health and well-being: they answer personal health questions [54, 67], coach sleep and fitness [48, 51, 113], support reflection on fitness-tracker data [104], and turn multimodal sensing into narratives about how a person is doing [118]. These systems, however, treat wearable data as one-off input and were studied in benchmarks, expert evaluations, or small deployments, not stress-support conversations. These limitations highlight key gaps in both theory and practice. Theoretically, stress is both perceived and embodied [21, 65]. However, conversational memory models capture only verbal disclosures, while passive-sensing research treats physiological signals as isolated inputs or detection targets rather than accumulating context. Practically, even memory-augmented chatbots remain blind to gradual physiological shifts. Beyond preliminary explorations [25, 75], no stress-support agent has maintained a persistent memory of wearable physiological features across conversations, nor have such systems been evaluated in longitudinal field deployments to determine how this memory functions and what value it adds. Addressing these gaps is critical because everyday stress accumulates gradually and often unnoticed; support reliant solely on self-reports risks overlooking manifest bodily strain, such as poor sleep. Just as contextualizing text with passive physical sensing enhances person-centered psychological predictions [90], pairing user accounts with physiological signals enables chatbots to deliver richer, person-centered support. To address these theoretical and practical gaps, we introduce MEM-STRESS , a conversational agent designed for multi-session stress support through a persistent memory of physiological features . Using wearable sensor data (here, an Oura Ring), the chatbot tracks physiological metrics against individual baselines and maintains a three-layered memory structure— episodic , pattern , and semantic (Section 3.2)—alongside past conversation summaries. Grounded in this contextual memory and the user’s self-report, MEM-STRESS guides brief reflective dialogues and recommends actionable Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) skills [60]. We selected DBT because its brief, structured exercises are well-suited for self-guided practice outside clinical settings and have demonstrated efficacy in university populations and digital tools [88, 95]. Integrating physiological memory offers three distinct advantages. First, it can surface subtle, unmentioned physiological shifts, such as accumulating sleep loss. Second, it provides an objective longitudinal record inde-pendent of participant recall, potentially mitigating self-report recency and salience biases [99] as well as LLMs’ tendency to mirror users [98]. Third, it allows the agent to tailor skill recommendations to current physiological states—for instance, suggesting body-calming techniques over problem-solving when recovery is compromised, addressing a personalization gap noted in existing tools [12, 55]. However, whether these physiological cues effectively transfer to and enhance conversational dialogue remains an open question. To evaluate this approach, we conducted a four-week field study with 60 adults experiencing everyday stress within a university community (Section 3.3). Participants were assigned to two non-randomized conditions: a Treatment group interacting with MEM-STRESS and a Control group using a chatbot that retained only conversation history. Every two days, participants discussed a recent stressor, rated their momentary affect on the Valence-Arousal-Dominance (VAD) scale [66] before and after chatting, and completed the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) [21] at screening and after every fourth session. We address three RQs (Research Questions): 

• RQ1 (Stress and Emotional Well-being): Does a chatbot with a memory of physiological features help people manage their stress more than a chatbot that remembers only conversations? 

• RQ2 (Conversation and Intervention): How does the memory of physiological features impact the stress-support conversations? 

• RQ3 (User Experience): How do people experience a conversational chatbot that remembers their physiological features? Our contributions are: (1) A memory of wearable physiological features for a stress-support chatbot. MEM-STRESS turns continuous wearable physiological features into a hierarchical memory that, with summaries of prior conversations, grounds DBT-informed stress support across sessions. (2) Longitudinal field study on physiological memory in stress support. A 4-week study with 60 participants (30/group; 772 sessions) collected 1,538 pre/post affect ratings and >1,000 Treatment ring-data days, tracking memory accumulation. To our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal field study of a physiology-grounded stress-support chatbot. (3) Design knowledge for chatbots grounded in physiological features. We derive design, ethical, and methodological implications: how to present physiological features, connect them to users’ own accounts, and combine physiological and conversational memory. 

2 Related Work 2.1 Passive Sensing for Stress 

Retrospective self-report such as the PSS-10 [21] misses within- and across-day fluctuations; EMA (Ecological Momentary Assessment) and experience sampling reduce recall bias with momentary ratings [24, 99] but still depend on what people notice and report. Momentary affect is often rated on the VAD (Valence, Arousal, Dominance) dimensions [66, 89], for example with the pictorial SAM (Self-Assessment Manikin), which is quick enough to repeat before and after a brief intervention [13]. Passive sensing captures stress without asking: smartphone sensing tracks students’ stress, mental health, resilience, and academic performance [73, 112], and wearable physiological features track day-to-day self-reported stress [101], classify high versus low stress better than phone features [91, 92], and, from a consumer ring, relate to perceived stress [11]. Yet in-the-wild detection remains difficult [16, 68], largely owing to individual differences [2, 119]. Physiological and emotional stress responses also correspond only weakly [15], and people have limited insight into their bodily signals [34]; physiological features may thus add what self-report misses but should be read against each person’s baseline and alongside their own account. Most such work detects stress rather than supporting reflection ; in the stage-based model of personal infor-matics, data collection alone does not produce reflection or action [18, 58]. Reflection tools include wearable-triggered stressor logging [76], Fitbit-grounded reflective dialogues that changed physical activity (Reflection Companion) [56], and LLM-generated narratives of fitness-tracker data (Narrating Fitness) [104]. Closest to ours, MindScape paired smartphone-sensed behavior with LLM-generated journaling prompts, improving college students’ self-reported affect and self-reflection over eight weeks [74], but uses behavior rather than physiological features such as HRV or recovery, and one-directional prompts rather than multi-turn conversation. 

2.2 Interventions for Stress Management 

We build on DBT, which teaches concrete skills in four modules: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness [60]. Skills use mediates DBT outcomes [72], brief skills formats show promise with university students [88, 109], and skills have been delivered via smartphone coaching [87] and a 4 • Shimgekar et al. 

conversational app whose users reported less depression and anxiety over four weeks [95]. We ask what changes when a conversational DBT coach draws on physiological features. Digital stress interventions yield small-to-moderate benefits [38] that shrink considerably after correcting for small-study bias [59], with low real-world retention [8]; lack of personalization often hinders engagement [12], yet personalization is rarely tested rigorously [41, 55], and context-insensitive support can mismatch changing needs [9]. Ecological momentary interventions tailor support to momentary states [40, 70], the JITAI (Just-in-Time Adaptive Intervention) framework formalizes when , what , and how much to provide [71], and systems deliver stress support conversationally [64] or trigger it from sensed stress [80, 93]; in a four-week workplace study, sensor-triggered prompts yielded more completed interventions than scheduled ones, without significantly greater stress reduction [43]. These efforts largely decide when to intervene; MEM-STRESS instead uses physiological features to shape what support says. 

2.3 Conversational Agents for Mental Health and Memory-Augmented LLMs 

Conversational agents offer scalable mental health support: early reviews found preliminary but favorable evidence [1, 110], and meta-analyses of randomized trials report modest-to-moderate efficacy, larger for generative agents [37, 57]. LLM systems now support journaling [53], cognitive restructuring [97], behavior change [10], conversational self-report [114], and public-health check-ins [46], but users report harmful or inconsistent responses, over-dependence, and forgetting [61, 100, 102], and reviews warn of inappropriate responses, stigma, and sycophantic mirroring [69, 98, 103]. These risks motivate us to ground responses in measured data and to examine where this helped or misfired. Memory architectures that summarize, retrieve, and update past conversations [17, 79, 81, 86, 116, 122] improve multi-session dialogue [117] and increase self-disclosure to public-health chatbots [47], although long-term memory remains challenging [62]. Yet in these systems, memory consists only of conversation. A few systems give agents sensed context. EMMA used smartphone-sensed mood to suggest emotionally appropriate micro-activities [35], and LLM-based systems use sensed data to deliver psychotherapeutic inter-ventions [77], curb problematic smartphone use [115], gather older adults’ health information [121], answer personal health questions from wearable data [54, 67], and coach sleep, fitness, and physical activity [30, 48, 51], though their accuracy in interpreting passive-sensing data for mental health is modest [29, 120]. HealthGuru, an LLM sleep chatbot using smart-ring data, was compared with a baseline chatbot over eight weeks with 16 participants [113], and a duoethnography pairing wearable-triggered stress events with LLM chatbots found only a fraction of flagged events warranted intervention [75]. Most such systems are prototypes, benchmarks, or small deployments, and those addressing stress are preliminary; to our knowledge, none has tested whether a memory of physiological features changes stress-support conversations in a multi-week field deployment against a chatbot with conversational memory. MEM-STRESS fills this gap by combining a hierarchical memory of physiological features with a summary of prior conversations. 

3 MEM-STRESS Design 

MEM-STRESS is a web-based chatbot designed to provide multi-session stress management by integrating wearable sensor data into conversational memory. We evaluated the system in a four-week, between-subjects field deployment comparing two chatbot configurations. Both versions shared the same user interface, language model, session structure, and Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) intervention framework. The Treatment 

chatbot (referred to as MEM-STRESS) combined a layered memory of Oura Ring physiological features with past conversation summaries, whereas the Control chatbot received conversation summaries alone. We hypothesized that supplementing conversation history with physiological data would result in greater stress reduction. 3.1 Conversational Agent and DBT Intervention 

Both chatbots use GPT-4.1-mini (2025-04-14) through our institution’s Azure OpenAI gateway. Each reply draws on the system prompt, a hidden context message (the reflection, pre-session VAD ratings, and group-specific memory), and the 12 most recent messages (prompts in Appendix A, settings in Table 5). The shared system prompt frames the chatbot as an empathetic AI companion, not a therapist, giving brief, warm replies in everyday language; it never diagnoses or mentions study groups and switches to crisis guidance if a participant raises a safety concern such as self-harm (Section 6). The conversation loosely follows Gibbs’ Reflective Cycle [36] without naming or forcing its stages, and participants can ask for advice at any time. When advising, the chatbot recommends exactly one DBT skill [60] from a closed list of 48 skills spanning the four DBT modules (Table 15): it identifies the participant’s main difficulty, then the fitting module, then the skill, explaining why it fits and how to do it. 

3.2 Hierarchical Memory 

A server retrieved each Treatment participant’s ring data via the manufacturer’s API (Application Programming Interface) nightly and just before each session. We call each measured quantity a physiological feature ; features span six domains (sleep, cardiac activity, readiness and recovery, activity, respiration, and self-entered activity tags) and include the ring’s day-level readiness (how recovered the body is) and daily stress (minutes classified as high stress or high recovery; full list in Table 18). As the ring computes most summaries per day, these data describe day-level change rather than moment-to-moment state. • Pattern memory: how the participant differs from usual. For each feature, a rule-based procedure compares the day before the session with the participant’s personal baseline (the mean of all earlier days from study day 1, not the screening PSS-10 baseline) and writes one plain-language statement of the change’s direction and size (e.g., “increased by X (+Y%)”); changes below 5% are labeled stable, and the participant’s own tags are added where available. 

• Semantic memory: what it may mean. Before each session, GPT-4.1-mini generates two texts (prompts in Appendix A.4): a one-paragraph chat summary of all previous conversations (recurring stressors, emotional trajectory, coping actions, and helpful or unhelpful responses) and a semantic description : a cautious, non-diagnostic interpretation of the participant’s recent state, drawn from the pattern memory and chat summary, linking changes across domains without claiming causality. The Control chatbot’s only memory is a shorter (three-to-six-sentence) chat summary. 

Why layers. Human memory separates specific experiences (episodic memory) from general knowledge about oneself (semantic memory) [108], and autobiographical memory is organized as a hierarchy, from specific events up to general patterns and life themes [23]. LLM agents adopt the same split, storing raw experience and building MEM-STRESS: Longitudinal Field Study of a Wearable-Informed AI Chatbot for Stress Management • 7

higher-level summaries on top of it [81, 105]. MEM-STRESS follows this structure: the episodic layer holds the raw ring records, the pattern layer captures how today differs from the person’s usual, and the semantic layer describes what those changes may mean. This structure suits our problem for three reasons. First, roughly 160 features a day cannot go into the prompt, and LLMs handle raw numbers poorly; condensing each layer from the one below keeps the context small, much as recursive summarization keeps long-term dialogue consistent [111]. Second, group-level norms often fail to describe individuals [31], and stress detection does not generalize across people [68], so the pattern layer compares each feature with the person’s own running baseline rather than population thresholds. Third, the semantic layer adds a cautious interpretation that the chatbot can put into everyday language, while the intact episodic archive keeps every statement traceable to the measured data, the same idea of storing observations and synthesizing reflections from them that underlies generative agents [81]. The system also ranks pattern statements by keyword relevance to the reflection and VAD ratings and adds the top eight as a personalization priority list, each with a hint on a fitting kind of coping step (e.g., “Offer a body-calming step because cardiovascular or breathing patterns may reflect activation”; Appendix A.1). Pattern memory, semantic memory, and this list form the Treatment chatbot’s group-specific memory in the context message, from which it also generates its opening message; the Control chatbot instead opens with a fixed template restating the participant’s reflection (Appendix A.2). The Treatment prompt makes pattern memory the primary driver of personalization and DBT skill choice but prioritizes the participant’s own account when it differs from the data. 

3.3 Participants and Procedure 

We recruited adults from a university community via mailing lists, word of mouth, and two group-specific campus flyers advertising the same four-week study with EUR 60 compensation (the Treatment version inviting people to “experience a smart wearable ring” while chatting with an AI chatbot) and linking to an online screening questionnaire with the PSS-10. Eligible people were 18 or older, experienced everyday stress, communicated in English, and reported no clinical mental health diagnosis or substantial psychological distress; we applied no PSS-10 cutoff. Assignment was non-randomized, following the flyer participants responded to, ring availability, and quotas. After online consent and registration, Treatment participants without a ring received one and connected their ring account to the study. Registration marked study day 1. The first three days formed a baseline period in which Treatment participants already wore the ring, giving the memory earlier days to compare against at the first session. Chat sessions then ran from day 4 to day 28 (Figure 1), each following Figure 2 (flyer, onboarding, scheduling, and session details in Appendix E). The 30-minute unguided session prompts the Oura Ring to log granular sensor data every second, replacing its standard 5-minute sampling rate. The analysis cohort comprises 60 participants, 30 per group (Table 16), most aged 25–34 (40 of 60) and holding a master’s (39) or doctoral (12) degree. Screening PSS-10 scores (range 11–31; mean 23.1, SD (Standard Deviation) 4.7; 3 low, 43 moderate, and 14 high stress) did not differ significantly between groups (Treatment 23.9 vs. Control 22.4; Welch’s 𝑡 = 1.22, Cohen’s 𝑑 = 0.31). 3.4 Measures 

The SAM [13], a non-verbal, pictorial instrument, measured momentary affect on the three dimensions of Mehrabian’s VAD model [66]: valence (how pleasant a feeling is), arousal (physical and mental activation), and 

dominance (feeling in control of, rather than controlled by, one’s situation). Participants rated each from 1 to 9 (slider starting at 5), anchored from unpleasant to pleasant, calm to tense (not the standard calm to excited, 8 • Shimgekar et al. 

so arousal ratings partly reflect tension), and low to high control. Following recall of and reflection on a recent stressor (Figure 2), the pre-session rating reflects participants’ state while it was salient. The PSS-10 [21, 22], our measure of change across the study, instead captures stress appraisal over weeks: how unpredictable, uncontrollable, and overloaded people have found their lives in the past month. Its ten items sum to a 0–40 total (scoring in Appendix E), which by convention we treat as low (0–13), moderate (14–26), or high (27–40) stress. Participants completed it online at screening (the baseline ) and on the study website after their 4th, 8th, and 12th sessions. The in-session instructions asked how participants had felt recently rather than in the last month, and the form followed a conversation, which may have lowered follow-up scores in both groups. After their last session, participants were asked to complete the final questionnaire (items verbatim in Appen-dix D): 

• Usability. The ten-item SUS (System Usability Scale) [14], scored 0–100, higher being better (scoring in Appendix E). 

• Agreement. Four custom items (not from a validated scale; 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) on whether the chatbot (Q1) understood the participant’s situation accurately, (Q2) provided suggestions relevant to their daily life, (Q3) gave explanations for its suggestions that made sense, and (Q4) gave good recommendations to manage stress. 

• Behavior change. Two items on whether the chatbot had changed how participants reflected on their stress or emotions and their daily routine or behavior (“No change”, “A little”, “Yes, somewhat”, “Yes, a lot”, or “Not sure”). 

• Open-ended questions. Eight free-text questions (e.g., the chatbot’s biggest advantage and disadvantage, suggested improvements), plus two Treatment-only questions on when the ring did and did not accurately reflect how participants felt or behaved. 

3.5 Collected Longitudinal Data 

The 60 participants completed 772 sessions (386 per group, averaging 12.9 of 13 scheduled; Table 17), and all 30 Treatment participants connected the ring. One further Treatment session had a chat but was not completed, so RQ2’s conversation analyses cover 387 Treatment sessions; 769 sessions had both pre- and post-session ratings (1,538 ratings). Participants also submitted 178 in-session PSS-10 questionnaires (at least one each); one lacked a session number and is excluded from RQ1’s mixed-effects model (237 readings including screening). Control participants wrote significantly more per session (more turns and words, and longer reflections), whereas Treatment participants reported significantly higher pre-session valence, which we consider when interpreting affect outcomes (Table 17; tests in Appendix E); stress types in the reflections did not differ significantly between groups. 

4 Results 

We report results by research question (RQ1–RQ3). Given this initial field study’s modest sample, several effects were not statistically significant; we still report their direction and size, because consistent patterns across measures are informative and motivate larger confirmatory studies. 

4.1 Changes in Stress, Affect, and Physiological Features (RQ1)

5 Discussion 

UbiComp research has mostly used physiological features to detect stress [42, 92], with poor generalization across people [2, 68, 119], or to decide when to intervene [43, 71]. MEM-STRESS instead remembers them across sessions, relative to each person’s baseline, to shape what the support says, and our findings trace a chain from this memory to participants’ experience and stress. First, the memory shaped every Treatment participant’s conversations: where the Control chatbot mostly mirrored participants’ words, the Treatment chatbot grounded its statements in their physiological features (RQ2). Second, participants noticed it. Even against a chatbot with conversational memory, Treatment participants praised tailored responses more (44.4% vs. 14.3%), more often felt understood (62.1% vs. 39.3% agreed), and less often doubted that the chatbot understood them (14.8% vs. 35.7%; the last two not significant; RQ3); T27 valued that it connected “the pressure I mentioned with my data”. For UbiComp, sensing may thus serve as context, not only detection: an agent that knows a person’s everyday reality may earn trust. Unlike prompts grounded in sensed behavior [74] or agents that remember what users 

said [17, 79, 86, 122], a memory of physiological features adds how the person has been doing ; it complements, not replaces, conversational memory (RQ3). Knowing a person’s reality, however, did not make the advice feel new. Participants experienced the memory as personalization, not better advice: they rated recommendations alike, and most in both groups found suggestions generic or repetitive (63.0% vs. 57.1%; Table 13), wanting more varied interventions. Both chatbots drew on one closed DBT list; the Treatment chatbot named TIPP more often (14.5% vs. 5.7%) but sometimes repeated formulaic framing of features (RQ2). Future agents could pair sensed context with more varied interventions. Finally, perceived stress declined. Affect improved within sessions with both chatbots (RQ1) [32, 57], but perceived stress fell significantly below baseline within Treatment (23.9 to 20.5) and numerically more than in Control, perhaps partly through regression to the mean (RQ1). The memory may act on this slower stress appraisal through reflection: data alone rarely prompt reflection [18, 58], conversation can [56]; more Treatment participants reported reflecting differently (69.0% vs. 46.4%, not significant; RQ3), and T24 described learning that their stress “showed in my physical metrics”. Yet Treatment participants wrote less in reflections and chats (Table 17); the memory may also have replaced some of their own reflection. As the groups differed beyond the memory and did not differ significantly in stress, this chain remains a hypothesis for a randomized study, and longer deployments could test whether the trust it suggests forms and lasts. 

5.1 Design Implications 

We derive six implications for conversational agents with a memory of physiological features. 

Make physiological features a question, not a verdict. Agents should pose features as questions about the person’s stressor, store the answer with the data, and let lived experience override the data, as when T-P3 explained with travel a measured rise in activity the chatbot had linked to stress (RQ2). Reports of misread sleep and workouts (RQ3) show why data should not be framed as ground truth: a question invites the correction a verdict forecloses. 

Move from single days to personal patterns. Because our pattern memory compared only the previous day with the person’s running average, it could restate changes participants had already noticed; some found these obvious and wanted links such as a walk followed by better sleep (T4; RQ3). Agents could mine person-specific patterns across days and sessions, including which skills helped (e.g., T-P15 felt their heartbeat slow after STOP; RQ2), and offer them for confirmation. 

Guard the underlying data. Almost half of Treatment participants reported sensor or tracking problems (RQ3), and a chatbot that confidently cites wrong or misread data risks the trust that grounding is meant to build. Agents should flag missing wear time, implausible values, and misclassified workouts, and say which day an observation describes. 22 • Shimgekar et al. 

Pair physiological and conversational memory. About a quarter of each group still wanted the chatbot to remember more of what they said (25.9% vs. 28.6%; RQ3), although both chatbots received a summary of earlier chats. A structured record of stressors, skills, and outcomes, kept beside the memory of physiological features, could let the agent recall what happened, not only how the person was doing. 

Evaluate memory use, not only outcomes. Checking that the memory reaches the dialogue, as RQ2 did, separates a failed manipulation from a non-significant difference like our stress result (RQ1). Logging which memory components each reply uses also lets designers audit how the memory shapes advice, e.g., TIPP co-occurred with heart-rate references (17.8% vs. 14.5% overall; RQ2). 

Treat the memory as sensitive. As mental health apps have shared user data with third parties without disclosure [44], agents should collect only what they use (ours never cited temperature; RQ2), let users see and delete the memory, avoid framing ordinary variation as a problem [7] (some sessions cited features despite little reported stress; RQ2), and add tested crisis escalation before use beyond self-guided support [69]. 

5.2 Limitations and Future Work 

Our findings should be interpreted within the context of a few key design and measurement considerations. First, group assignment was non-randomized, introducing baseline differences between conditions beyond the memory architecture itself. Additionally, our sample was demographically homogeneous, drawn from a single university community without post-study follow-up. Future evaluations with larger, more diverse cohorts will help establish broader generalizability. Second, sensing and system constraints shaped our analyses. Physiological metrics were evaluated for the Treatment condition, and daytime heart rate variability (HRV) was unmeasured as the ring records HRV exclusively during sleep. Furthermore, the memory pipeline relied on day-level aggregates, and administering in-session PSS-10 evaluations directly after conversations may have captured immediate post-chat reflection alongside general monthly stress. To build on this work, future research should: (i) evaluate perceived stress trajectories in a larger, randomized trial with long-term follow-up; (ii) isolate physiological memory by equipping Control participants with wearables; and (iii) integrate structured conversational memory alongside physiological context. 

6 Ethics 

Approval and consent. Our institution’s ethics board (name withheld for anonymous review) approved the study, whose planning, conduct, and reporting are consistent with the general principles of the ACM Publications Policy on Research Involving Human Participants and Subjects. Because the study processes health-related and wearable data, its ethics application included a data protection impact assessment. Before taking part, participants received an information sheet and privacy notice and gave informed consent on the study website. They were told that the chatbot is an AI research system, not a human or healthcare professional, gives no professional advice, and may respond inaccurately or inappropriately, and that they could stop anytime. Both groups were offered the same compensation (EUR 60 for completing the study). 

Participant protections. The study offered self-guided support for everyday stress, not a clinical intervention. Screening preceded collection of identifying information; people reporting a clinical mental health diagnosis or substantial psychological distress were excluded and directed to appropriate support resources, which all participants also received. Both chatbots were instructed never to diagnose and, if a participant mentioned self-harm, suicide, abuse, violence, or a medical emergency, to stop ordinary coaching and encourage contacting emergency services, local crisis resources, or a trusted person (Appendix A). Treatment participants without the ring received one and connected their ring account through the manufacturer’s official authorization flow. Researchers monitored conversations and session ratings throughout for signs of acute distress or safety concerns, to follow up and point participants to support resources. Data protection. All research data were pseudonymized with participant IDs; identifying information (e.g., the email address for session reminders), account tokens, and credentials were kept separately. Data were stored on institution-managed infrastructure, accessible only to authorized researchers over secure connections; the study website used encryption. The language model was accessed via our institution’s gateway, whose data protection plan excludes participant data from model training. Before analysis, identifying information (e.g., names, places, contact details) was removed from all participant text (reflections, conversations, questionnaire answers). Quotes are verbatim but labeled with study codes (e.g., T24, C2), without identifying details. 

Ethical implications. A memory of physiological features links sensitive health and stress data across weeks and may make a chatbot seem more authoritative than it is. MEM-STRESS therefore described physiological features in cautious, non-diagnostic language and prioritized participants’ own accounts over conflicting data. 

7 Conclusion 

We presented MEM-STRESS, a chatbot whose hierarchical memory of physiological features from a wearable ring, with a summary of earlier conversations, grounds multi-session, DBT-informed stress support. A four-week field deployment with 60 adults compared it with a Control chatbot that remembered only prior conversations. The memory reached every Treatment participant’s conversations: the chatbot spoke a physiological vocabulary and favored body-oriented skills and those fitting the referenced features, and its participants wrote more about the body. Only Treatment participants praised this data grounding, and they praised personalization more often; both chatbots were rated usable. Affect improved within sessions with both chatbots; perceived stress declined more in Treatment, though the group difference was not significant. Physiological features can thus serve not only to detect stress but as memory grounding supportive dialogue, especially alongside the person’s own account and conversational memory.

Physiological features. The server-side service retrieved each Treatment participant’s data every night at 23:59 and again just before each session, each time re-retrieving all data since the start of the study. The data comprise 13 daily data types, each from its own API endpoint, covering activity, sleep, readiness, stress, SpO 2

(Peripheral Blood Oxygen Saturation), and more, plus the heart rate time series. The features span six domains, listed in Table 18. Readiness is the ring’s daily estimate of how recovered the body is; it is built from contributor scores (0–100 ratings rather than physical measurements), such as resting heart rate, HRV balance (recent HRV relative to the person’s longer-term average), body temperature deviation from the person’s norm, recovery index, previous-day activity, and activity balance. Daily stress gives the minutes of the day that the ring classifies, from physiological features such as heart rate and HRV, as high stress or as high recovery. 

Episodic memory storage. Every retrieved record is stored as a JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) file, with one folder per day and one file per feature. Because every nested field of the ring’s data types is stored as its own feature, a day holds roughly 160 features, and a curated subset of stress-relevant features (e.g., heart rate, HRV, sleep stages, daily stress, and tags) is additionally stored under descriptive names. Pattern memory contains no clinical thresholds beyond its 5% stability margin.

SUS scoring. The SUS statements alternate between positive (e.g., “I thought the system was easy to use”) and negative (e.g., “I found the system unnecessarily complex”) and are rated from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Each positive item contributes 𝑣 − 1 and each negative item 5 − 𝑣 , where 𝑣 is the rating; the sum is multiplied by 2.5 to give a score from 0 to 100. Scores are not percentages. 

Stored session data. Each session is stored as an ordered list of participant and chatbot messages, together with the written reflection, session-level timestamps for the reflection, the pre- and post-session ratings, and submission, and the memory used to generate the conversation. 

Stress types. We coded the stress types in each participant’s written reflections, pooled across sessions, using a fixed taxonomy of 12 types derived from this dataset; a participant could have several types. LLM coders assigned the codes in batches of about ten participants, each given the full taxonomy and blind to the other batches’ codes; the authors then verified the codes. After academic or work performance pressure (83.3% in both groups), the most common types were workload, time pressure, or procrastination (70.0% Treatment vs. 83.3% Control) and relationship conflict (46.7% vs. 53.3%); several categories had few positive cases (Table 19).
