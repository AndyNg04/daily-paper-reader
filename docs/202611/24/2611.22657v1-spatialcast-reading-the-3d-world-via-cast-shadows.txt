Abstract
As multimodal large language models (MLLMs) advance to-
ward understanding and interacting with the physical world,
spatial reasoning becomes essential. Existing spatial bench-
marks have made substantial progress in evaluating spatial
relations, memory, and viewpoint reasoning, but have less
systematically assessed how models connect physical scene
conditions with visual observations. We introduce Spatial-
Cast, a benchmark that uses cast shadows to evaluate this
ability. Cast shadows provide observable evidence of a 3D
scene. Their appearance in images depends on object ge-
ometry and pose, illumination, and viewpoint. We examine
this relationship through three complementary tasks that
assess scene–projection consistency, predict intervention-
induced changes, and infer the physical changes behind
visual transitions. The benchmark contains 21,000 synthetic
questions with separate scene and geometry test partitions,
complemented by 200 SpatialCast-Real questions from 20
photographed scenes. Evaluations show that open-weight
MLLMs, including spatially trained models, remain near
chance on the synthetic benchmark, whereas closed-source
models perform substantially better but still struggle with
shadow-length and light-height judgments. We also use Spa-
tialCast training data for GRPO post-training, improving
open-weight MLLM performance on the benchmark. Further
evaluations show improvements on several external spatial
benchmarks, suggesting transfer of the learned capabilities.
These experiments support SpatialCast as a controlled re-
source for diagnosing spatial reasoning capabilities and
studying their learning and generalization.
1. Introduction
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) are increas-
ingly expected to move beyond interpreting images and
videos toward understanding and interacting with the phys-
ical world [35]. Such interactions require models to un-
derstand spatial structures and anticipate how visual obser-
vations change as an observer moves or a scene is modi-
fied [4, 14]. Evaluating these abilities is an important step
*Corresponding author.
toward MLLMs that understand and interact with the 3D
world. Moreover, recent perspectives on spatial intelligence
have highlighted new-view prediction as a visual counterpart
to next-token prediction in language modeling [9], under-
scoring the importance of understanding how scene condi-
tions give rise to visual observations. However, viewpoint is
only one of several physical factors that shape what we see.
Changes in illumination and object pose also alter visual ob-
servations, calling for a broader evaluation of how physical
scene conditions determine their 2D projections.
Existing benchmarks have examined geometric relation-
ships, including relative positions, distances, and orienta-
tions [4, 26], as well as spatial memory, perspective-taking,
and mental simulation from multiple or limited observa-
tions [31, 33]. More recent work extends these evaluations
to dynamic settings, examining the effects of object and
observer movements [20], projection and viewpoint transfor-
mations [21], and spatially constrained image editing [38].
However, existing benchmarks less systematically test how
physical changes alter visual observations. They also pro-
vide limited evaluation of whether models can infer these
changes from before-and-after observations. Cast shadows
offer a natural way to probe this relationship. As shown in
Fig. 1, the appearance of a cast shadow depends on object ge-
ometry, pose, illumination, and their combined effects on the
resulting projection. Cast shadows therefore provide observ-
able evidence about the underlying 3D scene. By controlling
the physical scene, we can systematically evaluate whether
models understand the resulting projections, anticipate their
changes under interventions, and infer the physical changes
behind observed transitions.
Building on this insight, we introduce SpatialCast, a
benchmark that uses controlled rendering to evaluate how
MLLMs reason about the physical relationship between 3D
scenes and their 2D projections. By varying scene conditions
while preserving their underlying physical constraints, Spa-
tialCast provides verifiable questions that probe this relation-
ship from three complementary directions. Projection Com-
patibility (T1) tests agreement between scene conditions and
projections through forward prediction and inverse inference
of lighting. Projection Propagation (T2) predicts the pro-
jection after a specified light, object-rotation, or viewpoint

Our contributions are as follows.
• Cast shadows as a physical readout for spatial reason-
ing. We leverage cast shadows as observable evidence
of the underlying 3D scene, whose image appearance is
governed by object geometry, pose, illumination, and view-
point. We design three complementary tasks to evaluate
scene–projection compatibility, predict the visual conse-
quences of physical interventions, and infer changes from
before-and-after observations.
• A controlled benchmark spanning synthetic and real
scenes. We use BlenderProc and Blender Cycles to con-
struct controllable 3D scenes and generate 21,000 syn-
thetic questions across 16 shape families and three scene
structures, with separate test partitions for new scene in-
stances and held-out geometric parameter ranges. We fur-
ther introduce SpatialCast-Real, comprising 200 questions
based on photographed lighting changes.
• Extensive evaluation and generalization. We evaluate
SpatialCast on both proprietary and open-weight MLLMs,
including spatially trained baselines, revealing distinct
performance profiles across models and reasoning tasks.
SpatialCast-GRPO training yields model-dependent im-
provements, including a 23.33-percentage-point gain on
Projection Propagation for the 8B model. The trained
models also show improvements on several external spa-
tial benchmarks, demonstrating the potential for cross-
benchmark generalization.
2. Related Work
Spatial Intelligence in MLLMs.
SpatialPIN and Thinking
with Blueprints incorporate 3D priors or structured object
representations [18, 19], while related methods use geomet-
ric primitives and bounding-box abstractions [12, 15]. Other
work improves spatial reasoning through metric supervi-
sion [4], geometric surrogate tasks [11, 23], and verifiable
objectives based on self-supervised spatial tasks or cross-
view matching [16, 36]. SpatialLadder and Spatial-MLLM
further explore progressive training and geometry-aware vi-
sual features [10, 29]. Several of these methods also provide
spatially trained baselines for examining whether learned
capabilities transfer to different spatial reasoning tasks.
Benchmarks for Spatial Intelligence.
Existing bench-
marks differ in their visual observations and reasoning tar-
gets. Spatial457 and OmniSpatial evaluate diverse spatial
abilities [8, 26], while VSI-Bench and MindCube exam-
ine spatial reasoning from videos or limited views [31, 33].
Multi-SpatialMLLM and MMSI-Bench further investigate
spatial understanding across multiple frames or images [30,
32]. Controlled settings enable more targeted evaluations,
including state reconstruction and transformations in cube-
based benchmarks [1, 7] and procedural scene complexity
2


in InfiniBench [25]. More directly related to scene changes,
SAT examines how object and observer movements affect
spatial relationships [20], while SpatialBlock evaluates 3D-
to-2D projection, viewpoint transformation, and structural
combination through synthetic block-stacking tasks [21].
GSI-Bench assesses whether generated image edits satisfy
specified spatial constraints [38], whereas CamChoreo identi-
fies camera-motion types and temporal intervals in videos [6].
These benchmarks primarily examine spatial relationships,
transformations, or visual outputs, while the physical rela-
tionship between scene changes and their observable conse-
quences offers a complementary perspective for evaluating
spatial reasoning.
Shadows and Geometric Reasoning.
Cast shadows pro-
vide geometric evidence about the underlying 3D scene,
as their formation depends on object geometry, pose, and
illumination. Prior work exploits shadows to reconstruct
plausible hidden object shapes while jointly estimating pose
and lighting, showing that multiple scene explanations may
be consistent with the same observation [13]. Differentiable
shadow mapping further enables gradient-based optimization
of physical scene parameters [28]. Other studies investigate
shadow detection from a vision-language perspective [27]
or optimize object pose and illumination for shadow-based
visual compositions [17]. ReNe provides real photographs
with calibrated light and camera poses for relighting and
novel-view synthesis [24]. The ambiguity of shadow-based
reconstruction also highlights the importance of specifying
known scene conditions and candidate hypotheses in inverse
reasoning tasks.
3. SpatialCast Benchmark
SpatialCast tests three inference directions through a shared
projection map. Figure 2 summarizes how the synthetic
benchmark is constructed and partitioned, alongside exam-
ples of real-image questions.
3.1. Scene Representation
A scene state z = (G, P, L, C) specifies object geometry G,
object poses and positions P, illumination L, and camera
configuration C (Fig. 1). A renderer R implements the
physical projection process, producing the object–shadow
observation
I = R(G, P, L, C).
(1)
Scenes contain a planar shadow receiver and one or two
objects. An intervention a updates specified components
of the state, producing z′ = Ta(z) and observation I′ =
R(z′), where Ta is the state transformation specified by a.
Light changes and object rotations affect shadow formation.
Camera changes alter the view of the resulting scene.
3.2. Geometry and Scene Construction
The geometry pool contains six primitive, four compound,
and six procedural shape families, totaling 16 (Fig. 2(a)).
Their silhouettes and exposed surfaces produce varied re-
sponses to light and pose changes. Supplementary Sec. A.1
lists all families with example geometry instances.
The three scene structures used in each task contain a
single object, two objects with separate ground shadows,
or two objects with overlapping ground shadows. Overlap
introduces a compositional observation in which the visible
shadow boundary can depend on both objects. The same
structure labels apply across tasks, supporting task-level
and structure-level analysis without changing the task defi-
nitions. Scenes are rendered with BlenderProc and Blender
Cycles [5].
3.3. Three Complementary Tasks
The three tasks in Fig. 2(b) share the scene representation
and physical projection model of Sec. 3.1. T1 matches scene
conditions to projections within a static scene. When the
scene changes, T2 predicts the resulting projection given
the intervention, while T3 uses the before and after obser-
vations to infer how the specified factor changed. Table 1
summarizes the inputs, prediction targets, and variants of
each task.

T3 asks which change pa-

light changes. From the before-and-after observations, the
model infers whether the light turned clockwise or coun-

and camera-viewpoint questions ask about the direction and
magnitude of rotation. Providing the changed factor defines
the hypothesis space for inferring the corresponding change
parameters from the observed transition.
Question and candidate construction.
Each synthetic
question has four options. Scene metadata identifies the
correct option, and models see only the images, prompt,
and options. T1 fixes the objects and camera. For forward
questions, we render the correct option under the light setting
specified in the prompt and the three distractors under three
alternative light settings. Inverse questions ask which of four
light settings produced the observed image, with the options
varying either in azimuth at a fixed height or in height at
a fixed azimuth. T2 renders the specified intervention and
three alternatives from the same initial scene. Distractors
omit a factor, reverse a turn, or change its magnitude. T3
pairs each turn direction with a rise or fall for the light, or
with two rotation angles for the object or camera. The four
combinations form the text options, and one is sampled to
generate the after image.
Quality control.
During generation, we reject scenes if
too little of an object or its ground shadow is visible, or if
a ground shadow enters the frame margin. For two-object
scenes, we measure ground-shadow overlap to verify the
specified scene structure. For T3, we check that the four text
options cover all combinations of two binary attributes, so
each attribute value occurs twice. We render the scene for
each of the four physical hypotheses, including for inverse
T1 and T3 questions, whose answer options are lighting di-
agrams and text descriptions, respectively. For each of the
six pairs of scene renders, we count pixels in the object or

3.4. Dataset Composition and Partitions
The synthetic benchmark contains 21,000 questions. Of the
8,400 T1 questions, 4,200 use forward prediction and 4,200
use inverse inference. The 8,400 T2 questions are evenly
divided between single-factor and joint interventions. The
4,200 T3 questions are approximately balanced across light
changes, object rotations, and camera viewpoint changes.
The dataset is partitioned into 15,000 training, 1,500 valida-
tion, 3,000 scene-test, and 1,500 geometry-test questions as
summarized in Fig. 2(b).
Scene-test evaluates generalization to unseen scene in-
stances independently sampled from the same scene distri-
bution as the training set. Geometry-test evaluates gener-
alization to held-out geometric parameter ranges within the
same shape families. The two test partitions contain 4,500
questions, with 1,800 T1, 1,800 T2, and 900 T3 questions (a
2:2:1 ratio). Supplementary Secs. A.3–A.4 provide detailed
counts and split audits.
3.5. SpatialCast-Real
To complement the synthetic evaluation, we construct
SpatialCast-Real from ReNe (ReLight My NeRF) [24], a
photographic dataset with calibrated camera and light poses.
Each photograph is captured with one light on at a time.
We group photographs by scene and camera pose, keeping
object geometry, layout, and viewpoint fixed while varying
illumination. From the calibrated light poses, we compute
the change in lighting between photographs and use it to
label each question.
SpatialCast-Real evaluates the light-change variants of
T2 and T3 (Fig. 2(c)). For T2, the model receives a refer-
ence photograph and the specified changes in light azimuth,
height, and horizontal distance. It selects the photograph that
matches these changes from four candidates. For T3, the
model compares before-and-after photographs and decides
whether the shortest turn between the two light azimuths is
clockwise or counterclockwise. A top-view diagram estab-
lishes the coordinate convention. The dataset contains 200
questions across 20 scenes (125 T2 and 75 T3, ten per scene).
Its 587 public images comprise 585 distinct photographs and
two coordinate diagrams. A photograph can appear in mul-
tiple questions. We evaluate all 200 questions separately
from the synthetic benchmark. Supplementary Sec. B details
the construction, and Fig. 8 shows complete T2 examples in
panels (a,b) and T3 examples in panels (c,d).

4.1. Experimental Setup
Compared Models.
Our four base models are Qwen3-
VL-8B Base and Qwen3-VL-4B Base [2], Qwen2.5-VL-
3B Base [3], and InternVL3-2B Base (Instruct check-
point) [37].
Spatially trained baselines include Spatial-
SSRL [16], SpatialBlock [21], Spatial-MLLM [29], and
SpatialLadder [10]. We evaluate all open-weight models,
including our SpatialCast-GRPO variants, on the 4,500 syn-
thetic test questions and 200 SpatialCast-Real questions. We
also evaluate GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, Claude Sonnet 5.5,
Gemini 4 Argon, and Qwen3.8-Max (0902) on SpatialCast-
Real. Of these, GPT-6.1 Sol and Gemini 4 Argon also com-
plete the full synthetic test set.
Evaluation Protocol and Metrics.
Each model receives
the question images, prompt, and answer options. We use
exact-match accuracy, counting unresolved or invalid an-
swers as incorrect. We compute accuracy separately for the
synthetic test set and SpatialCast-Real, weighting each ques-
tion equally within its test set. The synthetic overall score is
micro-averaged accuracy, with T1, T2, and T3 contributing
in a 2:2:1 ratio. The uniform random-choice baselines are
25% for the synthetic tasks and 34.375% for SpatialCast-
Real. We compute accuracy gains on SpatialCast from inte-
ger correct counts before rounding. Supplementary Sec. C.2
details model-specific inference settings and answer parsing.
SpatialCast-GRPO Post-training.
We train each of the
four base models from its original weights using Group Rel-
ative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [22]. After screening the
training split, we retain 11,186 questions, comprising 1,189
forward T1, 1,998 inverse T1, 6,000 T2, and 1,999 T3 ques-
tions. All four models use the same data order and train for
1,399 updates. At each update, we sample eight responses
per question and reward answer correctness and format com-
pliance, with a length penalty. We use each model’s final
checkpoint for evaluation. Supplementary Sec. C.1 details
optimization, screening, and the shared training schedule.
Supplementary Sec. D.2.2 describes additional 4B configu-
rations, including the stage-ordered curriculum.
External Benchmarks.
We evaluate transfer on Mind-
Cube [33], MMSI-Bench [32], and SPBench [10], with
MMMU [34] as a general multimodal reference. SPBench
contributes separate multiple-choice accuracy scores for
single-image (SI) and multi-view (MV) questions. The ag-
gregate transfer metric is the arithmetic mean of the five
scores before rounding. Supplementary Sec. D.6 provides
further evaluation details.

4.2. Benchmark Performance Across MLLMs
Overall performance.
We compare existing MLLMs to
assess their ability to reason about physical scene–projection
relationships. Sol and Gemini achieve 79.42% and 57.71%
overall on the synthetic test set, while the original open-
weight models and existing spatially trained baselines remain
close to chance (Table 2). This gap shows that the bench-
mark’s physical projection questions remain challenging for
current open-weight spatial models.
API models across reasoning tasks.
We examine how the
differences between the API models vary across reasoning
tasks and physical factors (Table 3). Both are weakest on
T1, while Sol peaks on T2 and Gemini on T3. Within T1,
both perform better on shadow direction and light azimuth
than on shadow length and light height. This shared pattern
reveals a performance gap between directional judgments
and judgments of length or height in this benchmark. Within
T2, Sol varies less across intervention configurations, while
Gemini performs better on joint LR changes than on any
single-factor intervention. Performance varies across inter-
vention configurations, revealing distinct reasoning demands
beyond the number of changed factors. Within T3, Sol is
strongest on camera-change attribution and Gemini on light
attribution. The two models perform similarly on light at-
tribution, while Sol’s advantages are substantially larger for
object-rotation and camera-change attribution. Supplemen-
tary Sec. D.1.1 gives subtype sample sizes and correct counts
by task and test partition.

Shadow

Ablation.
To
examine
whether
MLLMs use cast shadows for reasoning, we conduct in-
put ablations on 240 light-change questions (T2-L), holding
object geometry, pose, and camera viewpoint fixed within
each question. Shadow-only removes surface shading and
floor illumination variation while preserving cast shadows;
No Shadow removes cast shadows from the same flat ren-

Full Input (Supplementary Sec. D.3).
As shown in Table 5, Sol achieves 81.7% under Shadow-
only, comparable to 81.3% with full inputs, but drops to
25.0% without shadows. The trained 4B, 3B, and 2B models
show similar declines, from 42.9%, 35.0%, and 52.1% to
30.0%, 25.4%, and 23.3%, respectively. These consistent
declines suggest that cast shadows serve as important visual
evidence for reasoning about lighting changes. Removing
the initial observation also reduces performance for Sol and
all three GRPO models, indicating that the initial scene
provides an important reference for predicting the resulting
projection.

4.5. Cross-Benchmark Transfer
External benchmark transfer.
Table 7 shows that Spa-
tialCast training yields selective improvements on external
spatial benchmarks. The average accuracy increases from
37.51% to 39.12% for 8B, 36.10% to 40.34% for 4B, and
36.87% to 37.69% for 3B. For 2B, the five-metric average
decreases from 37.36% to 35.22%. All four models improve
on MindCube and SPBench-SI. On SPBench-MV, accuracy
rises by 8.19 percentage points for 4B, holds steady for 8B,
and falls by 1.63 points for 3B and 13.11 points for 2B. These
results suggest that learning from physical projections can
benefit broader spatial reasoning, but the transfer remains
model- and task-dependent.
5. Conclusion
We introduced SpatialCast, a benchmark that uses cast shad-
ows as a physical readout of the 3D world to evaluate how
MLLMs reason about the relationship between scene condi-
tions and visual observations. By examining compatibility,
propagation, and attribution through a shared projection pro-
cess, SpatialCast reveals distinct reasoning capabilities that
conventional aggregate evaluations can obscure. Our ex-
periments show substantial differences across models and
reasoning directions, with GRPO-based training consistently
producing the largest gains in propagation across three model
families. These improvements extend to held-out scenes and
geometric conditions, while evaluations on real images and
external benchmarks reveal varying degrees of generaliza-
tion. More broadly, SpatialCast treats shadows not merely
as visual patterns to recognize, but as physical evidence for
probing whether MLLMs can reason about the causes and
consequences of changes in the 3D world.

B. SpatialCast-Real Construction
B.1. Source Data and Ground-Truth Derivation
We use single-light photographs from all 20 ReNe
scenes [24]. Each scene contributes five questions from
camera 00 and five from camera 20, giving 100 questions per
camera and 200 overall (125 T2 and 75 T3). The questions
use 585 distinct photographs and two top-view diagrams.
Within each question, the scene and camera are fixed while
the light moves.
In ReNe’s world coordinates, O is the origin and +Z
points upward. For a calibrated light position (x, y, z), the
azimuth is θ = atan2(y, x), the height is h = z, and the
horizontal radius is ρ =
p
x2 + y2. Azimuth increases
counterclockwise from +X toward +Y when viewed from
above. For a reference and target photograph, we compute
the shortest azimuth turn and the percentage changes in
height and radius as follows.
∆θ = wrap[−180◦,180◦)(θtarget −θref),
∆h% = 100(htarget/href −1),
∆ρ% = 100(ρtarget/ρref −1).
(2)
For T2, Eq. 2 supplies the three changes in the prompt. The
photograph recorded at the target light position is the correct
candidate. For T3, the sign of ∆θ determines the turn di-
rection. Positive values indicate counterclockwise turns and
negative values indicate clockwise turns. Height and radius
may also change between the two photographs.
B.2. Question Examples and Quality Control
Figure 8 shows two complete examples of each task. Across
the dataset, the correct T2 option appears in positions
A/B/C/D 32/31/31/31 times. T3 has 38 counterclockwise
and 37 clockwise turns.
We check all six candidate pairs in each T2 question
(750 pairs overall). The minimum azimuth separation is 45◦.
Within a fixed region of interest on 256×192 previews, each
pair must have a normalized mean absolute difference of at
least 0.10, with at least 25% of pixels differing by 0.12 or
more. Blockwise structural similarity (block-SSIM) must
be at most 0.82. We retain the light and camera records,
question IDs, candidate mappings, and image hashes for
reconstruction.
C. Training and Evaluation Details
C.1. Data Selection and GRPO Optimization
We retain 11,186 of 15,000 training questions, comprising
1,189 forward T1 questions screened for visually distinct
candidates, 1,998 inverse T1 questions on light azimuth,
all 6,000 T2 questions, and 1,999 T3 questions on light or
camera changes. All four backbones use the same shuffle
(seed 1) for 1,399 updates of eight questions, repeating six
questions to fill the last batch.
GRPO samples eight responses per question and rewards
correctness (0.9) and format compliance (0.1). For correct
responses, the length penalty rises linearly from zero at 1,536
tokens to 0.1 at 2,048 tokens. All four backbones freeze
the visual tower and use AdamW (constant learning rate
10−6, weight decay 0.01), rollout temperature 1.0, a 2,048-
token limit, KL coefficient 0.01, and clipping ratios 0.2/0.3.
Appendix D.2.2 compares the additional 4B configurations.

Ablation conditions.
Shadow-only replaces object appear-

color by the original cast-shadow attenuation. It retains ob-
ject masks and shadow geometry while removing surface
shading and floor illumination variation. No Shadow uses
the same colors, masks, and compositing procedure with
shadow attenuation removed; the two conditions differ only
in the retained cast shadows. Both edits apply to the initial
image and all four candidates. No Initial removes the initial
image and its prompt marker from Full Input while retaining
the intervention description and all four candidate images.
Interpretation.
Removing shadows can make candidates
visually identical (Fig. 9); No Shadow therefore reports
original-label agreement after evidence removal, not ac-
curacy on uniquely answerable questions. The matched
Shadow-only versus No Shadow comparison isolates cast-
shadow evidence, whereas Full Input versus Shadow-only
also changes surface shading and floor illumination.
