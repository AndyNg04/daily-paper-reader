# ABSTRACT 

Annotators from different demographic backgrounds often disagree in systematic ways due to varying lived experiences, cultural contexts, and their relationship to the content. When such disagreement occurs, one group’s perspective may sometimes warrant prioritization because they hold an epistemically advantaged standpoint or bear greater stake in the outcome. For example, when assessing toxicity of posts on social media, it may be important to prioritize a targeted group’s viewpoint. We propose a Stakeholder-Aware Joint Multi-Task Learning (SAJ-MTL ) framework to jointly model demographic disagreement both across- and within- annotator groups. The model uses conditional gradient routing to handle sparsity induced by group-conditional labeling, and parameterization supports customized weighting of groups in different task settings. SAJ-MTL improves group-level performance in toxicity detection through better context-aware understanding of demographic-sensitive content. Only annotator group-membership information is required, which is more practical than existing methods requiring more privacy-invasive individual-level annotator details. Our scalable architecture is also computationally efficient to support large-scale deployment. 

# 1 Introduction 

Across many machine learning tasks, annotator judgments vary by demographic group [63]. Such disagreements depend not only on annotator demographics, but also on the critical relationship between the annotator identity and the group for whom the content is most relevant or familiar [17]. In medical diagnosis, Black and Latinx physicians in the US are historically more likely to serve in predominantly Black and Latinx communities [35], and therefore may develop more expertise on the conditions that prevail in these communities and on how symptoms present. In a different domain of annotating online discourse, the sentiment of the phrase “quite good” depends on whether the speaker is British or American, as well as the annotator’s familiarity with the idioms [39]. From stove design [21] to content moderation [64], judgment decisions are often highly context-dependent and significantly influenced by an annotator’s relationship to the content. Thus, when annotating for such highly subjective tasks, one must not only be cognizant and intentional about the relation between annotators and content, but when faced with disagreement, prioritizing certain viewpoints often becomes particularly essential in practice. Early crowdsourcing work predominantly treated disagreement as annotator noise to be minimized, collapsing multiple judgments into a single “ground truth” via majority vote [57, 34, 45]. However, there is growing recognition that subjective annotation tasks [62, 43] such as toxicity detection [53], stance identification [24], and sentiment analysis [6] are shaped by genuine differences in how demographic groups interpret content. Thus, a growing literature now argues that disagreement encodes meaningful social variation, and choices for how to handle disagreement are value-laden [20]. Fazelpour and De-Arteaga [17] adopt a standpoint theory lens [29] to posit that the relationship between annotator identity and the content being annotated is a crucial dimension through which sociocultural identity and diversity is arXiv:2611.21458v1 [cs.CL] 16 Nov 2026 relevant to the machine learning pipeline. Empirical evidence lends support to this perspective. For example, Davani et al. [11] empirically demonstrate that majority aggregation systematically erases minority viewpoints, a concern also raised on conceptual grounds by Noble [44], while [22, 54, 46] show that disagreement often reflects important structured group- and individual-level differences. While some works have tied annotator identity directly into models, this is typically done broadly as an auxiliary signal, not as a principled source of epistemic variation [70, 50, 32, 65]. Most existing methods considering annotator identity do not fully account for how the annotator-content relationship influences subjective judgments. This gap has been acknowledged in the literature; Binns [4] highlights that although some perspectives carry greater epistemic authority in fairness-critical settings, current modeling pipelines lack capabilities to explicitly operationalize such prioritization. Fleisig et al. [19], a notable exception, attempts richer modeling of annotator variation. To do this, however, it requires fine-grained individual-level data or surveys, which raises significant privacy concerns and is often infeasible because such fine-grained data is rarely available. Overall, we perceive an unmet need for a framework that: (1) models both across-group and within-group disagreements explicitly; (2) enables dynamic, context-dependent viewpoint prioritization; and (3) remains computationally tractable without requiring privacy-invasive individual annotator-level features. This modeling space remains underexplored, lacking architectures that embed disagreement and socially grounded prioritization into the predictive pipeline itself. To address these challenges, we propose a novel multi-task learning (MTL) architecture, Stakeholder-Aware Joint MTL (SAJ-MTL ), that explicitly models interactions between annotator and target demographics to model context-dependent weights on socially-grounded perspectives. SAJ-MTL treats annotator–target relationships as primary signals rather than incidental metadata. In regard to challenge (1) above, instead of smoothing away disagreement, the model captures both across-group and within-group disagreements via two protocols: Across-Groups (AG) vs. Across-and-Within-Groups (AWG). By conditioning model predictions on annotator and target identities, the framework preserves multiple perspectives rather than collapsing them into a single label. As for challenge (2) , SAJ-MTL enables dynamic viewpoint prioritization by allowing the model to integrate weighting functions that reflect epistemic authority, democratic considerations of representation, or task-specific priorities, rather than assuming a single canonical perspective. Finally, for challenge (3) , SAJ-MTL achieves tractable computation by factorizing annotator and target representations into an additive MTL architecture. Avoiding the quadratic explosion of per-pair modeling enables the framework to handle many stakeholder groups without sacrificing predictive performance and memory stagnation. We evaluate SAJ-MTL on online toxicity detection, a consequential setting wherein demographic disagreements directly shape annotation outcomes. Toxicity judgments are deeply subjective, tied to lived experience, historical context, and group-specific exposure to harm. As prior work shows, language that appears innocuous to one community may be offensive when directed toward another [53, 12]. Moreover, when annotators hold negative beliefs about a group, they are less likely to label speech targeting that group as hateful [10]. Toxicity datasets frequently employ majority-vote aggregation, which suppresses minority-group opinions and systematically biases models away from those most affected by harmful content [11]. By considering the relationships between annotator and target demographics, SAJ-MTL produces a more faithful representation of how different communities perceive harm and enables viewpoint prioritization that centers the perspective of those at risk of being harmed. Evaluation on two toxicity datasets show SAJ-MTL improves context-aware understanding of demographically sensitive content and improves prediction. 

Contributions: 1) Joint Stakeholder Modeling . SAJ-MTL explicitly models demographic interactions between annotator, target and content, with generality allowing broad use across diverse subjective annotation tasks with multiple stakeholders. Conditional gradient routing supports sparse samples per task. 

2) Disagreement-Aware Protocols . 4) Better Toxicity Detection . When applied to toxicity detection, SAJ-MTL models how different annotator–target groups perceive harmful language, yielding predictions that respect demographic variation, leading to more context-sensitive toxicity judgments and reduced fairness disparities across groups. 5) Reproducibility . We will share our source code via Github, including architecture code and detailed experimental protocols. 

# 2 Related Work 

We connect research in machine learning (ML) and natural language processing (NLP) on annotator disagreement, content relationships, and scalable MTL. 2Tasks with Annotator Disagreements. Crowdsourcing and annotation work has repeatedly found structured hetero-geneity in labels that correlates with annotator attributes [63]. Early work explicitly modeled annotator reliability and systematic biases [13, 67] while later work extended this to jointly learn annotator confusion matrices with classifiers [49]. Even tasks with seemingly objective ground truth are labeled differently depending on the background, expertise, familiarity and lived experiences of the annotators, which is well-documented across domains. Judgments about stove design are highly subjective, with ideal forms varying significantly across the world and socioeconomic groups [21]. In medical diagnosis, decisions can vary based on a clinician’s training, experience, and normative practices of their professional environment [1]. Radiologists trained in different clinical settings may legitimately disagree on whether a faint shadow in an X-ray signals pathology or artifact [30]. In cardiology, recognized symptoms of a heart attack, such as crushing chest pain radiating down the left arm, are historically based on presentations in White male patients, but women and Black patients often present with different or subtler symptoms [26]. These examples motivate models that preserve annotator-level variation [11]. 

Modeling Annotator-Content Relationship. Recent works model different dimensions of the annotator–content relationship directly in the modeling pipeline [63]. Jury learning [22] treat annotators as stakeholders and learns to predict annotator-specific labels. Personalized classifiers [70] adapt predictions to an individual annotators’ sensitivity using per-user annotation history, while noise-aware multi-annotator frameworks [32] estimate per-annotator reliability to denoise labels. These approaches capture individual preference, but none of these models the relationship between demographics of annotators and of another stakeholder (e.g. target) as context-dependent variation. Most directly related to our work, Fleisig et al. [19] model annotator disagreement for subjective tasks by conditioning on annotator and target identity, serving as a precursor to our approach and a primary baseline. While prior work demonstrates the value of conditioning on annotator identity, in practice it can be data-hungry or privacy-invasive by requiring per-annotator surveys or identifiers. However, Sachdeva et al. [51] show that annotator behavior in subjective tasks like toxicity prediction can be efficiently modeled by decomposing judgments into independent latent traits (see Appendix A for a psychometric interpretation of SAJ-MTL as a neural generalization of identity-sensitive IRT). Our work contributes to this body of literature by proposing a scalable multi-task learning approach that models the relationship between annotator and target demographics. 

Demographic Bias and Fairness in NLP. Models trained on aggregated annotations are well-known to reproduce and even amplify social biases. Sap et al. [53], Davidson et al. [12] show that marginalized perspectives can be systematically underrepresented when datasets collapse annotations. Dixon et al. [15] highlight disparities in toxicity detection across demographic groups. Studies underscore the importance of fairness-aware modeling approaches that explicitly consider group-level variation. 

Scalability in MTL. Scaling MTL models to many tasks (annotator × target heads in our work) is challenging. Naive per-task heads typically leads to multiplicative parameter growth in both memory and runtime [5], so recent architectures emphasize parameter-efficiency and sparse conditioning. Adapter modules [27] and adapter fusion [47] allow compact per-task updates, while Low-rank adaptation [28] and prompt/prefix tuning [38] keep the per-task footprint small. Mixture-of-Experts [56, 18] uses learned sparse routing to provide additional model capacity with controlled FLOPs, while multi-gate MoE [41] and PLE [61] propose task-specific gating for MTL. Techniques for gradient conflict mitigation [69, 8] address optimization interference among tasks and remain an active area of research. 

# 3 Methods 

We now formalize the problem of demographic-aware annotation and introduce our Stakeholder-Aware Joint Multi-Task Learning (SAJ-MTL) framework. After highlighting inherent scalability issues with a naive multi-task formulation, we detail our scalable factorized architecture designed to efficiently capture both annotator and target group conditionality, labeling protocols, training objective and viewpoint prioritization scheme. 

3.1 Notation & Problem Formulation 

We formalize the annotation setting as follows. Let X denote the space of input samples ( e.g. , text comments), which we map to a structured feature representation via a text encoder ( e.g. , BERT [14], RoBERTa [40], etc. ). Let Y denote the label space ( e.g. , toxicity score). Each comment is associated with a structured feature vector x ∈ X, an annotation label y ∈ Y , and metadata about the annotator-group and the target-group (mentioned or implicated in the content). We denote A = {a1, a 2, . . . , a |A|} as the set of annotator demographic groups, and T = {t1, t 2, . . . , t |T |} as the set of target demographic groups, with cardinality as |A| and |T | respectively. Each training instance i ∈ N is a tuple (xi, a i, t i, y i), where xi ∈ X is the content (feature vector of post), ai ∈ A is the annotator’s demographic group, ti ∈ T is the target’s demographic group, and yi ∈ Y {0,1} is the binary judgment noting 3the label given by annotator ai to instance xi when considered in relation to target ti (e.g. , is a post hateful towards a particular demographic). We assume the observed label is drawn from a conditional distribution yi ∼ P (y | xi, a i, t i),where both annotator- and target-group identities influence the group-conditioned interpretation of the content. This explicitly departs from both traditional single-truth formulations that assume yi ∼ P (y | xi) alone governs labeling, and stakeholder-aware formulations that condition predictions on the content and a single stakeholder at a time.

3.3 Modeling Annotator-Target Conditionality 

We model the subjective prediction of input x, annotated by demographic group a and referring to target group t, using separate representations for the input and each stakeholder identity. Specifically, the shared encoder (with parameters ϕ)produces a content embedding hϕ(x) ∈ Rd, while learnable embedding layers produce annotator embeddings ea ∈ Rda ,and target embeddings et ∈ Rdt , corresponding to the demographic groups a and t, respectively. These three embedings are then combined through the proposed conditional architecture yielding a stakeholder-conditioned representation: 

z(x, a, t ) = fcond (hϕ(x), e a, e t) (1) where fcond is a conditional parameterization function that composes the three components into a unified latent representation. The function may take various forms depending on modeling priorities. The fused representation is then passed through branch-specific module heads ga, g t, yielding conditional predictions: 

fθ (y | x, a, t ) = softmax [ ga(z(x, a, t )) + gt(z(x, a, t )) ] (2) 4This enables annotator-target specific losses to backpropagate through the shared encoder hϕ and the relevant group-specific embeddings ea, et. This selective mechanism allows the model to specialize efficiently for specific annotator-target contexts without interference from unrelated pairs, and is less prone to data sparsity issues. 3.4 Architectural Overview 

SAJ-MTL implements annotator–target conditionality with each annotator–target pair corresponding to a logical task. Parameters are composed from independent annotator- and target-specific modules, rather than instantiated explicitly (see Fig. 3 ). Whereas conventional MTL tasks are predefined and static, SAJ-MTL tasks are conditionally activated based on annotator and target metadata for each labeled instance. We implement the conditional parameterization function fcond as a linear additive function to empirically balance expressiveness and computational efficiency.

3.6 Viewpoint Prioritization Mechanism 

Subjective annotation tasks often require privileging certain perspectives over others, especially in contexts where lived experience or group-specific harm confers epistemic authority. To support such socially grounded decision-making, SAJ-MTL incorporates a viewpoint prioritization mechanism that modulates predictions based on the relationship between annotator and target groups. This allows the model to flexibly reflect platform policies, application goals, or normative choices about whose perspective should guide evaluation. This is especially valuable in domains like toxicity detection, where harm is not uniformly experienced and where platform governance may require accounting for group-specific viewpoints.

Subjective annotation datasets often contain multiple labels per instance that capture variation both across groups and within the same group. To formally model this, we consider two labeling strategies distinguishing how labels are indexed and used by SAJ-MTL: AG (Across-Groups) vs. AWG (Across-and-Within-Groups). This distinction is critical for learning differences in subjective data. The AG strategy focuses on disparate belief i.e. , modeling systematic differences in labeling behavior between distinct groups. In contrast, the AWG strategy addresses the ecological fallacy of assuming group-level trends apply uniformly to all individuals by explicitly modeling variation both across groups and within each group. This enables labels to capture the fact that not all members within a group necessarily agree. Such an approach enables a granular representation that avoids over-generalization while still capturing inter-group differences. 

AG (Across-Groups): Each sample receives a single, group-consolidated label per group representing the group’s average/majority perspective. Different label instances for the same sample therefore correspond strictly to different groups, treating each group’s judgment as a cohesive unit. This approach captures systematic differences between groups but does not account for individual disagreement within a group. 

AWG (Across-and-Within-Groups): A single sample can have multiple labels from within the same demographic group, in addition to labels from different groups. This allows the model to see several differing opinions from individuals in the same group. This captures both inter-group and intra-group differences, reflecting the rich spectrum of individual disagreement that exists inside any demographic category. AG captures systematic differences across demographic groups by learning from a single representative label per group, whereas AWG additionally preserves disagreement among individuals within the same demographic group. Because within-group disagreement provides additional information about the diversity of stakeholder perspectives, we hypothesize that AWG will learn richer stakeholder-conditioned representations and consequently achieve better predictive performance than AG. Our experiments in Section 5 evaluate whether preserving this additional disagreement translates into measurable improvements. 

# 5 Results 

We evaluate SAJ-MTL along three complementary axes: 1) predictive performance; 2) fairness across demographic groups; and 3) computational scalability. Per Section 4.3, all results are computed using the epistemic authority 

weighting function π(a, t ) (Section 3.6); corresponding to the strategy in TextAug baseline [19], i.e. , π(a, t ) = 1 , when group identities match ( a = t), else 0. We compare SAJ-MTL against CondMTL and TextAug baselines (Section 4.2). Our main results use DistilBERT as text encoder; see Appendix E foradditional results using RoBERTa-base. 

5.1 Predictive Performance on MHS with Annotated-Targets Table 1 reports classification results on the MHS dataset with human-annotated targets, and reports both micro- and macro-averaged metrics. SAJ-MTL consistently outperforms baselines, showing the benefit of explicitly modeling annotator–target relationships. In regard to SAJ-MTL variants, AWG labeling performs better than AG labeling, reaching a (macro/micro)-F1 of (0.65, 0.64), highlighting the benefit of modeling both across-group disagreements and within-group variation, which are lost when annotations are aggregated or treated as homogeneous within each demographic group. In terms of baselines, CondMTL, which models only the target demographic as a task, performs substantially worse across all metrics. This suggests that modeling the annotator group is critical for some subjective tasks such as toxicity detection, where judgments depend strongly on the relationship between the annotator and the content they are annotating. The inclusion of annotator demographics serve as an additional dimension for the model to learn a better toxicity boundary. Consequently, TextAug appears to struggle to disentangle how different communities interpret the same content. In sum, results affirm that annotator disagreement contains meaningful signal: modeling this can capture more nuanced interpretations of harmful language. 5.3 Scalability Table 5 reports the memory (number of trainable parameters) and runtime (model training for 10 epochs) of our SAJ-MTL vs. other models. Target-Only CondMTL, which does not jointly model the annotator-target interactions, serves as baseline. TextAug requires the largest parameter and runtime among all the models. Being a text-augmentation model, the DistilBERT layers were made trainable to learn updated feature representation from the augmented post. These unfrozen DistilBERT layers are responsible for the massive increase in the parameter space. Our scalable SAJ-MTL model only has four annotator and four target branches corresponding to its additive architecture in Fig. 3. While maintaining comparable results w.r.t. naive SAJ-MTL model, this proposed framework scales well in terms of both memory and runtime. Even with the four demographic groups ( Asian, Black, Latinx and White ) and two stakeholder (Annotator and Target) interactions, SAJ-MTL has 18% fewer trainable parameters than naive SAJ-MTL. The 30% runtime increase of SAJ-MTL is expected given its eight branches vs. only four in naive SAJ-MTL. From a computational viewpoint, as the group cardinality increases in any stakeholder, scalable SAJ-MTL can accommodate model training within the same machine, without GPU memory saturation. Additional simulation results of group scaling can be found in Appendix F . We note that AG and AWG represent different data protocols rather than different model architectures. Consequently, both variants have identical asymptotic computational and parameter complexity. 

# 6 Discussion 

Role of Disagreement in Annotation . A recurring theme across subjective NLP tasks is that annotator disagreement is not merely noise but a reflection of societal and individual variation in lived experience. Literature increasingly emphasizes that collapsing multiple labels into a single ground truth can erase meaningful minority interpretations [44] and misrepresent the underlying construct being annotated. Davani et al. [11] show that aggregating labels via majority vote systematically suppresses minority demographic variation, effectively erasing the valid perspectives of marginalized groups from the training signal. Gordon et al. [22] argue that heterogeneous judgments should not be discarded, as they encode essential information about how different demographic stakeholders distinctively perceive harm, offensiveness, and context. Fleisig et al. [19] highlight that relying on a forced consensus often leads models astray; they show that retaining un-aggregated annotations allows classifiers to learn robustly even when the majority opinion is contextually flawed or socially biased. The proposed methodology contributes to this body of work through a scalable multi-task learning framework for modeling disagreement in annotation tasks, which demonstrates significant improvements for modeling toxicity detection. 

Interpreting Viewpoint Prioritization in Practice. While our model can represent a wide array of demographic perspectives, the decision of which viewpoint to prioritize remains a task-specific normative choice. In SAJ-MTL, this prioritization is formalized through a weighting function π(a, t ), reflecting epistemic, pluralistic, policy-driven, or task-specific decisions about whose perspective should matter most. Following the perspective-aware framework of Basile et al. [3], our work supports a shift beyond a single gold standard toward a system that accounts for disaggregated human perspectives. This approach directly addresses the findings of Prabhakaran et al. In our framework, prioritization is not used to correct the model, but to enable explicit normative choices of how different voices are accounted for. For example, it can be used to center the voices of those with the highest stake in the outcome. In the context of anti-Asian hate speech, for instance, the model can be configured to prioritize the perspective of Asian annotators, ensuring the detection threshold aligns with the community most directly impacted. This flexibility enables a more equitable and context-aware moderation strategy that adapts to different normative choices without requiring retraining. SAJ-MTL thus operationalizes the principle that interpretation depends not only on what is being annotated, but on who is annotating and whom the content targets, without requiring policy priorities to affect the training pipeline. 

Generalization to other tasks. Although in this work we explicitly focused on toxicity detection, the SAJ-MTL framework naturally generalizes to many subjective or context-sensitive tasks in NLP and beyond. Tasks such as stance classification, sentiment analysis, misinformation verification, and even medical image analysis have well-documented sources of demographic variation in annotation. For instance, in medical image analysis, diagnoses can vary systematically based on a clinician’s training, experience, and the demographic presentation of the patient. Rather than treating such clinical disagreement as noise, SAJ-MTL could be used to factorize these expertise and patient-context variables to preserve critical diagnostic signals [71]. As noted by Sorensen et al. [58], as AI systems are increasingly deployed in global contexts, they must move away from average human preferences toward a value-pluralistic stance. Because SAJ-MTL’s conditional structure simply combines shared semantic encoders with annotator- and target-specific branches, it does not assume anything special about toxicity labels and can be applied wherever group-conditioned interpretation is relevant. Moreover, because inference-time aggregation is decoupled from model training, domain experts in these diverse fields can tailor viewpoint prioritization to reflect domain-specific norms, regulatory structures, or governance criteria without retraining the underlying model. 

# 7 Conclusion 

Human annotations in subjective tasks often reflect socially grounded disagreement rather than objective ground truth. Single-truth or majority-voted paradigms treat such variation as noise, discarding valuable signal about annotator and target-group perspectives. To address this, we introduced SAJ-MTL, a joint annotator–target–aware multi-task learning framework that explicitly models interactions between annotators’ demographics and content. Using a factorized conditional architecture with selective gradient routing, SAJ-MTL captures structured cross- and within-group variation while scaling linearly with the number of groups and requiring only minimal group-membership information in order to preserve privacy. A flexible viewpoint prioritization mechanism further decouples disagreement modeling from deployment-time choices, allowing practitioners to encode epistemic authority, pluralism, or policy constraints without 11 retraining. Applied to toxicity detection, SAJ-MTL improves macro-level performance on demographic-sensitive content and reduces group-level disparities, all without explicit fairness objectives. Beyond toxicity, SAJ-MTL offers a general template for learning from subjective annotations where stakeholder perspectives vary systematically. The additive factorization of annotator and target groups suggests a natural extension to a stakeholder triad that incorporates author demographics, as recent works show that author dialect and identity also shape toxicity perception [7, 42]. Integrating such signals additively would capture triadic dynamics while maintaining our privacy-preserving, group-level formulation. More broadly, future work could relax rigid group categories by learning hierarchical or latent group structures, enabling the model to discover intersectional identities obscured by broad demographic labels. Finally, SAJ-MTL’s architecture is well-suited to multi-modal applications: applied to medical imaging with a Vision Transformer encoder, it could reconcile systematic diagnostic disagreements across clinical traditions or specialties, by treating divergent expert judgments as signal rather than noise. 

# 8 Ethical Considerations 

The ethical stakes of modeling annotator disagreement are significant. Without explicit design choices, ML systems risk implicitly weighing majority viewpoints, thereby amplifying harms to marginalized or under-represented groups and reinforcing pre-existing biases [53, 12]. Still, decisions around how groups are defined, aggregated, or weighted require careful ethical consideration. Across application domains, anchoring these decisions on domain-knowledge, and engag-ing in participatory design practices may be important avenues to ensure their relevance and legitimacy. Additionally, it is important to note that there is a risk of misuse. It could be possible to define viewpoint prioritization functions that perpetuate or exacerbate marginalization by purposefully centering privileged viewpoints. Thus, transparency regarding viewpoint prioritization mechanisms deployed is essential. The proposed methodology does not explicitly enforce fairness. Instead, fairness benefits stem from improvements in modeling. This is, however, not guaranteed to happen, and it is essential to explicitly evaluate fairness performance, as illustrated in our experiments. In sensitive domains such as toxicity detection, minimizing performance gaps is not merely an algorithmic optimization metric, but a necessary safeguard against asymmetric harms. Errors may disproportionately affect i) the author of content, via unfair over-moderation or mischarecterization, and ii) the communities targeted by that content, via failure to filter out or appropriately react to harmful content. Careful evaluation across stakeholder groups is therefore required and necessary to avoid reinforcing existing social inequities. Our experiments rely exclusively on publicly available datasets [52, 37], and no additional human participants were recruited or annotations collected. We use the datasets subject to their appropriate access and usage conditions. The use of annotator demographic information introduces privacy and representation concerns. Although our methodology operates on group-level demographic attributes, such information may still be sensitive in some scenarios [60, 2, 66], and its use in deployed ML systems should therefore be subject to appropriate privacy guardrails, informed consent where applicable, and restrictions against discriminatory profiling.

# A SAJ-MTL as a Neural Generalization of Identity-Sensitive IRT 

This section provides a psychometric interpretation of SAJ-MTL through the lens of identity-sensitive Item Response Theory (IRT) [52]. We show that the proposed architecture preserves the additive inductive structure underlying latent trait models while extending them into a scalable neural conditional representation framework over annotator-target demographic spaces. We also note that while classical IRT formulations such as the 2PL model express probability multiplicatively as P = σ(a(θ − β)) , the identity-sensitive IRT variant of Sachdeva et al. [52] operates in the log-odds (latent response) space, where item, annotator, and demographic factors enter additively. SAJ-MTL preserves this additive inductive bias, extending it from scalar latent variables to high-dimensional neural representations. The IRT framework models annotation behavior through additive latent response factors corresponding to item characteristics and annotator-dependent perception effects. The term μlm captures alignment effects between annotator identity and targeted demographic identity. # B Experimental Details 

Setup. Experiments use a Nvidia 2060 RTX Super 8GB GPU, Intel Core i7-9700F 3.0GHz 8-core CPU and 16GB DDR4 memory. We use Keras [9] library on a Tensorflow 2.0 backend with Python 3.12 to train the networks in this paper. For optimization, we use AdaMax [33] with parameters ( lr =0.001) and 1000 steps per epoch. We also setup an EarlyStopping criteria in case the with a min_delta of 1E-4 and patience of 5. The random seed was set to 42 to avoid varaibility. 

Optimizer. We choose AdaMax (infinity-norm variant of Adam) due to its faster convergence from fresh starts, i.e. ,model training from random weights. It is also more stable than Adam in our case with sparse or unbalanced gradients. This makes tuning the learning rate less fragile. 

Regularization. We do not need any explicit regularization term in our SAJ-MTL loss formulation, since all the task branches are treated equally and each branch has a conditional binary cross entropy loss acting on it. For branches having extreme sparsity, we have independent ℓ2 regularization for smoothness, which we have explicitly set to λa,t = 0 .005 .However, during our epistemically weighted evaluation over both datasets, this additional term and its variation did not cause any metrics to fall outside the reported variance range. 

Training protocol : To ensure a fair comparison with SAJ-MTL, all models are trained using the same shared encoder architecture and optimization procedure. All models are trained on individual annotations rather than majority-vote labels, ensuring that disagreement signals are preserved across methods. For SAJ-MTL, annotator and target embeddings are initialized randomly and projected into the shared representation space using linear transformations Wa, W t before 17 being combined with the shared representation. Predictions are then produced through the additive combination of annotator-specific and target-specific heads as described in Section 3.3. By comparing these approaches, we evaluate whether explicitly modeling the interaction between annotator and target demographics, rather than incorporating metadata implicitly or modeling only target groups, can achieve improved predictive performance and fairness. 

# C Dataset Details 

We use the Measuring Hate Speech (MHS) corpus [52], available via the HuggingFace library [16], which includes 135,556 posts sourced from YouTube, Twitter and Reddit. Each post is annotated by one or more human annotators and includes: i) the inferred demograhic group(s) targeted by the post; ii) self-reported group-demographic attributes of the annotators; and iii) a binary toxicity label indicating whether the content is perceived as toxic or non-toxic 

towards the inferred target. Although the dataset has seven demographic race groups as target, we keep only four demographic groups - Asian , Black , Latinx and White , and omit three others ( Middle-Eastern , Native-American and 

Pacific-Islander ). This is due to extremely sparse annotator–target ( Ai, T j ) pair instances in the three omitted groups. For included groups, both annotators and targets were drawn as shown in Table 7 . Because individual posts may be annotated by multiple annotators, we perform the train–validation–test split at the post level to prevent content leakage across splits. Specifically, posts are randomly partitioned into 70%, 10%, and 20% subsets for training, validation, and testing, respectively. All annotations associated with a given post are assigned to the same split. Within each split, a post may still have multiple annotator labels, which allows us to model annotator-specific variation while preserving strict separation of textual content across splits. The dataset exhibits substantial variation in the frequency with which different demographic groups are targeted. For example, the Black community represents the statistical majority among targeted groups. # D Automatic Target Detection 

In many real-world deployment settings, the demographic group targeted by a post may not be explicitly available and must instead be inferred automatically. While MHS provides human-annotated target-groups, such targets will typically need to be automatically (noisily) detected. Consequently, stakeholder-aware prediction systems often rely on an auxiliary model to estimate target identities before downstream classification. We perform automatic target detection to evaluate SAJ-MTL under this more realistic setting, to assess the robustness of stakeholder-aware modeling when demographic target assignments contain errors. This analysis not only highlights the modeling strength, but also potential improvements with powerful encoders, weighing against computational trade-offs for practical deployment considerations. 19 F Scalability of SAJ-MTL 

A key design goal of SAJ-MTL is to scale nominally with increasing numbers of annotator and target groups. However, in the naive design as shown in Fig. 1, this leads to multiplicative parameter growth of O(|A|×| T |). SAJ-MTL addresses this challenge through a factorized parameterization in which annotator and target effects are learned independently and composed additively. With a single shared encoder, a small set of annotator and target embeddings, the total number of group-specific parameters scales additively as ( O(|A| + |T |)) (Fig. 2). This ensures tractability even in settings with dozens of annotator groups or highly diverse target identities. Concretely, rather than instantiating a separate head or parameter block for each (a, t ) pair, SAJ-MTL reuses a single shared encoder and a set of small annotator and target modules, allowing the model to represent structured disagreement without incurring combinatorial cost. In practice, this design leverages parameter-efficient model design ideas from [27, 28]. Beyond memory scalability, this parameterization enhances computational efficiency. Because only the annotator and target modules relevant to an example receive gradient updates, SAJ-MTL amortizes shared backbone updates and computation across tasks. By conditioning predictions on small learned embeddings rather than duplicating large network components, batch-level throughput remains stable even as the number of groups grows. Compared to frameworks such as Mixture-of-Experts [56, 41], which introduce routing and communication overhead, SAJ-MTL achieves scalability with minimal architectural overhead for embedding lookup and head routing. This design ensures fairness-aware modeling remains deployable in resource-constrained environments. Finally, additive factorization promotes generalization across sparse groups. Groups with limited labeled examples benefit from the shared encoder while still receiving individualized modeling capacity. Conversely, well-represented groups can develop more specialized representations through repeated branch-specific updates. This makes SAJ-MTL robust to the long-tailed, heterogeneous distributions common in subjective labeling settings. 1 2 3 4
