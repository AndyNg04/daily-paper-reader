Abstract
Remote collaborative platforms increasingly integrate generative AI to support group ideation. These platforms are sociotechnical configurations
in which spatial mediation, interaction modality, AI output format, and the mechanisms that make contributions visible to a group co-vary as
properties of a deployed system. This paper examines how such configurations shape collaboration among participants and their engagement
with AI during creative work. We report a comparative study of two commercially available platforms that instantiate contrasting configurations:
a planar shared canvas with text-based AI integration, and an immersive virtual reality workspace with concise AI output embedded in the shared
space. Fourteen all-women triads (42 participants) completed a structured ideation task consisting of human-only idea generation, AI-supported
idea expansion, and group convergence. We analyzed collaboration patterns, selection of AI-generated ideas, perceived idea quality, workload,
presence, and the lexical characteristics of generated ideas. The immersive configuration yielded greater spatial presence and introduced a
participation paradox, in which spatial freedom enabled exploration and fragmented group coherence; it was also associated with more balanced
role distribution during convergence, at the cost of higher physical demand. In both configurations, AI multiplied the idea pool, and ideas groups
authored with AI available were rated above participants’ own. AI output format, about 21 words per idea on the planar canvas and 3 in the
immersive workspace, tracked how participants evaluated AI-generated ideas and how often they selected them unaltered as finalists. Neither
configuration made AI contributions visible or attributable to the group: participants invoked AI individually and asynchronously in both. We
derive design implications for AI output format, group-visible AI invocation, and spatial support for convergence.
Keywords LLM, Brainwriting, human-AI Ideation
Introduction
Remote collaborative ideation has become an increasingly important
component of distributed teamwork, especially as remote and hybrid
work arrangements have grown more common in the wake of the
COVID-19 pandemic (Barrero et al., 2023; Buckman et al., 2025).
Research shows that remote teams, while effective in executing tasks,
are less likely to engage in conceptual work or generate disruptive
ideas (Lin et al., 2023). Challenges stem in part from constraints
inherent to virtual collaboration environments including reduced
spatial presence, limited access to non-verbal cues, and difficulty
sustaining spontaneous engagement among participants (Brucks and
Levav, 2022; Ansah et al., 2022; Olson and Olson, 2000; Friedman
et al., 2003). Women face additional challenges in these settings
including greater tendencies to be interrupted, lower visibility of
contributions, and differential reception of ideas, which may further
inhibit participation in creative collaboration (Gupta, 2020; Yassien
et al., 2021; Schiller et al., 2011; Hill and Hincapie, 2025).
A growing body of research suggests that virtual collaboration
may not only limit interaction bandwidth but also alter patterns of
attention and engagement (Minet et al., 2024; Macchi and De Pisapia,
2024). For example, Brucks and Levav (2022) found that participants
in virtual settings explored their environment less and recalled fewer
unexpected details, reflecting shifts in attentional allocation that are
directly related to ideation performance. Such findings highlight that
collaborative technologies do not merely facilitate communication,
but structure how participants attend, interact, and generate ideas.
At the same time, collaborative platforms are rapidly evolving.
Contemporary tools range from planar shared canvases such as
Miro (Miro, 2024) to immersive virtual reality workspaces such as
Noda (Noda, 2023), each offering different spatial, representational,
and coordination affordances (Arthur Technologies, 2023; Dimenteers

during collaborative sessions (He et al., 2024; Miro, 2024; Tao et al.,

While prior research has often examined workspace dimensionality or
AI assistance as isolated variables, less attention has been given to
how integrated collaborative systems structure the joint activity of
groups and AI. The relative lack of attention to integrated systems
is the motivation for our work.
The AI itself is a designed object within these systems, and how
it is presented carries consequences for how groups engage with it.
Holzinger et al. (2022) adapt the personas method to AI systems and
show that decisions about how an AI’s role, competence, and limits
are represented to users shape expectations, interpretation of output,
and willingness to act on what it produces. Seeber et al. (2020) argue
that a machine occupying a team role alters the coordination and
communication demands placed on the people around it, while Evans
et al. (2025) question whether the language of collaboration describes
an asymmetric relationship in which one party sets the objectives.
Empirically, Wan and Kalman (2026) find that assigning distinct
personas to a generative model changes the diversity of the idea set
a group produces with it. The two platforms we compare occupy the
minimal end of this design space: in each, the AI is an unnamed
function invoked on demand, with no stated role, no expressed
competence, and no conversational turn. Persona is therefore not
a variable we manipulate. What differs between the systems is the
form in which the AI’s contributions arrive in the shared workspace.
Three
areas
of
prior
research
inform
this
study.
Each
has typically examined one component of AI-supported group
ideation.
CSCW work on distributed collaboration established
that geographic and technical arrangements shape what groups
accomplish together (Olson and Olson, 2000), that persistent tensions
separate social coordination needs from the technical mechanisms
available to support them (Ackerman, 2000), and that technologies
and work practices are constituted through use (Orlikowski, 2000;
Crowston et al., 2019).
This tradition treats the system as a
medium between people, and it developed before generative models
began contributing content to the shared workspace. Research on
immersive collaboration examines spatial and embodied properties,
including immersion, co-presence, gesture, and avatar representation,
and reports their effects on group creativity (Fromm et al., 2020;
Gong et al., 2022; Olaosebikan et al., 2022; Ide et al., 2021);
AI is largely absent from these designs. Finally, research on AI-
supported group ideation varies the model, the prompt, or the
interaction protocol on a fixed workspace, typically a single planar
canvas or a text interface (He et al., 2024; Shaer et al., 2024;
Pu et al., 2025). Frameworks for human-AI co-creation describe
the interaction itself, including turn-taking, contribution type, and
communication (Rezwana and Maher, 2023), and set out an agenda
for machines acting as team members (Seeber et al., 2020), but they
model the human-AI relationship without specifying the spatial and
representational environment that hosts it.
The sociotechnical configuration lens we adopt takes the whole
arrangement as the unit of analysis: spatial mediation, interaction
modality, AI output format, and the awareness mechanisms that
make contributions visible to the group. Deployed platforms bundle
these properties,
and the bundle is what a group encounters.
Treating them as separable variables might conceal interactions
between properties, for example between the display constraints of
an immersive workspace and the verbosity a designer selects for its
AI output. Therefore, we ask how configurations shape attention
distribution, the negotiation of authority, and the interpretation and
integration of AI contributions during AI-supported group ideation.
Our choice to focus on configurations provides ecological fidelity, but
also carries an attributional cost, since a comparison of two complete
configurations cannot isolate the contribution of any single property.
We address this tradeoff in the Limitations section.

Our work distinguishes relations among people from relations
between
people
and
the
AI
system.
We
use
collaboration,
collaborative, and team for relations among the human participants
only. We use co-ideation for the joint activity of a group and an
AI system during idea generation, and co-elaboration for the act
of a participant extending, combining, or generating new ideas
from AI output. A collaborative platform is a system that mediates
collaboration among people; we do not describe any system as
collaborating with its users.
This usage follows Evans et al. (2025), who argue that collaboration
in its full sense requires participants who hold autonomy over the
ends of the joint activity and who can decline to take part. Evans
et al. (2025) propose using the term joint action “to describe what
happens when humans work with machines.” Others use collaboration
more inclusively for human-AI relations (Seeber et al., 2020). We
adopt the restrictive usage for a reason internal to this study: in
both configurations the AI was an on-demand function that could
not decline a request, could not initiate a contribution, and held
no turn in the group’s exchange. As we report below, participants
invoked it individually and asynchronously. Describing the AI as a
team member would attribute to it a standing in the group’s process
that the configurations did not give it.

inclusive design in technology-mediated collaboration (Criado-Perez,
2019; Freeman, 2016; Fox et al., 2015; Westendorf et al., 2017).
Ecologically, this focus aligns with the growing participation of

1.
A

for

Existing frameworks model the human-AI interaction independently

2.
AI output format as a design variable in group ideation.
In both configurations, the AI-supported phase enlarged the idea

visible
or

Related Work
Collaborative Ideation in Remote Settings
Exploring collaboration in video-mediated environments has a long
history in HCI research. Doug Engelbart’s famous “Mother of All
Demos” event (Ju, 2008), held in 1968, introduced ideas such as
the mouse and hypertext and demonstrated collaboration between
remote participants working on shared files (Engelbart and English,
1968). Since then, collaborative ideation in remote settings has been
studied across multiple decades.
Olson and colleagues instructed small teams to write down
requirements
for
a
fictitious
product,
comparing
face-to-face
interaction with remote collaboration via video-plus-audio and audio-
only channels (Olson et al., 1995). They concluded that teams
connected via video could generate ideas indistinguishable in quality
from face-to-face teams, though audio-only teams produced lower-
quality work. This work established that remote collaboration can
support idea generation under certain technological conditions.
More recent research has refined this picture. Brucks and Levav
(2022) found that videoconferencing negatively affects divergent
idea generation, although not convergent selection. They attribute
this reduced performance to decreased variability in gaze direction,
which narrows cognitive exploration. Prior research links reduced
gaze variability to narrowed attentional focus and reduced creative
performance (Friedman et al., 2003). Interestingly, narrowed focus
may benefit convergent tasks such as idea selection.
An
important
aspect
of
the
Brucks
and
Levav
study
is
the
distinction
between
divergent
and
convergent
phases
of
creativity (M¨uller-Wienbergen et al., 2011).
Divergence involves
generating multiple ideas,
while convergence involves selecting
promising options.
This distinction provides a useful lens for
examining how collaborative technologies structure different stages
of ideation.
Ansah et al. (2022) operationalized divergent and convergent
phases
using
two
established
creativity
tests
(the
Alternate
Uses Test (Guilford et al., 1978),
and the Remote Associates
Task (Mednick, 1968)), as well as real-world analog tasks. Their
findings demonstrate how structured tasks and interaction conditions
influence different phases of creativity.
Structured Ideation and Visual Artifacts
Structured approaches to generating and organizing ideas play a
crucial role in creative processes. These include brainstorming (Osborn,
1953), brainwriting (Wilson, 2013), Six Thinking Hats (De Bono,
1999), and concept and mind mapping (Novak and Ca˜nas, 2008;
Buzan and Buzan, 1996). Collaborative ideation methods can lead
to more creative solutions by exposing participants to diverse
perspectives and supporting associative thinking (Herring et al.,
2009; Dow et al., 2011; Lee et al., 2010; Siangliulue et al., 2015).

Digital collaborative platforms extend structured ideation methods
by embedding visual artifacts directly into shared workspaces.
In this work we focus on one type of structured ideation:
mind maps. Mind maps facilitate the externalization and spatial
organization of ideas in a shared visual space (Buzan and Buzan,
1996). They encourage associative thinking and help participants
identify connections across ideas (Novak and Ca˜nas, 2008; Eppler,

Commercial platforms such as Miro (Miro, 2024) and

as Noda (Noda, 2023), enable mind mapping by providing visual
artifacts that represent linking, grouping, and visualizing ideas.
Sociotechnical Systems, Distributed Cognition, and
Embodiment
Collaborative platforms can be understood as sociotechnical systems
in
which
spatial
affordances,
representational
structures,
AI
integration mechanisms, and coordination tools are interdependent.
In CSCW and HCI, sociotechnical perspectives emphasize that
technologies
and
social
practices
are
intertwined
in
shaping
collaborative
work
(Orlikowski,
2000;
Crowston
et
al.,
2019).
Collaborative systems are not neutral conduits for communication.
They are interdependent components of practice that structure what
becomes visible, how attention is distributed, and how contributions
are interpreted, negotiated, and integrated during group activity.
Long-standing discussions of the social-technical gap further highlight
that tensions often arise between social coordination needs and
the technical mechanisms available to support them (Ackerman,
2000).
Understanding
collaborative
platforms
as
sociotechnical
configurations foregrounds how integrated design choices shape
collaborative ideation.
This perspective is grounded in theories of distributed cognition,
which emphasize that cognitive processes extend beyond individuals
to include interactions among people, artifacts, and environments
(Hutchins, 1995; Kirsh, 1995; Klein et al., 2005). From this view,
collaborative ideation unfolds across participants and shared material
structures, including spatial layouts, interface elements, and AI-
generated artifacts.
Representational tools such as mind maps,
nodes, and spatial groupings do not merely record ideas but actively
participate in structuring cognitive activity by externalizing thought,
enabling comparison, and supporting iterative refinement.
Embodied cognition further suggests that thinking is shaped by
bodily interaction with space (Wilson, 2002; Shapiro, 2019). Research
in immersive environments demonstrates that gestures, movement,
and spatial positioning influence creative collaboration (Gong et al.,
2022; Grønbæk et al., 2024; Ide et al., 2021).
For example,
the Blended Whiteboard integrates physical whiteboards with an
expandable virtual canvas and embodied avatars to support flexible
collaborative configurations (Grønbæk et al., 2024).
Such work
illustrates how embodied interaction becomes one component of
a broader sociotechnical configuration that shapes how groups
generate, explore, and converge on ideas.
The sociotechnical tradition in CSCW developed around systems
that mediate between people. A generative model in the shared
workspace changes what the framework must account for:
the
system now contributes content that groups evaluate, attribute, and
negotiate over, and the presentation of that content is a design
decision on a par with spatial layout.
Together, these perspectives suggest that collaborative ideation
emerges from the dynamic interplay among participants, artifacts,
spatial arrangements,
and AI systems.
Analyzing collaborative
platforms as sociotechnical configurations provides a framework for
examining how design choices redistribute attention, effort, authority,
and uptake of contributions within AI-supported group creativity.
Immersive Collaboration Platforms
Immersive technologies provide affordances such as spatial co-
presence, embodied interaction, and shared artifacts (Olaosebikan
et al., 2022; Gong et al., 2022). Fromm et al. (Fromm et al., 2020)
demonstrated that immersion and co-presence can reduce negative
group effects during brainstorming and facilitate freer idea exchange.
Gong et al. (Gong et al., 2022) emphasized that immersion and
symbolic gestures encourage divergent thinking and inclusivity. Ide
et al. (Ide et al., 2021) showed that symbolic gestures can improve
communication clarity and increase engagement.
At the same time,
immersive environments introduce trade-
offs. Prolonged immersion may lead to cognitive fatigue (Fromm
et al., 2020). Symbolic gestures may increase cognitive load (Ide
et
al.,
2021).
Accessibility
and
usability
concerns
also
limit
adoption (Olaosebikan et al., 2022). These findings suggest that
immersive environments reshape collaboration through integrated
spatial and technical properties rather than dimensionality alone.
Recent telepresence research extends this design space with
avatars and environments reconstructed from captured imagery at
near-photographic fidelity: URAvatar
(Li et al., 2024) produces
head avatars that can be relit to match the shared scene, and
GaussianNexus (Huang et al., 2025) reconstructs a room in real time
so remote participants share a rendering of the same physical space.
These systems expand the range of representational fidelity available
to immersive collaboration. Commercially available platforms sit
well below this frontier, and they vary widely in representational
fidelity and awareness mechanisms. This variation reinforces the
importance of analyzing collaborative technologies as sociotechnical
configurations in which spatial mediation,
representation,
and
interaction affordances jointly shape group dynamics. Across this
literature, the collaborating parties are human; generative AI is
largely absent from the systems studied, and its integration into
immersive workspaces remains an open design question that the
following sections take up.
AI Support for Group Ideation
Research indicates that LLM-powered frameworks can enhance
ideation by supporting both divergent and convergent thinking. Tools
such as Scideator (Radensky et al., 2024), IdeaSynth (Pu et al., 2025),
and AI-Augmented Brainwriting (Shaer et al., 2024) enable iterative
idea generation and refinement. Integrated LLM features can surface
conceptual links and dynamically organize content (Ishizaka et al.,
2024; He et al., 2024).
AI integration reshapes not only idea production but also group
dynamics. Dell’Acqua et al. (2025) argue that AI alters performance
patterns, expertise signaling, and collaboration norms in knowledge
work. At the same time, generative models encode biases (Bender et
al., 2021), raise individual creativity while reducing the diversity of
what a population collectively produces (Doshi and Hauser, 2024),
and can reduce user agency (Shaer et al., 2024). An open challenge
is therefore integration in which AI enlarges the group’s idea space

make, and without homogenizing what different groups produce.
Designing the AI’s Role and Presentation
The work above characterizes the AI by what it produces.
A
second line of work characterizes it by how it is presented to the
people working with it. Holzinger et al. (2022) ground this in the
properties that distinguish human-AI interfaces from conventional
ones:
the system holds a degree of autonomy,
its outputs are
non-deterministic, and its internal process is opaque to the user.
Their open toolbox supplies guidelines, templates, and visualization
materials for constructing AI personas, with a worked example from
medical AI. The method is a design instrument, and the claim it
supports is that representation decisions operate before any single
output is evaluated: a user’s reading of an AI contribution depends
on the role, competence, and limits the system has been given.
A substantial vocabulary already exists for the roles an AI can
occupy in creative work. Lubart (2005) named four ways computers
partner in the creative process, as nanny, pen-pal, coach, and
colleague. Guzdial et al. (2019) derived roles empirically from creators
working with an AI game level editor, friend, collaborator, student,
and manager, and found that the role a creator perceived shaped
their experience of the same underlying system. Guo et al. (2025)
report the archetypes creative writers themselves apply to AI in their
practice, including muse, ghostwriter, and collaborator. At the team
scale, McComb et al. (2023) organize AI participation in design teams
along two dimensions, whether the AI serves as tool or guide and
whether it reacts or intervenes proactively, yielding classes of tools,
partners, analytics, and coaches.
A parallel line describes how the co-creative exchange itself is
structured. Kantosalo and Toivonen (2016) distinguish alternating
from task-divided co-creativity. The COFI framework of Rezwana
and Maher (2023) maps this space in the most detail, organizing
co-creative systems by interaction between the collaborating parties
(participation style,
task distribution,
timing of initiative,
and
the communication channels available in each direction) and by
interaction with the shared product; coding 92 systems against it,
they find most systems communicate nothing to the user beyond the
shared product itself. Koch et al. (2019) demonstrate cooperative
ideation in which the system learns which suggestions to offer next
from the designer’s responses. Zhang et al. (2025b) synthesize two
decades of this work through the lens of agency, mapping agency
patterns and control mechanisms across 134 papers from top-tier HCI
venues. General guidance for human-AI interaction addresses when
a system should take initiative and how it should behave (Horvitz,
1999; Amershi et al., 2019), though such guidance is calibrated to
task completion and error recovery, while creative settings can treat
ambiguity and open interpretation as design resources (Gaver et al.,
2003; Sengers and Gaver, 2006).
Whether an AI can occupy a team role at all is contested.
Seeber et al. (2020) derive a research agenda from a survey of 65
collaboration researchers, organized into design areas spanning the
machine artifact, the collaboration, and the institution, and framed
as dualities in which each anticipated benefit carries a corresponding
harm. Evans et al. (2025) argue the opposing position introduced: in
their analysis, current systems hold at most autonomy over means,
never over ends, which makes them designed artifacts within a joint
action, not collaborators within it. Empirically, presentation choices
have measurable group-level consequences: Wan and Kalman (2026)
find that ideating with several differently configured AI personas
preserves the diversity of the resulting idea set, where a uniform
configuration reduces it.
Two properties of this literature situate our study. First, its
vocabularies presuppose an AI whose role is expressed to users
through naming, framing, or conversational conduct; the platforms
we examine express none of these, which concentrates the AI’s
entire presentation in one channel, the form its output takes in the
shared workspace. Second, nearly all of this work operates at the
scale of one creator and one system, from Lubart’s roles through
COFI’s dyad to the writers Guo et al. (2025) interviewed; McComb
et al. (2023) and the machines-as-teammates agenda are team-level
exceptions, and neither examines how role and presentation operate
when several people share a single AI in a common workspace. That
is the arrangement in deployed group platforms, and it is the setting
our comparison addresses.
Each of these bodies of literature supplies one piece of the setting
our study examines: how remote groups ideate, how structure and
visual artifacts scaffold that work, how sociotechnical arrangements
and embodiment shape it, what immersion changes, what AI support
contributes, and how the AI itself can be designed. Our study focuses
on their conjunction, where spatial mediation, AI output format,
and awareness mechanisms vary together as properties of deployed
platforms.
Methods
We
conducted
a
between-subjects
comparative
study
of
two
widely adopted commercial collaborative platforms that represent
distinct sociotechnical configurations for AI-supported ideation. Each

interaction modality, and AI output style in different ways. Studying
mature, real-world systems enables examination of how contemporary
configurations of spatial design,
interaction techniques,
and AI
integration shape group interaction, collective engagement with AI
suggestions, cognitive load, and collaborative outcomes (Olson et al.,
1995; Brucks and Levav, 2022).

•
RQ2:

Ideation Technique
We employed mind mapping
as the shared ideation structure.
It externalizes ideas into persistent, spatially organized artifacts
and supports non-linear, associative thinking
(Buzan and Buzan,
1996; Novak and Ca˜nas, 2008). Mind mapping facilitates parallel
contributions, flexible reorganization, and clear visual representation
of idea structure, which aligns well with our goals of examining how
configurations shape group processes of idea generation, modification,
negotiation, and AI integration.
Our study compares two commercially available platforms, both
of which support synchronous mind mapping. The two platforms
represent distinct sociotechnical configurations, which we describe
next.
Sociotechnical Configurations
We
conducted
a
between-subjects
experiment
in
which
our
participants completed a three-phase ideation task in one of the
following two distinct setups:
Configuration 1: Planar Shared Canvas with Text-Based AI
Integration (Miro)
Miro is a digital whiteboard that supports infinite canvas mind
mapping with real-time multi-user editing. AI support was integrated
as a text-based expansion feature that generated detailed,
full-
sentence suggestions organized automatically into mind map nodes
(Miro, 2024). For AI support, Miro uses the LLM GPT-4o-mini (see
Figure 1).
Participants worked on desktop computers in separate areas of the
lab and communicated via open audio. Interaction occurred through
mouse and keyboard input, and AI-generated ideas appeared as full-
sentence text nodes on the flat canvas.
This configuration emphasized detailed AI output read on a two-
dimensional surface, and screen-based interaction.
Configuration 2: Immersive VR Workspace with Spatialized AI
Integration (Noda)
Noda is a three-dimensional immersive VR workspace to create and
manipulate mind map nodes. Users were represented as avatars
within a shared virtual environment and interacted with artifacts
through embodied gestures and spatial positioning.
AI support was embedded as a node-based brainstorming feature
that generated concise idea suggestions inserted directly into the
spatial mind map. AI output in this configuration was brief and
spatially embedded within the immersive environment. Noda’s AI
feature is powered by the GPT-3.5 Turbo model. Figure 2 illustrates
the steps taken to use the AI “brainstorm” feature. Participants
used the Meta Quest 2 VR headset while seated or standing in
separate areas of the lab and communicated via voice within the
virtual environment. In both platforms, nodes remained in the
workspace for the session; they differ in how they are read. Noda
labels float in three-dimensional space, and their legibility depends
on a participant’s distance from and orientation toward them (See
Figure 7).
This

AI Features as Deployed
Both AI features are closed commercial products,
with limited
documentation. In each platform, participants used the feature’s
default behavior: the only input available to users is the text label
of the node being expanded, which serves as the prompt (Figures 1
and 2 show the invocation flows). Neither platform exposes system
prompts, generation parameters such as temperature, or model
configuration, and neither vendor documents them; we could not
control or record these settings, and differences in them cannot be
ruled out as contributors to the observed differences in output. The
constrained prompt surface is itself a property of the configurations:
both features expose a single invocation gesture and no conversational
channel, a constraint we return to in the design implications.
The two models also differ in benchmark capability; OpenAI
reports GPT-4o-mini outperforming GPT-3.5 Turbo on MMLU (82%
against 70%) (OpenAI, 2024). Model capability is therefore one of the
differences between the configurations, alongside output format.
The two configurations differ on multiple properties including spatial
mediation, AI model, the length and format of AI output (Miro’s
full-sentence suggestions averaging 21 words against Noda’s keyword-
style suggestions averaging 3), in interface design and interaction
modality (mouse and keyboard at a desktop against controller-
based interaction in a headset), in how collaborators are represented
(cursors against avatars), in text-entry method (keyboard typing
against pointer-based typing on a virtual keyboard, one character
at a time), in voice channel (a separate conferencing call against
voice within the virtual environment), and in training procedure (7.5
minutes of video plus 3 minutes of practice against 11.5 minutes
of mixed instruction).

the comparison: the study contrasts two complete sociotechnical

Study Task: Ideation using Mind Mapping
The study task was designed to support both divergent and
convergent phases of collaborative ideation within a realistic scenario.
Participants assumed the role of a philanthropic committee tasked
with allocating $100,000 to increase sustainability on campus. The
task consisted of three structured stages, allowing examination of
how each configuration shaped idea generation, AI engagement,
and collective decision-making. This task was adapted from the
framework outlined in
Ansah et al. (2022) and comprised the
following subtasks:
1.
Subtask 1: Human-only divergent thinking (8 minutes):

Study Design
We conducted a comparative study (between-subjects) involving 42
participants. Participants were randomly assigned to groups of three,
forming a total of 14 groups. These groups were evenly distributed
across the two configurations, with seven groups in each condition.
Randomization ensured an unbiased allocation of the groups to each
configuration. The study protocol and procedures were reviewed and
approved by the institutional ethics committee.
Procedure
At the beginning of the study, the researchers briefed participants on
the purpose and procedures of the study. Next, participants signed
an informed consent form. The rest of the procedure was as follows
for the two configurations:
Platform Configuration 1:
Planar Shared Canvas with
Text-Based AI Integration (Miro). Each participant was seated
at a computer in a separate area of the research lab and provided
with noise-canceling headphones. All participants, along with one
researcher in another room,
joined a Zoom meeting.
A second
researcher remained in the room to observe and help with any
technical difficulties. Next, the researcher on Zoom shared links to
two tutorial videos for using Miro, which totaled 7.5 minutes. The
participants then accessed a sample Miro board and were given three
minutes to explore its features using a practice prompt. Once all
participants completed the training, the researcher on Zoom provided
a link to the shared Miro board. The participants collaborated on the
study task within Miro, using Zoom for voice communication. The
session was recorded via Zoom. The Miro board was saved for later
analysis.
Platform
Configuration
2:
Immersive
VR
Workspace
with Spatialized AI Integration (Noda). Each participant was
positioned in a separate area of the research lab. Researchers assisted
them on how to wear and adjust a Meta Quest 2 VR headset to
ensure comfort and proper fit. Participants were also given brief
instructions about the handheld controls and navigation buttons.
Next, participants underwent a 5-minute general VR training on how
to wear the headset and use the controllers. This was followed by a 5-
minute guided in-app tour of the Noda application. This guided tour
was provided by the app itself and allowed participants to practice
how to create mind maps, edit nodes, and move around the virtual
space. A 1.5-minute video detailing how to use the AI feature in
Noda was shown to participants in addition to the guided tour. Once
training was completed, one researcher, also equipped with a VR
headset, created a shared Noda meeting room. Participants joined
the meeting room in VR and collaborated on the study task within
the immersive 3-D environment. A second researcher sat in the lab
without a headset to track time and read out task instructions. The

proposed during Subtask 1 “The ideas I contributed were...”); (b)
the ideas generated by the AI assistant during Subtask 2 (“The ideas

questionnaire is included in the Appendix, Table B.8.
Participants
We report on data from 42 undergraduate students,
who were
recruited by email (no male participants) to participate in the study
in groups of three (14 groups). Participants received a $30 gift card.
We chose to focus on all-women groups to minimize gender-related
variability and allow a more controlled examination of how the
configuration influences group ideation. This decision aligns with the
rising participation of women in the remote workforce (Ortiz-Ospina
et al., 2023; Barrero et al., 2023; Buckman et al., 2025; Aksoy et al.,
2025).

Qualitative Analysis
We conducted a thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2012) of
the open-text post-task responses,
using a codebook developed
collaboratively by two researchers. The analysis proceeded in five
stages. First, both researchers independently familiarized themselves
with all responses, attending to explicit content and latent meaning,
and generated initial codes for each question. Second, they compared
their codes and consolidated them into a shared codebook per
question, resolving disagreements by returning to the original data,
merging overlapping codes, and eliminating redundant ones. Third,
each researcher independently applied the consolidated codebook to
the full dataset. Fourth, the researchers collaboratively identified
themes,
defined as patterns of shared meaning relevant to the
research questions, and refined them iteratively until both reached
agreement. Fifth, inter-coder reliability of the independent coding
pass was assessed as percentage agreement, ranging from 85.6% to
99.0% across the six open-text questions (challenges and positive
experiences of team interaction,
AI contribution and hindrance
to idea generation and to teamwork, idea-source preference, and
platform helpfulness; Supplementary Tables B.1 to B.6 give the full
wording). All codebooks are included in the Appendix.
Video Analysis
Two researchers independently analyzed the video recordings of all
14 sessions (screen and audio recordings for the Miro platform;
recordings captured within the virtual environment for the immersive
platform), coding each subtask for spatial arrangement, movement,
and collaboration patterns. The codebook is provided in Table B.7.
The spatial codes were operationalized per platform. Participants
were coded as working at short distance if positioned next to one
another: in the Noda platform, an avatar needing only to turn
in place to see the other avatars; in the Miro platform, mind
maps and cursors appearing together in the viewport. They were
coded as at long distance when reaching another participant’s work
required teleporting (Noda) or when cursors and maps fell outside
the viewport (Miro).
Orientation codes (facing toward or away
from other participants) apply only to the Noda platform, where
avatars have a facing direction; the planar Miro platform offers no
analogous cue. Collaboration patterns in Subtask 3 were coded from
role distribution: groups in which one or two participants acted as
navigators (providing verbal or written suggestions) while the others
acted as drivers (controlling the platform, implementing ideas, or
leading the discussion) were coded as unequal collaboration, and
groups in which all three participants alternated between these roles,
contributing both written and verbal input, were coded as equal
collaboration.
These operationalizations capture analogous but not identical
constructs across the two platforms. In particular, spatial separation
in the Miro platform partly reflects the canvas area required for
text, so cross-platform comparisons of distance codes are reported
descriptively. The equal and unequal collaboration classifications
rest on the role-distribution codes, which do not depend on spatial
readings.
Inter-coder reliability, assessed as percentage agreement, was 100%,
89.64%, and 85.87% for Subtasks 1, 2, and 3, respectively.
Computational Text Analytics
We used a divergence-based method for identifying distinctive and
underrepresented terms, implemented via Learning via Surprisability
(LvS) (Mokryn et al., 2025; Mokryn and Ben-Shoshan, 2021).
LvS identifies surprising terms in each category by comparing the
term distribution in that category with a reference distribution
derived
from
the
dataset,
using
a
decomposition
of
Jensen–
Shannon
divergence.
This
enables
the
identification
of
both
overrepresented
terms
and
underrepresented
or
absent
terms.
Importantly, underrepresented or absent terms are not simply rare
overall: they are terms that are relatively common in the broader
dataset but appear much less frequently, or not at all, in a specific
category (Ben Shoshan et al., 2026). In our framework, these absences
are informative because they reveal ideas that would normally be
expected but are missing from a particular category. Here, we refer
to idea-level appearance as one occurrence, regardless of the number
of times the term is mentioned in that idea.

LvS Data Preparation
For each idea we normalized the text (NFKC), removed leading cost
markers of the form “(high)”, “(medium)”, or “(low)”, lowercased,
tokenized, dropped possessive suffixes, removed the 318 English stop
words in the scikit-learn list, and reduced the remaining tokens to
stems with the Porter stemmer. Each idea contributes at most one
count per stem, regardless of how often the stem occurs in it. From
these idea-level counts we formed a normalized presence distribution
for each category and a reference distribution equal to the unweighted
mean of the two category distributions.
LvS Operation
Following Mokryn et al. (2025) and Mokryn and Ben-Shoshan
(2021),
each stem’s signed contribution to the Jensen–Shannon
divergence between a category and the reference was computed with
the reference implementation (https://github.com/ScanLab-ossi/
LvS-for-Datasets). A stem entered a category’s signature if its
absolute contribution was at least 1% of that category’s total absolute
divergence; stems meeting the threshold for either category were
assigned to the category with the larger signed contribution. Tables
8 and 9 print stems as their most frequent surface form; organ covers
organize and organization, and import covers importance.
Significance
Significance was assessed with Pearson’s chi-square statistic (one
degree of freedom, no continuity correction) when both groups had
adequately populated counts, and with a two-sided Fisher exact
test otherwise. Fisher’s test was used whenever a term appeared
in fewer than five ideas in either group or whenever any expected
cell count fell below five; chi-square was used only when neither
condition applied. All reported p values are two-tailed. Resulting p
values were corrected with the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-
rate procedure, with the correction family limited to the terms
that passed the LvS filter for that comparison, since the filter had
already narrowed the candidate set before testing began. Odds ratios
for Fisher tests are the conditional maximum-likelihood estimates
returned by fisher.test. Signature extraction was implemented
in Python 3 (pandas,
NLTK, scikit-learn) using the reference
LvS implementation; significance tests used base R (version 4.3.3:
chisq.test, fisher.test, p.adjust). All scripts and their output
are included in the supplementary bundle.
Results
RQ1: Shaping interaction patterns, attention distribution,
and perceived collaboration
To answer RQ1, we examined: (a) collaboration strategies; (b)
participants’ perceived workload (NASA-TLX) and sense of presence;
and (c) participants’ perceptions of the collaborative process.
Collaborative Strategies
Here we describe the findings from the video analysis. See Appendix
Table B.7 for the video analysis code book.
During subtask 1, which involved individual work, participants had
no restrictions regarding movement or the placement of their maps
on either platform. In this subtask, the majority of groups (12/14)
were observed working at long distance from one another, as defined
in the Video Analysis section.
In Miro,
participants tended to build their maps away from
each other on different areas of the shared whiteboard. In Noda,
participants usually worked in separate corners of the virtual room.
Only in two groups (N1 and N7) did participants work within a short
distance of each other. In addition, a notable behavior was observed in
all but one Noda group (N3) participants’ virtual avatars faced away
from each other. Since Noda mind maps are designed to appear as
if hanging in mid-air, it is sensible for users to turn away from their
colleagues so as to have a relatively clear background for their own
work.
Movement during subtask 1 in both platforms was minimal. In
Miro, six of the seven groups found a space on the board and did
not adjust or shift their mind map during the task. Within the
Noda platform, similar patterns were exhibited, where in six of seven
groups, participants stayed in the spot they had spawned in and did
not move, other than to turn their heads.
In subtask 2, which introduced AI, participants were again not
given instructions on how to interact with each other. Similar to
subtask 1, a majority of participants, regardless of condition, worked
individually. Among the Noda groups, 4/7 still had participants
facing away from each other during the task. Figure 7 shows this
arrangement in one group at the end of the subtask.
In the Miro platform, the addition of the AI did not lead to
positional changes in most groups, and 6 of the 7 groups continued
developing their individual mind maps from Subtask 1. However,
one Miro group (M1) moved to a central area of the board to
collaboratively create a new map using AI-generated ideas. A similar
pattern was observed among the Noda groups. Six out of seven groups
maintained work on their original maps from subtask 1, while in
one group (N7), a participant moved to a new location within close
proximity to another participant to create a new AI-augmented mind
map.
As participants familiarized themselves with the Miro or Noda
platforms during subtask 2, more participants would visit or move
closer to the maps of other participants. This increased movement
to check the mind maps of other participants did not seem to
be collaborative, as it involved a participant staying near another
participant’s mind map for a few seconds before returning to their
own mind map.
In Subtask 3, participants were instructed to work collaboratively.
Although all groups demonstrated some level of collaboration, they
differed in how evenly responsibilities were distributed across the
three group members. Groups were coded as showing equal or unequal
collaboration from the distribution of navigator and driver roles, as
defined in the Video Analysis section.
In the Miro platform, 3 of the 7 groups demonstrated equal
collaboration during Subtask 3,
with participants sharing both
navigation and driving responsibilities.
The remaining 4 groups
showed unequal collaboration, with one or two participants taking
the driver role.
In the Noda platform, 5 of the 7 groups demonstrated equal
collaboration, with all participants contributing ideas verbally and
taking turns modifying the mind map.
In these five groups,
participants also tended to face one another when marking their
final three choices. During discussion, participants generally faced
the current speaker, except when the speaker briefly turned away
to consult their notes. The two groups that did not exhibit equal

Workload (NASA-TLX) and Presence
We measured workload using the NASA-TLX. The only statistically
significant difference between the two platforms was observed for
Physical Demand (How physically demanding was the task). A
Mann–Whitney U test indicated that participants in the Miro
platform (median = 1.0) reported significantly less physical demand
than participants in the Noda platform (median = 1.9, U = 126.0, p

Participant perceptions of presence differed between the two
platforms. Participants in the immersive Noda platform reported
significantly higher ratings than those in the planar Miro platform
on four statements: “In the computer generated world I had a sense
of ‘being there.’ ” (Mdn = 6 vs. 4, p < .001), one of the largest
differences in the questionnaire, “Somehow I felt that the virtual
world surrounded me” (p < .001), “I had a sense of acting in the

.010), and “I felt present in the virtual space” (p = .003). Participants

further items differed significantly in directions that likely reflect how

Participant Perceptions of Collaboration
Participants in the immersive Noda platform reported slightly higher
levels of comfort with the collaborative process than those in the
planar Miro platform, although most differences were not statistically
significant. One exception was comfort with feedback (Did you feel
comfortable providing feedback to your team members and receiving
feedback from them?). Participants in the Noda platform (median
= 5) reported greater comfort in giving and receiving feedback than
participants in the Miro platform (median = 4), this difference was
statistically significant (Mann–Whitney U = 148, p = 0.047). Full
results are reported in Table A.3 in the Appendix.
Participants
were
asked
two
open-ended
questions
about

The main challenges they reported were a

Six participants reported no negative experiences of team interaction

5 in Miro and 6 in Noda)

that it was “odd interacting without being able to perceive the team’s

by participants in the Noda platform (5/42). These participants
felt that the virtual environment negatively affected their spatial
awareness. As one participant explained, “it just felt like we were
very disconnected from each other as we were very far away in the
virtual space”.

participants reported positive experiences related
to compatibility and respectful discourse with their teammates.
More than half of the participants (24/42) described their group

were in the Miro platform and 10 were in the Noda platform. With
respect to verbal interaction, 12 of the 42 participants attributed their
positive experience to a general sense of agreement among teammates
(7 in Miro and 5 in Noda).
Another positive experience that several participants noted was
related to the tasks and the platform used. Some participants (8/42)
found that the tasks gave them new insight and perspectives (4 in
Miro, and 4 in Noda). Some participants in the Noda platform (4/21)
mentioned positively the ability to spatially view each other’s ideas
as they were brainstorming. One such participant recalls, “It was cool
to see the brainstorm nodes like from a different perspective as the
other person was working on theirs”.

Discussion
This study examines how different collaborative platforms affect two
aspects of human–AI ideation: first, how they structure interaction,
attention, and effort within groups (RQ1), and second, how they
shape the evaluation and integration of AI-generated ideas (RQ2).
Across both research questions, the findings show that differences
between platforms are driven by how spatial layout, interaction
modality, and AI output format structure group interaction and AI
use.
The
Noda
platform
provides
spatial
co-presence,
embodied
interaction, and concise, spatially embedded AI suggestions. The
Miro platform provides text-based interaction, and longer, more
elaborated AI output. In Noda, participants collaborate more evenly
and report a stronger sense of presence, but also higher physical
demand.
The AI produces a larger number of short,
spatially
embedded suggestions. These suggestions are less often selected
unaltered as finalists (3 of 20 in Noda, 10 of 21 in Miro; Table 10)
and are rated lower than both human-generated and group-authored
ideas. In Miro, interaction is more text-based. The AI produces longer
suggestions, which are more frequently selected as finalists and are
rated as more insightful.
These differences might reflect how AI outputs are presented and
processed within the workspace. In Noda, suggestions are brief and
distributed in space, which requires participants to locate, interpret,
and compare them. In Miro, suggestions are longer and sit as full
sentences on a flat canvas, which makes them easier to read, evaluate,
and discuss. As a result, the same AI function contributes differently
to group decisions across configurations.
In the following, we discuss each of these findings in depth, and
derive design implications for sociotechnical collaborative platforms
that aim to support both effective human-human collaboration and
productive human-AI co-ideation.

RQ1: Shaping Interaction Patterns, Attention Distribution,
and Perceived Collaboration
As expected, the Noda platform yielded significantly higher spatial
presence and a stronger sense of immersion than the Miro platform,
aligning with theories of distributed and embodied cognition that
link richer spatial environments to associative thinking and creative
engagement (Hutchins, 1995; Kirsh, 2010; Klein et al., 2005). The
Noda configuration also imposed greater physical demands, while
mental demand and effort did not differ significantly (Table A.2).
This is a more limited cost than the cognitive fatigue reported
for complex virtual environments (Fromm et al., 2020; Ide et al.,
2021). Beyond confirming these expected presence and workload
differences, our findings offer three contributions that extend current
understanding.
First,
we identify a participation paradox in the immersive
configuration: the spatial freedom that let participants explore and
organize ideas also let them drift apart,
reducing collaborative
awareness and group coherence.
Although participants reported
greater immersion and greater comfort giving and receiving feedback
in Noda,
a distinct subset reported a sense of disconnection
attributable to large virtual distances between avatars. This finding
builds on Kuˇt´ak et al. (2019)’s call for “careful spatial design”
in immersive systems,
but sharpens the claim:
spatial distance
is not merely a usability concern but a mechanism that could
directly counteracts the social presence and participation benefits
that immersive environments are designed to produce.
Second,
by
examining
commercially
available
platforms
as
integrated sociotechnical configurations, we show that the observed
collaboration patterns are not a function of two-dimensional against
three-dimensional
space
alone.
Miro’s
cursor-based
awareness,
sentence-length text nodes,
and screen-bounded shared canvas
constitute an integrated sociotechnical configuration that structures
attention and coordination in specific ways, just as Noda’s avatar
embodiment and three-dimensional placement of nodes constitute a
distinct one. This framing provides an ecologically valid account of
how contemporary collaborative tools, as they are actually designed
and deployed, relate to how groups experience ideation and how
evenly they participate.
Third, the Noda configuration was associated with more balanced
role distribution during convergence: five of seven Noda groups shared
navigation and driving roles, against three of seven in Miro. Previous
work on VR brainstorming has focused primarily on idea quantity
and creative outcomes (Fromm et al., 2020; Gong et al., 2022). Our
video analysis adds role distribution as a further dimension. In the
five balanced Noda groups, the pattern co-occurred with specific
embodied behaviors: avatars facing one another during discussion,
and participants gathering at the center of the room or moving
between one another’s maps during convergence. These are observed
coordination behaviors, recorded independently of self-report, and
they suggest that spatial co-presence, even through low-fidelity avatar
representations, can accompany more balanced group participation.
Our coding cannot order the two: groups that had settled into
balanced participation may have oriented toward one another as
a consequence, orientation may have supported balance, or both
may reflect group dynamics we did not measure. We therefore read
facing as a behavioral marker of balanced collaboration. The design
implication, making orientation cues available, does not depend
on the direction of the effect. This extends Gong et al. (2022)’s
account that symbolic gestures and proximity cues in VR can
support inclusivity, by grounding the claim in behavioral data from
a structured ideation task using a commercially available platform.
Taken together, these findings indicate that immersive environments
similar to Noda could offer meaningful advantages for presence
and
balanced
participation,
but
might
impose
cognitive
and
communicative overhead that current platform designs have not
fully resolved. Notably, neither platform adequately supported group
awareness of AI contributions: participants invoked AI individually
and asynchronously, and there were no mechanisms to make AI-
generated content immediately visible or attributable to all group
members simultaneously — a limitation that shaped how groups
evaluated and negotiated AI suggestions across both configurations.
The challenge for design is therefore not simply to maximize
immersion,
but
to
structure
spatial
configurations,
awareness
mechanisms,
and AI integration styles that together translate
embodied engagement into productive, equitable collaboration — a
point we return to in the design implications section.
RQ2: Mediating Human-AI Co-Ideation
Evaluation of AI-generated ideas
Participants’ evaluations of AI-generated ideas differed across the
two configurations. In Miro, participants rated AI-generated ideas as
more insightful, and marginally more innovative, than participants
in Noda did (Table 7). In Noda (Table 6), AI-generated ideas were
rated significantly lower than both human-generated and group-
authored ideas on relevance, innovation, and insightfulness, with
large effect sizes.
In Miro (Table 5),
participants rated group-
authored ideas highest but showed smaller differences between AI-
generated and human-generated ideas. Selection followed the same
pattern (Table 10): 10 of the 21 finalist ideas in Miro were unaltered
AI-generated nodes, against 3 of the 20 finalists in Noda. Selection
of an unaltered AI node is the only measure of uptake the workspace
export supports; group-authored ideas may draw on AI output
without leaving a recoverable trace, so uptake through co-elaboration
is not measured.
The comparison that matters for a platform intended to augment
group ideation is between what groups produced with the AI available
and what they produced without it, and on that comparison both
configurations came out ahead: group-authored ideas were rated
above participants’ own human-only ideas, every group-authored idea
was selected as a finalist, and the idea pool grew threefold in Miro and
fivefold in Noda. Two qualifications apply. The within-participant
comparison is between rated sets, not matched ideas, so it shows that
the AI-supported phase yielded ideas participants valued more, not
that any particular idea improved. And Subtask 3 required selection
by design, so the refinement observed in the convergent phase reflects
the structure of the workflow as much as the configuration. The
configurations differ in what participants selected, with unaltered AI
nodes in Miro and human-authored nodes in Noda. The rest of this
section addresses this difference.
The finding we consider most consequential for design is that
these differences track the form in which AI output arrived in the
workspace. Miro’s AI produced ideas averaging 21 words; Noda’s
averaged 3 (Table 4). This sevenfold difference in verbosity is a design
choice made by each platform’s developers in response to its display
constraints, and it co-occurred with large differences in perceived
innovation and insightfulness and in the rate at which AI-generated


20
Accepted to Interacting with Computers, 2026, Volume , Issue
ideas were selected as finalists. AI output format therefore functions
as a first-class design variable in collaborative ideation platforms,
on a par with spatial layout and awareness mechanisms. Work on
user-centered constraints on LLM output has shown that length and
structure shape how individuals integrate generated content into their
work (Liu et al., 2024); our data indicate that the same choices shape
how groups evaluate, negotiate, and adopt that content collectively.
The principal alternative account is model capability. The Miro
AI feature was powered by OpenAI GPT-4o-mini, whereas the Noda
AI feature was powered by OpenAI GPT-3.5 Turbo, and the two
models differ on benchmarks (OpenAI, 2024). Two observations
weigh against capability as the sole explanation. For relevance, the
most surface-level of the three judgments, there was no significant
cross-platform difference (Table 7), suggesting both models drew
on comparable domain knowledge. Where the platforms diverged
was on innovation and insightfulness, the dimensions most sensitive
to depth, elaboration, and persuasiveness. Further, the Noda AI
produced substantially more ideas than Miro’s (633 against 304),
so GPT-3.5 Turbo was not failing generatively; it was presenting its
output in a format constrained by interface design. On our reading,
presentation explains the pattern better than capability alone. The
two accounts are nevertheless bundled in this design and cannot be
fully separated (see Sociotechnical Configurations and Limitations).
Two mechanisms are consistent with the presentation account.
Prior research suggests that more detailed textual output generated
by large language models can be perceived as more persuasive,
thereby
increasing
acceptance
in
collaborative
decision-making
contexts (Breum et al., 2024). The Miro AI may also have adhered
more closely than the Noda AI to Grice’s cooperative principle,
which holds that contributions to conversation should provide an
appropriate amount of information and do so clearly (Grice, 1975).
At three words per idea, the Noda AI’s contributions may have been
too brief to be informative or credible to participants weighing them
against their own.
A further account concerns the participants and not the AI.
Brucks and Levav (Brucks and Levav, 2022) found that face-to-
face collaboration supported better divergent thinking than remote
collaboration, and attributed the difference to variability in gaze
direction. Participants in Miro collaborated through a screen-based
interface, which likely concentrated visual attention on a shared
display, whereas participants in Noda interacted at least part of
the time in a more face-to-face arrangement within the virtual
environment.
If participants in Noda experienced greater gaze
variability and stronger divergent thinking during Subtask 1, they
may have generated stronger ideas of their own and found the
AI’s contributions less insightful by comparison. We did not record
gaze direction in either platform, so this interpretation remains
speculative. The questionnaire offers only partial support for this
account: group-authored ideas were rated as more relevant in Noda
than in Miro, but ratings of participants’ own ideas did not differ
across platforms (Table 7), so any strengthening of human ideation
in Noda was not strong enough to register there.
Group Awareness of AI Contributions
Neither configuration made the AI’s contributions legible to the
group. In both, the AI was an on-demand function invoked by one
participant on one node: the invocation was not announced to the
others, its output appeared at the invoking participant’s location,
and the only marker of AI authorship was a leading asterisk in Miro
or a node color in Noda (Figures 5 and 7).
Both configurations accept a single node label as the prompt for
AI contributions and expose no conversational channel, so a group
cannot steer the model, ask it to revise, or tell it which of its
suggestions were taken up. The corpus shows how little co-elaboration
followed: 15 ideas across the 14 groups were authored during the AI-
supported phase (7 in Miro, 8 in Noda; Table 2), and all 15 became
finalists (Table 10). Groups valued what they built from AI output
when they built it, but neither configuration made producing such
an idea an easy or a group-visible move.
This finding locates a gap in the frameworks reviewed in Related
Work. Role vocabularies and the COFI framework model one creator
and one system (Rezwana and Maher, 2023); the team-level work of
McComb et al. (2023) and Seeber et al. (2020) addresses the AI’s
position in a team without specifying how its output is surfaced,
attributed, and steered when several people share it in one workspace.
The two deployed platforms answer that question by default, with
individual invocation and no attribution, and the result in both was
an AI that enlarged each participant’s idea space without entering
the group’s exchange. We discuss the design consequences, shared
invocation, attribution, and a group-steerable prompt surface, under
Implications for Design.
Focusing on Women Participants
Focusing
on
women
participants
allowed
us
to
minimize
the
variability related to gender dynamics and provided clearer insights
into specific ideation processes for women. Previous studies indicate
that gender composition significantly affects group interactions,
communication
styles,
and
creativity
in
collaborative
settings
(Schiller et al., 2011; Hill and Hincapie, 2025; Criado-Perez, 2019).
Women often encounter different challenges in remote collaborative
ideation, including participation barriers in environments mediated
by digital media (Gupta, 2020; Marchant, 2021; Bradley et al., 2021;
Yassien et al., 2021), biases in idea evaluation (Proudfoot et al., 2015),
and differences in the use of AI (Humlum and Vestergaard, 2025).
Our findings contribute to the growing body of work that
emphasizes the need for inclusive design practices that advocate
and account for gender-specific collaboration dynamics, particularly
in remote and AI-supported contexts (Delcourt et al., 2022; Fox
et al., 2015; Criado-Perez, 2019). Future research should extend
these insights by investigating mixed-gender and other demographic
configurations to further inform inclusive collaborative technology
design.
Implications for Design
Our
findings
offer
insights
for
the
design
of
sociotechnical
collaborative platforms for online group ideation, concerning the
presentation of LLM output, the legibility of AI contributions to the
group, spatial design, and awareness among participants.
Integrating LLMs for Effective Ideation Support
If AI output format shapes collective evaluation and adoption, as
the RQ2 findings indicate, then platforms should treat verbosity,
structure, and spatial embedding as design parameters open to users
and not as fixed properties of the feature. This points to a need for
platform-aware, context-adaptive AI output design. Concretely, we
recommend that platforms support adaptive AI verbosity: users and


Accepted to Interacting with Computers, 2026, Volume , Issue
21
groups should be able to request brief keyword-style suggestions when
they want to maintain ideation momentum, or detailed elaborations
when they want to evaluate and compare specific ideas.
This
is particularly critical in immersive environments, where display
constraints make longer text difficult to read and navigate, but
where brief AI contributions may be insufficiently informative to be
persuasive or credible. In these settings, voice-based AI delivery may
serve as a viable alternative modality for richer AI contributions,
though this requires attention to voice design principles to avoid
reinforcing gender biases (Hwang and Won, 2024; Heo et al., 2025;
Zhang et al., 2025a).
Beyond verbosity, our findings also suggest that AI integration
should be designed to reinforce human creative contribution instead
of bypassing it. Across both platforms, participants rated the ideas
their group authored during the AI-supported phase more highly
than the AI-generated ideas alone. Our corpus also indicates how
little co-elaboration occurred: 15 ideas across all fourteen groups
were authored during that phase (origin not separable; Table 2), and
neither configuration made producing one an easy or a group-visible
move. This supports a design principle of scaffolding human-AI co-
elaboration: instead of presenting AI suggestions as finished ideas to
be accepted or rejected, platforms should make it easy for groups
to build on, rephrase, and combine AI contributions with their own
ideas. Design principles for generative AI applications (Weisz et al.,
2024) suggest a distinction between “design for exploration” and
“design for optimization”, a distinction that maps well onto the
divergent and convergent phases of collaborative ideation. Future
platforms should support phase-specific AI behavior: more expansive
and varied during divergence, more focused and elaborative during
convergence, with mechanisms for groups to signal which mode they
are in.
Making AI Contributions Legible to the Group
The deficit reported under Group Awareness of AI Contributions
calls for a class of awareness mechanism directed at the AI’s role in
the workspace, distinct from the mechanisms that make participants
aware of one another. In both platforms, participants invoked AI
individually and asynchronously, producing uneven awareness of AI-
generated content, and the only markers of AI authorship, a leading
asterisk in Miro and a node color in Noda, were not legible across
the workspace. Both platforms also constrain the prompt surface
to a single node label, with no conversational channel, a design
choice that bounds how groups can steer the generative model. Three
mechanisms follow. First, shared, synchronous AI invocation that
announces itself to all members, so that an invocation is a group
event and its output arrives where the group can see it. Second,
attribution markers that persist and remain legible at a distance, so
that the provenance of a node is visible during convergence as well
as at the moment of generation. Third, a prompt surface the group
can address jointly, so that steering the model, asking it to revise,
and telling it which suggestions were taken up become group acts
and not private ones. This design space remains largely unaddressed
in current tools.
Spatial Design for Immersive Collaboration
Our findings point to a specific design tension that goes beyond the
general call for “careful spatial design” in immersive environments
such as Noda (Kuˇt´ak et al., 2019): the same three-dimensional
affordances that enable rich, embodied engagement with ideas also
allow participants to drift apart spatially in ways that reduce group
coherence. This tension has direct implications for how immersive
ideation platforms should be structured.
Designers should treat spatial layout as an active mechanism for
regulating the balance between individual exploration and group
convergence,
and not as a neutral backdrop for collaboration.
Concretely,
this
suggests
designing
for
phase-sensitive
spatial
dynamics: during divergent ideation, participants may benefit from
spatial freedom to create and explore their own idea clusters; during
convergence,
the platform should actively support or encourage
proximity, perhaps through subtle spatial cues, avatar pull-toward
gestures, or shared focal artifacts that draw participants into a
common area. The Blended Whiteboard (Grønbæk et al., 2024)
provides an example of reconfigurable collaborative space. Future
platforms should extend this logic to structured ideation workflows
by aligning spatial affordances with task phases.
Additionally,
our findings indicate that even minimal avatar
representations are relevant to participation equity: the Noda groups
whose avatars faced one another were the groups coded as showing
balanced collaboration. This behavioral finding suggests that avatar
orientation, which is currently largely incidental in commercial VR
platforms, should be treated as a designable awareness mechanism.
Platforms should support orientation cues, directional audio, and
spatial proximity indicators that make other participants’ attention
and engagement legible without requiring explicit communication.
Enhancing Awareness and Communication Among
Participants
Both platforms lacked robust support for non-verbal communication,
but the mechanisms of awareness differed in illuminating ways: in the
Miro configuration, awareness was mediated through cursor position
and artifact co-location; in the Noda configuration, it emerged
through avatar orientation and spatial proximity.
This contrast
reveals that workspace awareness is a property to be designed in
alignment with the interactional affordances of each configuration,
and not a single feature to be added.
For planar platforms, features such as live cursor tracking (Gutwin
et al., 2004; Yang et al., 2023), collaborative highlighting, and
contribution visualization (Gutwin et al., 2004; Ollesch et al., 2021)
can make participants’ activities legible in real time. Our findings
support extending these mechanisms to make convergence activity
particularly visible, specifically, who is moving toward, selecting, or
annotating ideas during decision phases, since Subtask 3 was the
phase in which groups came together and in which unequal role
distribution appeared. Prior work has shown that shared awareness
of contribution patterns can support more equitable participation
(Rocha et al., 2025), a finding our data extends to AI-augmented
group ideation. For immersive platforms, avatar expressiveness (Li
et al., 2024), high-fidelity environments (M¨uller et al., 2022), and
telepresence (Huang et al., 2025) are promising directions.
Limitations and Future Work
This study has several limitations related to the sample, task design,
and platform differences, which may affect the generalizability of the
findings. Below we outline these limitations and highlight directions
for future work.
Sample. The study involved 42 student participants, all of whom
were women. While this relatively homogeneous sample reduced


22
Accepted to Interacting with Computers, 2026, Volume , Issue
variability in the collected data, the results may not generalize to
mixed-gender groups or to professional teams with domain expertise.
Future studies should examine more diverse participant populations,
including mixed-gender teams and groups with varying levels of
expertise, to better understand how group composition influences
collaborative ideation in sociotechnical platforms.
Task. The experimental task was designed to simulate short-term
creative work with distinct phases for divergent and convergent
thinking. Although this structure allowed us to evaluate immediate
ideation outcomes,
it may not reflect the dynamics of longer-
term collaborative projects,
in which users gradually adapt to
platform affordances and develop shared working practices. Because
creativity, collaboration patterns, and reliance on AI can evolve over
time, longitudinal studies are needed to understand how sustained
collaboration, group learning, and AI use develop across repeated or
extended sessions.
Platform differences.
The study compared two commercially
available platforms, Miro and Noda, as deployed. They differ in the
properties listed under Sociotechnical Configurations, including the
underlying model, the length and format of AI output, interaction
modality, and text entry. This design has the ecological validity of
studying what groups actually use, at the cost that no observed
difference can be attributed to any single property of a configuration.
Text entry illustrates the problem. In Noda, participants typed one
character at a time on a virtual keyboard with a pointer; in Miro they
used a physical keyboard. This cost falls on human-authored text
only, and it offers a second account of two Noda-side observations:
the shorter human ideas (3.5 against 5.0 words, Table 4) and the small
number of group-authored ideas composed during the AI-supported
phase (8 of the 796 Noda ideas). Where entering a sentence is slow,
participants may keep ideas terse and elaborate verbally instead of
in the workspace, which the workspace export does not capture.
Novelty is a further candidate: participants were not screened for
prior VR experience, and unfamiliarity with an immersive workspace
may have raised engagement and exploration in Noda independently
of its design. Future work should hold the platform constant and
vary one property at a time, for example the verbosity of AI output
within a single workspace, to separate the contributions of spatial
design, interaction modality, novelty, and AI integration.
Measures. The MEC-SPQ was developed for and validated in
immersive virtual environments (Vorderer et al., 2004), and its items
apply unevenly to a planar shared canvas. Two items differed across
platforms in directions consistent with this asymmetry of instrument
fit (Table A.1), and cross-platform presence comparisons should be
read accordingly. In addition, the idea-quality items referred to ideas
created by “the team”; the intended referent, stated in Methods, was
the set of ideas the group authored during the AI-supported phase,
and we cannot rule out that individual participants read the word as
referring to their fellow participants more generally.
Conclusion
This
study
provides
empirical
insight
into
how
sociotechnical
collaborative platforms and LLM integration jointly shape remote
collaborative ideation. By comparing two different sociotechnical
configurations,
we show that platform configuration shaped the
quantity and form of generated ideas,
as well as collaboration
patterns, spatial presence, physical effort, and the ways groups relate
to AI contributions. AI support multiplied idea production in both
platforms, and the ideas groups authored with AI available were
rated above participants’ own. Participants nevertheless evaluated
and selected AI-generated ideas differently across configurations, a
difference that tracked how AI output was designed to appear in
each configuration, about 21 words per idea in Miro and 3 in Noda,
more closely than it tracked model capability, though the two cannot
be fully separated in this design.
More specifically, the Noda configuration introduced a participation
paradox
in
which
spatial
freedom
supported
exploration
and
fragmented group coherence, and was associated with more balanced
role distribution, with five of seven groups sharing roles and moving
toward a shared map during convergence.
In both configurations, AI output entered the workspace through
individual, asynchronous invocation, with no mechanism that made it
visible or attributable to the group and no prompt surface the group
could address jointly. Together with the role of AI output format in
shaping evaluation and selection, this defines a design space for AI
in group ideation: shared invocation, group-visible attribution, and
output whose form adapts to the workspace and to the phase of the
task.
By focusing on women participants, this study also contributes
to ongoing efforts to increase gender-inclusive design research in the
context of remote and hybrid knowledge work with AI support. This
matters in light of the growing participation of women in hybrid
and remote knowledge work, where equitable participation is both
particularly salient and increasingly consequential. Future research
should extend this work by examining more diverse participant
populations, studying collaboration longitudinally, and conducting
more controlled comparisons that isolate the effects of spatial design,
AI output format, and awareness mechanisms. Such work can help
refine design principles for sociotechnical platforms that balance
embodiment, workload, and effective human-AI co-ideation.
