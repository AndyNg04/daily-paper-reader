# Effects of Locomotion and Viewpoint Control on Immersive Robot Telepresence for Navigation and Inspection 

Ammaar Zaman, Neven ElSayed, Oliver Prentner, Mohamed Ellethy, Mostafa M. Lotfy, Maram Selsabila Mahmoudi, and Eduardo Veas 

Abstract —In many robot telepresence interfaces, movement and viewing are managed through handheld inputs. This can divide attention between steering, looking, and inspection. We compared joystick versus omnidirectional treadmill locomotion and joystick-based versus head-guided camera control in awithin-subject 2×2 study in which 17 participants used a virtual reality headset to guide a quadruped robot through a physical maze while locating marked wall symbols and capturing them as visual evidence. Mixed-effects analyses showed that head-guided camera control was associated with faster completion, lower wall-contact cost, lower workload, and more favorable presence, usability, technology acceptance, and flow than joystick-based camera control. The strongest overall objective performance was observed in the joystick locomotion plus head-guided camera condition, whereas the omnidirectional treadmill plus head-guided camera condition emerged as the strongest experiential compromise. Exploratory camera analyses showed that head-guided camera control reduced switching, increased reliance on the pan-tilt view, and supported a more stable viewing strategy across the maze. These strategy differences were consistent with part of the performance advantage, especially for wall-contact duration. Targets captured remained near ceiling and simulator sickness remained low across all conditions. Overall, the results indicate that viewpoint control was the stronger differentiator in this combined navigation and inspection task, while locomotion still shaped the objective and experiential tradeoff. 

Index Terms —Robot telepresence, immersive teleoperation, viewpoint control, virtual reality, body coupled interaction, omni-directional locomotion, quadruped robot. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Mobile robot telepresence is increasingly relevant in scenar-ios where people must act in spaces that are distant, hazardous, or difficult to access directly. In these settings, the operator must do more than drive a remote platform from one point to another. The operator must also inspect the environment, no-tice relevant details, and make decisions through mediated per-ception. Telepresence has developed from simple mobile video access toward richer robotic systems that support navigation, inspection, collaboration, and remote action across domains such as industry, healthcare, and hazardous field work [1], [2].

In parallel, immersive interfaces have become more prominent in robot operation, with recent reviews showing growing interest in VR based control, visualization, and interaction [3], [4]. This shift matters because immersive telepresence involves concurrent driving, inspection, and viewpoint management. In many practical situations, operators must keep making progress while searching for cues, checking objects or struc-tures, and documenting what is found. This creates a dual task burden in which navigation competes with visual inspection for attention and control. Work on multiple resource use and task switching suggests that performance can suffer when perceptual and control demands compete for overlapping re-sources [5], [6]. In telepresence, this burden is often expressed through viewpoint management. The operator must decide when to keep moving, when to reorient attention, and how to coordinate robot motion with the currently available view. Prior teleoperation research shows that camera and viewpoint control can directly shape workload, visual attention, usability, and operational performance [7]–[9]. These challenges drive a shift from telepresence as remote control toward telepresence as body coupled interaction at a distance. Conventional telepresence interfaces control robot motion and viewpoint through handheld input. They can be convenient and familiar, but they divide attention across multiple control loops. A body coupled interface translates body motion to robot control and head motion to viewpoint, linking movement and looking to bodily action. Prior tele-presence work connects agency, self-location, and perceptual transparency to how naturally the operator can act through the remote system [10], [11]. Head-based viewpoint control has shown promise in teleoperated tasks, but the joint role of body coupled locomotion and camera control in immersive robot telepresence is underexplored [9], [12], [13]. We study this question in a robot navigation and inspec-tion task. Participants wear a VR headset and teleoperate a quadruped robot through a physical foam-wall maze, while searching and capturing wall symbols as visual evidence. The study compares conventional joystick control for locomotion and viewpoint with omnidirectional walking and head tracking respectively in a within-subject 2 × 2 design. Hereby, whole-body locomotion on an active omnidirectional treadmill is paired with head tracking for loco-viewpoint control of a quadruped robot. Since the robot has no human-like body, the study focuses on the relation between user action, camera control, robot response, and remote visual feedback [3], [14]. arXiv:2611.23939v1 [cs.RO] 18 Nov 2026 2

II. B ACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

Robot telepresence combines sensing, communication, and robot control so that an operator can navigate, inspect, ma-nipulate, or collaborate at a distance. Recent reviews describe a shift toward immersive systems that integrate richer sensing pipelines in virtual reality [1], [2]. The present study centers on how the operator turns partial remote views into action while the available feedback from the robot continues to change. Situation awareness in remote operation refers to perceiving task relevant elements, understanding their meaning, and an-ticipating what may happen after the next command. In a navigation and inspection task, this includes judging clearance, maintaining route understanding, locating targets, and relating each camera view to the robot body. 

A. From teleoperation to immersive robot telepresence 

VR has become an increasingly relevant interface for robot operation. A systematic review of VR interfaces for controlling and interacting with robots organizes prior work around visu-alization, robot control and planning, interaction, and usability [3]. Across this literature, VR is used to improve 3D un-derstanding, operator performance, situational awareness, and interface intuitiveness during remote tasks [3], [15]. Prior im-mersive systems also proved valuable for remote exploration, mobile robot operation, and telemanipulation, especially with spatially consistent views and control coupled to the user’s body motion [16]–[18]. For navigation-oriented telepresence, the relevant question is how naturally the operator can move, look, and act through the remote platform. 

B. Camera control and viewpoint management 

A central issue in this literature is that camera and view-point control are not secondary interface details. They shape how operators divide attention between moving, searching, and acting. In many robot tasks, the operator must balance progress while deciding where to look next and interpreting the relation between the view and the robot’s motion. This makes viewpoint management part of the task itself rather than a passive support layer [7], [19]. This issue becomes especially important in navigation-and-inspection tasks, where users must coordinate locomotion with ongoing visual selection. Viewpoint control can itself become a source of control burden because operators may need to decide whether to move the robot, reorient the camera, switch views, or pause to recover spatial understanding. Earlier comparisons of camera and motion control in teleoperated robots show that the control mapping assignment affects task performance and operator workload [20]. Recent work links active camera control to perception-motion coupling strategies, cognitive load, visual attention, usability, and operational performance [7], [8]. Studies on head movement, gaze, and other viewpoint control techniques show that remote camera control can change both performance and subjective preference [9], [12]. Work on viewpoint transitions further suggests that deciding when to move between views or control modes is itself a meaningful part of teleoperation behavior [13]. Taken together, these studies suggest that camera control is both a visualization choice and part of the sensorimotor coupling between the operator, the interface, and the remote robot. This is directly relevant to the present work, where one of the key questions is whether a more body coupled viewpoint mapping can reduce the burden of managing visual perspective during robot navigation and inspection. 3

C. Spatial understanding and body coupled telepresence 

Operators often have to infer layout, orientation, and future movement consequences through a limited set of remote views. Spatial understanding becomes fragile when visual and motor reference frames do not align. Prior work showed that remote virtual environments can differ from real environments in spatial presence, performance, and behavior [21]. Other teleoperation work notes that restricted viewpoints, frame-of-reference conflicts, and limited sensory context reduce spatial awareness and increase control burden [19], [22], [23]. This problem is not limited to what is visible in the current frame. Spatial knowledge also involves maintaining route structure, object locations, heading, and relations among the user, the viewpoint, the robot, and the surrounding scene. Cog-nitive map research describes how spatial knowledge supports navigation, memory, and flexible future behavior [24]–[26]. In robot telepresence, spatial knowledge has to be built from mediated camera views and updated while the robot moves. For navigation and inspection, these issues are especially important because, while users move and interpret the scene, should they decide to reorient they must map the control to the camera or robot. Here the idea of body coupled telepresence becomes relevant. Recent work argues that performance im-proves when the operator experiences stronger agency, self-location, and body-referenced coupling with the remote system [10]. Broader telepresence and embodied interaction literature discusses the intersection between presence and bodily expe-rience in mediated systems and why body coupled control mappings matter for remote interaction [11], [27]. This perspective is not limited to strictly humanlike remote bodies. Research on agency and body-referenced coupling suggests that these experiences can extend beyond highly anthropomorphic forms, which makes this line of work rel-evant for humanoid teleoperation and for telepresence through mobile legged robots. Prior work on perspective and body related experience in VR demonstrated that viewpoint, agency, and self-location can separate from one another. First-person views support strong body related experience and accurate interaction, while third-person or alternating viewpoints may support broader spatial awareness [28], [29]. This distinction is relevant for this study where the experimental question con-cerns whether useful action feedback and viewpoint coupling can still support remote operation if the robot is not humanoid. Hence, the present system is termed partial body coupled telepresence. The operator’s head motion maps to a mechanical pan-tilt camera, and body motion on the treadmill maps to bounded quadruped velocity commands. The study asks whether these mappings provide useful action feedback from a non-human robot body during navigation and inspection. 

D. Locomotion and omnidirectional treadmills 

A second major design axis in immersive telepresence concerns how the user moves. Controller-based locomotion is common due to its simplicity and convenience, but it does not provide the same bodily cues as walking. VR locomotion research has shown that locomotion technique affects presence, cybersickness, spatial knowledge, and navigation behavior [30]–[32]. A broader typology of VR locomotion techniques emphasizes differences in movement continuity, body involve-ment, and control mapping [33]. Recent work further shows that locomotion choice influences spatial orientation and user experience during navigation tasks [34], [35]. This distinction is useful because travel is only one part of the wider navigation task. A locomotion technique may move the user efficiently, but the same technique must also preserve route continuity, support inspection, and leave enough attention for the next action. This is why locomotion cannot be interpreted only as a scale from less bodily to more bodily input. Its value depends on whether movement, visual feedback, and task demands fit together. Omnidirectional treadmill systems differ from one another. Active omnidirectional treadmills such as Omnifinity Om-nideck move the walking surface keeping the user near the cen-ter and tracking body motion for input. Passive omnidirectional treadmills rely on low friction surfaces and user generated foot motion. Belt based systems such as CyberWalk use controlled belt motion to support walking within a mechanical platform [36]. They all differ from unconstrained real walking because the device constrains gait, balance, speed, comfort, adaptation, and who can use the system safely [37], [38]. Within this literature, omnidirectional treadmills are inter-esting because they reintroduce walking-like input while keep-ing the user within a constrained physical space. Prior work has examined their usability, cybersickness, gait characteris-tics, and navigation performance in virtual environments [38]– [40], including gait and movement behavior during treadmill-based virtual walking [37]. Most of the past research focused on virtual navigation rather than immersive robot telepresence. It remains unclear whether the benefits often associated with more body coupled locomotion carry over directly to robot telepresence tasks that combine movement with inspection. 

E. Gap and motivation 

Prior work shows that viewpoint control affects teleopera-tion performance and experience, while locomotion technique modulates behavior in immersive environments [2], [3]. These lines of work are often studied separately. What remains less explored is how walking-based locomotion and head-guided camera control jointly affect a telepresence task that requires both movement and inspection. This gap is especially relevant for non-humanoid remote bodies such as quadruped robots, where body coupling, locomotion, and viewpoint control do not map cleanly onto a human avatar. III. A. Robot platform 

The experiment used a Unitree AlienGo quadruped robot. It has two onboard computers for low-level and high-level control. High-level motion control is handled by the onboard Mini PC, which has access to robot state information and implements the control interface used in this study, together with directional movement commands. The robot was used in 

walking mode for active locomotion and received bounded tar-get velocity commands f(forward: fwd, backwards: bwd, strafe left: lstr, strafe right: rstr, rotate left: lrot, rotate right: rrot). The command limits were 0.5 m/s for forward, backward, and lateral motion, and 1.5 rad/s for yaw. Robot control is realized over WiFi through the high-level control computer. For robot safety, the controller monitors IMU roll and pitch, gyroscope and accelerometer signals, foot-force unloading, and passive-drop conditions. On a safety trigger, the robot enters a safety routine with zero command velocity and transitions to damping mode if stability does not resolve. The robot platform is extended with two stereo cameras (ZED Mini) offering two views: a lower fixed Robot Body Camera (RBC) mounted at the head of the robot and an independently controllable Pan-Tilt Camera (PTC) mounted above the torso on a pan-tilt system actuated by two MG996R servomotors mounted in a 3D printed frame. The cameras connect via USB 3.0 to an Nvidia Jetson Orin Nano viewpoint controller mounted on the robot torso and powered from the robot core. The pan-tilt system applied soft servo limits of 30–240 ◦ for pan and 120–220 ◦ for tilt. The two cameras are calibrated using a standard checkerboard-based stereo calibration procedure so that their forward views align to a common straight-ahead reference when centered. The PTC is positioned so that looking downward retains visibility of the robot body width. The camera heights and quadruped body geometry did not reproduce a human eye height or human body shape, so the apparatus provided partial body coupled telepresence rather than a humanoid telepresence setup. The viewpoint controller communicates through WiFi, receiving commands, updating pan-tilt status, and streaming video from both synchronized stereo cameras. The video pipeline operates at 15 Hz, capturing stereo frames at 2560 × 720, resizing them to 960 × 540, and compressing them with JPEG quality 70 for transmission. At any moment the system transmitted the active camera stream used by the operator rather than treating both camera views as separate experimental video channels. 

B. Telepresence platform 

The telepresence application defines the interface for cap-turing the operator input for motion and viewpoint control and transferring them to the robot while rendering the robot viewpoints for the operator. It is implemented in Unity3D. The application receives streams from each left and right unit in the stereo camera separately and renders them on the corresponding left and right eye textures for stereo display in the headset. The system offers two locomotion modalities (joystick, walking) and two modalities for viewpoint control (joystick, head). Velocity and camera-control commands are emitted at 20 Hz. The four interface combinations are ab-breviated as JJ for joystick locomotion with joystick camera control, JH for joystick locomotion with head guided camera control, OJ for Omnideck locomotion with joystick camera control, and OH for Omnideck locomotion with head guided camera control. 

Locomotion modalities. The telepresence application gener-ates velocity commands according to the locomotion modality. Two modalities are used. In joystick locomotion, controller axes are converted into target robot velocities. In treadmill locomotion, tracked body motion on the omnidirectional tread-mill is converted into target robot velocities. Instead of a direct distance mapping between operator and robot travel, velocity commands are used because they match the robot high level walking interface and allow both locomotion inputs to share the same robot command path. In both modalities the Unity application smoothed small changes, suppressed jitter near zero input, and constrained commands before sending them to the robot. The robot side used the most recent received command rather than executing a queued sequence of motions. 

Locomotion through joystick . VR controllers control for-ward, lateral, and yaw motion directly. The left thumbstick controls forward motion and yaw, the right thumbstick controls lateral motion. Hereby, joystick and treadmill conditions share the same robot command type after input processing, making locomotion comparison about the human input mapping rather than a different robot walking controller. 5

Locomotion through omnidirectional treadmill , robot mo-tion is derived from tracked body translation and yaw on the treadmill. The left controller is attached to the torso with an action-camera body strap to provide the body reference direc-tion.Treadmill motion provides the basis for computing robot velocity updates. The mapping therefore used the participant’s body motion as a continuous velocity input while the treadmill kept the participant near the center of the local walking area. 

Viewpoint control modalities. Viewpoint control is tied to robot control for the RBC. Its viewpoint follows robot position and body orientation. The right controller primary button switches between RBC and PTC. The latter is controlled by: 

Joystick viewpoint control uses the right joystick to control pan (left/right) and tilt (up/down). Small joystick inputs were ignored by a dead zone, and valid inputs updated the pan and tilt target within the servo limits. 

Head viewpoint control uses head orientation tracking from the headset as control commands for the PTC. Pan and tilt are derived from headset orientation relative to a reference frame using a 1.5 ◦ dead zone and a recenter function. The pan and tilt controller smoothed and clamped updates to the same physical range used in the joystick camera condition. Recenter commands returned the PTC to its forward reference. 

Mixed locomotion–viewpoint control modalities. In the JJ condition, joystick controls for locomotion conflict with joystick controls for viewpoint. A modal switch (left-controller Y button) toggles between robot-control and pan-tilt control modes. No such conflict exists in other conditions. In OJ, locomotion is controlled independently through treadmill mo-tion. In JH, pan and tilt follow head motion independent from joystick locomotion. In OH, treadmill locomotion is independent of pan–tilt control through head motion. The RBC remains body aligned in all conditions. Robot motion was body relative in all conditions and independent from the selected camera view. Participants could move the robot while viewing either the RBC or the PTC. Thus, JJ represents a fully manual baseline with shared controller resources. Other manual mappings with separate camera controls could reduce the cost of switching between robot and camera control. 

C. Communication Architecture and Data Flow 

The telepresence system is implemented as a distributed local-network architecture centered on a Dockerized Private Cloud (DPC) running on a dedicated local workstation. The DPC is the main on-premise orchestration layer for com-munication between distributed robotic components [41]. It decouples telepresence controls, transmitted through persistent Socket.IO connections to the DPC, from communication with ROS-based robot services. This allows Unity-based compo-nents and robot-side processes to exchange commands and telemetry within the same communication fabric. 

D. Robot Navigation and Inspection Task 

A robot navigation and inspection task is implemented to study the effects of two locomotion modalities and two viewpoint-control modalities under a 2×2 design. Participants teleoperate a Unitree AlienGo quadruped robot through a physical foam-wall maze while wearing a VR headset. The task goal is to move the robot from start point to end point while capturing wall symbols as visual evidence. The task combines route completion, viewpoint control, visual search, and target documentation within the same trial. The maze has a fixed outer footprint of 4.2 m × 9.0 m and is built from 1.25 m high foam walls. Wall segments are modular, and the study uses 1.2 m and 1.8 m wide wall sections. Openings for entry, exit, and turns are 1.2 m wide, all turns are 90 deg. Three maze variants are created by shifting the position of openings relative to the start and end direction while preserving the same overall footprint, so that participants cannot rely on memorizing a single repeated layout. Targets are placed across the maze walls according to a predefined schedule. Circle symbols serve as task-relevant targets to be captured in screenshots, triangle symbols populate the remaining wall locations as non-target distractors. A trial contains 5, 6, or 7 circle targets, balanced across the 12 trials completed by each participant. The traversable maze is divided into five ordered sections from start to end, hereby the target schedule balances upper-versus-lower placement across these sections. This varies target visibility across viewpoints and robot positions without favoring any one interface condition. Two camera views are available: the fixed Robot Body Camera (RBC) and the independent Pan-Tilt Camera (PTC). Participants are free to switch and use either. The two views differ in geometric affordances. Being coupled to robot head-ing, what enters the RBC view depends on the robot body pose relative to the maze wall. It hereby offers a body aligned view for steering, wall clearance, and recovering the robot heading. But some targets may require additional body reorientation or larger viewing distance to become fully visible. Instead, the PTC allows participants to redirect the viewpoint without changing robot heading in the same way. The PTC is served an independent inspection view for search and target capture while the robot could remain oriented for navigation. Hence, it reduces the need to rotate or reposition the robot body simply to bring a target into view, making viewpoint management part of the task itself rather than a separate support function. The task is a controlled navigation and inspection scenario focused on movement, active viewpoint management, and visual documentation in robot telepresence [2], [3]. 

Calibration, Safety, and Failure Handling. The task was implemented as an extension of the telepresence platform, including a virtual replica of the maze and robot in Unity, aligned to the physical environment through Vicon-based tracking. This allowed physical robot motion and maze con-tacts to be mirrored in the virtual scene and logged as collision events. Participants navigated from the live stereo robot camera feed rendered in the headset rather than from the simulated maze. The Unity replica supported alignment, triggers, logging, contact detection, experiment monitoring, and safety checks. Between trials, the physical maze was manually re-aligned to predefined floor markings and the robot was returned to the start position and orientation. Before each trial, participants were asked to verify that they could switch cameras, move the robot using the locomotion modality of the current condition, and capture a sample screenshot. 6

V. E XPERIMENTAL DESIGN AND PROCEDURE 

A. Participants and Ethical Considerations 

A total of 17 participants (12 men, 5 women) aged M =29 .59 years ( SD = 6 .05 , range = 20 − 48 ) completed the study. All of them had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Eleven participants reported prior VR experience, five reported experience operating a robot or remote-controlled device, and six reported prior exposure to omnidirectional treadmills. They all completed all 12 trials, for a total of 204 complete trials. Participants were recruited through campus flyers, mailing lists, and student-group recruitment channels. Inclusion criteria were age 18 years or older, normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Exclusion criteria were pregnancy, self-reported sus-ceptibility to motion sickness or VR discomfort, current use of medication likely to impair balance, alertness, or comfort during the study, or any self-reported condition that would make treadmill use unsafe. All participants received voucher compensation for their participation. All participants provided informed consent before the study. The study was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee. 7

B. Repeated-Measures Design 

The study used a 2 × 2 within-subjects design with loco-motion modality (Joystick vs Omni) and viewpoint (camera) control (Joystick vs Head) as independent variables. This produced the four interface combinations defined in Sec-tion IV-B, JJ, JH, OJ, and OH. The conditions represent a systematic analysis of body coupling with different bodily links in viewpoint and locomotion [14]. Each participant completed four condition blocks, one per interface condition. Block order refers to the serial position of a condition block within the full session (1–4), counter-balanced across participants using a Latin-square schedule. Within each block, participants completed three trials, one in each maze variant; trial in block refers to the within-condition trial position (1–3). Maze refers to the three maze variants, and target count refers to whether a given trial required 5, 6, or 7 targets to be captured. Each participant therefore completed 12 trials in total. Maze variant and target count were part of the task schedule rather than primary independent variables. They were included to vary route geometry and inspection demand while preserving the main 2 × 2 interface comparison. A participant-specific counterbalancing schedule balanced condition order and maze order across the sam-ple, while a predefined target-assignment schedule balanced inspection demand and upper-versus-lower target placement without favoring any one interface condition. In the final dataset, each interface condition contributed 51 trials, each maze variant appeared 68 times, and trials requiring 5, 6, or 7 targets each appeared 68 times. 

C. Training and Familiarization 

Before each condition block, participants completed a short training session designed to match the upcoming interface condition. It consisted of five structured scenarios that required basic robot movement, camera switching, screenshot capture, and navigation while using the full range of motions supported by the current locomotion and viewpoint mappings. The sce-narios included moving between predefined cardinal positions, switching between cameras, capturing sample screenshots, and navigating while capturing screenshots. Participants were also instructed to account for the robot body footprint while navigating. Training was criterion based. It typically lasted approximately 10 minutes per block and continued until the participant successfully completed at least four of the five sce-narios. No participant required training beyond this criterion. This familiarization reduced unsafe or confused use, but it did not remove practice effects or the possibility that the four interfaces had different adaptation demands. 

D. Experimental Procedure 

Participants stood at the center plate of the omnidirectional treadmill in all conditions. Using the same standing posture across conditions kept the operator station, headset use, camera views, and safety procedure consistent. They wore the VR headset and noise-cancelling headphones with white noise during task execution. Control mappings and camera-operation behavior followed the interface configuration described in Section IV-B. Each trial began at the marked maze start point and ended when the robot reached the end point. Participants were instructed to complete the maze as fast as possible, avoid colliding with the walls, and capture all required wall targets within their intended maze sections using one target per screenshot. There was no time limit per trial. Because both views could support both navigation and target capture, participants were not instructed to assign different task roles to the two cameras. Instead, they were free to use the available viewpoints in whatever way they found most effective for completing the route and documenting the targets. Scheduled breaks were provided between trials and condition blocks, typically lasting 5–10 minutes. Each condition block lasted approximately 50–55 minutes, and the full session lasted approximately 3.5 hours. 

E. Screenshot Scoring and Questionnaires 

After each trial, participants removed the headset and com-pleted the post-trial questionnaire battery on a tablet. The ques-tionnaire order was equipment comfort and technical issues, NASA-TLX items, simulator sickness symptom ratings, PQ items, SUS items, TAM items, Flow items, and task-specific camera/control items. The sickness items were administered after each trial only and averaged into a symptom score on the original 0 to 3 item scale. Screenshot files were stored with filenames linked to trial metadata and the active camera. After the experiment, screen-shot completion was derived against the predefined target schedule using a semi-automated workflow based on the stored filenames, the participant-specific target-assignment ta-bles, and documented adjustments when a single screenshot contained more than one valid target. Triangle distractors were excluded from the target count. Repeated captures of the same target were counted once for completion. When one screenshot contained more than one scheduled circle target, each visible scheduled target was credited after manual validation. Screenshots that could not be matched to the target schedule were reviewed against the trial metadata and ex-cluded from completion scoring when they showed distractors or unusable captures. In the analysis reported here, target-completion percentages were capped at the required target total for estimation. The resulting target capture measure is a trial level completion score for visual documentation, especially because performance was near ceiling.

G. Statistical Analysis 

The analyses used trial level mixed models because each participant contributed repeated observations across condi-tions, maze variants, and trial positions. Participant was in-cluded as a random intercept in all primary models. Loco-motion and camera control were the primary fixed effects. Maze variant, target count, block order, and trial in block were included as task and order controls because they reflected route variation, inspection demand, and practice within the session. Gaussian outcomes were analyzed with linear mixed models. Positive continuous, count, bounded completion, and zero heavy outcomes were analyzed with generalized linear mixed models using Gamma, negative binomial, grouped binomial, or Tweedie families as appropriate. Model comparisons were used to decide whether to retain the locomotion by camera control interaction. Type III tests summarized fixed effects, and estimated marginal means on the response scale were used for condition summaries and Bonferroni adjusted pairwise comparisons. VI. R ESULTS

D ISCUSSION 

Teleoperation work treats viewpoint design as part of the control loop because camera and motion control mappings change what the operator can attend to, how quickly the remote scene can be understood, and how much effort is spent maintaining a usable view [7]–[9], [20]. The present study extends this argument to a task where navigation, inspection, and visual documentation had to happen together. The main result is not that one input device was uniformly better than another. Instead, the task exposed where coordination costs entered immersive robot telepresence. Viewpoint control shaped performance and experience more consistently than locomotion modality, which shows that the camera mapping was part of the task structure rather than a secondary display choice. This contribution becomes clearer in the camera behavior results. Accounts of transparent telepresence emphasize that control input and visual feedback must remain easy to relate during action, otherwise the interface itself becomes part of the task [10], [14]. In this study, HGC changed how participants used the available views and reduced the need for repeated view negotiation. In a navigation and inspection task, every camera transition adds a small interpretive cost. The operator must judge whether the current view is sufficient, switch if needed, recover the scene from the new viewpoint, and then continue moving or inspecting. The section-level results and exploratory strategy models point in the same direction. Viewpoint transition work is useful here because switching views is a control decision that can interrupt how the operator understands the current scene and prepares the next action [13]. HGC appears to have reduced this interruption by supporting a more stable viewing strategy across the route. The locomotion findings are best read through the same task fit logic. VR locomotion research has shown that more body involvement can support experience and spatial updating, but it can also introduce balance, regulation, learning, or control de-mands when the wider task does not fit the method [42], [43]. Our results follow that tradeoff. Omnidirectional treadmill locomotion did not reliably improve task efficiency, yet OH produced favorable experiential ratings and a stable camera-use pattern. This suggests that body coupled locomotion can improve the felt quality of remote operation when it is paired with low-overhead viewpoint control, but it cannot compensate for a demanding camera mapping. The quadruped platform clarifies the scope of this tele-presence claim. Telepresence accounts that emphasize agency, self-location, and transparent action through a remote system do not require the design argument to rest only on humanoid morphology [10], [11], [14]. Here, the robot body, gait, camera height, and motion limits differed from the operator’s body, and both head motion and treadmill motion were mapped to bounded robot mechanisms. The claim is therefore limited to partial body coupled telepresence. Even so, the interface provided task relevant body cues. The RBC supported heading and wall-clearance judgments, the PTC could return to a forward reference, and looking downward preserved visibility of the robot body width. What mattered was whether the operator could connect their control actions to useful remote visual feedback during movement and inspection. Several limits define the scope of these conclusions. The sample size was fixed at 17 participants, which limits sensi-tivity to smaller effects. Target completion was near ceiling, so that outcome could not strongly distinguish the four in-terfaces. The task was a controlled laboratory navigation and inspection scenario with foam-wall contacts under low speed supervision. Practical systems would need collision avoidance, speed adaptation, shared autonomy, or locomotion assistance for less controlled spaces. Glass to glass visual latency was not measured, although the system logged round-trip timing information. The four conditions also combined posture, body coupling, and controller allocation, and seated joystick or swivel chair operation may be more realistic for some deploy-ments. Treadmill based telepresence adds physical effort and accessibility constraints. Finally, the camera strategy models are exploratory and support a mechanism interpretation, but they do not establish causal mediation. The practical implication follows from both previous camera-control work and the present robot task. Prior studies connect camera control and viewpoint transitions to workload, attention, and remote action quality [7]–[9]. Our results show that this connection remains critical when the operator is also using a body coupled locomotion interface and controlling a real quadruped robot. For navigation and inspection tasks, omnidirectional locomotion therefore needs a camera-control method that keeps visual inspection easy to maintain while moving. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

In this immersive robot telepresence task, the way the remote viewpoint was coupled to the user mattered more con-sistently than locomotion modality alone. Head-guided camera control reduced camera-management overhead, supported a stable viewing strategy, and was associated with better objec-tive performance and more favorable subjective experience. These effects emerged on a quadruped robot, suggesting that effective immersive telepresence depends less on morphology alone than on how naturally user actions couple to useful remote visual feedback. For telepresence design, viewpoint-control mapping is a central design decision, and body coupled locomotion is most promising when paired with equally low-overhead viewpoint control. No Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise preference contrast was significant, so this pattern is reported descriptively.
