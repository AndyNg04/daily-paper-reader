Abstract 

The training of socially intelligent language models often relies on large language model (LLM) judges, which are expensive, noisy, and vulnerable to reward hacking. Multi-player social games offer a natural alternative: they require open-ended communication to coordi-nate with teammates and infer hidden roles, while their rules provide an objective outcome which can serve as a verifiable reward for train-ing. Using three social games, we explore how much social intelligence Qwen3 models can acquire through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning with verifiable re-wards (RLVR). Results show that against a much stronger Gemma-3-27B model, SFT and RLVR raise the win rate of Qwen3-4B from 33% to 81% on the three trained games, and from 39% to 54% on three additional held-out games. In contrast, in-context strategies such as prompting improve the win rate of Qwen3-4B by only about 6 pp across the same games we train and evaluate on. Furthermore, we show that the gains from SFT and RLVR hold across the model sizes 1.7B, 4B, and 32B. Qual-itative analyses of agent behavior show signs of stronger social intelligence; for example, trained agents tend to share information more openly, check each other’s claims against pub-lic evidence, and coordinate their votes. More broadly, our results demonstrate that training LLMs on verifiable outcomes in social games can cultivate generalizable social behaviors, which offers a scalable path for building so-cially intelligent agents. 

1 Introduction 

As agents based on large language models (LLMs) have become more powerful, they have increas-ingly been used in social tasks, from collaborating on behalf of humans (Hua et al., 2026) to simulat-ing human social behavior (Park et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2026). These applications require social intelligence: LLM agents must infer what others know (Kim et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024a), and decide how to coordinate, collaborate, exchange, and compete with each other (Zhou et al., 2024b). These capabilities are hard to train because social interactions are nuanced and often lack objectively verifiable outcomes. Existing methods therefore rely largely on LLM judges to provide training sig-nals, using either filtered imitation (Wang et al., 2024), preference optimization (Kong et al., 2025), or reinforcement learning (Yu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). However, LLM judges are expensive, noisy (Zheng et al., 2023), and can be exploited via reward hacking (Wang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2026). Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) uses rule-based verification to provide ob-jective reward signals and has produced significant gains in LLM reasoning (Lambert et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025). Social games provide such verifiable signals in the context of social interactions, because while interactions may be nuanced, winners are ob-jectively determined by set rules. This offers a natural extension of RLVR to social contexts. Prior work has shown that self-play on two-player games can improve LLM reasoning (Cheng et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026). However, multi-player social games require more complex social reasoning, as agents must coordinate with team-mates, infer hidden roles, and persuade players. In this paper, we introduce a systematic investigation of how multi-agent social games can be used to train language models for improved social game-play and potentially improved social intelligence in general. We use S OCIAL GYM , an environment containing 22 social games across five categories: normal-form, economic, bluffing, hidden-role de-duction, and social strategy (He et al., 2026). To explore these questions, we train Qwen3-4B (Yang et al., 2025) using group relative policy op-timization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) on three hidden-role games from S OCIAL GYM . We use the objective game outcome, or whether the game was won or lost, as the verifiable reward. We then eval-uate against Gemma-3-27B (Gemma Team et al., 2025), a model with a base version much stronger than Qwen3-4B on S OCIAL GYM games, on both training and held-out games. An overview of our model training and evaluation pipeline is depicted in Figure 1. Our results show that RLVR substantially im-proves performance on trained games, raising Qwen3-4B’s win rate from 57% after SFT to 81% after RLVR, while yielding modest gains on held-out games (40% to 54%) and against un-seen opponents (46% to 57% against Qwen3.8-27B). These improvements hold across model sizes on the S OCIAL GYM leaderboard, where trained Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B match the overall Elo of GPT-4o, and trained Qwen3-32B ranks second only to GPT-5-mini. Across training configura-tions, single-seat training provides the most sta-ble gains compared to whole-team training or self-play. Behaviorally, trained agents learn to share private information more openly, check claims against public evidence, and coordinate votes with teammates. However, training for too long causes overfitting, often leading to formulaic messages and rigid habits that degrade held-out performance. Taken together, our findings demonstrate that verifi-able outcomes in multi-player games can cultivate transferable social reasoning without relying on expensive or noisy LLM judges, pointing toward diverse, regularized social environments as a scal-able path to generalizable LLM social intelligence. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Training Social Intelligence 

Most methods for training social agents learn from an LLM judge, often utilizing the SOTOPIA-EVAL (Zhou et al., 2024b) framework, which rates how well each agent achieves its goal in interactive role-play scenarios. SOTOPIA-π (Wang et al., 2024) im-itates interactions filtered by LLM ratings, SDPO (Kong et al., 2025) applies preference optimization to dialogue segments, and Sotopia-RL (Yu et al., 2025) and SocialRL (Wang et al., 2026) run RL on turn-level rewards derived from an LLM judge. OMAR (Jiang et al., 2026) uses a single model to play every role of a conversation in self-play, in-cluding in the multi-player game Werewolf. Hua et al. (2026) train a separate Qwen3-4B policy with RL in each of six two-party negotiation and co-ordination domains, such as price bargaining and meeting scheduling, and find that training in one 2domain helps another most when the two have a similar interaction structure. In contrast, our re-ward is a single score per game computed by the rules, with no LLM judge anywhere in training. Unlike prior work, we train on several multi-player, team-based social games and test whether the gains carry over to held-out games. 

2.2 RL in Language Games 

Self-play RL on games with rule-decided outcomes can strengthen LLM reasoning, as shown in adver-sarial taboo with SPAG (Cheng et al., 2024), zero-sum TextArena games (Guertler et al., 2025) like Kuhn Poker with SPIRAL (Liu et al., 2026), and cooperative or competitive games with MARSHAL (Yuan et al., 2026), which also improves held-out play. Yet all of these settings involve only two play-ers and no hidden teams. Multi-player social deduc-tion games, widely used to evaluate the social capa-bilities of LLM agents (Xu et al., 2023; Light et al., 2023; Lan et al., 2024; Bailis et al., 2024), are now increasingly used as training environments. Ex-isting training methods for multi-player language games typically add game-specific structure or re-wards for particular behaviors. Cicero (Meta Fun-damental AI Research Diplomacy Team (FAIR) et al., 2022) combines a dialogue model with strate-gic planning in Diplomacy. For Werewolf, Xu et al. (2024) train an RL policy that selects among LLM-generated actions, LSPO (Xu et al., 2025) alter-nates game-theoretic optimization in a latent strat-egy space with preference fine-tuning, and MaKTO (Ye et al., 2025) applies KTO to responses collected from multi-agent play. Sarkar et al. (2025) reward messages by their influence on other agents in a game based on Among Us, and Zheng et al. (2026) reward utterances for their persuasive impact. Most of these methods also train within a single game. Closest to us, Zhang et al. (2026) fine-tune LLMs with RL on hidden-role games and find that termi-nal win-loss rewards alone do not reliably teach social deduction. We ask what such rewards do teach, and whether those gains transfer to games never seen in training. 

2.3 Generalization from RL Training 

Whether RL training on outcome rewards motivates the learning of generalizable reasoning strategies or narrow shortcuts is a central question. Compared with pure SFT, RL with outcome rewards general-izes better to unseen task variants (Chu et al., 2025) and across domains (Huan et al., 2026), and it for-gets less of what the base model knows (Shenfeld et al., 2026). However, it remains debated whether RLVR teaches new abilities or merely reweights ex-isting ones (Yue et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). For social reasoning specifically, Sarangi and Salam (2026) find that RLVR on theory-of-mind tasks im-proves small models in distribution but fails to carry over when the task structure changes. Multi-player games introduce a second dimension of generaliza-tion beyond task structure, as other players are not static. A policy trained on game outcomes could easily memorize conventions specific to its train-ing games or exploit the habits of a fixed training opponent rather than learning transferable social skills. To distinguish genuine social reasoning from narrow overfitting, we therefore evaluate transfer across both held-out games and unseen opponents. 

3 Experimental Setup 

3.1 Terminology 

We define terms used in the rest of this paper. A 

game is a S OCIAL GYM environment, such as Were-wolves or Spyfall. A deal is a starting configuration of roles and models. An episode or rollout is a com-plete playthrough of a deal from start to terminal win or loss. A turn is a single response or action from a player in an episode. 

3.2 Games 

We train language models using hidden-role team games from S OCIAL GYM (He et al., 2026). We choose hidden-role games because they best sepa-rate models on the leaderboard, and they effectively test the ability to deduce and persuade. We train on Werewolves, Undercover, and Insider, and hold out Spyfall, Resistance, and Chameleon for evalua-tion. Rules for these games are in Appendix A. In each game, four to six players form two teams: a 

majority side and a hidden side , with all identities concealed. Players receive role-specific informa-tion and, on each turn, speak, vote, or take a game-specific action. An episode ends when the game’s rules declare one side the winner, and this win or loss outcome, derived by the game engine, is the signal that both of our training stages learn from. 

3.3 Supervised Fine-Tuning 

We primarily fine-tune Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (Yang et al., 2025), but also use Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-32B to test generalization across model sizes. To teach each model to produce messages 3and actions in the required format, we first fine-tune with LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) on turns played by the two highest-rated models on the S OCIAL GYM 

leaderboard, GPT-5-mini and GPT-4o, and the highest-rated open-weight model, Gemma-3-27B. For each training game, each of these models plays 54 episodes: three on each of two sides, against each of the nine other models. We only train on turns from won episodes; this ranges from 24 to 45 episodes for each model and game combination, with 292 episodes in total across all three models and all three games. 

3.4 Reinforcement Learning 

We use group relative policy optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) adapted to multi-turn games. In our main single-seat setting, the trainable pol-icy controls one seat, frozen copies of the SFT model fill its teammates’ seats, and Gemma-3-27B (Gemma Team et al., 2025) plays the opposing team. At each update, we sample 36 deals (12 per training game, fixing seating and the environ-ment seed) and play each deal eight times with independent generation samples served via vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023). On each turn, the policy out-puts a JSON action following the game’s schema; unparseable outputs default to no action with a reward penalty, and the engine assigns positive or negative terminal rewards for wins or losses. We standardize rewards across the eight rollouts of each deal, assign the resulting advantage to all trainable turns in each rollout, and update the policy with AdamW (learning rate 3 × 10 −5) by minimiz-ing the advantage-weighted negative log-likelihood over completion tokens plus a KL penalty (coef-ficient 0.02 ) to the frozen SFT policy. We report the main checkpoint at 180 updates and train to 240 to examine how longer training affects held-out performance. To test seat control (RQ2), we also evaluate whole-team training , where the policy controls all seats on one team against Gemma-3-27B, and self-play , where one policy controls both teams with separate team-level advantages. 

3.5 Evaluation 

We evaluate policies along two axes: controlled head-to-head play and multi-game tournament strength. For head-to-head play , we construct 720 fixed deals (60 per side across the three training and three held-out games, with fixed seating and envi-ronment seeds) and test paired win-rate differences with exact McNemar tests. At test time, the policy controls an entire team rather than a single seat, test-ing standalone team play against both the training opponent (Gemma-3-27B) and an unseen opponent (Qwen3.8-27B). For Qwen3-4B, we compare the untrained model, the same model with a GPT-5-generated SPaRTan playbook (He et al., 2026), the SFT checkpoint, and RLVR checkpoints (main run at 180 and 240 steps, a rerun, and ten lighter runs); for Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-32B, we compare SFT and RLVR. For tournament strength , we test trans-fer beyond hidden-role games by evaluating the SFT and RLVR checkpoints of all three sizes on 17 competitive S OCIAL GYM games 1 across all five categories against nine ranked models, estimating overall and per-category Elo (with 95% intervals) against a fixed board alongside GPT-4o and GPT-5-mini as references. 

3.6 Behavior and Transfer Analysis 

To understand how play changes during training (RQ3), we combine four measures, primarily on the 720 evaluation episodes: (1) Rule-based log metrics : we track role-specific actions (e.g., major-ity votes on hidden players, Resistance fail cards, Chameleon secret-word leaks) and surface degen-eration (near-verbatim copying and malformed ac-tions). (2) Verified speech-act labels : blinded to checkpoint and outcome, Claude Sonnet 5.5 labels the policy’s public speeches across twelve social and deceptive categories; following the monitor– verifier split of Li et al. (2026), we retain a la-bel only if its supporting quote appears verbatim (Appendix C). (3) SOTOPIA-EVAL : GPT-6-Luna scores policy seats across seven social dimensions (Zhou et al., 2024b), and we classify the weak-nesses cited in its believability rationales. (4) Out-of-game transfer : we evaluate checkpoints on AI-LieDar (Su et al., 2025) to test whether restraint or deception learned in games carries over to assis-tant dialogues with incentives to conceal negative information.

4.2 RQ2: Single-Seat Training Yields the Strongest, Most Stable Gains 

Scaling from two-player environments to multi-player environments introduces the design choice of which seats the trainable policy plays during RLVR. To test whether performance gains depend on this choice, we train the same Qwen3-4B SFT model under three settings: (1) single-seat training where one trainable policy plays alongside frozen SFT teammates against Gemma-3-27B, (2) whole-team training where the policy controls all seats on one team against Gemma-3-27B, and (3) self-play where one policy controls both teams. All three settings initially improve play at a similar pace, raising the win rate on the training games from the SFT model’s 56.7% to roughly 68% within 60 steps: 68.1% for single-seat training and 68.6% for self-play after 40 steps, and 67.5% for whole-team training after 60 steps ( p < 0.001 each). Notably, self-play reaches this level without ever facing Gemma-3-27B, further confirming that outcome-based gains do not require a specific training oppo-nent. Over longer training, however, single-seat train-ing surpasses both alternatives in held-out general-ization and training stability. At its best checkpoint (180 steps), single-seat training wins 81.1% of the training-game episodes and 54.4% of the held-out episodes. 4.3 RQ3: RLVR Builds Coordination, Shifts Deception, and Overfits 

Win rates alone do not explain how policies im-prove. We track the main Qwen3-4B run across four checkpoints on the same 720 deals (untrained, SFT, and RLVR at 180 and 240 steps) using the measures from Section 3.6 (Table 3). Imitating winning turns via SFT primarily teaches restraint and coherence: Chameleon citizens name the se-cret word far less often (90% to 28% of speeches), and incoherent speech drops from 17% to 5%. This restraint extends beyond the games, lowering out-right falsification on AI-LieDar (Su et al., 2025) from 62% to 47% of dialogues. RLVR then teaches the majority side to coor-dinate. Across all five voting games, including held-out games, majority players vote for a hidden player more often (Figure 4), call votes on named players more frequently (8% to 32% of speeches), and cite specific evidence more often in training games (11% to 17%) and Spyfall (1% to 21%), though Chameleon citizens revert to giving the se-cret word as their clue (5% to 20%). 5 Conclusion 

We asked whether rule-decided outcomes in multi-player social games provide sufficient signal to train language models for social play. Our re-sults show that they do. Training Qwen3-4B with RLVR on three hidden-role games against the much stronger Gemma-3-27B raises its win rate from 57% after SFT to 81% on training games and from 40% to 54% on held-out games, whereas the strongest prompting baseline, a per-game SPaR-Tan playbook written by GPT-5, reaches only 42% and 43% respectively. The gains hold against an unseen opponent, across 1.7B, 4B, and 32B pa-rameters, and on the 17-game S OCIAL GYM leader-board, where the trained 1.7B and 4B models match the Elo of GPT-4o. Among the training setups, single-seat training, where one trainable policy plays alongside frozen SFT teammates, yields the largest and most stable gains, showing that verifi-able outcome rewards do not require self-play. Our behavior analysis shows what the reward teaches: the majority side learns to coordinate its votes, and its gains transfer to the unseen opponent, while the hidden side shifts from known falsehoods to accu-sations that the game record cannot refute. Check-point selection using held-out games is essential, as training for too long leads to overfitting on train-ing games and erosion of performance on held-out games. Together, these findings show that rule-decided game outcomes offer a verifiable, judge-free training signal for social reasoning. Broader game collections and opponent pools are a natural next step toward generalizable social intelligence. 8Limitations 

First, we train on three hidden-role games, and our evidence for transfer comes from three held-out games of the same category and the 17-game SOCIAL GYM leaderboard. On that leaderboard, Elo gains come mostly from the hidden-role games and Bargaining, while the normal-form, bluffing, and social-strategy games do not change signifi-cantly. Training and evaluating across multiple game categories would test broader social reason-ing capabilities. Second, every run trains against a single opponent, Gemma-3-27B. The major-ity side’s gains transfer to an unseen opponent, whereas the hidden side’s do not (37.5% against Qwen3.8-27B both before and after RLVR), sug-gesting that a diverse opponent pool may be needed to learn transferable deceptive strategies. Third, we train only on the Qwen3 model family, and held-out gains vary across runs and training lengths. We report the main run at its best held-out checkpoint; the ten lighter runs, each evaluated at a fixed step without checkpoint selection, also end above the SFT model on held-out games, though a separate set of validation games would make checkpoint selection fully independent of the reported results. Fourth, part of our behavior analysis relies on LLM judges. We reduce this dependence by retaining only speech-act labels whose supporting quotes appear verbatim, checking judged trends against rule-based log metrics, and using three SOTOPIA-EVAL judges, but we did not run a human eval-uation. We also do not have humans participate in our games. Finally, hidden-role games reward deception: our trained agents frame known inno-cents more often and, on AI-LieDar, shift from outright falsification toward concealing or equiv-ocating. Applying outcome-based RL to social agents outside research should therefore monitor how deceptive behavior shifts in and out of games.

A Game Rules 

We summarize the rules as the S OCIAL GYM en-gine (He et al., 2026) stated them to the players in our runs. In every game, players speak in a fixed seating order and vote simultaneously. 

Werewolves (training). Two Werewolves (the hidden side), who know each other, play against four villagers: a Seer, a Witch, and two Villagers. Each night, the Werewolves choose a player to kill, the Seer learns whether one player is a Werewolf, and the Witch may save the night’s victim or poison a player, each potion only once. Each day, the night’s deaths are announced, every living player speaks once, and the player with the most votes is executed. The villagers win when no Werewolf remains, and the Werewolves win when they equal or outnumber the villagers. 

Undercover (training). Four Civilians share a secret word, and two Undercovers (the hidden side) hold a different but related one, such as vacuum cleaner against lawn mower . Each player sees only their own word. Each round, every remaining player describes their word in one sentence without naming it, and then the player with the most votes is eliminated and their side is announced. The Civil-ians win when both Undercovers are eliminated, and the Undercovers win when one Undercover and one Civilian remain. 

Insider (training). The Insider (the hidden side) knows the secret word, and four Citizens know only its category. The players take turns asking yes/no questions, which the game answers, up to 15 in total, and then all guess the word at once. If nobody guesses it, all players lose. B Run-to-Run Variation 

Ten lighter runs. To measure how much one RLVR recipe varies between runs, we train ten runs of 240 steps from the same SFT model (Fig-ure 7, Table 4). They use a lighter recipe than the main run: each update samples 18 deals (6 per training game) instead of 36, so a step has half the episodes, and the next step’s rollouts are played while the current update runs, with truncated im-portance weights correcting for the one-update lag. Five runs share the deals and the LoRA initializa-tion and differ only in sampling; the other five use different seeds for both. On the training games, every run improves at every checkpoint, and the runs differ by a standard deviation of 1.9 to 2.5 pp. On the held-out games, the mean rises from the SFT model’s 39.7% to 45.8% after 240 steps, with a standard deviation of 2.0 to 3.3 pp across runs, and runs that share deals and initialization spread as widely as runs with different seeds.

Copying and formulaic speech. A speech of the policy counts as a near-copy when the sets of its words and of one of the 40 speeches before it in the same episode overlap by at least 60% (Jaccard), both having at least six words. Its source is the best-matching earlier speech, by a teammate, an opponent, or the speaker itself. A speech reuses a template when, with every player’s name replaced by one token, its words overlap by at least 80% with those of a policy speech for the same side of the same game in another deal. Template reuse in the training games is 9% for the SFT model, 8% to 11% after 40 to 180 steps, and 18% after 240 steps; in the held-out games it stays below 2%. 

Ten runs. The rule-based measures on the final checkpoints of the ten lighter runs repeat the main run’s shifts in every run: votes on a hidden player in the training games rise from the SFT model’s 63% to between 76% and 82%, unanimous villager votes in Werewolves from 35% to between 57% and 76% of rounds, Seer speeches that announce an inspection from 35% to between 60% and 100%, and near-copies from 19% to between 45% and 59% of speeches. Votes on a hidden player in the held-out games rise in nine of the ten runs (47% to between 44% and 62%). Every run also makes Chameleon citizens give away the secret word as their clue more often than the SFT model does (5% to between 8% and 37% of clues). 

AI-LieDar. We ran the exp2 setting of Su et al. (2025) (290 dialogues), with GPT-6-Luna playing the human and both judges, so only our checkpoints can be compared with each other. The untrained model is truthful in 15.5% of dialogues, partially lies in 22.4%, and falsifies in 62.1%; the SFT model in 26.2%, 27.2%, and 46.6%. Its replies are about half as long (371 against 672 characters) and more often name the problem (“To be transparent, there is a known issue with the zipper on model A”), where the untrained model says “I can confidently say there are no known issues.” RLVR lowers fal-sification further, to 40.1% of dialogues after 180 steps and 30.8% after 240 steps, which is 15.9 points below the SFT model on the same dialogues (95% bootstrap interval 9.3 to 22.5). The difference moves to partial lies (39.1% and 45.3%) rather than to truthful answers (20.8% and 23.9%, neither sig-nificantly different from the SFT model’s 26.2%). The RLVR agent also takes no action on about 4% of its turns, against 0.1% for the SFT model; one dialogue per RLVR checkpoint in which the agent never answered could not be judged and is excluded.
