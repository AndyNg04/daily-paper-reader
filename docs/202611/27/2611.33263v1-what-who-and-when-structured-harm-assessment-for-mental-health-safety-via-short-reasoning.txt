Abstract 

The growing use of large language models (LLMs) for mental health support raises important safety concerns. Existing guard models primarily identify categories of harmful content, often overlooking the context needed to determine an appropriate response. We introduce a clinically grounded taxonomy that characterizes harm along three dimensions: what the harm is, who is involved, and when it occurs. It further separates harm characterization from intervention decisions, thus enabling safety policies to be specified outside the model weights through explicit, clinician-curated instructions that can be updated and audited. We validate the taxonomy with expert clinicians and validate a panel of LLM judges to scale annotation. Relabeling public benchmarks in this taxonomy shows that they rarely contain harm directed at the user, which accounts for nearly half of the harm in real conversations. We then train MindGuard2, a 35B-A3B reasoning-based guard model using supervised fine-tuning on short reasoning traces followed by reinforcement learning with a reasoning-length penalty, to minimize inference overhead while preserving the benefits of reasoning. On two public benchmarks and real-world conversations, MindGuard2 achieves balanced accuracy comparable to the substantially larger GLM-5.3 at approximately 10 × lower median latency, advancing the accuracy–latency Pareto frontier. Furthermore, it achieves low false-positive rates at high recall, helping balance the need to detect unsafe situations against the risk of unnecessary interventions that may disrupt support and undermine user trust. We release a safety guardrail model with SOTA performance trained on synthetic data, together with synthetic conversations and annotations of public benchmarks in our taxonomy. 1

# 1 Introduction 

As the global burden of mental health conditions grows (World Health Organization, 2026), many people turn to general-purpose large language models (LLMs) for mental health support (Stade et al., 2026; McBain et al., 2026), raising critical concerns about their clinical safety. Standard safety approaches largely follow the guard-model paradigm (Inan et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025), in which a dedicated lightweight classifier screens each user turn and flags unsafe content, triggering a downstream protocol. These models are typically trained to detect predefined topics such as self-harm, violence, and sexual content. However, in mental health conversations, these topics may arise for various reasons and with varying user needs in the moment: a user sharing past traumatic experience calls for a different re-sponse than someone facing an imminent crisis, just as disclosing one’s own self-harm ideation differs from recounting a relative’s unsafe experience. However, current guard models tend to collapse these situations into the same flag, making it difficult to distinguish when and which intervention is warranted. Furthermore, deciding when to flag involves a difficult but consequential trade-off: missing a genuine crisis signal can have severe consequences, while over-flagging can trigger unnecessary interventions that disrupt conversations, undermine trust, and drive users away from what may be their only source of mental health support in that moment (Siddals et al., 2024; Ni and Yang, 2025). MindGuard (Farinhas et al., 2026) addressed this tension with a clinically grounded taxonomy that separated safe content from unsafe content, including self-harm and harm-to-others. To avoid unnecessary escalation, disclosures of past harm or third party harm were labeled as safe. However, this conflated two distinct judgments: whether harm is present and whether the system should act on it. A user discussing a past episode of self-harm and one describing everyday work stress would therefore receive the same 

safe label, despite presenting different clinical contexts. Moreover, because the boundary between acting and not acting is tied to the label, adapting to a different clinical policy requires relabeling the data and retraining the guard model.

To address these limitations, we introduce a taxonomy that represents harm by a structured harm risk triplet . For each unsafe user turn, the model describes the harm along three axes and in this order: what 

the harm is, who it runs between (from source to target) and when it happens. Self-directed harm is not a separate category but a configuration of the who axis, in which the user is both source and target. The decision of whether and how to act is then made outside the model, by a deterministic mapping from the predicted triplet to a safety protocol, curated by clinicians, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This allows policies to be edited and audited without relabeling data or retraining. We then train MindGuard2, a reasoning-based guard model to predict these triplets efficiently. We show that large reasoning models perform best on this task, but both their size and lengthy reasoning traces incur latency and computational costs that can make them impractical for screening every user turn. We also observe that reinforcement learning (RL) steadily increases reasoning length. We, therefore, use supervised fine-tuning on short reasoning rollouts, followed by RL with a reasoning-length penalty to control inference costs. Finally, we investigate whether the model can identify its own uncertain predictions through explicit confidence estimates, enabling selective verification by a more capable model. # 2 Harm risk triplets (3Ws) 

Our harm taxonomy, illustrated in Fig. 2, first distinguishes safe from unsafe turns. Emotional discomfort alone, venting, and figurative language are considered safe. We consider a turn unsafe when it discloses, expresses, continues, or advances actual harm or crisis, whether directed at the user or others, including past harm. Each unsafe turn is then characterized through a structured risk triplet (3Ws) , comprising three complementary dimensions: the nature of the harm ( what ), its source and target ( who ), and its temporal context ( when ). In particular, the when axis distinguishes past and ongoing harm from future harm, with the latter further differentiated by the degree of intent and proximity to action. Together, these dimensions distinguish situations that a single harm category cannot, while providing a struc-tured description independent of intervention policy. This structured description enables downstream routing to appropriate response protocols through con-figurable, potentially clinician-curated decision trees that map predicted triplets to safety protocols. This decoupling of harm assessment from intervention policy allows protocols to adapt to different jurisdic-tions and evolving clinical needs without redesigning the taxonomy, re-annotating data, or retraining the classifier. Further, this approach is consistent with clinical safety assessment, which detects and differentiates risk signals, and determines risk formulation with appropriate next steps (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2024). For example, suicide risk assessment considers suicidal thoughts (ideation), intent, planning, and other factors relevant to determine the appropriate clinical approach. The Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS), for example, is one of the most widely used screening and assessment instruments and differentiates severity of suicidal ideation, intensity of ideation, suicidal behaviors, and attempt lethality (Posner et al., 2011). Though this level of depth in assessment is out of scope for most LLMs, it illustrates why the presence or category of harm alone is insufficient to determine an appropriate response. 

2.1 Evaluating harm descriptions: 3W score formulation 

To evaluate 3W harm descriptions, one could compute exact matches between a predicted set of triplets and a gold set. However, this approach is overly coarse: a single dimension-level mismatch is sufficient to make an otherwise matching triplet count as incorrect, regardless of which dimension differs. We, therefore, introduce the 3W score (3WS) , which quantifies the similarity between predicted and gold triplet sets while allowing partial credit for partially matching triplets. The score assigns separate weights to each dimension, reflecting their relative importance in harm characterization. In particular, one may give greater weight to what and who , which capture the type and participants of harm, and a lower weight to when , which involves more nuanced temporal judgments and can be more ambiguous. # 3 Data and annotations in the 3W space 

As the 3W taxonomy is newly introduced, existing datasets lack annotations directly compatible with its label space. We therefore assemble datasets from existing sources and newly generated synthetic conversations in three stages. First, we collect conversations from these sources (§3.1). Second, we assess the consistency of clinical annotators applying the taxonomy (§3.2), providing evidence of its reliability. Third, we evaluate whether large reasoning models can reproduce clinician annotations on clinically annotated subsets, enabling us to scale annotation across the training and evaluation sets (§3.3). Table 1 provides an overview of the datasets, their sources, and annotation procedures, which we detail in the following sections. 

3.1 Conversation sources 

Risk Governance (RiskGov) dataset. We use the publicly available RiskGov dataset introduced by Areias et al. (2026) as an independent evaluation source. It contains synthetic multi-turn conversations between an LLM counselor and a simulated patient seeking mental health support. These conversations were seeded from Cradle scenarios, which were first mapped into patient profiles that guided the interac-tions. We filter out safe turns consisting only of brief acknowledgments or highly similar messages, then subsample the remaining turns while preserving the original dataset’s label prevalence and distribution across splits, turn lengths, and turn positions. This yields an evaluation set of 4,660 turns from 661 conversations. 

SimMH-Chat dataset. We use the publicly available SimMH-Chat dataset (Transluce, 2026) as a second independent evaluation source. It contains 48,956 simulated mental health conversations between 157 synthetic personas and 78 assistant-model variants, with up to four replicas per pair. For each persona, we select four assistant models uniformly at random and take one conversation from each. This yields an evaluation set of 8,440 turns from 628 conversations, covering all personas and all 78 assistant models. As every conversation has the same probability of being selected, the composition of the set reflects that of the original corpus. 

Real conversations from AI-led mental health support (RealMHS). RealMHS is a collection of real user–agent interactions from an LLM-based mental health wellness application. These interactions were anonymized by replacing names with consistent pseudonyms and disclosed personal information (email addresses, phone numbers, account numbers, and street addresses) with synthetic values. We split the data by user to prevent leakage between training and evaluation, and we cap the data at 50 turns per user, except for manually annotated conversations, which are included in full in the test set. 

Synthetic Conversations. Inspired by previous work (Pombal et al., 2025; Areias et al., 2026), we gen-erate multi-turn synthetic conversations using a variant of the MindSim framework (Mendonça et al., 2026) to broaden the diversity of harm scenarios and user profiles represented in our datasets. We simu-late interactions between an LLM-based counselor and an LLM-based patient, with the latter assigned a detailed profile from a diverse pool of user profiles to ensure persona consistency throughout the conver-sation. Conversations are seeded with real-world scenarios from the Cradle dataset (Byun et al., 2026). To cover single turn instances and all possible risk scenarios, we also generate single user turns for each plausible (what, who, when) triplet, and pair them with a benign twin written for the same persona. 3.2 Taxonomy validation through clinical annotations 

We assess whether the 3W taxonomy can be applied reliably through annotations by three licensed psychologists from three countries (USA, Canada, Portugal). The psychologists first discussed examples, calibrated their judgments, and refined the annotation guidelines. We then froze the guidelines and conducted an agreement phase, in which all three psychologists annotated the same turns, followed by a coverage phase where a single psychologist conducted the annotations, to expand annotations. The agreement sets contain 182 RiskGov turns and 98 RealMHS turns, while the coverage sets contain 813 RiskGov turns and 399 RealMHS turns. Table 2 reports agreement on safe/unsafe classification and harm description for both RiskGov and RealMHS (see top rows). For safe/unsafe classification, Fleiss’ κ (Fleiss, 1971) was 0.70 on RiskGov and 0.71 on RealMHS, suggesting substantial agreement (Landis and Koch, 1977), and indicating a consistent application of the taxonomy across clinicians. For harm description, we assess pairwise agreement conditional on annotators classifying a turn as unsafe: when two annotators agree that a turn is unsafe, do they describe the harm in the same way? Annotators align on the what and who dimensions in over 89% of jointly unsafe turns. The when dimension displays less agreement, with annotators often differing between adjacent stages (e.g., forward ideation vs forward intent). This is particularly pronounced in RealMHS, where agreement on when is 0.53, compared with 0.89 on RiskGov, suggesting that temporal characterization may be more ambiguous in real conversations. These differences contribute to the relatively low exact triplet match, particularly on RealMHS (0.34). A secondary source of disagreement is whether annotators represent a disclosure as one harm or multiple harms, which also affects exact triplet matching. The 3W score, which allows partial credit for partially matching triplets, provides a complementary picture: it reaches 0.89 on RiskGov and 0.79 on RealMHS. This indicates that, despite the lower exact matching, annotators’ harm descriptions are largely similar across the three dimensions. 

3.3 Scaling labels with Large Reasoning Models 

Clinical annotation covers 1,492 turns, far fewer than needed to train a model or to evaluate on the full test sets. We therefore label at scale with large reasoning models acting as judges (LLM judges), validated against the clinical annotations of §3.2: a judge panel produces the gold labels of the validation and test sets, and a teacher produces the training labels. The teacher is excluded from the panel, so models are never evaluated against their own supervision. Teacher. We select GLM-5.3 (753B parameters, 40B active) as the teacher, using majority voting over three samples at high reasoning effort. It is the strongest individual judge on the full clinician-annotated sets, achieving the highest recall among the judges and best or near-best balanced accuracy. This accuracy, however, comes at a cost: on RiskGov and RealMHS, GLM-5.3 generates a median of 445 and a 90th percentile of 3,117 reasoning tokens per turn (Table 3), which, together with its size, makes it too slow to screen every user turn in deployment (§4). 

Evaluation data distribution. Fig. 3 shows the resulting label distributions across the datasets considered in this study, highlighting their complementary coverage of the taxonomy. The publicly available datasets, RiskGov and SimMH, are dominated by physical harm, particularly self-harm in SimMH and both self-harm and harm carried out by the user in RiskGov. However, these datasets provide limited coverage of other configurations of harm. For instance, harm directed at the user by others accounts for at most 5% of their triplets, compared with 49% in RealMHS. Real-world conversations thus reveal a broader range of scenarios, including substance misuse and situations in which the user is at risk of harm from others. This highlights the importance of evaluating guard models on real-world data that reflects the diversity of harm encountered in mental health support settings. 

Training data distribution. Although RealMHS is more balanced than the publicly available datasets, it still does not provide sufficient data to train a model across the full triplet space. We therefore generate synthetic training data to cover configurations that occur less frequently in real conversations (see §3.1). Its scenario families are defined by the direction of harm and represented evenly, resulting in harm between other parties accounting for close to half of the synthetic triplets (49%). Together, these datasets provide complementary coverage of common real-world scenarios and less frequently observed configurations. These distributions illustrate why a new label space requires purpose-built data: existing resources reflect the distinctions they were designed to capture, leaving other configurations largely uncovered.

64 Training an efficient reasoning guard model 

Large reasoning models achieve strong performance at detecting harm and characterizing it with 3W triplets, as we show in §6, but their latency and computational costs make them impractical for screening every user turn in production. Our teacher, GLM-5.3, illustrates both sources of this cost: it activates 40B parameters per token and generates a median of 445 reasoning tokens per turn on RiskGov and RealMHS, rising to 3,117 at the 90th percentile (Table 3). An efficient reasoning guard must therefore address both: the number of active model parameters and the length of generated reasoning traces. To address the first, we initialize MindGuard2 from Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (Qwen Team, 2026a), a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model with 35B total parameters but approximately 3B active parameters per token. Its sparse activations make it a suitable starting point for efficient inference. To address the second, we train MindGuard2 with two complementary stages: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on short, correct reasoning traces, followed by reinforcement learning (RL) with an explicit penalty on excessive reasoning length (ablations in Table 3). We train on synthetic data and real conversations (§ 3), labeled with GLM-5.3 (§3.3). The resulting model performs structured risk assessment as follows. Given a user turn and its preceding context, it classifies the user turn as either safe or unsafe . For unsafe turns, it additionally identifies each distinct harm and characterizes it using a 3W triplet ( what , who , when ), where who is a source–target pair (who src , who tgt ), accompanied by a short explanation for each triplet. The model produces its output in a structured JSON format, preceded by an explicit reasoning block. 

4.1 Supervised fine-tuning 

We first perform SFT to teach the model the task, output format, and desired reasoning behavior. Rather than relying on a single generated trace per training example, we generate multiple candidate traces and select a set of short, correct completions. For each training prompt, we generate 32 candidate rollouts using Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026b) with low reasoning effort. We choose this model to generate traces because it belongs to the same model family, so its traces lie closer to the student’s own distribution and are easier to learn, and because it can produce concise reasoning, while retaining the MoE architecture of Qwen3.6-35B-A3B for our guard model to reduce inference costs. Out of the 32 candidates, we retain up to four rollouts with the shortest reasoning traces that satisfy the output-format constraints and match the teacher labels, including the full set of 3W triplets for unsafe examples. Prompts for which no candidate passes these criteria are excluded from SFT but retained for RL, allowing the model to learn from more challenging examples in the subsequent training stage. This procedure yields 72,883 training examples from 18,848 prompts, with an average of approximately 3.9 selected completions per prompt. We fine-tune all model parameters using the resulting dataset. The loss is computed over the assistant response, including both the reasoning block and the structured prediction. Further details on trace generation, selection, and optimization are provided in Appendix D. 

4.2 Reinforcement learning 

Starting from the SFT checkpoint, we further optimize the model using a variant of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) to improve triplet correctness while controlling reasoning length. Following Dr. GRPO (Liu et al., 2025) and DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), we omit group standard-deviation normalization of advantages and normalize the loss over tokens rather than sequences, discarding groups whose rollouts all receive the same reward. Training is asynchronous (Prime Intellect Team et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025): rollouts are generated by a policy at most two updates behind the trainer, and the policy-gradient term is importance-weighted by πθ /πμ, where πμ is the generating policy. We adopt the binary trust-region masking of DPPO (Qi et al., 2026), which excludes tokens whose probability changes by more than a threshold in the direction of the advantage, and regularize drift with a squared log-ratio penalty τ (log πθ − log πμ)2 against πμ (Kimi Team et al., 2026b); no reference-model KL term is used. 

Reward function. Our reward function combines classification correctness, triplet correctness, measured using the 3WS defined in Eq. (2), output validity, and generation length. For a well-formed output, the reward is the sum of five components: 

R = Rclass + R3W + Rexact + Rexplanation + Rlength . (3) The reward components serve complementary purposes. Rclass penalizes incorrect safe/unsafe classifi-cations. For turns correctly classified as unsafe, R3W rewards triplet correctness following Eq. (2), and 7Rexact adds a bonus for an exact triplet match. Rexplanation penalizes explanations exceeding 200 char-acters. Finally, Rlength is a generation-length penalty that discourages unnecessarily long reasoning: it penalizes generated tokens beyond a budget By that depends on whether the teacher label is safe or unsafe. The definition of each component, the budgets, and the reward for malformed outputs are given in Appendix D.3. We train for 900 steps, using 64 prompts and 32 rollouts per prompt at each step. Further implementation details and optimization hyperparameters are provided in Appendix D. 

Confidence. Token-level probabilities are in general a poor proxy for confidence in an answer (Lin et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2023). In particular, for a reasoning model, the token probability of the output is conditioned on the sampled reasoning, which has often already committed to an answer. We conduct an additional experiment where we ask the model to report an explicit confidence estimate between 0 and 1. Following Li et al. (2026), this estimate is supervised during training using the fraction of correctly classified rollouts in the same GRPO group. We subtract a squared-error penalty (Appendix D.3, Eq. (9)) from each rollout’s advantage, encouraging the model to estimate the probability that it classifies the turn correctly. 

# 5 Experimental setup 

We compare MindGuard2 against a range of baselines, from low-latency decision models to substantially larger reasoning models, on datasets annotated with our harm taxonomy. Our main goal is to assess whether our guard can match the harm-detection and triplet-prediction performance of more expensive reasoning models at substantially lower inference latency. We also ablate the training recipe to examine its effects on prediction quality and reasoning length. Finally, we investigate whether training the model to report confidence can help identify uncertain verdicts for potential verification by a human or a more capable model. We first describe the test sets, baseline models, evaluation metrics, and latency measurement protocol. 

Test sets. We evaluate on the three test sets of Table 1: RiskGov (§3.1), RealMHS (§3.1) and SimMH (§3.1), all labeled by the gold panel described in §3.3. 

Models. We evaluate our approach at three stages: the Qwen3.6-35B-A3B base model with our prompt, after SFT, and after SFT followed by RL. We compare against three groups of baselines: (i) Non-reasoning guards , which return a verdict without the 3W taxonomy, including Llama Guard 3 8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Llama Guard 4 12B (Meta, 2025), and MindGuard 4B and 8B (Farinhas et al., 2026); (ii) Decision models , which classify turns in a single forward pass and predict triplets but do not provide textual explanations, including CLEF-Flash 9B, CLEF 27B (Chen et al., 2026), elephant-1 9B, and mammoth-1 27B (Kirkby et al., 2026); and (iii) Reasoning baselines , using the same prompt as our model, including the teacher model GLM-5.3, GLM-5.2, gpt-oss-safeguard-120b, and Qwen3.8-27B (our SFT trace generator). We additionally investigate whether existing guard architectures can be adapted to our taxonomy through training on our data. Specifically, we train Qwen3Guard-Gen-4B and Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B (Zhao et al., 2025) using the MindGuard training recipe (MindGuard 4B and 8B, retrained). For the non-reasoning guards and decision models, we tune decision thresholds on the validation sets to improve performance; these are reported in Appendix E (Table 16). In contrast, our model and the reasoning baselines produce explicit verdicts without threshold tuning. 

Performance metrics. We treat unsafe as the positive class and report balanced accuracy (BA), defined as the mean of recall (R) and specificity ( 1−FPR ), as well as precision (P) and F1. Unlike precision and F1, BA and FPR are independent of class prevalence. For harm description, we report the 3W score (Eq. (2)) and exact triplet-set match (EM), averaged over gold-unsafe turns with majority panel support for the reference triplets. Missed unsafe turns receive a score of 0. Unless otherwise specified, we evaluate each model using one sample per turn. Analyses requiring multiple samples, such as operating-point curves and inference-parameter robustness, use 32 samples per turn. We report 95% percentile confidence intervals from 1,000 bootstrap resamples at the conversation level, preserving within-conversation dependence. We assess pairwise differences against our final model (unless otherwise specified) using a two-sided paired bootstrap over conversations with 10,000 resamples, considering p < 0.05 statistically significant. 

Latency. We measure end-to-end latency on a fixed sample of 300 turns for all models, randomly sam-pling 150 turns from RiskGov and from RealMHS. Models run sequentially on an eight-B300-GPU node, with one request at a time and prefix caching disabled. # 6 Results and analysis 

Can MindGuard2 match its teacher at a fraction of the latency? Fig. 4 compares BA and latency across the three test sets. Our final model lies beyond the Pareto frontier of the baselines, achieving accuracy comparable to the GLM-5.3 teacher at substantially lower latency (p50 below 1 s versus 5 s). It also outperforms the other reasoning baselines, GLM-5.2 and gpt-oss-safeguard-120b, which incur higher inference costs. Decision models offer lower latency but generally lower accuracy, and do not produce textual explanations for auditing. On the clinician-annotated subsets, our model achieves BA of 0.86 and 0.84 on RiskGov and RealMHS, respectively, compared with 0.87 and 0.84 for the GLM-5.3 teacher (Appendix E). Fig. 5 further compares performance across classification thresholds on RiskGov and RealMHS. Our model achieves the lowest false-positive rate at both 90% and 95% recall, reducing unnecessary interventions that may disrupt conversations, undermine trust, and lead users to disengage, while maintaining high sensitivity to unsafe turns. Does (accurate) reasoning need to be long? We ablate the contributions of SFT and the reasoning-length penalty, evaluating all RL variants after 300 steps (Table 3). Our final recipe reduces median reasoning length from 681 tokens in Qwen3.6-35B-A3B to 101, and the 90th percentile from 2,018 to 474, while improving performance. Fig. 7 shows that reasoning length increases during RL without SFT (a). SFT on short reasoning traces provides a more concise starting point, although reasoning length still increases during RL (b). Combining SFT with the reasoning-length penalty instead reduces reasoning length throughout RL (c). Importantly, this reduction comes at no performance cost since our final recipe matches or outperforms the no-penalty variant, suggesting that encouraging more concise reasoning does not compromise prediction quality. We further ablate disabling reasoning entirely during training and inference. What happens when the judges disagree? Fig. 8 compares performance on turns where the LLM judge panel is unanimous and those where it is split. Across both datasets, balanced accuracy is substantially lower on split-panel turns (0.62–0.68 versus 0.97–0.98), with a similar pattern for 3WS. This suggests that the model struggles more on cases where LLM judges also disagree. 

Does the model catch the most critical cases? Our model achieves recall of at least 0.96 on turns involving imminent harm across all three test sets, as shown in Fig. 11 (Appendix E.4), which breaks down recall by the when and what dimensions of the gold triplets. 

Can the model identify when to defer? In high-stakes mental health settings, uncertain predictions could be deferred to a human or a more capable model for verification. Unlike prior work that verifies all unsafe predictions to reduce false positives (Areias et al., 2026), we investigate confidence-based deferral for both safe and unsafe verdicts. Since token log-probabilities are not a reliable confidence measure for reasoning models, we ask the model to report its confidence explicitly. Fig. 9 shows the balanced accuracy of accepted verdicts as a function of the fraction deferred for verification. Without confidence training, deferring low-confidence predictions does not consistently improve balanced accuracy. In contrast, our confidence-trained model achieves higher accuracy as more turns are deferred, maintaining similar overall classification performance. Models without confidence training tend to assign high confidence to safe predictions even when incorrect, limiting their ability to identify unreliable verdicts (Appendix E, Fig. 12). 

Does MindGuard2 generalize to other safety tasks? We evaluate MindGuard2 on five out-of-domain safety tasks: WildGuardTest (Han et al., 2024), Nemotron-Safety-Guard-Dataset-v3 (Joshi et al., 2025), PolyGuardMix (Kumar et al., 2025), CompanionSafe (Liao et al., 2026), and AICompanionBench (Ren et al., 2026). This assesses whether training on the 3W taxonomy preserves the model’s ability to follow other safety policies. As shown in Table 14 (Appendix E.7), MindGuard2 achieves higher balanced accuracy and F1 than its base model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, across all five tasks, while generating 2–4 times fewer completion tokens. These results suggest that training improves broader safety assessment capabilities rather than overfitting to our taxonomy.

# 7 Related work 

General-purpose guard models. General-purpose LLM safety typically relies on guard models that clas-sify input (and sometimes output) using a fixed taxonomy of harm categories (Zeng et al., 2024; Han et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2025; Ghosh et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). While these taxonomies differ in coverage and granularity, they all describe content by topic ( e.g. , self-harm or violence), which says little about who is involved and when the harm takes place. In mental health contexts, where such content is often central to the conversation, the appropriate response depends precisely on this information. 

Policy flexibility and reasoning-based guard models. Flexible-policy guard models support user-defined policies at inference time (Inan et al., 2023). In some cases, these are large reasoning models (OpenAI, 2025a), which incur latency costs that are impractical for screening every user turn. Our approach also supports policy flexibility in two ways. First, intervention policies can be changed without modifying the guard model or its harm definitions, by updating the clinician-defined rules that map 3W triplets to actions. Second, although our model is trained to assess harm using a fixed taxonomy, its reasoning capabilities may also allow it to adapt to different assessment instructions at inference time, without retraining. 

Safety for mental health. Several benchmarks and safeguards have been developed for mental health (Nelson et al., 2025; Byun et al., 2026; Belli et al., 2026). Closest to our work, MindGuard (Farinhas et al., 2026) is a lightweight classifier that flags self-harm and harm-to-others risk, and Areias et al. (2026) pair it with a model that verifies each flag before triggering a clinical protocol. Both rely on a guard model trained on synthetic conversations to output a verdict (safe or a risk category). In contrast, MindGuard2 outputs a description of the harm, and we train and evaluate it on real conversations as well. 

Confidence and deferral. In high-stakes settings, some predictions can be deferred to a human or a larger model for verification. A key question is when to defer. For AI-mediated mental health conversations, Areias et al. (2026) propose verifying all unsafe predictions with a larger model to reduce false positives, but this approach does not address false negatives, since safe predictions are never verified. In other domains, prior work has proposed using confidence or uncertainty estimates to guide selective prediction and deferral (Varshney and Baral, 2022; Chen et al., 2024; Gupta et al., 2024). We study whether MindGuard2’s stated confidence can similarly guide verification, including for predictions classified as safe. Prior work has investigated eliciting confidence from LLMs (Lin et al., 2022; Xiong et al., 2024). Most closely related, RLCR (Damani et al., 2026) finds that standard RL can degrade calibration and trains reasoning models to report confidence after reasoning, using a Brier-score reward alongside correctness. Our confidence reward (§4, Appendix D.3) instead follows Li et al. (2026), targeting the fraction of correct rollouts for the same prompt rather than the correctness of each individual rollout. 

# 8 Conclusions 

We introduce a framework for characterizing harm in AI-based mental health support. Rather than relying on harm categories alone, our taxonomy describes each harm through three dimensions: what the 12 harm is, who is involved, and when it occurs. Crucially, the decision to intervene remains outside the model weights, in explicit, clinician-defined mappings from harm description triplets to safety protocols. This allows intervention policies to account for nuances such as the individuals involved and the temporal context of harm, and remain interpretable and auditable rather than relying on implicit distinctions learned by a classifier. Policies can also be adapted to different jurisdictions without relabeling data or retraining the model. We show that clinicians can apply this taxonomy consistently, particularly when identifying unsafe turns, the type of harm and who is involved, while its temporal dimension is more ambiguous. Their annotations can be scaled using a validated panel of LLM judges and a teacher model. Our annotations also reveal that scenarios in which harm is directed at the user, which include nearly half of the harm in real-world conversations, are practically absent from existing public benchmarks. Our reasoning-based guard achieves performance comparable to the GLM-5.3 teacher at a fraction of the latency, enabled by training for concise reasoning without compromising prediction quality. It also achieves lower false-positive rates at high recall than the other guard models we compare, which can help limit unnecessary interventions while maintaining sensitivity to unsafe turns. Together, these contribu-tions advance the accuracy of detection and response to the multitude of form that risk and safety can take in AI mediated mental health support. 

# Limitations 

Our approach uses the LLM judge panel’s majority vote as the reference label and teacher-generated labels for training, without explicitly modeling uncertainty in ambiguous cases. Disagreements among expert clinicians show that text-based risk assessment is open to interpretation, particularly for the temporal ( when ) dimension. Future work could represent this uncertainty explicitly rather than reducing ambiguous judgments to a single label. Our model also assesses risk at the turn level, using preceding conversational context but without tracking risk longitudinally across sessions. Extending the approach to model evolving risk and detect gradual escalation over time is an important direction for future work (Simon, 2004; Sumers et al., 2025). Although our taxonomy aims to provide broad coverage of harm in mental health conversations, it was designed as a fixed label set and may not capture every relevant harm configuration. However, having a reasoning model as the guard model allows for the taxonomy to be adapted or expanded as clinical needs evolve, as suggested by its performance on policies not seen in training (Appendix E.7). Finally, this work focuses on describing harm in a way that separates the description from the decision to intervene. Designing and validating the clinical policies that map these descriptions to interventions is a clinical task that depends on the deployment context, including jurisdiction and service model, and we leave its systematic evaluation to future work. 

# Ethics statement 

RealMHS consists of conversations from a commercial AI wellness application available to adults in the US. Users accepted the application’s terms and privacy notice, which permit the use of de-identified data for research. Data was de-identified before the start of the project (§3). Only authorized staff, including the licensed psychologists who annotated the data, accessed the de-identified conversations. No data was sent to external model providers, and we do not release it. The model we release is a version of MindGuard2 trained only on synthetic conversations with comparable performance. Our model describes potential harm in a conversation, but it does not diagnose and is not a substitute for clinical judgment, and clinicians must define and review the policy that maps its output to an intervention in each deployment. Finally, our evaluation covers English-language conversations, with real data from adults in a single country, so performance may differ for other languages and populations.

To account for this, the 3WS in Eq. (1) uses a temporal credit function instead of an indicator function. As defined in Fig. 10, this function assigns half credit to adjacent temporal categories. An exception is the distinction between imminent active and ongoing non-imminent : predicting the former when the latter is the reference receives partial credit, but not vice versa. This asymmetry favors overestimating rather than underestimating urgency.

# B Synthetic Clinical Dialogues 

We generate synthetic conversations to complement real-world data and improve coverage of underrep-resented configurations in the 3W taxonomy. Following an approach similar to Areias et al. (2026), we construct scenarios and compatible user profiles, which we extend with conversational trajectories to simulate multi-turn dialogues. To avoid overlap with RiskGov, which uses Cradle validation and test scenarios, we use only Cradle training scenarios. Furthermore, our generation procedure uses different models and prompts. 

Scenarios. We seed scenarios from Cradle (Byun et al., 2026), a corpus of real help-seeking accounts annotated with temporal and severity information. Each seed is mapped to one or more what categories and used to generate scenarios for eligible (what, who) combinations, one for each opening tense attested by the seed’s temporal labels, each with a sampled narrative stance. Categories without eligible seeds, such as financial exploitation, as well as under-represented ones such as elder neglect, are generated without a seed from a set of distinct angles. Profiles. Each verified scenario is expanded into a simulated user profile. GLM-5.2 first extracts at-tributes supported by the scenario (using the seed account only as secondary evidence), followed by deterministic propagation of their dependencies. A stratified sampler fills the remaining attributes to-wards the same demographic targets in every (what, who) combination, so that demographics carry no signal about the risk label. GLM-5.2 then generates a detailed backstory in a sampled expressive style. The scenario’s temporal labels, opening tense, and narrative stance are preserved. 

Dialogues. We simulate conversations between two LLMs: a counselor and a simulated user conditioned on the generated profile. The user follows a move-based protocol based on MindSim (Mendonça et al., 2026): before each utterance it selects a dialogue move through a tool call (e.g., sharing, deflecting, expressing hopelessness), which we extend with crisis-specific moves such as disclosing ideation, seeking methods, disclosing an attempt, recanting, and normalizing harm. To capture how risk emerges, escalates, or resolves within a conversation, we pair each profile with every eligible trajectory from a library of conversational arcs. These include escalating, de-escalating, oscillating, or chronically stable risk when the user is the source of harm, and upfront or guarded disclosure of ongoing or past events when the user is a victim or witness. We also include historical-only and false-positive scenarios. Arc eligibility is constrained by the user’s role and the scenario’s opening tense. Single-turn examples. In order to cover single turn responses and all (what, who, when) combinations, we have GLM-5.2 write a user turn expressing that triplet risk in the voice of a profile from the pool. GLM-5.2 also writes a benign twin of similar length for the same persona, which serves as a hard negative. Turn length is sampled to match the length distribution of the simulated conversations. About half of the turns continue a prefix of a simulated conversation, cut at a user turn; the remaining are single turn. 

Scale. Dialogue generation draws on 92 Cradle training seeds plus seedless scenarios, yielding 710 pro-files, 910 profile–arc pairs, and 1,944 conversations with an average of 18.user turns. Each user turn, together with its preceding context, forms a candidate training example. We retain 10,265 (55%) exam-ples by removing safe closing turns and exact duplicates, then downsampling the remaining examples to per-class and per-(what, when) quotas. Consequently, retained turns are distributed across conversation positions rather than forming complete dialogues. We release these synthetic dialogues. 

# C Extended results on taxonomy validation 

Table 7 compares individual LLM judges and judge panels against clinician annotations for harm detection and characterization. Each individual judge’s prediction is determined by a majority vote over three samples, while panels classify a turn as unsafe when the number of unsafe votes reaches the specified threshold. For agreement-phase turns, the reference is the majority vote of three clinicians; for additional coverage turns, it is the annotation of a single clinician, with a different clinician assigned to each dataset. Triplet scores are computed over reference-unsafe turns. These results inform the selection of the teacher model and the composition of the gold judge panel, from which the teacher is excluded.

D.2 Supervised fine-tuning 

Trace generation and selection. We sample 32 candidate traces per prompt from Qwen3.8-27B with low reasoning effort, using the same system prompt as the target model, temperature 1.0, top-p of 0.95, top-k

of 20, and a maximum generation length of 4,096 tokens. Among candidates that pass format validation and match the teacher’s aggregated labels, we retain up to four with the shortest distinct reasoning traces, resulting in a total of 72,883 rows. 

Optimization. We perform full-parameter fine-tuning using AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with a learning rate of 10 −5, weight decay of 0.01, and gradient clipping at 1.0. We use cosine learning-rate decay with 64 warm-up steps and train for three epochs (639 optimization steps). We pack sequences into windows of 32,768 tokens, with a batch size of 32 windows per optimization step. 

D.3 Reinforcement learning

D.4 Checkpoint selection 

We select checkpoints on the validation sets by balanced accuracy and triplet exactness: among the checkpoints whose mean balanced accuracy over the two validation sets is within one standard error of the best, we take the one with the highest exact triplet match. E.6 Stated confidence 

We evaluate stated confidence using verdict consistency and expected calibration error (ECE; Naeini et al., 2015; Guo et al., 2017). For each of the four models in Table 13, we sample 32 responses per turn on the full RiskGov and RealMHS test sets, using a prompt that requests a verdict, the corresponding triplets, and confidence in the verdict. The base model, the SFT model, and our standard SFT → RL model answer this prompt zero-shot; only the confidence-trained variant encounters it during training. Table 13 reports verdict consistency across the 32 samples and ECE between stated confidence and the correctness of each sampled verdict, computed using five fixed confidence bins ( < 0.80 , 0.80 –0.89 , 0.90 –

0.93 , 0.94 –0.96 , and ≥ 0.97 ). Fig. 12 further shows accuracy within each bin, separately for gold-safe and gold-unsafe turns, revealing differences obscured by the pooled ECE. On gold-unsafe turns, models without confidence training often assign high confidence to incorrect safe verdicts. Among samples with stated confidence of at least 0.94, pooled across RiskGov and RealMHS, the base model achieves only 68% accuracy, the SFT model 51%, and the standard SFT → RL model 88%, compared with 97% for the confidence-trained variant. Thus, without explicit confidence training, high stated confidence in a safe verdict does not reliably rule out missed unsafe turns.

E.8 Ablating reasoning 

To isolate the benefits of reasoning, we repeat our SFT and RL training pipeline with reasoning disabled throughout. In this setting, SFT targets contain only the structured output, without reasoning traces, and RL also operates without reasoning. Table 15 shows that SFT without reasoning sometimes out-performs reasoning-based SFT. One possible explanation is that the SFT reasoning traces are generated by a different model (Qwen3.8-27B), introducing a mismatch between the generated reasoning and the student’s own reasoning process. Indeed, we observe that the reasoning-based SFT model tends to fail on examples where Qwen3.8-27B also fails. The benefits of reasoning become clearer after RL: the reasoning-based model achieves higher BA, EM, and 3W scores across all three datasets, with statistically significant gains on RiskGov and SimMH, and the largest on SimMH, which differs more from the training data.
