## Abstract 

Online hate speech is an ever-growing issue with negative impacts on both groups and in-dividuals, potentially causing users to with-draw from online conversations and debate. With some prominent social media platforms reducing their moderation efforts, construc-tive speech has emerged as an alternative strat-egy for countering hate. Automated detec-tion of constructive and hateful speech can aid monitoring and improve understanding of their prevalence and online dynamics. In this work, we collect and annotate a large dataset of English and Danish social media comments for hateful tone, constructive tone, and agree-ment with parent comments. We fine-tune several multilingual LLMs to classify along these three dimensions and compare context-enriched and non-context-enriched model ar-chitectures. The E5-large LLM performs best on both the context-enriched (82% average F1) and non-context-enriched (81.6% average F1) classification tasks, demonstrating the potential of multilingual LLMs for scalable detection of hateful and constructive speech across lan-guages. 

## 1 Introduction 

Online spaces are an important part of everyday life in the 21st century. Approximately two-thirds of the world’s population uses social media, and this number continues to grow (Kemp, 2022). Though online conversations can serve as healthy forums for sharing, expressing, interacting, and debating across countries and cultures, they can also foster hate speech harming individuals and groups. A UN-ESCO (2025) report reveals that 67% of internet users have encountered hate speech online. In 2023, nearly half (49%) of young active internet users aged 16–29 in EU countries reported encountering hostile or degrading messages online, with Den-mark recording the second-highest share at 68%.

Furthermore, among this age group, “political or social views” was the most frequently cited reason for why individuals or groups were targeted, ac-counting for 35% across the EU. This category was particularly prominent in Estonia (60%), followed by Finland (56%) and Denmark (49%) (Eurostat, 2024). Exposure to online hate and violent content has several negative personal and societal conse-quences. At the individual level, it is associated with lower well-being (Oksanen et al., 2014) and happiness (Keipi et al., 2018), as well as increased aggression (Anderson and Bushman, 2001) and re-duced empathy (Funk et al., 2004). It can also lead to stress (Saha et al., 2019), distress (Leets, 2002), sadness (Reichelmann et al., 2021; Keighley, 2022), 1arXiv:2611.32889v1 [cs.CL] 24 Nov 2026 and depressive symptoms (Madriaza et al., 2025). At the societal level, online hate speech discour-ages participation in public discourse. As more and more individuals withdraw from debates (Marz˛ eda, 2025; Gelber and McNamara, 2016; Chen, 2017), distance themselves from online spaces (Pabian et al., 2026), or engage in self-censorship (Celuch et al., 2024), the democratic potential of online spaces is threatened, as these forums become more and more polarized (Kim et al., 2021). Such con-cerns are also reflected in current policy debates about particularly young people’s use of social media. For instance, Australia has in 2024 intro-duced a minimum age requirement for social media use (Australia, 2024), and similar discussions have emerged in Denmark and the EU more generally. While most users scroll by or withdraw from hateful content (Vogels, 2021), others choose to act against it in various ways (Hansen et al., 2023). One of the most promising bystander reactions for reducing the negative impact of online hate is counterspeech, i.e., written responses to aiming to counter hateful content. Counterspeech can take different forms, with research suggesting varying effectiveness at reducing future hostility (Hangart-ner et al., 2021; Gennaro et al., 2025b; Lasser et al., 2025). In this work, we distinguish between con-structive and non-constructive forms of counter-speech, with constructive counterspeech defined as responding in a generally prosocial way. In partic-ular, constructive counterspeech seeks to challenge hate by signaling its disapproval, and/or siding with, shielding, or comforting an individual vic-tim or victimized group. This can be achieved in various ways, such as trying to evoke empathy, call-ing out hateful behavior, correcting misperceptions, or generally calling for a better culture of debate (Hansen et al., 2023). Research has shown that constructive counterspeech is more effective than non-constructive (e.g., hateful or ridiculing) forms of counterspeech (Albanyan et al., 2023; Alyahya and Aldayel, 2025). Understanding (constructive) counterspeech is particularly important in a time of growing plat-form reluctance toward moderation practices. In early 2025, Meta announced a shift toward greater emphasis on free expression and reduced content moderation (Kaplan, 2025), adopting an approach similar to that of X (X Corp., 2024). In contrast, a large share of internet users believe that hate speech should be addressed by social media plat-forms themselves (Ipsos and UNESCO, 2023). In this context, crowd-sourced moderation through counterspeech may become increasingly important. To understand these online dynamics, it is neces-sary to monitor both hate speech and various forms of counterspeech at scale. Natural language pro-cessing (NLP) methods offer a promising approach for automatically detecting such content, enabling researchers to examine the prevalence of hate and constructive speech, as well as whether construc-tive speech improves online discussions. To enable such analysis, we introduce a multilingual, multi-dimensional classifier that jointly labels texts for hate speech, constructive speech, and agreement with parent comments. 

1.1 Our Contribution 

In this work, we collect and annotate a large dataset of English (a high-resource language) and Dan-ish (a mid-to-low-resource language) (Joshi et al., 2020) social media comments along three dimen-sions: hateful (yes/no/unclassifiable), construc-tive speech (yes/no/unclassifiable), and agreement (agree/disagree/unclear). We fine-tune four multi-lingual LLMs for the two detection setups (context-enriched and non-context-enriched), and conduct a comparative evaluation across models. This is, to the best of our knowledge, the first study to jointly model hate speech, constructive speech, and agreement in a multidimensional and non-exclusive framework, allowing instances to, si-multaneously express hate and constructive speech. However, a similar idea has been explored by Al-banyan and Blanco (2022), albeit with a more nar-rowly focused approach. Together, the three dimensions allow the tracking of the precise dynamics of hate and (constructive) counterspeech as they unfold in online comment sections, using just a single classification model. Furthermore, this work introduces the first anno-tated dataset and constructive speech classification model in Danish, addressing a key gap in research on mid-to-low-resource languages. 

## 2 Previous Work 

2.1 Challenges in automated detection 

Automatically detecting hate speech remains a chal-lenging task. Hate speech is often ambiguous and highly dependent on context, making it difficult to distinguish between genuinely hateful content and harmless uses of similar language (Wilson and Land, 2020; Markov and Daelemans, 2022; Sheth 2et al., 2022). In addition, hateful expressions con-tinuously evolve through new slang, coded lan-guage, and platform-specific conventions (Pérez et al., 2023). As a result, effective detection cannot rely solely on the presence of profanity or offensive keywords (explicit hate), but should ideally also de-tect more implicit or subtle forms of hate (Gao and Huang, 2017). Detection systems used in, for example, moderation must also balance the identi-fication of harmful content with the protection of legitimate expression, reflecting broader tensions between content regulation and free speech. Detecting constructive speech and other forms of pro-social speech presents similar challenges. Definitions of constructive speech are inherently subjective, and responses perceived as constructive by some users may be viewed as condescending, hostile, or ineffective by others (Ping et al., 2025). Furthermore, interpretations of constructive com-munication vary across cultural and social contexts, complicating the development of consistent anno-tation schemes and automated detection systems (Fesaghandis and Maity, 2026a,b). We note that, in this work, we focus on encoder-based classification models. While the field of hate speech detection has seen rapid development ac-companying the ever-growing capabilities of LLMs (e.g., using decoder models as single or few-shot classifiers (Rathje et al., 2024)), the effectiveness of these approaches when compared to more clas-sical approaches of using fine-tuned encoder mod-els remains an open question (Zhang et al., 2025), and come with their own set of issues (Fasching and Lelkes, 2025). We believe that for the fore-seeable future, there remains a need for special-ized lightweight models that can be run locally on smaller machines to enable the analysis of large-scale data at a reasonable speed and cost. How-ever, we benchmark our own model against Jev (TypeSafe AI, 2026), a so-called "decision model" optimized to make fast text classifications. This benchmark test showed that our own fine-tuned models substantially outperformed Jev, particularly in Danish (see Appendix). 

2.2 Detecting Hate Speech 

Detecting hate speech has been widely approached as a supervised classification problem, with meth-ods evolving from traditional machine learning to neural networks and more recently transformer-based models (Alkomah and Ma, 2022). A large body of hate speech detection research has focused on English (e.g., Burnap and Williams, 2016; Kshir-sagar et al., 2018; Malmasi and Zampieri, 2017). Non-English hate speech detection has also been explored, although to a more limited extent and often in language-specific settings (e.g., Del Vigna et al., 2017; Pereira-Kohatsu et al., 2019). When exploring multilingual hate speech detec-tion, one common approach has been to mix En-glish with Non-English language(s) (e.g., Ahmad et al., 2025; Farooqi et al., 2021; Mnassri et al., 2024; Siddiqui et al., 2024). Hate speech detection has also been explored in Nordic languages, including Norwegian (Hashmi and Yayilgan, 2024), Swedish (Fernquist et al., 2019), and Danish (Sigurbergsson and Derczyn-ski, 2020), although research in these languages remains more limited. 

2.3 Detecting Constructive Speech 

Detecting constructive speech remains a relatively underdeveloped NLP task, largely due to the ab-sence of a shared definition or taxonomy and the limited availability of high-quality annotated (mul-tilingual) datasets (Fesaghandis and Maity, 2026a; Mathew et al., 2019). Existing work has primarily focused on English data, where early studies demonstrated that con-structive speech can be detected with moderate per-formance using supervised models (Mathew et al., 2018; He et al., 2021; Jacobs et al., 2022) . Sub-sequent research has highlighted the importance of conversational context, with studies such by Yu et al. (2022), Fialho et al. (2024), or Albanyan and Blanco (2022) who incorporated preceding dis-course in their classification setup (which we call context-enriched in our approach), leading to im-proved performance. Taking a different angle, some research focusses on differentiating counterspeakers from hate speak-ers. That is, rather than classifying a single piece of text, these studies take individual users as the focus of analysis (Garland et al., 2020; Mathew et al., 2020). As perhaps the most ambitious attempt thus far in counterspeech detection, Lasser et al. (2025) trained a German classifier for counterspeech that also covers various more fine-grained counter-speech strategies (e.g., sarcasm, providing facts, offering opinions, etc.). However, since most strate-gies were too rare to be reliably detected, the final classifier collapses most categories into broader categories: opinion, prosocial, sarcasm, insult, or 3other/uninterpretable. This also echoes earlier find-ings by Yu et al. (2023), who reported only moder-ate performance in classifying counterspeech strate-gies. However, most studies rely on data from a single social media platform, limiting the generalizability of findings across platforms. In addition, multi-lingual work remains limited and often focuses on a mix of small sets of high-resource languages (Chung et al., 2019, 2021; Möhle et al., 2023). 

2.4 Limitations of Current Approaches 

Most existing systems for detecting constructive speech, toxicity, or hate speech operate on isolated comments without sufficient context, often classify-ing a single message without access to the surround-ing conversation, such as the parent comment or the full thread. While a few recent studies incorporate conversational context (Yu et al., 2022; Fialho et al., 2024; Albanyan and Blanco, 2022), this is still not the norm. This limitation is important because the meaning of a message often depends heavily on its context and external knowledge (Sheth et al., 2022). Furthermore, there is currently a lack of com-prehensive models that capture the full dynamics of online discourse, as most existing work focuses on binary or single-label classification. In particu-lar, hate speech and constructive speech are often studied separately, despite representing related as-pects of interaction within the same conversational context. Jointly modelling these dimensions may improve computational efficiency and classifica-tion robustness by allowing the model to leverage shared information across related tasks (Collobert and Weston, 2008), while also enabling more com-prehensive large-scale analyses of online discourse. Closest to our work is Albanyan and Blanco (2022), who classify replies to hateful tweets hier-archically: first as agreeing or disagreeing with the hateful parent, then, if disagreeing, whether they justify the disagreement or attack the author, and, if agreeing, whether they add further hate. Our ap-proach differs in three ways. First, our labels apply to every comment in a conversation tree, includ-ing the source post, whereas their scheme labels only replies to hate and cannot detect initial hateful comments. Second, every dimension includes an unclassifiable label, so spam and incomprehensi-ble comments are handled explicitly rather than forced into a yes/no decision (see also Lasser et al., 2025). Finally, their model has not been released

## 3 Methodology 

3.1 Data Collection 

The dataset consists of pairs of texts (social media comments). The first text (called the reference text) can be any kind of text, such as a top-level post, a reply, or a quote tweet. The second text (the reply text) is always a reply to the reference text. For the non-context-enriched model, only the reply text is used as input. For the context-enriched model, both the reference text and the reply text, combined with a [SEP] token, are used as input. We aimed to include texts in multiple languages, sourced from different platforms and covering a wide range of topics to create a diverse and appro-4priate dataset for annotation. Data was collected from Twitter (now X), Reddit, and Gab 1.The total dataset consists of 7,388 pairs, of which 6,199 (84%) are English and 1189 (16%) are Dan-ish. The pairs were collected and annotated in sev-eral stages. For the English texts, we sampled 5839 text pairs from a larger corpus of tweets about US politics (Rasmussen and Petersen, 2023) (after an-notation, 5199 pairs remained). A further 500 text pairs each from Reddit and Gab were taken from a collection by Qian et al. (2019). Different strate-gies were employed for collecting high-quality and relevant source data. While the Rasmussen and Petersen (2023) data were based on Twitter data from 2839 users whom they later surveyed, Qian et al. (2019) used hateful keywords to identify rele-vant conversations. The English texts were posted between 2008 and 2019, with most tweets dating from 2016 to 2019 and revolving mainly around American politics and Trump’s presidency. The 1189 Danish texts were sampled from a larger collection of Danish tweets (Rasmussen et al., 2024) collected using the official Twitter API and the R package academictwitteR (Barrie and ting Ho, 2021). The dataset primarily consists of tweets posted by Danish individuals (i.e., excluding official and celebrity accounts). The Danish tweets were sampled and labelled in two stages (see data annotation below), with tweets dating from 2009 to 2022, though most from 2020 to 2022. This lat-ter period encompassed several significant events, including the 2022 Danish general election. Most Danish tweets are likewise about politics; however, other casual topics also occur in the data. A dataset overview is provided in Table 1.

These include responses that aim to promote more respectful dis-course or provide reasoned criticism. However, unsupported accusations (e.g., labeling someone as racist without justification) are not considered con-structive, whereas reasoned arguments explaining such claims are. For the English Twitter data containing 5,839 pairs, each text pair was coded by at least three coders. Inter-rater agreement for the different di-mensions was relatively low (Krippendorff’s alpha: hateful: 0.46; constructive: 0.45; agreement: 0.50), reflecting the well-documented difficulty of the task (Fonseca et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023). Disagreements were first jointly assessed, with remaining undecided cases resolved by the data collection leader. The smaller Danish dataset containing 189 pairs was labelled by four researchers from the research group, with ties being jointly resolved. Class imbalance was identified across two di-mensions (hateful and constructive). The imbal-ance is present both within each language subset and in the combined dataset. The majority class dominates in all cases. For example, non-hateful content constitutes approximately 77–85% of the data across languages, while hateful instances re-main relatively scarce (10–16%). Similarly, non-constructive speech instances account for roughly 72–82%. Such class imbalance introduces a risk that models will disproportionately learn to predict 6the majority class, potentially achieving high over-all accuracy while performing poorly on minority classes. This is particularly critical in tasks such as hate detection, where the minority class is often of greatest interest. An overview of class imbalance is provided in Table 2. The observed class imbalance is consistent with previous research showing that hateful and toxic content constitutes only a minority of online com-munication (Gennaro et al., 2025a; Kantor, 2020). At the same time, we believe our dataset contains a sufficient number of instances for the minority classes to enable meaningful model training and evaluation, balancing representativeness with prac-tical usability. 

3.3 Classifier base models 

We conducted experiments using four pretrained multilingual encoder-based language models (all openly available via Hugging Face): 1. XLM-RoBERTa-large (Conneau et al., 2020) 2. multilingual-e5-large (Wang et al., 2024) 3. Twitter-XLM-RoBERTa-base (Barbieri et al., 2022) 4. mmBERT-base (Marone et al., 2025) To support reproducibility, we supply the exact Hugging Face IDs and revisions in the appendix. These models were selected for their strong multi-lingual capabilities and prior evidence of effective cross-lingual transfer without explicit language-specific training, enabling efficient knowledge transfer (Barbieri et al., 2022; Conneau et al., 2020; Marone et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). We prioritized models that can be deployed lo-cally with relatively low computational overhead, supporting practical applicability in real-world set-tings. For each base model, we fine-tune two vari-ants: one using only the reply text, and one incor-porating contextual information in the form of the reference (parent) text. This yields eight model configurations in total. 

3.4 Task setup 

The problem is formulated as three independent multi-class classification tasks for the three dimen-sions (hateful, constructive, agreement), each with three mutually exclusive labels (see data annotation section above). The model produces a vector of nine logits for each input, which are partitioned into three groups of three, one for each task, and then converted into probabilities. At the prediction stage, the label with the highest probability within each group of logits is taken as the predicted label. Model selection is based on the highest average validation accuracy across the three tasks. All base models are fine-tuned on the collected dataset and optimized through hyperparameter tuning. 

3.5 Hyperparameters 

Models were trained using the same 80/20 train-ing–validation split. Training was performed with a batch size of 16 for up to 20 epochs, with early stopping applied based on average validation per-formance (patience = 4 epochs). Optimization was carried out using AdamW with a learning rate of 

2 × 10 −6 and a weight decay of 0.1. The models were trained using a multi-task objective, comput-ing the cross-entropy separately for each of the three classification tasks and averaging the three losses. We used a plateau-based learning-rate de-cay (Bottou, 2012), which reduced the learning rate by a factor of 0.5 after two epochs without im-provement in validation loss, down to a minimum learning rate of 1 × 10 −7.

3.6 Data preprocessing & tokenization 

A simple preprocessing pipeline was employed. Usernames were replaced with @user, except for usernames appearing more than 100 times in the dataset (keeping public figures and prominent ac-counts). URLs were replaced with <link>. Emo-jis and hashtags were retained. For non-context-enriched models, only the tokenized reply text was used as model input. For context-enriched models, both the reference text and the reply text were to-kenized and concatenated into a single sequence, separated by a [SEP] token, following prior work (Hickey et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2022). The maxi-mum sequence length was set to the default of 512 tokens for all models. The average length of reply texts was 32 tokens, while the average length of concatenated reference–reply pairs was 84 tokens.

## 4 Discussion 

Overall, the models achieved strong classification performance across all three tasks, indicating that multilingual language models can effectively detect hate speech, constructive speech, and agreement. Performance was lowest for the agreement task, suggesting that agreement and disagreement are more difficult to identify than hate and constructive speech. Across tasks, the unclassifiable/unclear la-bels were the most challenging to predict, followed by the positive ("yes") labels. Incorporating the reference text resulted in only marginal and incon-sistent improvements across models and tasks. The findings indicate that multilingual language models can be used to monitor both hate speech and constructive speech at scale. This could enable researchers to examine the prevalence of hateful and constructive content, as well as their dynamics and interaction within online discussions. 

## 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we collected and annotated a diverse social media dataset of reference–reply text pairs for joint detection of hate speech, constructive speech, and agreement in English and Danish. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first annotated dataset that jointly annotates all three dimensions in a non-exclusive framework and the first dataset for Danish constructive speech detection. We fine-tuned four multilingual transformer models in both context-enriched and non-context-enriched settings. Overall, the models achieved strong classification performance, with average F1 scores ranging from 78.6% to 82.0%. The effect of including conversational context var-ied across models: three models showed only marginal improvements, whereas Twitter-XLM-RoBERTa-base showed a decrease of 2.0 percent-age points. The best-performing configuration was multilingual-e5-large with context, reaching an av-erage F1 score of 82.0%, compared to 81.6% with-out context. These findings generally point to the potential of multilingual language models for large-scale and non-exclusive detection of hate speech, construc-tive speech, and agreement, including for Danish. The dataset and models presented here provide a foundation for future research and practical appli-cations aimed at monitoring online discourse and analyzing the dynamics between hate speech and constructive responses on social media. 

## Limitations 

This study has three main limitations. First, the models operate exclusively on text (and rely mostly on social media comments about politics), whereas hate speech and constructive speech are often expressed through multimodal content such as images, videos, and memes (Chhabra and Vish-wakarma, 2023; Kiela et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2019). 8Second, the annotation scheme relies on rel-atively broad labels (e.g., yes/no/unclassifiable), which do not distinguish between different types or strategies of hate speech and constructive speech, that have been theorized to be of varying effective-ness (Albanyan and Blanco, 2022; Albanyan et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023). Third, the dataset includes only English and Dan-ish data. While the multilingual models used in this study have demonstrated strong cross-lingual trans-fer capabilities, the results can be expected to be most reliable for these two languages. Future work could address these limitations by incorporating multimodal data or utilize natively multimodal models, developing more fine-grained classification schemes, and extending or evaluating the approach to additional languages and cultural contexts.

18 D Appendix: Benchmark test against Jev 

To evaluate our model performance against recent, state of the art classifier models, we used Jev (ver-sion 1.13) (TypeSafe AI, 2026) to classify all of our model training data on the dimensions outlined above (hateful, constructive, agreement). We set-tled on Jev since it is fast, cheap, works as mul-tilabel classifier, and can be used as a zero-shot classifier, thus promising to provide a classification infrastructure at scale without the need for expen-sive training data. We ran Jev on 14 October 2026 on all 7,388 reply–reference pairs (6,199 English, 1,189 Dan-ish). Each pair was passed to Jev as a JSON object with two fields, reference (the original post) and 

reply , mimicking the context-enriched scenario, together with three independent choice questions, one per dimension (Table 5). Each choice question consists of an instruction ,which poses the annotation question and refers to the two fields by name, and criteria , which define the possible answers in plain language. As in the human annotation, every question has a third, resid-ual class ( unclassifiable or unclear ). The questions were sent in a single request, answered indepen-dently of one another, and we took the class with the highest probability as Jev’s prediction. The En-glish instructions were used unchanged for both languages. We provided no labelled examples and did no prompt tuning, and instead relied on the codebook for task definitions. Since performance differed substantially for En-glish and Danish data, we provide performance metrics for combined and English / Danish sepa-rately.
