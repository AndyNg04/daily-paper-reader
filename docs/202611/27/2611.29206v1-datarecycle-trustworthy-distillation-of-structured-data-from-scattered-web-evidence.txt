# DataRecycle: Trustworthy Distillation of Structured Data from Scattered Web Evidence 

Can Liu, Tengteng Zhu, Xinyu Jiang, Xiaolin Wen, Yong Wang 

Abstract —The web contains vast amounts of scattered quanti-tative evidence that is difficult to organize into comparable and verifiable structures. Yet its unstructured, sparse, and sometimes conflicting nature makes a systematic distillation costly and error-prone. Existing approaches force users to make a trade-off: search engines return document lists that require labor-intensive extraction and reconciliation, while generative AI systems pro-duce fluent summaries that obscure data provenance, introduce reporting bias, and hide inconsistencies. We present DataRecycle ,an interactive system that helps transform scattered web evidence into structured tables of comparable numerical facts. Given a user’s query or statement, DataRecycle decomposes it into enti-ties and attributes, retrieves candidate evidence, and organizes scattered, potentially conflicting records in a table-based evidence view. To address key challenges in automated data distillation (i.e., incomplete retrieval, untraceable generation, sparse data distribution, and multi-source inconsistency), DataRecycle adopts an ontology-guided, top-down data acquisition strategy, visualizes the recorded lineage from retrieved snippets to extracted values, and aligns heterogeneous data across dimensions such as time, definition, and scale. We evaluate DataRecycle through module-level experiments, a usage scenario, and a user study, showing that the system helps users organize numerical web evidence into provenance-linked tables and inspect uncertainty and conflicting sources. 

Index Terms —Data aggregation, uncertainty visualization, fact verification 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

While existing curated datasets provide a solid foundation for systematic analysis, they often cover only a limited portion of the knowledge available in the world [1]. Much of the most timely and fine-grained data, such as news, corporate disclosures, and market reports, first appears as unstructured text on web pages [2] before being incorporated into official datasets. Converting such evidence into official structured datasets requires substantial time and effort, leading to an inevitable lag between real-world events and dataset updates. Consequently, to capture emerging trends, underrepresented sectors, or the long tail of entities and metrics outside the scope of major data providers, researchers often need to turn to the web as a data source.

Despite this potential, much of the available web data remains underutilized because it is largely unstructured. Unlike structured datasets, in which entities, attributes, and relation-ships are explicitly defined, valuable quantitative facts on the web are scattered across different websites. These facts are typically buried within unstructured text, which makes system-atic analysis difficult. For instance, assessing the global green energy landscape requires aggregating data points from diverse sector-specific reports. Many such data points are absent from global statistical datasets; even when included, they are often poorly aligned, reported in inconsistent units, or associated with conflicting values because of differences in methodology or reporting cycles. Therefore, distilling comparable structured facts directly from the web is essential for bridging the gap between curated datasets and evidence reported in the wild. Current tools largely follow two document-centered work-flows: search engines return ranked pages, while generative engines [3] summarize retrieved pages into prose. Traditional search engines return ranked lists of web pages, leaving users to navigate multiple pages, locate relevant facts, align them across entities, and manually resolve contradictions. A recent system, Compendia [4], partially reduces this burden by generating visual narratives from retrieved documents, but it remains document-centric. In particular, it does not construct a table of the underlying facts, making fact-level coverage difficult to inspect. Generative engines such as Perplexity 1 and ChatGPT 2 improve usability by synthesizing direct answers, but their answers are still shaped by both retrieval coverage and generation-time selection. Even when relevant pages are retrieved, numerical facts may be selectively summarized, merged into prose, or omitted, making it difficult for users to inspect which entity–attribute cells are supported, missing, or conflicting across sources. Beyond verifying individual data points, users often need context to interpret what a numerical value actually means. This requires the same attributes to be aligned across entities, yet document retrieval often makes such alignment inconsistent because different retrieved pages emphasize different aspects of each entity. For example, if the top retrieved documents for Country A emphasize installed capacity whereas those for Country B focus on subsidies, a generative engine may produce a narrative that fails to compare the same metric across countries. Moreover, gen-erative engines often smooth over such inconsistencies and obscure data sparsity. By collapsing conflicting evidence into a single narrative, they hide the provenance and distribution

To address this problem, we first identify the fundamental challenges that hinder the transformation of scattered web fragments into verifiable and structured data (C1-C4).  

> •

C1. Incompleteness of retrieval results. Bottom-up re-trieval and summarization methods based on relevance often fail to cover the full scope of a target schema. Search engines and generative engines cannot guarantee retrieval of all data facts required to construct a complete comparison table.  

> •

C2. Untraceability of generation process. The data genera-tion process in existing approaches often lacks traceable lin-eage. Users cannot verify how specific values are extracted or computed from source documents, making it difficult to distinguish grounded facts from model hallucinations.  

> •

C3. Sparsity of data distribution. Quantitative data is often sparsely distributed and isolated across unrelated documents. A critical data point may appear only once in a lengthy report, which makes it difficult to aggregate dispersed fragments into a coherent structure. For example, when users compare attributes across multiple countries, relevant evidence may not appear in the same document unless prior analyses have already consolidated it.  

> •

C4. Inconsistency in multi-source evidence. Data retrieved from heterogeneous sources often contains conflicts. These discrepancies arise from both factual errors and mismatches in dimensions, such as differences in time frames, scopes, or attribute definitions, which complicate validation. To address these challenges, we propose DataRecycle , an interactive system for trustworthiness-oriented distillation of structured tables of comparable numerical facts from scattered web evidence. Given a user query or statement, DataRecycle 

decomposes it into constituent entities, attributes, and scope, retrieves relevant web sources, and integrates fragmented evidence into a unified view. To guide retrieval toward schema-level coverage (C1), DataRecycle adopts an ontology-guided top-down approach. Instead of relying only on relevance-ranked retrieval, which can leave some schema cells under-covered, the system first specifies a target schema and then retrieves and extracts evidence for each entity-attribute cell. To support traceability (C2), DataRecycle records and visualizes the system-observed lineage from retrieved snippets to final data values, allowing users to inspect the extraction and computation steps represented in the pipeline. To address data sparsity (C3) and potential conflicts (C4), we intro-duce a context-aware alignment mechanism that normalizes heterogeneous data points into a unified coordinate system, for example, by standardizing temporal, unit, and attribute dimensions, together with a multidimensional source-quality signal that helps prioritize potentially conflicting evidence for further inspection. To further support the inspection of conflicting multi-source evidence (C4), we design a compact uncertainty encoding based on the complementary cumulative distribution function (CCDF) for dense tabular comparisons, summarizing source-supported value distributions while pre-serving comparability across entities. With DataRecycle , users can transform natural-language queries into structured compar-ison tables, trace evidence for each cell, and inspect conflicting perspectives interactively. Our main contributions are as follows:  

> •

We present a trustworthiness-oriented data engine for dis-tilling comparable structured facts from scattered web sources, integrating retrieval, evidence extraction, context-aware alignment, and source-aware aggregation.  

> •

We develop DataRecycle , an interactive system that sup-ports provenance-aware table construction and uncertainty inspection, including a compact CCDF-based encoding for dense comparison views.  

> •

We extensively evaluated DataRecycle : a usage scenario to showcase its workflow, quantitative benchmarking of the evidence extraction and record-level auditing modules, a controlled comparison of CCDF bars and density plots, and a user study on the provenance interface, which collectively demonstrated its effectiveness and usability. II. R ELATED WORK 

This work is related to prior research on web data extraction, data trustworthiness, and uncertainty visualization. 

A. Web Data Extraction 

Prior work on web data extraction mainly follows two paradigms: automated extraction and structure discovery with constraint modeling. 

Automated Extraction. Early work focused on scalable information extraction using predefined patterns or relation-learning models, such as Snowball [5] and OpenIE [6]. Subsequent systems shifted toward probabilistic and declar-ative frameworks, including DeepDive [7] and Knowledge Vault [8], which integrate heterogeneous signals for large-scale knowledge base construction. These approaches support high-throughput extraction, but they typically assume relatively stable schemas or rely on post hoc normalization. Chartify-Text [9] uses an LLM to infer tables from text containing numerical data and generate charts from those tables. 

Structure Discovery and Constraint Modeling. Beyond direct extraction, recent studies have investigated how to uncover latent structures from noisy, heterogeneous text. Struc-ture profiling techniques use clustering and representation learning, for example, StructVizor [10] and Tsai et al. [11], or graph-based formulations such as clique-expansion hyper-graphs in HINTs [12], to identify implicit schemas. In parallel, constraint-guided methods improve consistency by incorporat-ing domain knowledge, including state-based decoding [13], in-context log encoding [14], purpose-driven inference [15], and hierarchical blueprint representations [16], [17]. These methods improve structural coherence, but they remain largely automated and opaque to end users. SciDaSynth [18] lets users query a screened set of scientific papers, check extracted values against the papers, and refine the table. Existing systems such as ChartifyText and SciDaSynth structure information from supplied texts or screened collec-tions of papers. DataRecycle starts from a comparison query and retrieves numerical evidence from the open web. It uses a 3

shared ontology to align records by entity, attribute, time, and scope. When reports still disagree, it shows the distribution of reported values and links them to their sources. 

B. Data Trustworthiness 

Assessing conflicting web sources requires source reliability estimation and consistency validation [19]. 

Source Reliability Estimation. This line of work assesses the credibility of information providers through both algo-rithmic modeling and human-centered analysis. Automated approaches infer source reliability from signals, such as inter-action patterns and propagation behaviors [20], and structural anomalies using graph-based techniques [21], through fine-grained [22] and bias-aware [23] probabilistic inference. Com-plementarily, visual analytics research has framed trust as an interpretable and human-in-the-loop process, emphasizing data provenance [24], transparency [25], and interactive sensemak-ing [26]. Building on these principles, several representative systems include those for the detection of anomalous user-generated content [27], [28], and the analysis of anomalous information spreading [29]. 

Consistency Validation. The second line of work estab-lishes truth by validating extracted facts against logical con-straints or prior knowledge. Existing methods enforce logical rules [30], [31] or align extracted facts with external knowl-edge bases [8] to detect inconsistencies. In parallel, visual analytics systems, such as QualityTrails [32], enable users to trace data transformations and assess consistency through transparent and interactive workflows. FOX [33] groups facts extracted from a dataset by analytical scope and helps users explore outliers within each group. Existing approaches often provide limited support for veri-fying open-domain web content. Our framework addresses this by combining source reliability estimation and cross-source consistency inspection in a transparent process, helping users examine trust-related evidence more explicitly. 

C. Uncertainty Visualization 

Communicating uncertainty is essential for reliable analyt-ical decision-making, especially in settings where data are incomplete, noisy, or potentially conflicting [34], [35]. Ex-isting visualization approaches for uncertainty communication generally follow two paradigms: extrinsic auxiliary elements and intrinsic channel modifications [36]. 

Extrinsic Techniques. Extrinsic methods add auxiliary graphical elements to depict distributions or error ranges with-out changing the original marks. Common examples include error bars [37] and uncertainty glyphs [38]–[40]. For instance, Winglets [38] attach dual-sided strokes to scatterplot points, using their length and orientation to encode cluster association and uncertainty. Such designs make uncertainty explicit and can improve perceptual grouping beyond color alone. 

Intrinsic Techniques and Integrations. Intrinsic methods instead modify existing visual channels, such as opacity and sketchiness [35], [41], to communicate confidence [42], [43]. Other encodings, including value-suppressing palettes [44], Bayesian surprise maps [45], and quantile dotplots [46], [47], have been shown to reduce inference errors. Recent systems further integrate uncertainty into analytical workflows, including feature attribution systems such as Explainer [48] and VBridge [49], natural-language interfaces such as Data-Tone [50] and Eviza [51], temporal ambiguity analysis [52], and storytelling [53]. Existing techniques provide limited support for representing conflicts across multiple sources. To address this limitation, we propose a novel encoding that combines bars with cumulative distribution function curves to visualize uncertainty and data distribution. III. O VERVIEW

A. Ontology-Based Parsing 

To address contextual misalignment in unstructured web text, we define an ontology-inspired six-tuple schema that serves as a coordinate system for extracted facts. Each ex-tracted fact is represented in six dimensions: Entity ( E), Attribute ( A), Time ( T ), Scope ( S), Value ( V ), and Unit ( U ), as summarized in Table I. For example, from the sentence “In 2022, Toyota sold 2 million cars and BMW sold 1.5 million cars in Europe,” we extract two distinct facts. Both share the same attribute (car sales), time (2022), and geographic scope (Europe), but they capture different entities and values: Toyota at 2 million units, and BMW at 1.5 million units. Crucially, the assignment of these dimensions depends on context. While “Europe” functions as a shared scope constraint in the previous example, in a query such as “Tesla sales in Germany and India,” the countries instead become entities because they serve as the primary subjects of comparison. This ontology directly addresses a root cause of data inconsistency. Consider a query for the “annual car sales of Tesla in Germany,” which might return two seemingly contradictory values: 200,000 and 250,000. Without contextual awareness, a system might incorrectly identify them as a factual conflict. However, our ontology-based coordinate space distinguishes these values by assigning them to different temporal contexts, for example, one to 2022 and the other to 2023. Formally, because they differ along the time dimension, no true conflict exists. By attaching contextual metadata to every data point, the schema transforms isolated values into context-bearing, inspectable records and enables users to align facts along shared dimensions, such as country, year, and metric, for presentation in a comparable tabular form.

We operationalize this schema through four transformation types:  

> •

Entity handling: Expands the comparison set with related entities to reduce sparsity and support cross-entity analysis.  

> •

Attribute handling: Maps underspecified analytical intents to measurable attributes and adjusts attribute granularity when needed. 5 

> •

Time handling: Extracts timestamps to align facts on a shared temporal axis and avoid conflating values from different years.  

> •

Scope handling: Enforces scope matching over contextual constraints in S so that only contextually aligned facts are compared. These operations provide a common vocabulary for the transformations supported by the interface; user interactions are implemented through one or more of these operations. 

B. Web Crawling and Evidence Extraction 

Based on the proposed ontology, we retrieve and extract evidence for each table cell defined by an entity, attribute, time, and scope. 

Web crawling and text collection. For each cell, we construct a query by combining the entity name, attribute keyword, temporal constraint, and, when applicable, the scope, such as a country or region. We send each query to Google via the Serper API 3 and retrieve up to 20 ranked results by default. The limit is configurable. We download the returned pages and clean them by removing HTML tags, navigation menus, and advertisements. We then extract the cleaned text body and remove duplicate paragraphs to avoid repeated content [8]. The resulting corpus spans diverse sources, including government reports, news articles, NGO documents, independent media reports, and personal blogs. 

LLM-Assisted Evidence Extraction. We use an LLM-based pipeline to transform unstructured text into ontology-consistent structured evidence. Specifically, we prompt the LLM to extract relevant evidence for each target cell and return the result in a structured format. The prompt includes the field definitions derived from the ontology, together with an illustrative example, to guide the model toward consistent outputs. For each text segment and target cell, defined by scope, entity, attribute, and time, the LLM returns one or more records containing the entity, attribute, time, scope, value, unit, a relevance score, and the supporting sentence(s). For example, suppose the target cell corresponds to entity = Germany , attribute = solar power total installed capacity , and 

time = 2025 . If a retrieved web page contains a sentence such as “At the end of 2025, the total installed solar capacity in Germany reached 117 GW,” the LLM extracts a record with value = 117 , unit = GW , and the quoted sentence as supporting evidence. These supporting sentences serve as human-readable justifications, while the structured fields are used for downstream aggregation and visualization. Multiple records may correspond to the same cell. We retain all such records for subsequent deduplication, conflict resolution, and confidence estimation. 

C. Data Consolidation and Conflict Resolution 

The data merging process is conducted in two sequential steps: interval-based data consolidation and LLM-assisted con-flict resolution.

Interval-Based Data Consolidation. The first step aims to merge non-conflicting values extracted from different sources whenever possible. Such cases often arise when sources report values at different granularities or in different interval forms. For example, Source A may report a value larger than three, Source B may report 3.5, and Source C may report 3.54 .The intervals implied by the three reports overlap, so we treat the reports as compatible. To assess this consistency, we convert the reported values into intervals and examine their overlaps. Numerical precision can implicitly convey uncer-tainty. When uncertainty is not explicitly reported, the last significant digit implies an interval of approximately ±0.5 at that level of precision. For example, 3.78 implies an interval of 

[3 .775 , 3.785] [54]. By representing values as intervals, we can identify compatible reports and consolidate them accordingly. For a group of mutually consistent values, we use the highest-precision value as a display representative while retaining all supporting records for inspection. This choice preserves the finest reported granularity but does not treat numerical precision itself as evidence of factual accuracy. After this step, multiple distinct results may still remain, and we therefore further resolve these apparent discrepancies through conflict resolution, as their underlying causes may differ. 

LLM-Assisted Conflict Resolution. After the consolidation phase, unresolved discrepancies may still exist. To address them, we introduce an LLM-assisted approach to conflict res-olution. This module examines the original textual evidence, the extracted values, and the conflicting results to diagnose the underlying cause of each discrepancy. We categorize the causes of conflicts into the following four types and apply tailored resolution strategies:  

> •

Entity misalignment: The extraction incorrectly targets the wrong entity or scope (e.g., extracting global values when U.S. values are required). Resolution: Data points identified with entity misalignment are filtered out.  

> •

Value or unit extraction errors: These are system-level failures during the NLP extraction process, including incor-rect numeric parsing or unit conversion errors. Resolution: 

Upon detecting such errors, the system automatically trig-gers a re-extraction process for the affected data points.  

> •

Attribute semantic ambiguity: The extraction is techni-cally correct, but the queried attribute is inherently ambigu-ous and requires further specification (e.g., “solar capac-ity” could mean newly installed capacity” or “cumulative capacity”). Resolution: The system defers to the user by prompting them in the chat view (subsection V-A). The user can either specify a more precise definition or choose to retain both interpretations as newly defined attributes.  

> •

Genuine Factual Contradiction: The extracted records appear consistent with their source texts, but the underlying sources report incompatible values. Because such conflicts cannot be resolved automatically without additional domain evidence, the system retains the competing records and summarizes their relative source-supported weights using the source-quality scores (detailed in subsection IV-D). The distribution and the conflicting sources are then exposed in the user interface, empowering users to make the final 6

D. Source Reliability Quantification 

We define the trust score T (s) as a normalized, heuristic source-quality score for a document s. It combines citation evidence, information density, and stylistic neutrality to prior-itize sources within the retrieved evidence set; it is not intended as a calibrated probability that an individual claim is correct.  

> •

Citation Depth (vcite ). Motivated by prior work on knowledge-based trust [55], we use the presence of traceable citations as one inspectable proxy for source quality. We quantify citation depth using a hyperbolic tangent function to normalize the count of references as 

vcite = tanh(( Next + κ · Nacad )/τ ),

where Next represents the count of valid external hyperlinks, and Nacad denotes the count of references adhering to academic standards. The parameter κ > 1 weights academic citations higher than general links, and τ is a saturation con-stant controlling the marginal utility of additional citations. Empirically, we set κ = 2 to emphasize scholarly rigor and 

τ = 5 to model the principle of diminishing returns (e.g., 5 weighted citations yield tanh(1) ≈ 0.76 ).  

> •

The interface of DataRecycle is designed to support seam-less transitions between high-level exploratory analysis and low-level data verification. It is organized into four coordinated components: the traceability inspector (Figure 6 A ) for de-tailed provenance inspection, the metadata panel (Figure 6 B ) for schema management, the main table view (Figure 6 C ) for result presentation, and the chat view (Figure 6 D ) for intent-driven interaction. 

A. Chat View and Metadata Panel 

The chat view (Figure 6 D ) serves as the natural-language entry point to DataRecycle . It supports several key interaction capabilities:  

> •

Initializing analysis intent. Users can issue high-level, fuzzy queries (e.g., “Compare green energy capacities”), which the system automatically parses to construct an initial table schema with appropriate entities and attributes. 7 

> •

Iterative schema refinement. Users can refine the table by adding or modifying entities, attributes, or scopes through natural-language commands.  

> •

Human-in-the-loop semantic clarification. When at-tributes are ambiguous or overly coarse, users can negotiate more precise definitions with the system. In response, the system suggests refined schema variants and updates the table accordingly.  

> •

Proactive ambiguity and conflict surfacing. When the background data engine detects semantic ambiguities or conflicting definitions (e.g., inconsistent interpretations of a single attribute), the assistant proactively raises an “ambi-guity detected” alert and suggests schema refinements, such as splitting or redefining attributes. The parsed results of user queries are explicitly displayed in the metadata panel (Figure 6 B ), which is located above the table. This panel organizes the analysis into three struc-tured facets: Scope and Time , Entities (e.g., countries), and 

Attributes (e.g., solar power capacity). Users can directly intervene in this structured representation, for example, by clicking the “+ ADD” button to introduce new entities or refine contextual constraints, ensuring that the system’s retrieval boundaries remain aligned with their analytical intent.

B. Table View 

The table view (Figure 6 C ) serves as the primary workspace for data presentation. Each row represents an entity, and each column represents an attribute. To handle the inherent uncertainty and multiplicity of web-extracted data, the table provides two specialized visual encodings, as shown in Figure 5.  

> •

Probability distribution encoding. For each cell, we vi-sualize multiple extracted values collected from different sources. We build the density from the original records with finite intervals and use one-sided bounds only to check compatibility. Each eligible extracted value xi is modeled as a uniform local density pi(x) over an interval 

Ii = [ xi − δi, x i + δi], where the half-width δi > 0 is determined by the reported precision of xi. The density satisfies pi(x) ≥ 0 and R pi(x) dx = 1. Specifically, 

pi(x) = 1 /(2 δi) for x ∈ Ii and pi(x) = 0 otherwise. Given trust scores {ti}ni=1 , we aggregate these local densities into a normalized, source-weighted evidence-support distribution, 

P (x) = Pni=1 αipi(x), where αi = ti/ Pnj=1 tj and Pni=1 αi = 1 . Thus, sources with higher trust scores con-tribute more strongly to the final distribution. The evidence-support model uses this mixture directly, without kernel smoothing. Its density is piecewise constant, and its CCDF is continuous and piecewise linear. In Figure 5 A , each source is represented by a small dot positioned according to its extracted value along a horizontal value axis shared by all cells in the same attribute column. To reduce overplotting, nearby dots are vertically jittered or stacked. The aggregated distribution P (x) is shown as an overlaid density curve. This encoding reveals variance, consensus peaks, and outliers, allowing users to assess how concentrated or dispersed the evidence is for a given metric. As illustrated in Figure 5, this example contains four sources. Three sources report the value 8.872 GW, each with a trust score of 80% (computed as described in subsection IV-D), whereas one source reports 2.34 GW with a trust score of 20%. Accordingly, the density curve places greater emphasis on the cluster around 8.872 GW while still preserving the lower-confidence evidence at 2.34 GW.

CCDF bar encoding. Although the probability-distribution encoding is intuitive for a single cell, it leaves much of the cell area unused when applied to large tables. C. Traceability Inspector Panel 

The Traceability Inspector (Figure 6 A ) is a collapsible drawer that exposes provenance details for the selected cell. When a user selects a specific data cell in the table, the corresponding traceability panel is automatically revealed. At the top of the inspector, we present an overview of the consensus peaks, summarizing the aggregated evidence for the selected cell. Beneath this overview, the panel lists a set of evidence cards, each corresponding to an individual source that contributes to the selected cell. Each evidence card exposes rich data lineage, including the source URL and its domain type (e.g., news outlet, government report), the extracted semantic tags (entity, attribute, time, and unit), and the exact text span from the original webpage that serves as evidence. Users can interact with these cards to open the original source in an external browser, assess the quality of the automatic extraction, or explicitly exclude erroneous or low-quality sources from the final aggregation. VI. E VALUATION 

Our evaluation comprises four parts: a usage scenario illustrating the workflow, module benchmarks of evidence 9

extraction and record-level auditing, a controlled perceptual comparison of the two encodings, and a system user study collecting usability and perceived-usefulness feedback. 

A. Usage Scenario 

Using the green energy sector as an example, relevant data are typically distributed across websites with inconsistent statistical criteria. Traditional search engines often return noisy or localized results. 

From Fuzzy Query to Initial Table and Metadata. Our approach allows users to begin exploration with a fuzzy natural language query. For example, a user may ask in the chat view (Figure 6 D ), “How do the green energy installed capacities of the United States, China, Germany, and India compare for 2025?” The system semantically parses the query and extracts the corresponding metadata, including Scope (Time), Entities, and Attributes (e.g., Solar, Hydropower, and Wind). The extracted metadata are structured and displayed in the metadata panel (Figure 6 B ). Based on these dimensions, the system automatically generates an initial two-dimensional table view (Figure 6 C ). At this stage, the user can further refine the table, for example, by adding the UK as a new entity through either the table view or the chat view. 

Human-in-the-loop Conflict Resolution. After confirming the table structure, the user prompts the system to fetch data. The backend crawler then performs cell-level retrieval. However, open-web data are often contradictory because of mismatched entity granularity, differences in temporal scope, or attribute ambiguity. To address these issues, the Audit Agent performs preliminary cleaning and supports a human-in-the-loop workflow. As shown in the chat view (Figure 6 D ), when the backend detects semantic ambiguity in an attribute, for example, when “solar power capacity” may refer to cumulative capacity or newly added capacity, it issues an “Ambiguity Detected” prompt. The system then suggests either retaining the original attribute or splitting it into more precise columns along additional dimensions, allowing the user to actively guide the data structuring process. 

Visual Exploration and Lens-based Inspection. Once the table is populated, the system provides rich visual analytics support. By default, the table presents data as the probability distribution encoding to reflect variance or uncertainty. To facilitate intuitive high-level comparisons across countries, the user can switch to the CCDF bar encoding (Figure 6 C2 ), which visually encodes data magnitude, for example, when comparing population or wind cumulative power across enti-ties. Furthermore, for cells containing complex or conflicting values, the user can activate the semantic lens (Figure 6 C1 ). Hovering over or focusing on a specific area opens a detailed lens that juxtaposes distinct extracted values (e.g., 12.40 vs. 347.00) with their corresponding trust scores (computed as described in subsection IV-D) and evidence sentences, helping users quickly assess data reliability. 

Multi-Source Traceability and Verification. Ensuring data accuracy is critical in policy research. When a user wants to audit a specific cell’s value (e.g., Germany’s solar cumulative capacity of 117 GW), they can click on the corresponding data node (as indicated by 1 ). This action expands the traceability inspector (Figure 6 A ) on the left. This panel visualizes the “Consensus Peaks” of the extracted values (e.g., clusters around 117 GW and 106.2 GW). Crucially, it lists the exact provenance for each data point, including source URLs (e.g., 

emi-bg.com ), the original textual context, and the extracted time and attribute tags. This transparency allows researchers to verify and select data sources for further analysis.

C. Perceptual Comparison 

We conducted a controlled user study comparing our CCDF-based bar encoding with a density-plot encoding (i.e., the probability distribution encoding described in subsection V-B) on uncertainty interpretation tasks. 

Task Design. We designed five tasks to cover the core operations involved in analyzing uncertain distributions. T1 (Extremes ) asks participants to quickly identify the cell with the maximum or minimum value in a dense 5×5 matrix, evalu-ating how well an encoding supports rapid visual scanning. T2 (Consistency ) asks participants to identify the most consistent and the least consistent cells in the matrix. T3 ( Probability Estimation ) asks participants to estimate the probability that a value exceeds a given threshold. T4 ( Cluster Weight ) asks participants to select the cluster with the largest probability mass. The component weights determine the reference answer. T5 ( Entity Comparison ) asks participants to identify the entity with the highest probability of exceeding a given threshold. Reference answers are computed from the tail probabilities of the generated distributions. The stimuli are normalized Gaus-sian mixtures generated with varying component locations, spreads, and weights. Both encodings reuse the same fixed stimulus sequence and answers. T1 and T2 each contain five trials per subgoal. The tutorial explains density area and CCDF height with worked examples and revealed answers. Before each task category, participants familiarized themselves with example cases. 

Experimental Setup and Statistical Analysis. A total of 12 participants were recruited for the study. Each participant completed all five tasks using both the CCDF and Density encodings. The five task categories were presented in a fixed order. Within each category, participants completed the trials for one encoding as a block, followed by the trials for the other encoding, before proceeding to the next category. Encoding order was counterbalanced across participants: six used CCDF first and six used Density first. Ten trials were scheduled per task and encoding (100 per participant). Par-ticipant means were computed from available trials without imputation. We recorded task completion time and accuracy for each task. For statistical analysis, we first averaged results within each participant for each task, and then conducted two-tailed paired t-tests on participant-level means. Reported 

p values use the Student t distribution with 11 degrees of freedom and are unadjusted for multiple comparisons. For the probability estimation task (T3), we computed accuracy as 100 − absolute percentage-point error. 

Results. As shown in Figure 8, CCDF achieved significantly higher accuracy in T1 ( Extremes , p < . 05 ), T3 ( Probability Estimation , p < . 001 ), T4 ( Cluster Weight , p < . 001 ), and T5 (Entity Comparison , p < . 001 ); no significant difference was observed for T2 ( Consistency ). For completion time, CCDF was significantly faster in T1 ( p < . 001 ) and T3 ( p = .002 ), with no significant time differences in T2, T4, or T5. 

D. User Study 

To collect usability feedback from users with diverse but relevant backgrounds, we recruited 12 participants (P1–P12, comprising 8 males and 4 females) from a range of academic and professional fields, including data analysis, computer science, international trade, business engineering, and soft-ware development. We treated participants with either relevant professional roles or at least three years of experience in visualization, data analysis, software development, or web-based information organization as expert users. By this cri-terion, 7 participants (P1, P2, P3, P6, P7, P11, P12) were expert users. This cohort included two visualization experts (P3, P11), three data analysts (P1, P2, P7), two software developers (P6, P12), four graduate students (P4, P8, P9, P10), and one undergraduate student (P5). All participants reported substantial experience in retrieving unstructured data from the Internet and performing data cleaning and analysis in their daily academic research or professional work. We also collected participants’ experience with data visualization tools; on average, they reported 4.08 years of experience. The study was conducted in a local experimental setting. Participants used a 27-inch monitor and interacted with the system through a Chrome browser.

D ISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK 

Limitations. DataRecycle organizes numerical web evi-dence into comparable tables with links to the sources. Sev-eral limitations remain. 1) Evaluation scope. The module benchmark measures extraction and auditing, the perceptual study tests how participants interpret the encodings, and the user study captures perceived usefulness. Even when retrieved sources agree, search rankings and limited retrieval depth can leave out relevant or contradictory reports. We did not compare evidence coverage, contradiction detection, or cost across retrieval depths. For source weighting, we tested how rankings change as the weights vary. We have yet to compare the scores with independent assessments of source reliability or compare 12 

verification outcomes under uniform or alternative weights. In the user study, we did not systematically assess whether participants correctly detected or corrected errors. We also did not compare final table accuracy, evidence coverage, or verification time in a controlled study. 2) Schema omissions. 

Omitted attributes may leave no empty cells. Users can add them by refining the schema. The module benchmark uses manually specified targets and supplied texts, so it cannot assess retrieval coverage, initial schema completeness, or whether users find and recover omissions. 3) Source de-pendence. Paragraph deduplication cannot identify dependent sources. Weighting republications and paraphrases separately can overstate support for the same evidence. Grouping depen-dent sources before weighting could reduce this effect. 

Context-Aware Data via Local RAG. DataRecycle cur-rently focuses on open-web data, while many organizations and individuals maintain large volumes of private “dark data” [58], such as business logs, analytical reports, emails, and historical dashboards. A promising direction is to in-corporate local RAG mechanisms for structuring such pri-vate, heterogeneous repositories without exposing sensitive content to external LLM services. This extension requires clear processing boundaries: sensitive data could be retrieved and modeled locally, whereas abstracted or sanitized repre-sentations could be passed to cloud-based LLMs for further reasoning. Such local–cloud collaboration may help balance organizational context, analytical capability, and data security. 

Beyond Numerical Representations. DataRecycle cur-rently focuses on numerical data, where facts can be aligned in a shared ontology space and presented in tabular form. How-ever, many real-world scenarios are better expressed through links and relationships among entities, forming relational structures that require conceptual disambiguation. A future di-rection is to extend the ontology space beyond attribute-value pairs to represent entity relationships and graph-structured data. This extension would support richer relational seman-tics, but also introduces challenges in modeling higher-order relationships and maintaining semantic alignment across het-erogeneous sources. 

Inspecting Potential Source Manipulation. Recent re-search highlights the risk of manipulating content retrieved by generative engines [3], [59]. In such attacks, malicious actors flood the web with manipulated content to intentionally contaminate the retrieval pool. Retrieved content can then lead to misleading answers, for example, a scam phone num-ber [60]. The provenance and duplication views let users trace apparent agreement back to its sources and inspect repeated or conflicting reports. Future work could test whether displaying source relationships and publication timing helps analysts spot coordinated manipulation, such as sudden spikes in publication volume or clusters of highly repetitive reports. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

This work addresses the challenge of transforming scattered web evidence into verifiable structured data for analysis. 

DataRecycle adopts a schema-guided, top-down retrieval strat-egy that targets explicit table cells and makes coverage gaps visible, together with a provenance-centered design that makes the data lineage from raw snippets to final cells inspectable. The evaluations indicate that DataRecycle supports evidence extraction and helps users inspect and interpret conflicts across sources. By combining algorithmic trust estimation with interactive provenance inspection and schema refinement, 

DataRecycle helps users stay in control of the analytical process. Looking ahead, several directions may extend the system. Incorporating local retrieval-augmented generation could support context-aware structuring over private organiza-tional data while respecting privacy constraints. Extending the system beyond numerical tables could support richer relational representations in a broader ontology space. ACKNOWLEDGMENT
