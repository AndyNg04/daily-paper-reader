# Modeling Event-Conditioned Emotional Expression Response with LLM Agents in a Synthetic Social Simulation 

## REBECCA MARIGLIANO ✉, Carnegie Mellon University, USA 

## PETER CARRAGHER, Carnegie Mellon University, USA 

## KATHLEEN M. CARLEY, Carnegie Mellon University, USA 

Emotions are central drivers of human behavior, yet most behavioral models fail to capture their role in shaping perception, decision-making, and collective action. Application areas such as emergency response and public health are especially constrained by the lack of emotion-aware modeling. While large language models (LLMs) are increasingly integrated into agent-based models (ABMs), little work has examined how to endow LLM agents with emotional reasoning or validated their ability to reproduce empirically observed emotional patterns. This study addresses this gap by introducing a hierarchical prompting framework that conditions LLM agents with demographic, psychological, and contextual cues to generate event-conditioned emotional responses to real-world events. We validate the resulting event-conditioned emotional responses against large-scale social media reaction data derived from the Social Science One Facebook URL Shares Dataset [45], complemented by demographic and personality benchmarks. Our simulation results reproduce both polarized and consensus-driven emotional patterns with significant fidelity, supporting the application of LLM agents for emotion-aware modeling in behavioral simulations. We also find, however, that systematic gaps in emotional expression of LLM agents and human populations exist, such as the underexpression of anger relative to human baselines. As such, these findings highlight the need for continued calibration if LLM agents are to serve as proxies for human populations in behavioral modeling. By quantifying their divergence from human populations in terms of emotional expression, we hope that our methods can aid in this effort. During the same period, more than 40% of Americans reported adverse mental health conditions [13], overwhelming existing support systems. Accounting for emotional amplification and diffusion could have enabled more effective planning and targeted interventions. Similar dynamics occur in online misinformation and persuasion. Dampening affective responses can reduce engage-ment with misinformation that exploits fast, emotion-driven reasoning rather than deliberative thought [16, 21, 46]. Conversely, emotional appeals can be used to promote credible information, as demonstrated in public health messaging during the COVID-19 pandemic [48]. Modeling emotional response is therefore important for policy design and behavioral simulation. Emotional reasoning influences risk perception, decision making, and collective behavior [21]. Emotional contagion contributes to panic and polarization, while trust, empathy, and cooperation depend on emotional reciprocity [41]. Models that ignore emotional reasoning omit an important component of social behavior and opinion dynamics [29], with evidence showing it is as important as deliberative reasoning in these settings [44]. Despite this, traditional models of behavior and opinion dynamics emphasize belief updating over emotional response. To address this limitation, we make three contributions. First, we introduce a hierarchical framework for generating LLM personas capable of producing event-conditioned emotional responses across country, community, and individual levels. Second, we formalize an event-conditioned emotional response model linking trait-level affect, event salience, and stance to expressed emotion. Third, we evaluate the framework using Facebook reaction data from the overturning of Roe v. Wade and the expansion of the 988 Suicide Hotline during the Covid pandemic, assessing whether synthetic personas reproduce observed demographic differences in emotional expression. Our framework achieves this by conditioning personas on demographic information, moral foundations, OCEAN personality traits, ideological context, and event-specific information through structured multi-stage prompting. The resulting personas engage in repeated network-mediated interactions through a shared communication environment, allowing emotional responses to evolve across successive interaction timesteps. We evaluate the framework through both internal and external validation. Internally, ablation experiments assess response quality using measures of emotional intensity, topical relevance, and persona consistency, together with implementation-level validation of demographic fidelity, hierarchical consistency, opinion generation, and schema integrity (Appendix C). Externally, generated emotional response patterns are compared with Facebook URL Shares engagement data, demonstrating that the personas reproduce several demographic and event-conditioned trends observed in real-world discourse [45]. Supplementary COVID-19 experiments further extend this evaluation to prolonged and politically polarized public-health discourse. The resulting demographic and emotional trends are consistent with prior findings on age-related emotional regulation [54]. While our results demonstrate the potential of LLM-based synthetic social simulation for emotional modeling at the population level, they do not establish a psychological equivalence between LLM personas and human subjects, nor do they seek to diminish the importance of human subjects in behavioral research. More broadly, the findings contribute to ongoing discussions of whether LLM-generated personas can support meaningful social simulation in resource constrained conditions [14]. Agreement with observed age-, anger-, and sadness-related trends supports applications such as “Silicon Sampling” [8, 10, 28, 38], while remaining gaps, including the underrepresentation of anger, highlight the need for continued empirical calibration [43, 49]. As such, we hope that this work clarifies the positive use cases of LLM personas for emotional and behavioral modeling, while also serving as a foundation for their continued calibration in synthetic social simulations.

2 Related Works 

Emotional response in political contexts is motivated by persistent findings that emotional content spreads rapidly on social media [5], with anger showing particularly strong viral characteristics [9], and emotional contagion being especially pronounced in digital settings [24]. Computational approaches have evolved from early emotion theories and sentiment polarity detection to richer emotional profiling systems grounded in psychological theory and supported by lexicons and modern language models. 

Cross-Cultural Modeling. Plutchik’s theory of primary emotions [39] underpins widely used computational resources such as the NRC Emotion Lexicon [36]. While work has shown universality in some emotion signals (e.g., facial expressions) [15], cultural variation in recognition and expression norms remains well documented [20], motivating calibration of emotion resources trained primarily on Western data [23]. To this end, cross-cultural research efforts have extended traditional western-oriented emotion lexicons by calibrating them for broader applicability [26, 47]. 

Moral Foundation Theory and Political Expression. Moral Foundations Theory provides a structured account of cross-cultural and ideological variation in moral reasoning [17]. Cultural dimensions and expression norms have been linked to systematic differences in affective expression across societies [19, 32], including distinctions between American and Canadian discourse patterns [1, 27]. Within the United States, ideological groups differ in moral-emphasis profiles [17], and perceived moral violations are associated with distinct emotional responses (e.g., sadness, anger, contempt) [4, 11]. 

Personality Modeling. Cross-cultural differences also appear in personality distributions [42]. Big Five traits are associated with systematic variation in affective expression: extraversion correlates with positive affective expression and neuroticism predicts negative emotion intensity [33, 50]. Individual differences in regulation further shape expression patterns, with neuroticism linked to maladaptive regulation and conscientiousness to more controlled expression [18]. 

LLM Personas. Recent work suggests LLM-based agents can sustain coherent personas over extended interactions [37] and can produce behaviorally plausible responses distributions when conditioned on persona specifications [3]. Prompting methods also provide finer control over model behavior by explicitly eliciting intermediate reasoning and structured outputs through staged, multi-step prompts [52, 57]. These advances motivate structured persona conditioning as a practical pathway for integrating richer psychological and contextual constraints into synthetic agent behavior. 

Limitations. Emotion expression varies by communication modality, with differences observed between text-based and vocal contexts [6]. More broadly, LLM-generated personas may reflect biases in training data [7, 53]. In addition, some emotions may be systematically underrepresented in LLM outputs (e.g., attenuated anger), raising concerns about ecological validity for polarized scenarios. Finally, validation is often limited by topic coverage, constraining generalizability. These limitations reinforce the need for continued calibration and cross-platform validation before deploying LLM personas in high-stakes behavioral modeling. 

3 Method: Hierarchical Persona Synthesis 

Persona generation proceeds hierarchically: countries → communities → individuals. At each stage, structured prompts are passed to an LLM to produce records in a predefined schema. A validation layer checks completeness and demographic consistency; invalid generations are retried until valid outputs are obtained. This design helps maintain consistency between country-, community-, and individual-level attributes.

3.4 Quality Control 

Each LLM output is parsed and schema-validated; invalid records trigger retries. Batches proceed to analysis only if ≥ 70% of outputs are valid, improving reproducibility and reducing spurious generations. For batches with > 70% valid outputs, invalid outputs are iteratively replaced until batch validity reaches > 95%. Validation checks ensure that generated personas satisfy demographic and schema constraints, avoiding impossible combinations such as a child with a bachelor’s degree and a high income level, following the validation approach described in [30]. The hierarchy therefore helps keep generated responses consistent with the persona’s assigned demographic, ideological, and personality profile.

5.1 Prompt Construction 

Each prompt combines four information sources: (i) demographic attributes, (ii) psychological attributes determined by the experimental condition, (iii) event-specific information including stance and salience, and (iv) the persona’s current conversational context. Experimental conditions ( L) differ only in the psychological attributes included (Table 3), while all other prompt components remain fixed. Complete prompt templates are provided in Appendix A.4. 

5.2 Response Generation 

Each persona generates one social-media-style response per interaction timestep using the shared OpenAI GPT-4o-mini backend. Responses are conditioned on the complete persona specification, the current event, and the accumulated conversational context, while demographic and psychological attributes remain fixed throughout the simulation. 

5.3 Emotion Analysis 

Immediately after generation, responses are analyzed using a Plutchik-based emotion classifier. The resulting emotion scores are compared with each persona’s baseline ( ebase ) and event-conditioned ( e(𝑡 )) emotion profiles to evaluate persona consistency and support the validation measures described in Section 7.

6 Experimental Conditions 

Experiments are conducted using the personas described in Section 3. Personas are assessed across five prompting levels ( L), two high-salience sociopolitical scenarios ( S), and |𝑇 | = 10 interaction timesteps to examine how contextual information and event framing influence emotional responses over repeated interactions. 

6.1 Scenarios ( S). 

We consider two salient and ideologically distinct scenarios: 

• Roe v. Wade Overturn (June 2022): A polarized Supreme Court decision on reproductive rights, used to evaluate how political orientation and moral foundations shape emotional responses. 

• 988 Suicide Hotline (July 2022): A mental health policy intervention with broad public relevance, used to examine responses to public health policy. The Suicide Hotline scenario complements the primary scenario by representing an event with broad societal relevance and comparatively limited ideological polarization. The two scenarios also differ in both engagement patterns and demographic response distributions.

7 Validation

For external validity, we compare the rates of emotional expression in generated responses with emotional engagement data drawn from social media.

7.2 Validation Against Social Media Data 

Finally, we compare the distribution of emotional expression in LLM responses with Facebook engagement data for each event. Facebook reactions are treated as observable indicators of affective engagement rather than direct measures of emotional state. Because reactions (e.g., Love, Angry, Wow, and Sorry) do not map directly to Plutchik emotion categories, comparisons are made at the demographic and population level rather than through one-to-one emotion matching. Using the Social Science One URL Shares dataset, which provides aggregate counts of Facebook reactions [45], we query event-related URLs, group reactions by age, gender, and political affiliation, and compare the resulting demographic distributions with those generated by the LLM personas. This evaluation therefore assesses whether the synthetic personas reproduce population-level patterns of emotional expression, rather than validating individual emotional states.

9 Discussion 

The results of this study indicate that hierarchically constructed LLM personas can reproduce several empirically observed patterns in event-conditioned response while preserving the practical advantages of simulation-based research. Validation against Facebook reaction data, combined with systematic ablation testing across prompt-complexity levels, provides support for the framework’s behavioral plausibility. At the same time, the remaining distributional differences between empirical reactions and persona-generated responses point to important calibration targets for future work.

9.1 Hierarchical Prompt Engineering and Prompt Structure 

The ablation results show why hierarchical prompting is effective. Adding demographic, moral, psychological, and event-conditioned emotion cues improved response quality, increasing emotional intensity by 237% and persona consistency by 60% from baseline to the full D+M+O+E condition. These gains did not simply make responses more emotional; they produced selective, context-appropriate shifts. For the polarizing Roe v. Wade stimulus, fear and sadness rose sharply (+665% and +464%) while anger fell from 0.227 to 0.100 ( −56%); for the mental-health-related Suicide Hotline 988 stimulus, joy rose by 528% and neutral responses fell by 97% (Table 6). Conditioning therefore redirects affect toward event-appropriate emotions rather than amplifying all of them, and the decline in anger under the full condition is consistent with the under-expression of anger reported in the Abstract. These findings highlight the value of hierarchy. Country-level demographics provide macro-level structure, com-munities add local variation, and individual personas incorporate psychological depth through OCEAN traits, moral foundations, and event-conditioned priors. Together, these layers constrain outputs across demographic, ideological, and emotional dimensions, reducing incoherent persona behavior. At the population level, the framework also reproduced social structure. Institutional community generation produced balanced category distributions (16.4–16.8%) while preserving within-group variation. This shows that hierarchical prompting can model individual, group, and societal patterns within a consistent simulation framework. Overall, the ablation and demographic results support hierarchical prompt engineering as a useful method for building structured and empirically interpretable synthetic populations. 

9.2 Believability for Research Simulation 

In this work, realism refers to the preservation of interpretable demographic, ideological, and emotional response patterns rather than the exact reproduction of individual human behavior. The framework is intended to support controlled simulation studies by generating synthetic populations that preserve broad population-level patterns. Across the evaluated scenarios, the framework reproduced several observed demographic and topic-specific trends without manual parameter calibration. The framework produces demographic and emotional response patterns that are consistent with the evaluated scenarios, providing a practical approach. This method enables controlled social simulation without requiring human participants or sensitive personal data. By combining demographic, ideological, and psychological attributes, it enables the exploration of population-level responses to complex events. The hierarchical generation process can also be reweighted to represent census-aligned or underrepresented populations, allowing researchers to examine groups that are difficult to observe in online data. 

9.3 Scenarios and Interventions 

These results indicate that hierarchically constructed LLM personas can generate individual-level emotional responses and support the study of collective dynamics across scenarios. The framework couples macro demographic baselines with meso community structure and micro psychological traits, making the sources of variation explicit and the outputs more interpretable. The modular country →community →individual template also improves scalability by reusing shared context across personas under a fixed schema and validation layer. These properties support scenario-based experimentation in which population responses to emerging events are simulated from empirically grounded community priors. 10 Conclusions 

This study provides initial evidence that hierarchically conditioned LLM personas can reproduce several interpretable demographic and event-conditioned response patterns. By grounding personas in country-level context, community membership, psychological traits, and event-specific cues, the framework produced responses that aligned with several observed patterns in Facebook reaction data. Ablation testing showed that richer persona conditioning improved emotional intensity, topical relevance, and persona consistency, while the evaluated cases captured both polarizing dynamics on anger-prone topics (e.g., Roe v. Wade) and broader demographic gradients (e.g., increased sadness with age). These findings suggest that hierarchical prompt engineering improves the behavioral plausibility of LLM-generated personas for synthetic social simulation. However, the current evaluation is limited to a small set of high-salience events in U.S. and Canadian contexts. Further validation across additional cultures, platforms, and event types is needed to assess the generalizability of the framework. Overall, this work offers a foundation for extending deliberation-centric ABMs with agents capable of producing event-conditioned responses under structured prompting. Future work should expand validation across additional countries, platforms, time periods, and event types, and further calibrate emotion-specific parameters before applying the framework to high-stakes policy or intervention settings. 

10.1 Limitations 

Several limitations should be considered. First, the evaluation covers a limited set of events, so its generalizability to other cultural, political, and discourse contexts remains uncertain. Although the supplementary COVID-19 analysis broadens the scope, further longitudinal and cross-platform validation is needed. Second, LLM-generated personas may reflect biases from their training data and prompt design, affecting how demographic groups, moral values, and political orientations are represented [7, 53]. These concerns underscore the need for continued validation, calibration, and interpretability in synthetic social simulation. Third, the framework captures aggregate emotional and demographic patterns but does not yet model more complex dynamics, such as recursive reasoning, network contagion, or sustained temporal adaptation. The trait-to-affect projection also uses a linear weight matrix W, although relationships between personality and affect may be non-linear. Critics caution that LLM-based personas may conflate linguistic coherence with psychological realism [43, 49], lack the nuance needed for qualitative analysis [22], or reflect implementation-specific artifacts rather than generalizable behavior [56]. Accordingly, all experiments used the same LLM backend (GPT-4o-mini) to isolate the effects of hierarchical conditioning. For extensive cross-model evaluation, we refer the reader to original AURORA model, which forms the basis of this work [30]. At the same time, related work shows that structured and empirically grounded agent architectures can exhibit meaningful behavioral regularities [2, 3, 8, 37, 55]. Recent research also emphasizes the importance of validation and theoretical grounding in social-science applications [14]. Overall, the results support a middle ground: LLM personas should not replace human subjects, but they can help model collective behavior and test theory-driven hypotheses in controlled, ethical settings.

10.2 Future Work 

Future research should validate the framework across more platforms, cultural contexts, and stimuli beyond politics and mental health. Longitudinal COVID-19 discourse offers a useful benchmark because it captures evolving narratives, shifting trust, and sustained crisis conditions. Future work should also add dynamic network processes, such as opinion diffusion and collective identity formation, and include bias auditing, fairness constraints, and real-world testing in crisis communication, misinformation response, and policy messaging. Because LLM outputs can diverge from empirical patterns, especially for negative emotions such as fear and anger, improved emotional calibration remains a key priority. These steps would strengthen the framework’s robustness, transparency, and ethical grounding.

B COVID-19 Vaccine Emotional Response Validation 

We further validate synthetic persona plausibility using COVID-19 vaccine discourse, comparing emotional reactions to vaccine-effectiveness and vaccine-harm narratives against Facebook URL Shares data. Figure 10 reveals several important similarities and differences between the Facebook reactions and the synthetic personas across vaccine-supportive (“vaccines work”) and vaccine-harm (“vaccines death”) narratives. In both datasets, vaccine-supportive narratives consistently generated stronger positive emotional engagement, particularly through high levels of joy/love reactions and elevated anger toward opposing viewpoints. In contrast, vaccine-harm narratives produced comparatively weaker positive engagement and stronger sadness- and surprise-oriented reactions, suggesting greater uncertainty, distrust, and emotional caution.
