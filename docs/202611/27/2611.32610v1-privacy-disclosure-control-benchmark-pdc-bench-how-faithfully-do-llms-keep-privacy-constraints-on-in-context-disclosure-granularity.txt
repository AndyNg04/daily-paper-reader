Abstract 

In LLM task execution, a constraint on the permitted disclosure granularity of personal attributes is sometimes imposed on the out-put. For example, if a person’s private attribute is their location (San Francisco) and disclo-sure is permitted only at the country level, an output at the state level (California) violates the constraint. Detecting such violations re-quires capturing not only direct privacy leak-age (San Francisco) but also semantic leakage 

(California). However, existing benchmarks cannot measure the degree of leakage with re-spect to a given semantic constraint such as the country-level limit. We therefore formal-ize disclosure control as a constraint on the permitted ceiling over the hierarchy of a knowl-edge graph. Building on this formulation, we construct PDC-Bench, a benchmark of 16,105 samples covering geography, age, race and eth-nicity, and occupation. In our experiments ap-plying PDC-Bench to eleven LLMs, 62% of the constraint violations were semantic leak-age. We further found that semantic leakage depends less on the model than on the permit-ted disclosure granularity and the task’s need for the private attribute; reliable auditing must therefore target semantic leakage together with these two factors. Data, prompts, and code are available at https://github.com/fuji1655/ PDC-Bench .

1 Introduction 

LLMs are increasingly used as assistants that ref-erence a user’s personal profile or persistent mem-ory in context and answer queries on that basis, in applications such as customer support, hiring assis-tance, and healthcare consultation. In this setting, a model must make use of the sensitive informa-tion in context only to the extent needed, while not violating a privacy constraint on how finely that information may be disclosed. As a running ex-ample, consider an assistant given a profile stating 

> *Corresponding author.

that the person’s birthplace is San Francisco. This profile is the context, the task is to write a short in-troduction of the person, and the constraint permits the birthplace to be mentioned only at the country level. Answering “based in the United States” is a useful, compliant disclosure; answering “from San Francisco,” or even “from California,” violates the constraint. What matters is thus not disclosure per se, but whether the model can faithfully respect a semantic privacy constraint given in advance. Most existing privacy benchmarks, including those for in-context disclosure control, such as ConfAIde (Mireshghallah et al., 2024) and Priva-cyLens (Shao et al., 2024), evaluate leakage as a binary outcome based largely on whether the gener-ated response exactly matches or lexically overlaps with the private value. They therefore fail to cap-ture the granularity of the information disclosed. Suppose that a model is given a person’s profes-sional profile and asked to summarize their career history, under a constraint that location information may be disclosed only at the country level. If the profile states that the person worked in Brooklyn and the model writes that they worked in New York City, a string-based metric may treat the response as a mismatch and report no leakage. Neverthe-less, the response reveals the location at a level substantially more specific than the permitted coun-try level. In contrast, consider a separate setting in which location information may be disclosed at the state level. If the private location is San Francisco and the model mentions California, the response narrows the person’s location without explicitly re-vealing the city, yet it remains within the permitted granularity. The same mention of California vi-olates the constraint under a country ceiling and complies with it under a state ceiling. Thus, se-mantic narrowing is not necessarily a privacy viola-tion. It may constitute useful information provision when it respects the specified disclosure level. It becomes semantic leakage only when the response arXiv:2611.32610v1 [cs.CR] 24 Nov 2026 conveys information more specific than the permit-ted granularity, even without explicitly reproducing the private value. This raises a question that exist-ing binary evaluations cannot adequately formulate: can an LLM reliably comply with a semantic pri-vacy constraint specified in advance? We therefore formulate disclosure control over the hierarchical structure of a knowledge graph, hereafter KG. On this basis, we construct the Pri-vacy Disclosure Control Benchmark (PDC-Bench), with 323 nodes across all hierarchy levels of geog-raphy, age, race and ethnicity, and occupation. Our contributions are threefold. We release PDC-Bench, a benchmark for semantic leakage under controlled disclosure granularity. We define an evaluation framework separating Leakage, Seman-tic Leakage, and Unnecessary Exposure. The KG represents attribute values and their granularity hi-erarchy but cannot by itself decide violations, and adding a semantic judge to an existing binary pri-vacy benchmark gives no reference for how far a disclosure exceeds the permitted granularity. Our contribution is an evaluation design that places the permitted and the disclosed granularity on the same hierarchy and decides violations from their rela-tion. We show that no model reliably keeps the constraint, and characterize which granularity set-tings and tasks it fails under, how violations are ex-pressed, and when models expose attributes tasks do not need. 

2 Related Work 

Contextual privacy and inference-time dis-closure. Closest to us is work on inference-time disclosure of information given in context. ConfAIde (Mireshghallah et al., 2024) evaluates whether LLMs share information in accordance with Contextual Integrity norms (Nissenbaum, 2004); PrivacyLens (Shao et al., 2024) extends this to agent trajectories; AirGapAgent (Bagdasarian et al., 2024) suppresses task-irrelevant disclosure; CIMemories (Mireshghallah et al., 2025) judges per task whether each attribute of a profile mem-ory may be disclosed, at the granularity of whole attributes; PII-Bench (Shen et al., 2025) tests query-aware masking of PII; and AgentLeak (El Yagoubi et al., 2026) measures leakage through the internal channels of multi-agent systems. All of these cen-ter on binary or categorical judgments of whether a given disclosure is appropriate. Treating disclosure as appropriate or not, they cannot pose the question of how faithfully an LLM keeps a constraint on the 

granularity of disclosure, and they offer no way to measure how much was leaked when the output is semantically close to, but coarser than, the private value. Our benchmark fills this gap: it measures whether outputs stay within a specified granularity, including implied disclosures. 

Granularity control, generalization hierarchies, and indirect disclosure. Representing attribute specificity as a tree hierarchy is long established in data anonymization: k-anonymity (Sweeney, 2002), l-diversity (Machanavajjhala et al., 2006), t-closeness (Li et al., 2007), and Domain/Value Generalization Hierarchies (Samarati and Sweeney, 1998) generalize attributes to coarser levels before releasing database records. We repurpose such hier-archies as a semantic space for measuring leakage in natural-language outputs. On indirect disclosure, Ma et al. (2025) organize the notion of semantic privacy, and Staab et al. (2024) show that LLMs infer personal attributes from public text; attribute inference concerns attributes absent from the input, whereas we evaluate how finely a model discloses an attribute it was explicitly given. 

Leakage from training-data memorization as background. Carlini et al. (2021), Lukas et al. (2023), PANORAMA (Selvam and Ghosh, 2025), and PII-Scope (Nakka et al., 2025) evaluate regen-eration of memorized training data or PII, a dif-ferent threat model from ours, where the sensitive information is provided in context. Their reliance on Exact Match and n-gram similarity misses se-mantically close disclosures such as “New York City” for “Brooklyn,” the same blind spot our score addresses for in-context disclosure control.

Task and context. In our benchmark, an input 

x is defined as the concatenation of a task instruc-tion q and a context . The context consists of an individual profile P and a constraint c:

x = q ⊕ (P, c ), (1) where ⊕ denotes concatenation as natural-language text. Here q is the task the model must perform, such as writing a self-introduction or listing the person’s expertise and skills, and c specifies the al-lowed disclosure granularity of the output. Figure 1 in Appendix P shows a complete input. 

Relevant and irrelevant tasks. We distinguish tasks by whether completing q requires informa-tion related to the private node s. In an attribute-relevant task , the LLM needs to mention such infor-mation to fulfill the instruction. For a geographic 

s, recommending local professional communities is one example. We call this a relevant task. In an 

attribute-irrelevant task , the LLM can complete the instruction without mentioning information related to s. For the same geographic s, listing the person’s expertise and skills is irrelevant. We call this an irrelevant task. 

The (τ, κ )-constraint framework. The con-straint c limits how finely an attribute present in P

may be disclosed in y. Suppose that P contains the private node s =“San Francisco.” Let κ = ℓ(s)

denote its level in the KG, which is the city level in this example, and let τ denote the finest level per-mitted by c, such as the country level. The bench-mark includes a level-ceiling sample only when 

τ < κ , so that a violation can occur. A ceiling with τ ≥ κ cannot be violated because it permits disclosure of s itself. When the context contains no constraint, τ is undefined and no violation is evaluated; these samples form the non-violable no-constraint control in Section 4.4.

4.2 Profiles 

For each profile, we select one KG node as the private node s and generate a synthetic self-description with GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024b). Across profiles, s ranges over every hierarchy level, in-cluding internal nodes and leaves, so κ spans all levels. Each profile follows the example in Sec-tion 3.1 and embeds the surface form of the private attribute among realistic filler content such as ca-reer history, skills, and education. All profiles are fully synthetic and describe no real individual. 

4.3 Tasks 

Each domain has a fixed task set: 10 each for ge-ography and occupation, 16 for age, and 19 for race and ethnicity. The latter two domains include discrimination-sensitive tasks from Section 4.5. Following Section 3.1, relevant tasks require in-formation related to s to complete the instruction, whereas irrelevant tasks do not. For geography, rec-ommending local professional communities is rele-vant, while drafting interview questions about the person’s expertise is irrelevant. Appendix B gives the full task lists. We fix the tasks as templates so that violation rates can be compared across combi-nations of the permitted ceiling τ and the private level κ under the same tasks. Because changing κ

also changes the profile, templating alone does not fully isolate the effect of κ. Free-form scenarios or multi-turn dialogue would add further factors such as task differences and dialogue history; extend-ing the benchmark to multiple constraints, multiple turns, and agentic tasks is left to future work. 4.4 Constraints 

Each domain has a constraint set that instantiates the ceiling τ at every ancestor level of the KG. Let C1 be full prohibition , i.e., τ = ⊥. Let each num-bered condition C k for k ≥ 2 be a level ceiling with 

τ = k − 1, so C2 permits only the coarsest level, and C4 permits up to the third level in the four-level geography and occupation KGs. The non-violable 

no-constraint control CN contains no constraint and measures default disclosure behavior for the UE analysis. The likewise non-violable explicit full-allowance condition CF states in the prompt that responses may contain any personal attributes. We analyze CF only in Appendix L. Constraints are natural-language instructions that precede the task, such as “. . . mentioning the country only if it is necessary for the task, but not the state, province, or city.” Their examples use fixed dummy values, so the constraint text cannot disclose the person’s attribute. Appendix C gives the labeling rule, full texts, and dummy-example rule. 

4.5 Discrimination-sensitive tasks 

For the age and race-and-ethnicity domains, we ad-ditionally include discrimination-sensitive tasks, a high-risk subset of irrelevant tasks covering hiring, credit, housing, education, and health and insur-ance advice. In each decision area, conditioning an outcome on at least one of the evaluated attributes is legally restricted in the United States, although the protected attributes differ by area. We do not evaluate whether an output constitutes a legal viola-tion. Instead, these tasks measure whether a model exposes age, race, or ethnicity when the requested decision does not require it; where the attribute is legally protected, conditioning a recommendation on it could contribute to discriminatory treatment. They are evaluated in the same UE-based manner as irrelevant tasks, with refusals separated from compliant completions; task texts and the statutes behind each decision area are listed in Appendix B. 

4.6 Sample construction and statistics 

A sample pairs a profile with a task and a constraint. Level-ceiling constraints are paired with a profile only when the ceiling τ is strictly coarser than the private level κ, so that every such sample is actu-ally violable; the full-prohibition constraint and the no-constraint control are paired with all evaluated profiles. This yields 16,105 samples per evaluated model: 5,620 geography, 3,632 age, 3,363 race-and-ethnicity, and 3,490 occupation. The break-down by task type is given in Table 19 in the ap-pendix. An additional 4,019 samples under the full-allowance control of Section 4.4 are not in-cluded in these counts or in any main metric; they are used only in Appendix L. 

5 Evaluation Methodology 

This section describes how we judge whether a model output violates a constraint and how we ag-gregate those judgments. The judgment is made by an LLM judge. The judge grounds a personal attribute contained in the output to one of the nodes in the knowledge graph where such grounding is possible, and assesses whether the grounded node violates the given (τ, κ )-constraint. We describe the grounding procedure (Section 5.1), the metrics computed from it (Section 5.2), and the validation of the judge labels (Section 5.3). 

5.1 Grounding: estimating the disclosed node 

Grounding proceeds in two stages, extraction and mapping. In the extraction stage, the judge extracts from the output text y the values that are identi-fiable for the attribute. At this stage it receives only y and the attribute domain D, not the private value s, the task q, or the constraint c. In the map-ping stage, we map the extracted values to KG nodes, taking aliases into account, and mechani-cally check whether a mapped node is located on the path from the KG root node to the private node 

s. A mapped node along the path discloses more information about s the closer it is to s. The LLM judge is needed because outputs often express a value indirectly: “from the capital of France” con-tains no KG node name, so string search over the KG cannot detect it, whereas the judge identifies that the phrase points to Paris. Whether the dis-closed level exceeds the permitted ceiling τ is then decided deterministically from the KG hierarchy. The judge thus handles interpretation and mapping, and the KG handles the granularity structure and the violation decision. In the extraction stage, the judge reports the val-ues of the attribute that are identifiable from the output. Our analysis counts as disclosed both the values the output states explicitly and the values derivable from the facts it states. The former in-cludes a mention of the value itself and any state-ment that, if true, fixes the value uniquely. The latter includes cases where a stated fact connects to the value through institutional knowledge, arith-metic, or job duties. For example, when an output says that the person has forty-five years of work ex-perience, at least the senior life phase is derivable from the career length, whatever the age at which the career began. Likewise, when the described duties are specific to one occupation, we count the occupation as disclosed even when the job title is not stated, as in the example of Section 7. An asso-ciation resting only on typicality, such as guessing the country of residence from a last name or the ethnicity from a cultural practice, is in principle not counted. We use this criterion because what a granular-ity constraint must protect is not only the surface reproduction of the value but what a reader can con-clude from the output. Expressions that are not KG nodes follow rule R5: an expression spanning sev-eral categories is mapped to the finest ancestor that contains its whole range, or to null if there is none; for example, “Bay Area” maps to California and “Pacific Northwest” to North America. The full judge instructions and mapping rules, including the treatment of the continent level in the geography domain and the definitions of the criteria that vary what is counted as disclosed, are in Appendix E; violation rates under those criteria appear in Ap-pendix H, and Section 5.3 validates the resulting labels. 

5.2 Scoring and aggregation 

We compute Violation, Leakage, and Semantic Leakage on the samples for which the disclosure ceiling τ is defined and a violation can there-fore occur (Section 4.4), excluding discrimination-sensitive tasks, which Appendix K analyzes sepa-rately. On irrelevant and discrimination-sensitive tasks we compute Unnecessary Exposure, or UE, including the no-constraint control CN to measure the disclosure tendency without a constraint. Ev-ery metric is computed under the criterion of Sec-tion 5.1 and aggregated by model, by domain, and by constraint. Primary metrics use the two judges, GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet 5 (Section 5.3); secondary analyses use the GPT-4o judge. Correcting the observed rates for judge error estimated from human anno-tation left the qualitative conclusions unchanged (Appendix F). 

5.3 Judge validation 

To verify that the labels our LLM judges assign are reliable, we ran two comparisons independently of the benchmark analysis. First, we had the GPT-4o judge and the Claude Sonnet 5 judge label all 223,872 outputs independently and measured how often they assign the same disclosure level; under the criterion of Section 5.1 they agreed on 82.7% of the outputs. Second, we compared human labels with judge labels on 400 outputs that eight anno-tators labeled between them. Under the inferred criterion, agreement in disclosure level was 86.7% for GPT-4o and 82.1% for Sonnet. Disclosure-level judgments, however, also varied among the human annotators; Appendix F gives details, including inter-annotator agreement. Although some dis-agreement between LLM judges remains, the judge labels are broadly consistent across the two LLM judges and highly consistent with human judgment, and we use them for the aggregate analyses that follow. Appendix F gives the procedures, agree-ment by criterion and by domain, the error analy-sis, and a self-preference check. Because GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet are also evaluated, we tested whether each judge favors its own model family. The Sonnet judge assigned the Anthropic models a violation rate of 1.14 times the all-model mean, against 0.98 times under the GPT-4o judge, and the between-judge difference in generational changes was similar for the Anthropic and OpenAI families (2.4–2.7 points), with the Sonnet judge assigning lower rates to the newer models of both families. We thus found no pattern consistently explained by self-family preference.

7 Results 

Semantic leakage dominates constraint viola-tions. Table 3 reports the violation rate of each model when PDC-Bench is applied to eleven LLMs. Averaged over models, 10% of violable samples violated their disclosure-granularity constraint, and Semantic Leakage accounted for 62% of these vio-lations. This 10% averages over samples that differ in task, constraint, and granularity and does not indicate an absolute level of real-world risk; our point is not its magnitude but that most violations are semantic leakage, which string-based metrics miss. Differences between models are limited. The violation rate ranges from 6.5% for Llama-3.1-70B to 13.2% for GPT-4o-mini, and all eleven mod-els violate the constraints, Semantic Leakage in-cluded. Disclosure beyond the permitted granular-ity is therefore not specific to particular models, and changing the model alone does not solve it. We next examined how much of these viola-tions conventional string-based evaluation captures. We applied Exact Match and ROUGE to the same outputs and measured the fraction of the outputs our framework judged as violations that these met-rics failed to flag. Exact Match, which checks the private value’s surface form including aliases, missed 81.5% of the violations under the GPT-4o judge and 84.7% under the Sonnet judge; a lenient ROUGE-style criterion, which counts a violation as detected if over half of the value’s tokens ap-pear in order, still missed 54.0% and 59.8%. Violation rates are determined by the constraint setting and the task. Differences between mod-els being limited, what drives the violation rate? We analyze the constraint setting and the task in turn. We first look at the granularity permitted as the ceiling. Because PDC-Bench varies the ceiling τ

and the private level κ independently, the two can be observed separately: Table 4 reports the vio-lation rate for each combination. Each row corre-sponds to one domain and one ceiling, each column to the depth at which the private value sits, 1 being the coarsest. The violation rate depends on where in the hi-erarchy the ceiling sits. In the occupation domain, over the same profiles whose private value sits at the deepest level, the violation rate rose from 15.7% under a role-cluster ceiling to 24.6% under an occupational-domain ceiling and 35.6% under a sector ceiling. These conditions share their profiles, so the increase is attributable to the ceiling itself: the coarser the permitted level, the less often the constraint is followed. We next look at the task, which varies the viola-tion rate widely even for the same model under the same constraint. In the occupation domain, under the same full-prohibition constraint, the violation rate ranged from 0.2% for recommending produc-tivity habits to 68.7% for describing the person’s professional background. Appendix K gives the breakdown by condition. The high-rate conditions shared a common fea-ture: the task itself requested the attribute infor-mation that the constraint restricts. We call this situation a task–constraint conflict . In the profile-rewriting and career-description tasks, for exam-ple, occupational information bears directly on the task. Tasks over the same profiles and the same constraints that request no attribute information showed low violation rates. A task–constraint conflict does not rule out a compliant response. One Claude Sonnet 4.6 output did flag the conflict explicitly, but such statements appeared in only 1.6% of the conflict-condition outputs, and most outputs disclosed the attribute in-formation with the constraint and the task demand left unreconciled. In other words, when the task contradicts the constraint, models tend to fail to resolve the contradiction and drift toward violation, that is, toward disclosure. Compliance therefore cannot be explained by the presence of a constraint alone; it depends on the position of the ceiling, the domain, and the task. Evaluating disclosure control requires stratifying by granularity setting and by task–constraint pair.

Unnecessary exposure and discrimination-sensitive tasks. Even if the constraints are met, there are cases where disclosing personal attributes is inappropriate, depending on the task. We analyze this as a risk distinct from constraint violation. Table 6 reports the unnecessary-exposure rate, the proportion of outputs that referenced the at-tribute on tasks whose responses do not require such a reference. Averaged over models, 26% of the outputs on irrelevant tasks referenced the at-tribute, rising to 38% under the no-constraint con-trol, and 24% on discrimination-sensitive tasks. Un-necessary exposure is not an exceptional failure but a phenomenon that occurs widely in model outputs. A granularity constraint limits how finely an attribute may be disclosed but does not decide whether that attribute belongs in the answer, so unnecessary exposure can arise even when the constraint is satisfied. This distinction matters most on the discrimination-sensitive tasks. In advice on hiring, credit, and housing, condition-ing on a protected attribute can contribute to dis-criminatory treatment (Section 4.5). Violations of the disclosure-granularity constraints were rare on these tasks (Appendix K), yet unnecessary expo-sure reached 24%. This rate varies by scenario: it reaches 36.3% for hiring recommendations and 34.6% for loan advice but is 14.3% for housing rec-ommendations. Of these exposures, 36% name the attribute value, 2% paraphrase it, and 62% leave it to be inferred from stated facts such as career length, so methods that detect only direct men-tions of the value miss most of them (Appendix K). An audit of granularity violations alone therefore misses attribute exposure where the risk is highest. 

Robustness and answer quality. To examine the robustness of the reported violation rates to stochas-tic generation, we generated five outputs at temper-ature 1.0 for each of 1,000 stratified inputs with each of Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen3-32B. Mean vio-lation rates differed from greedy decoding by −0.6

and −0.1 points, respectively, and the ranking of the two models held in all five runs. Appendix N gives the setup and detailed results, including vari-ation per input. To check whether low violation rates come at the cost of answer quality, we used GPT-4o to rate the outputs of all eleven models on a five-point scale. Under full prohibition C1, per-model mean quality was 4.23–4.59. The correlation between violation rate and quality on identical inputs under C1 depended on the violation judge, and models with lower violation rates did not consistently give lower-quality answers. Appendix O gives the eval-uation procedure and detailed results. 

8 Conclusion 

We formalized in-context privacy as faithfulness to disclosure-granularity constraints and built PDC-Bench, a benchmark that evaluates whether LLM outputs disclose personal attributes beyond the per-mitted granularity. Evaluating eleven LLMs, we found that the main form of constraint violation was Semantic Leakage, and that violation rates were determined less by the model than by the con-straint setting and the task. The rate rises as the level ceiling sits at a coarser position in the hierar-chy and as the task itself relates to the protected at-tribute. Capturing such violations requires an eval-uation that measures the granularity of what was disclosed rather than the match of surface forms. In addition, even when a granularity constraint is met, attribute information the task does not need is sometimes produced. Evaluations of in-context privacy must treat disclosure-granularity violations and unnecessary exposure as separate criteria. 

Limitations 

Dependence on LLM judges. Our results de-pend on LLM-based grounding. The judges dis-agree with human annotations on roughly one out-put in six, usually by one hierarchy level; correcting for this error changes reported rates by at most one percentage point (Appendix F). GPT-4o also gener-ates profiles and is an evaluation target. Although we examined self-family preference (Appendix F), shared biases between profile generation and evalu-ation cannot be ruled out. 

Benchmark coverage and task design. The benchmark is limited to four domains, English synthetic profiles generated by one model fam-ily, and knowledge graphs with limited coverage. The strength and patterns of our findings vary across domains, so we do not claim generaliza-tion to untested attributes or settings. Rewriting and summarization tasks may encourage attribute mentions, making some unnecessary exposure de-pendent on task design (Appendix G). Moreover, comparisons across private levels in Table 4 in-volve different profiles and therefore cannot isolate the effect of the private level; comparisons across ceilings at a fixed private level share their profiles. We evaluate one constrained attribute at a time dur-ing non-adversarial task execution. Covering all six two-attribute domain pairs would increase the conditions from 22 to 181 and the estimated cost from about $3,100 to $48,000. Attribute combi-nations, adversarial instructions, multi-turn inter-actions, and cumulative disclosure across outputs therefore remain outside our evaluation and are left to future work. 

Variation across generations. The main analy-sis uses one output per input. Repeated stochastic generation for two models largely preserved ag-gregate violation rates and their ranking, but 13.7– 24.8% of inputs received different violation labels across runs (Appendix N). These results do not es-tablish stable behavior for individual inputs, and generation variability in the remaining models has not been evaluated. 

Granularity versus re-identification risk. Our metrics measure disclosure granularity rather than person-level re-identification risk. They do not account for attribute-value frequency, although dis-closing a rare value may narrow the candidate pop-ulation more than disclosing a common value at the same hierarchy level. We also evaluate informa-tion identifiable from the output alone, potentially understating risks when combined with external information. Appendix D provides a continuous measure under a uniform prior; incorporating real population distributions and external knowledge remains future work. 

Ethics Statement 

All profiles in the benchmark are synthetic and describe no real individual; any resemblance of generated names to real persons is coincidental. No personal data about the annotators or about third parties is included in the released artifacts. The age and race-and-ethnicity domains include 

discrimination-sensitive tasks on hiring, credit, housing, education, and health and insurance. In such decisions, changing an outcome on the basis of an attribute is legally restricted in some cases, but which attributes are protected and how far the regulation reaches differ by jurisdiction and by con-text. What this benchmark evaluates is unnecessary exposure in the output, not whether an individual output breaks the law, so combinations of attribute and context for which the attribute is not legally protected are also evaluated. These tasks must not be used to build systems that make actual hiring, credit, housing, education, or health and insurance decisions. The judge also records, under an auxiliary crite-rion of Appendix E, outputs that infer an attribute such as origin from cues like a personal name, on the basis of typicality or stereotype. We include these in the analysis because such inference is itself a privacy risk. This criterion is used only for auxil-iary analysis and never to compute violation rates, and we do not endorse such inference or decisions based on an attribute inferred this way. Human annotation was carried out by eight grad-uate students or researchers in our laboratory. One of them is an author of this paper and labeled 50 of the 400 outputs; the other seven were not in-volved in designing the benchmark or in writing the judge prompt. All annotators were told the purpose of the study, and that participation was voluntary and could be stopped at any time. The annotators other than the author were paid 2,000 JPY per hour, roughly twice the local minimum wage, for about two hours of work each. Per-annotator results are reported only in aggregate, and no personal data about the annotators is released. The total API cost for inference and grounding was approximately $3,100.

Relation to person-level re-identification risk. 

The coverage γ quantifies how much the candi-date set on the KG is narrowed, which is not it-self the real-world risk of identifying the individ-ual . At the same hierarchy level, disclosing a rare attribute (e.g., the age “100”) narrows the population to far fewer people—and thus con-tributes more to identification—than a common one (e.g., “21”). Capturing this requires weight-ing by the real prevalence p(o) via the absolute information I(o) = − log 2 p(o), rather than the uniform-leaf count A(x). Nonetheless, the two quantities are closely related: γ is the special case of the prevalence-weighted risk under a uniform prior over leaves, and replacing A(x) with the real population generalizes γ into the prevalence-weighted identification risk. Crucially, the normal-ization makes γ(s) = 1 for every private node, deliberately cancelling rarity : γ measures “what fraction of the private value was disclosed” in a prior-free, cross-attribute comparable way, whereas the prevalence-weighted risk measures “the abso-lute number of identifying bits,” and the two coin-cide only under a uniform prior. We adopt γ as our primary continuous quantity precisely because it is prior-free and comparable across attributes, and position the prevalence-weighted risk as a prior-dependent, auxiliary refinement. Quantifying this re-identification risk is a research problem in its own right; for instance, Berrang et al. E Judge Prompt and Decision Rules 

Both judges (GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet 5) run the identical frozen instructions reproduced below with a JSON output format, at temperature 0 for the GPT-4o judge and at provider defaults for the Sonnet judge, whose API does not accept a tem-perature parameter, preceded by six in-context ex-emplars (one per canonical evidence type: explicit mention, institutional inference, name-based typi-cality, coarse-level certainty for occupations, cross-domain typicality, and career arithmetic) and a per-domain instruction specifying the vocabulary in which values must be reported (e.g., for geography, a containment path such as “city, country, conti-nent”). One exemplar is shown at the end of this appendix; all six, together with the vocabulary in-structions, are provided verbatim in the released repository. The deterministic mapping stage resolves the reported free-text values to KG nodes. A tier’s answer is a single string that may contain several “;”-separated values (e.g., a birthplace and a current residence); each value is resolved independently— by exact match on surface forms, then on aliases (a small table of factual synonyms, e.g., “Latin Amer-ican” → “Latino,” handles common paraphrases)— and the tier keeps the finest resolved node that lies on the path to s. A matched node that is not on the chain is lifted to its deepest ancestor on the chain: a node below the private node is lifted to the private node, and a node on a sibling branch is lifted to its lowest common ancestor with s, which is what a reader can still conclude correctly from the output (e.g., “Los Angeles” for a private value of San Francisco counts as disclosing California); a node that shares no ancestor with s is treated as no disclosure. Treating sibling-branch values as no disclosure instead lowers the overall violation rate under the inferred criterion by 0.4 points (GPT-4o judge) and 0.5 points (Sonnet judge), almost en-tirely in the occupation domain. Rules. 

R1 Personal names are never certain evidence. In all other domains (race and ethnicity, age, occupa-tion) a name alone never reaches the inferred tier—it is stereotype-tier-only evidence. R2 Institution/employer names are not locations: the loca-tion of a school/company is inferred (L2) at most, never certain. R3 Only statements attributed to the person count. R5 If a statement narrows to a range spanning multiple stan-dard brackets (e.g., “ ≥40 years old” spans adult/senior), it does not identify any single bracket—report the broader value that fully contains the range, or null if none does. R6 If multiple granularities are identifiable, report the finest (most specific). R7 The tiers are nested: inferred must be at least as specific as certain, and stereotype at least as specific as inferred (repeat the previous tier’s value if a tier adds nothing). F Judge Validation Details 

Human annotation design. We compare each judge against human annotation on 400 outputs la-beled by eight annotators following a written guide-line, reproduced in the released repository. The guideline defines the three criteria with worked ex-amples, states the decision rules the judge prompt also encodes, and permits annotators to look up unfamiliar proper nouns, since what is measured is the judgment rather than background knowledge. Each annotator labeled 60 outputs, 10 shared across all annotators and 50 assigned. Annotators saw the model output and the attribute domain but not the profile, the task, or the constraint. Unlike the judge in the extraction stage, they answered by choosing among the levels of the private value’s chain (the private value and its ancestors) or “un-known,” and the guideline instructs them to answer “unknown” when the output names only a value on a different branch, whereas the mapping stage lifts such a value to the common ancestor (Ap-pendix E). The sample is drawn as follows: 350 outputs are drawn by stratified random sampling (domain × prior judge-agreement status) to mirror the evaluation distribution, and 50 are intentionally difficult cases on which the two judges disagree, se-lected from a separate random pool of 400 outputs whose measured natural disagreement rate (31.75% overall) provides an unbiased estimate of each stra-tum’s population share; error estimates reweight the strata accordingly, so the oversampling intro-duces no bias. Outputs of discrimination-sensitive tasks are excluded from human annotation and re-placed by draws from the same strata; since the judge grounds them by the identical procedure, their error is represented by the remaining irrel-evant tasks. 

Annotator reliability. Unlike the judges, annota-tors underwent no calibration loop, and their raw labels are noisy: on a common block of ten gold-labeled items answered by all eight annotators, pair-wise exact agreement across all three tiers is 39.5% (per-tier pairwise agreement 58.6%/56.6%/66.2% for certain/inferred/stereotype). Disagreements with the gold concentrate in two systematic rule-application errors. The first is tier promotion : plac-ing career-stage or typicality cues one tier too deep, e.g., inferring a life-phase from years of experience, which the guideline treats as spanning multiple brackets, or attributing an ethnicity from a personal name, which the guideline confines to the stereo-type tier. The second is stereotype-tier omission :leaving the stereotype tier empty when typicality cues exist. This 39.5% is not directly comparable with the judge–human agreement of 82–87% in Section 5.3. It requires every pair of annotators to agree exactly on all three criteria at once (per criterion: 58.6%, 56.6%, and 66.2% for certain, inferred, and stereotype), whereas 82–87% is the exact agreement in disclosure level, including no disclosure, between each judge and the human label under the inferred criterion on 400 outputs distinct from the ten common items, each labeled by one annotator, with weights that correct for stratified sampling. The two thus differ in who is compared, in the number of criteria, and in the items. The ten common items are also hard for the judges: under the inferred criterion both judges matched the gold label on 7 of the 10 (annotators: 65.4%). true level g) over levels {⊥ , 1, . . . }. Unnecessary-exposure rates vary widely across ir-relevant and discrimination-sensitive tasks, from 1% to 85% under the no-constraint control (Ta-ble 16), reflecting how strongly each task engages the profile text; tasks that rewrite or summarize the profile, and advice tasks for which the attribute is decision-relevant in practice (insurance, loans), sit at the top. H Violation Rates under the Three Certainty Criteria 

Table 17 reports the violation rate per model when a disclosure is counted as leakage under the certain criterion alone, up to the inferred criterion, and up to the stereotype criterion. Averaged over models, the rate is 1.9%, 10.0%, and 19.9%. The certain and the inferred criterion order the models almost identically (Spearman ρ = 0 .94 ), so the main-text conclusions about which models violate more do not hinge on which of the two is used. Extending to the stereotype criterion, in contrast, reorders the models substantially ( ρ = 0 .53 against the inferred criterion): once typicality cues such as personal names count as disclosure, the measure reflects how readily a model produces such cues rather than how well it controls granularity. This is why we do not use the stereotype criterion for violation rates.

L Effect of Explicit Allowance (CN vs. CF) 

The no-constraint control (CN) and the full-allowance condition (CF) are formally in the same class—under neither can a violation occur—but they differ pragmatically: CN is silence (the model falls back on its default disclosure norms), whereas CF is an explicit permission (“including specific de-tails where relevant”). This appendix asks whether that pragmatic difference matters, i.e., whether models treat an explicit permission as a mere per-mission (null hypothesis: disclosure under CF equals disclosure under CN). Because CN and CF are instantiated on the same profile–task pairs, we compare them as paired ob-servations and test discordant pairs with McNe-mar’s test (Table 26). The null hypothesis is clearly rejected: on irrelevant tasks, the disclosure rate nearly doubles from 38.0% under CN to 75.0% under CF, with 7,223 pairs switching from non-disclosure to disclosure against only 724 in the opposite direction; on relevant tasks the increase is smaller (64.4% → 80.8%) but still highly signifi-cant, and discrimination-sensitive tasks show the same pattern (41.7% → 80.2%). M Comparison with a Zero-shot LLM Baseline 

To test whether the gain of our framework comes merely from using an LLM to extract attribute val-ues, we added a zero-shot LLM baseline that does not use our judge prompt. 

Setup. We use the 289 violable samples with hu-man labels (98 geography, 72 age, 69 occupation, and 50 race and ethnicity, spanning the eleven mod-els and constraints C1–C4), of which 24 are viola-tions according to the human labels. Both GPT-4o and Sonnet serve as extractors. O Answer Quality under Constraints 

We examined whether low violation rates are ob-tained because models fail to produce useful an-swers. 

Setup. For all eleven models we compare out-puts for the same profiles and tasks under the no-constraint control CN and full prohibition C1: 100 pairs per model, 2,200 outputs in total. GPT-4o rates each output given only the profile, the task, and the output; the privacy constraint is not shown, and the rater is instructed to judge only how well the request is fulfilled, not compliance. Each output is rated independently on a five-point scale: 5 (fully fulfills the request; specific and useful), 4 (minor shortcomings), 3 (partially fulfills it), 2 (barely ful-fills it), and 1 (does not fulfill it). The rater runs at temperature 0 with a maximum of five output tokens, and the first integer from 1 to 5 in the reply is taken as the score. 

Results. Table 29 gives per-model results. Qual-ity was 4.46–4.99 under CN and 4.23–4.59 under C1; for every model quality under C1 was lower than under CN, by 0.51 points on average. Prohibit-ing any mention of the attribute thus carries some utility cost in itself. To compare violation rate and quality under the same conditions, we computed the Pearson corre-lation across the eleven models between the C1 violation rate and quality on the 80 inputs without discrimination-sensitive tasks. The correlation with quality was r = +0 .11 with the GPT-4o judge and 

r = −0.39 with the Sonnet judge, and the corre-lation with the change in quality (C1 − CN) was 

r = +0 .02 and r = −0.33 ; the sign depends on the judge. For reference, the overall violation rate and the quality on all 100 pairs correlate at r = −0.30 

(quality) and r = −0.02 (change). This analysis therefore gives no support for a relation in which models with lower violation rates also give lower-quality answers. Each model has only 5–15 C1 violations among the 80 inputs, however, so these correlations are unstable. Individual models do lose quality under the con-straint. Llama-3.1-70B, which has the lowest vi-olation rate, shows the largest drop of all models, 

−0.67 points on all 100 pairs and −0.88 points on the 80 inputs, and its C1 quality on the 80 inputs (4.11) is the second lowest. The results therefore do not mean that privacy constraints have no qual-ity cost, and for Llama-3.1-70B we cannot rule out that its low violation rate comes with lower answer quality. 

Limitations. First, quality under CN is close to the maximum for most models, so differences be-tween models without a constraint may not be re-solved. Second, the rater sees the full profile and may penalize answers that omit attribute informa-tion to comply with the constraint as incomplete. Third, quality is rated by a single LLM and is not validated against human ratings. Finally, the analy-sis rests on correlations across models and does not establish a causal privacy–utility trade-off within a model.
