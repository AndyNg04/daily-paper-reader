# Abstract 

Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly used for embodied perception and reasoning, yet their physical un-derstanding is largely evaluated on questions answerable from images. Many properties critical to manipulation, such as an opaque bottle’s fill level or an object’s com-pliance, may only be revealed through touch. We intro-duce TouchBench , an egocentric vision-language-tactile benchmark that distinguishes visually answerable questions (Tier A ) from those involving properties hidden from the camera ( Tier B ). It pairs head-mounted video with synchro-nized 137-channel whole-hand tactile sensing, comprising 900 clips from five participants across 11 tasks, with 900 absolute and 4,102 relative questions. Four state-of-the-art VLMs average 66 .4% accuracy on Tier A relative ques-tions but only 49 .8% on Tier B, near chance ( 50% ). To bridge this gap, we propose Tactile-as-Prompt (TaP), which presents 34 interpretable tactile statistics as text, enabling VLMs to reason with touch without a learned tactile en-coder. TaP raises zero-shot Tier B relative accuracy to 

62 .9% and reaches 73 .1% after leave-one-user-out fine-tuning, outperforming video alone ( 67 .4% ) and a learned tactile-token encoder ( 67 .3% ). The gains extend to unseen objects and users. On a separate 20-hour in-the-wild cor-pus, tactile text improves fine-tuned contact-question accu-racy from 67 .1% to 75 .5% . These results highlight the limits of vision-only physical reasoning and establish language as an effective interface for tactile perception. 

# 1. Introduction 

Vision-language models (VLMs) are becoming the percep-tion and reasoning component of embodied systems, from instruction-following manipulation policies [4, 13, 21, 49] to embodied question answering [10, 29]. Their evaluation, however, remains centered on the camera: benchmarks of physical and intuitive reasoning [1, 2, 8, 32, 39, 40] pose

questions that are answerable from the visual input by con-struction. Yet many properties that determine how an object should be handled cannot be recovered from an image. Two identical bottles differ in content only when lifted, two balls of the same color differ in compliance only when squeezed and the texture of a surface disappears once the hand covers it. Humans resolve these through touch (Fig. 1). This raises two questions. Do current VLMs fail on such properties, or do contextual cues let them answer anyway? And if they fail, in what form should a whole-hand tactile signal enter the model? A low score on a hard benchmark cannot settle the first question, because it may reflect a missing percept or merely a difficult task. We therefore build the control into the data. Every question in TouchBench , our benchmark for vision-language-tactile models (VLTMs), is labeled by visual an-swerability: in Tier A the queried property is visible in the video, and in Tier B the capture hides it, for example be-hind an opaque wall or under the hand. When a task spans both tiers, the attribute, question and options are shared and 1arXiv:2611.26883v1 [cs.CV] 19 Nov 2026 only the capture condition differs (e.g., a transparent ver-sus an opaque bottle). A model that is accurate on Tier A but at chance on Tier B therefore points to a missing per-cept rather than to task difficulty. The benchmark pairs ego-centric video with a synchronized 137-channel tactile glove over 900 clips from 5 participants, 11 tasks and 6 attribute dimensions, with absolute questions about one clip and rel-ative comparisons between two clips. Four state-of-the-art VLMs average 66 .4% on Tier A rel-ative comparisons but only 49 .8% on Tier B, where chance is 50% , and the gap persists when both tiers are restricted to the same tasks. Near-chance accuracy does not mean ran-dom answers: on Tier B, three of the four models choose the second clip in 71% to 82% of comparisons and the fourth in 

55% , although the correct answers are balanced. For the second question, the conventional approach trains an encoder that maps tactile signals into the model’s embedding space [14, 19, 38, 41], which requires paired data that is scarce for dexterous manipulation. We in-stead propose Tactile-as-Prompt (TaP): each clip is sum-marized by 34 interpretable statistics of force, contact geometry, vibration and wrist motion, which are written into the text prompt. Like vision-language-action mod-els that expose low-dimensional state to the backbone [3] and work that gives sensor readings to language models as text [18, 20, 36], TaP relies on the language backbone to read physical quantities directly. It needs neither training nor model weights, so it can apply to proprietary models. Averaged over the four models, TaP raises zero-shot Tier B relative accuracy from 49 .8% to 62 .9% , and tactile text alone reaches 68 .7% . With leave-one-user-out (LOUO) fine-tuning, video plus TaP reaches 73 .1% , against 67 .4% 

for video only and 67 .3% for learned tokens, and the gain persists on unseen objects with an unseen user. On 20 hours of public in-the-wild egocentric video, with 17 ,544 evalua-tion questions whose answers are measured from the tactile signal, fine-tuning with tactile text improves accuracy from 

67 .1% to 75 .5% . Our contributions are: 1. TouchBench , an egocentric video-tactile benchmark in which visual answerability is a controlled variable, with 900 absolute and 4,102 relative questions, each labeled Tier A or Tier B. 2. A systematic evaluation of four VLMs showing that they answer near chance when the queried property is hidden from the camera, with Tier A as the control, and that on Tier B their answers depend on clip order. 3. Tactile-as-Prompt , a training-free tactile interface, eval-uated zero-shot, after fine-tuning and on unseen objects and users, compared with a learned-token encoder, other training-free formats and a feature-only classifier, plus tactile text on a 20-hour in-the-wild corpus. 

# 2. Related Work 

Tactile sensing and representation learning Vision-based tactile sensors such as GelSight [42], GelSlim [12] and DIGIT [23] image contact at high resolution, while taxel arrays and sensorized gloves measure pressure across the whole hand [34]. Learned tactile representations predict grasp success [6], fuse vision and touch for control [24], translate between the two modalities [25, 37] and are pre-trained on large unlabeled corpora [19] or bound to vision and language [38]. We compare a learned encoder of this kind with interpretable statistics supplied as prompt text. 

Touch and language models Building on multimodal binding [15], touch-vision-language datasets align tactile images with descriptions [14], unified embeddings connect touch to text and images [38] and tactile-language models reason about object properties from sensor readings [41]. Recent systems extend tactile reasoning to ordinal com-parison and visual-tactile conflict [22], interactive reason-ing [27] and larger multisensor corpora [28]. All of them use learned tactile representations. A separate line of work feeds sensor readings or time series to language models as text [18, 20, 36], composes pretrained models through lan-guage [43], summarizes multisensory robot observations as text for failure reasoning [26] or aligns motion-sensor em-beddings with video and language [30]. Relatedly, vision-language-action models expose proprioceptive state to the backbone [3]. We compare a learned-token interface and a statistics-as-text interface on the same supervised data. Text also enables tactile evaluation of proprietary models. 

Physical and embodied reasoning benchmarks Bench-marks probe physical commonsense in language [2], intu-itive physics from video [1, 32, 39], audiovisual common-sense [40], embodied question answering [10, 29] and phys-ical understanding in VLMs [8]. Their questions are an-swerable from the perceptual input, so a low score cannot separate a missing percept from failed reasoning. Touch-Bench supplies this control by partitioning questions by vi-sual answerability. Touch-R1 [22] uses a complementary control, crediting tactile reasoning only when real tactile in-put outperforms counterfactual inputs. 

Egocentric video and hand-object interaction Egocen-tric corpora such as Ego4D [16], Ego-Exo4D [17] and EPIC-KITCHENS [9] capture manipulation at scale, and hand-object datasets add contact detection [33], grasp cap-ture [7] and per-vertex contact [5, 35]. A few recordings pair egocentric video with tactile gloves [11, 47], and re-cent work learns to predict hand pressure or contact from egocentric video alone [44–46]. # 3. TouchBench 

## 3.1. Data collection 

Five participants manipulated everyday objects while we recorded egocentric video from a head-mounted camera and tactile data from a sensorized glove on the right hand. The glove records 137 channels at about 98 Hz, comprising 132 pressure taxels (60 on the fingertips, 72 on the palm) and 5 per-finger flexion channels. A wrist-mounted inertial measurement unit also reports orientation as a quaternion. Video and glove streams are synchronized in software via a timestamp code displayed in the video. The participants, P1 to P5, contributed 75 minutes of manipulation and 900 syn-chronized clips , each subsampled to 32 frames for model input. 

## 3.2. Tasks and questions 

The eleven tasks are organized into six attribute dimensions (Fig. 2). Scoring the occluded-texture task as two material pairs gives twelve scoring dimensions. Each clip supports two question formats. An absolute question queries a sin-gle clip (e.g., “how tightly is the cap screwed on?”) with two options (four for the elastic-band count). A relative 

question compares two clips from the same dimension (e.g., “which pillow is softer?”) with two options, yielding a 50% 

chance level. The benchmark contains 900 absolute and 

4,102 relative questions. Pairs may span visibility condi-tions within a dimension. These 922 mixed-tier pairs are excluded from tier-specific results (Tab. D1). 

## 3.3. Visual answerability: Tier A and Tier B 

Every question is labeled by whether its target property is visible in the video. In Tier A , the property is visible, such as the texture of an unoccluded surface or the fill level of a transparent bottle. In Tier B , the capture condition hides it, such as the same texture under the hand or the fill level of an opaque bottle. Tiers therefore follow from the capture design and were assigned once per capture subcategory. A capture subcategory is labeled Tier A only when its target property can be determined from the pixels without relying on object identity or another statistical proxy. We audited the subcategories with suspicious zero-shot scores by an-swering their items from the frames, question and options alone. In the Tier A structure pair, where video-only models score below chance, the 8 items that could be judged agree with the gold answer, so neither labels nor wording are in-verted. The semi-transparent amber bottle shows no fill dif-ference in any inspected frame and was moved to Tier B. For the elastic bands, the thickness of the stretched bun-dle is a partial visual cue (13 of 20 pairs judged correctly with low confidence), but the count itself is not visible, so the task stays in Tier B. A visible control pair was judged correctly in all 10 items, and no gold label was changed (Sec. A). Five scoring dimensions (bottle, glove, phone and the two texture pairs) contain both tiers and seven contain only Tier B, so aggregate tier comparisons also reflect task composition. Tier A is a control rather than a target: a model that fails on Tier A fails at the task itself. Conversely, a Tier B label does not prevent a model from learning a visual proxy, such as the thickness of a stretched band bundle, which Sec. 5.2 suggests happens after fine-tuning. 

## 3.4. Evaluation protocol 

We evaluate three input conditions, video only (V), tactile only (T) and both (V+T), in four settings. Zero-shot eval-uation uses all items. Tier-specific relative results use the 3,180 same-tier pairs (543 Tier A and 2,637 Tier B), and absolute items comprise 218 Tier A and 682 Tier B exam-ples. Leave-one-user-out (LOUO) fine-tuning trains on four participants and evaluates on the fifth. Training is capped at 48 pairs per scoring dimension, and no evaluation clip or source segment appears in training. Pooling the five folds yields the same items as the zero-shot rows. The unseen-object setting tests on 225 additional clips that one par-ticipant recorded with new object instances, while training only on the other participants. The web-scale setting uses questions constructed without human annotation (Sec. 5.7). Unless noted otherwise, accuracy is the unweighted mean over capture subcategories (6 in Tier A, 16 in Tier B), with each relative pair attributed to its first clip, so that sub-categories with many pairs do not dominate the average. Item-weighted results for the main comparisons, given in Tab. D1, preserve the pattern. Chance is 50 .0% for rela-tive questions. For absolute questions, chance is 50 .0% on Tier A and 48 .4% on Tier B, because the four-option band count is a Tier B task. Where an answer-wording prior ex-ists (Sec. 5.7), we report it alongside chance. 

# 4. Tactile-as-Prompt 

## 4.1. Problem formulation 

A clip is a pair (V, T ) of 32 RGB frames V = {I1, . . . , I 32 }

and a glove stream T ∈ RL×137 with a wrist quater-nion track Q ∈ RL×4. Given a question q with options 

O, the model predicts an answer ˆa ∈ O . A tactile in-terface maps (T , Q) into a form the language backbone can consume. A learned-token interface trains an encoder 

Eθ : ( T , Q) → Rk×d into the d-dimensional embedding space and must learn θ from paired supervision. Tactile-as-Prompt instead composes a fixed feature map with a fixed serializer, 

σ ϕ(T , Q), ϕ : RL×137 ×RL×4 → R34 , (1) where σ renders the 34 values as one line of text. The prompt is p = [ instruction ; V; σ(ϕ(T , Q)); q; O]. For a relative question, the video and tactile blocks of both clips precede the question. V omits the tactile block and T omits the frames. The question and options remain identical, and the system line specifies the supplied modalities. 

## 4.2. Tactile descriptors 

The 34 statistics are computed per clip from the baseline-subtracted glove channels and the wrist quaternion, in four groups (Fig. 3). Force (14) covers the peak, 95th percentile, mean and spread of total pressure, the peak of each fingertip pad, the fingertip and palm totals and their ratio, and flex-ion. Spatial (8) covers contact area, concentration and the temporal profile of the grasp. Spectral (10) covers Welch band powers between 8 and 49 Hz, the spectral centroid and a jitter term, and Wrist (2) covers wrist stability. Whole-hand totals sum all channels, including the bend channels, 4so they are signal summaries in sensor units rather than cal-ibrated forces. Values are written at fixed precision and in a fixed order with units and plain names, for example “contact area 41 taxels, concentration gini 0.62”, so the same string can be sent to any model. The statistics were designed on a pilot recording of one participant, whose clips later form the P5 fold. Section B gives full definitions. 

## 4.3. A learned-token alternative 

As a conventional alternative, we also train a learned tactile encoder using the same supervised data, back-bone (Qwen3.5-9B with LoRA) and schedule. A 1.79M-parameter temporal convolution encoder maps the glove stream to k=32 tokens, projected through a learned gate into the language embedding space. The encoder is pre-trained via frame-level contrastive alignment with frozen visual features on 50 minutes of our free-form manipula-tion data and the 20-hour public corpus of Sec. 5.7, then fine-tuned with the backbone. Its configuration was selected among fusion, gating, normalization and pre-training vari-ants, so we treat it as a reference baseline rather than a tuned optimum (Sec. C). 

# 5. Experiments 

## 5.1. Setup 

We evaluate InternVL3-8B [48] and Qwen3.5-9B [31], and two proprietary models, Claude Sonnet and Claude Opus ( claude-sonnet-5 and claude-opus-5 ). The InternVL3-8B and Qwen3.5-9B models receive 32 frames per clip and are scored by the option letter with the largest next-token logit, with thinking disabled. The Claude mod-els, queried on 14 October 2026, receive the frames of each clip as two contact sheets in the user message, with all tools disabled, and are prompted for a single letter at de-fault sampling settings. All of their responses are valid. Fine-tuning freezes Qwen3.5-9B and trains LoRA adapters (r=32 , α=64 , dropout 0.05 ) on the attention and MLP pro-jections with AdamW (peak learning rate 2 × 10 −4, linear schedule, 3% warm-up, bfloat16, effective batch size 16), for five epochs on relative and ten on absolute questions, on one H100 per run. Benchmark entries are one run per arm, and unseen-object entries average three seeds (two for tactile only). Sections C and F give details and prompts.

## 5.4. Relative and absolute questions behave differ-ently 

Question format matters as much as modality. On abso-lute Tier B questions, TaP moves the fine-tuned model from 

63 .3% to 66 .5% , a non-significant difference (McNemar 

p = 0 .47 ), and the zero-shot average only from 50 .2% to 

51 .1% . The model uses touch far more effectively to com-pare two clips than to calibrate a judgment: an unnormal-ized peak-force reading identifies the firmer of two balls but not whether a ball is firm. We report this negative re-sult rather than restricting the benchmark to the format on which the method succeeds.

## 5.7. Scaling to annotation-free web data 

To test tactile text at scale and with a different glove, we construct contact questions automatically from 20 hours of in-the-wild manipulation in EgoTouch, a public egocentric tactile corpus released with TouchAnything [47]. A Claude model segments each recording into captioned events, and each question asks which of two events within a five-second clip has the larger mean or peak force, mean or peak contact area, or duration, with “About the same” for near ties. The answers are thus measured from the tactile signal rather than generated by a model. Splitting by recording yields 104 ,210

# 6. Conclusion 

We introduced TouchBench, an egocentric video-tactile benchmark in which visual answerability is a controlled variable. State-of-the-art VLMs perform near chance on physical properties hidden from the camera while succeed-ing, on average, when those properties are visible, although fine-tuned video models partly recover hidden properties from visual proxies. Whole-hand touch recovers much of this gap on relative comparisons: interpretable tactile statis-tics written into the prompt require no training, apply to pro-prietary models, improve relative comparisons on unseen users and objects and outperform the tactile-token encoder we trained on the same data. 

Limitations With 900 clips from five participants, recorded with one right-hand glove, the benchmark is small, and generalization to unseen users varies across folds: the fine-tuned TaP model’s Tier B accuracy ranges from 66 .0% 

to 86 .0% and its gain over video only from 0.0 to 12 .0

points. On the dimensions that contain both tiers, fine-tuned TaP does not improve over video alone. Tiers follow the capture design and were assigned by one author, without an agreement study. Fine-tuning uses one backbone and the learned-token baseline uses one selected encoder, so a stronger encoder may narrow the gap. Claude models were sampled once per item, without a dated snapshot. TaP does not calibrate absolute judgments, the glove’s 98 Hz sam-pling leaves phone vibration at chance and the web-scale 8corpus covers only contact properties. 

Broader impact Touch-grounded models could make as-sistive and robotic systems safer around objects whose state cannot be seen. Conversely, such models inherit the limits of the sensor and of its summary (here, no vibration above the sampling rate and no calibrated absolute judgments), so they should not be the only safeguard where the state of an object matters, for example when handling fragile or haz-ardous items.

# A. Benchmark details 

Fig. A1 shows the recording setup and the synchronized viewer used to verify the alignment between the video and the glove stream. Participants P1 to P5 contributed 152, 216, 182, 142 and 208 clips, defining the five leave-one-user-out folds. Fig. A2 shows the distribution of clips across the eleven tasks and the two tiers. 

Data collection This work evaluates models on record-ings of object manipulation and does not study the people who made the recordings, so no IRB review was conducted. All participants signed a consent form before recording. 

Sampling rate and synchronization The glove streams at about 98 Hz: the recording metadata give 97 .7–98 .3 Hz per segment, and timestamp differences give a median of 

98 .5 Hz across clips. Video and glove are aligned in soft-ware. A QR code shown in the video encodes the Unix epoch in microseconds to fix each video’s start time, which is then subtracted from the glove timestamps. Three record-ings were refit with a unit clock rate to remove approxi-mately 0.4–0.6 s of end drift. The residual video-to-glove offset after synchronization was not measured, because con-tact onsets in these grasps cannot be timed precisely in ei-ther stream. Each clip is subsampled to 32 uniformly spaced frames for model input. 

Question construction and folds Relative pairs are drawn from within a single scoring dimension, so that the two clips differ in the property being tested rather than in the question being asked. Every evaluation pair contains two clips of the held-out participant, whereas training pairs may combine clips of the four training participants. The training split is capped at 48 pairs per scoring dimension (576 per fold) to keep dimensions balanced. The evalua-tion split is uncapped, with 606/152, 1,040/216, 767/182, 525/142 and 1,164/208 relative/absolute items for P1 to P5. No evaluation clip or source segment appears in a training pair of the same fold. Because each item belongs to the fold of its held-out participant, pooling the five folds yields the exact 4,102 relative and 900 absolute items of the zero-shot rows. Pairs may span visibility conditions within a dimen-sion, such as a fully occluded metal mesh paired with a fully visible metal board. These 922 mixed-tier pairs are reported in Tab. D1. 

Averaging and chance Unless noted otherwise, accuracy is the unweighted mean of capture-subcategory accuracies. Each relative item is attributed to the task and subcategory of its first clip. The two texture dimensions share their sub-categories for this average, yielding six Tier A and sixteen Tier B subcategories (Tab. A1). Every relative question has two options. Absolute questions have two options except for the elastic-band count, which has four. Chance accu-racy for absolute questions is the mean of 1/k over subcat-egories, yielding 50 .0% on Tier A and 48 .4% on Tier B. 

Tier assignment As stated in Sec. 3.3, tiers were assigned once per capture subcategory rather than per item: one au-thor inspected the capture conditions and marked a subcat-egory Tier A only when its target property could be deter-mined from the pixels without using object identity or an-other statistical proxy. Tab. A1 gives the complete mapping. 

Label and tier audit We audited three groups whose zero-shot scores were suspicious, with the texture mate-rial pair as a control, by answering each item from its frames, question and options alone and then comparing with the gold answer. (a) Structure pair, fully visible and par-tial (Tier A) , where all video-only models score far below chance: 8 of 10 items could be judged from the frames (the other two show both surfaces mostly covered), and all 8 agree with the gold answer. The question names the target explicitly (“Which surface is more mesh-like / open (metal mesh rather than a solid metal board)?”), so neither the la-bels nor the wording is inverted. Rather, the zero-shot mod-els systematically pick the solid board, whereas the fine-tuned video model does not ( 99 .3% ). (b) Semi-transparent bottle (Tier A) : the amber bottle shows no liquid line or fill difference in any inspected frame. The 5 of 10 pairs that compare two amber bottles could not be judged, and the other 5 were answerable only from the transparent bottle in the pair. The amber bottles in the unseen-object split look identical. This subcategory is therefore reassigned to Tier B. (c) Elastic bands (Tier B) : in 20 relative pairs, 16 could be judged with low confidence from the thickness of the band bundle stretched between the hands, and 13 of these agree with the gold answer. In 10 absolute items, the band count was read correctly once. The labels are cor-rect and the tier is kept, because the count itself is not vis-ible. The bundle thickness is a partial visual proxy, which is consistent with the above-chance zero-shot scores. (d) 

Control (material pair, Tier A) : 10 of 10 items could be judged, all agreeing with the gold answer. No gold label was changed. After this reassignment, Tier A has six cap-ture subcategories and Tier B has sixteen. # B. Tactile descriptors 

Tab. B1 defines the 34 statistics that Tactile-as-Prompt seri-alizes, grouped into four blocks. These four blocks serve as the units for the descriptor-group ablation in Sec. D. Fig. B1 shows the raw channels that these statistics summarize. 

Implementation notes The implementation rectifies all 137 channels for the whole-hand totals and spatial statis-tics, including the five bend channels, so those totals are mixed sensor-unit summaries rather than calibrated force. In contrast, finger and palm subsets contain pressure chan-nels only. Spectral activity selects channels with a peak above 15 and a value above 10 in at least 5% of frames, including bend channels when they qualify. Welch powers sum the bins with l ≤ f < h , using a sample rate esti-mated from the timestamps. At the typical rate of about 98.5 Hz, the Nyquist frequency lies just above 49 Hz, so the upper bands end at the last bin below 49 Hz. The tree base-line reads the same 34 features, standardized with training-participant means and standard deviations, an affine trans-form that leaves tree splits unchanged. 

Serialization The statistics are formatted in a fixed or-der (force, spatial, spectral, wrist) as semicolon-separated clauses with plain-English names and units. Sec. F provides the exact template and rounding rules. Prompt values keep the extracted sensor scales: they are rounded, the fingertip-to-palm ratio is clamped to 99 .9 and no z-scoring is applied. 

# C. Models and training 

Zero-shot evaluation The open-weight check-points are OpenGVLab/InternVL3-8B and 

Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B . Question and option text is fixed across input conditions, while the system preamble describes the supplied modalities (Sec. F). The open models receive 32 frames per clip and are scored without generation: thinking is disabled and the option letter with the largest next-token logit among the offered options is selected. Every question has two options (A or B) except the absolute elastic-band count, which has four (A to D). The proprietary models were queried on 14 October 2026 through a non-interactive command-line interface with all tools disabled. Every response reports the model as claude-sonnet-5 or claude-opus-5 . The 32 frames of each clip are placed in the user message as two contact-sheet images, followed by the same instruction, question and options. A single letter is requested at the default sampling settings because this interface exposes no temperature control. Only outputs consisting of an option letter are scored. All zero-shot responses on TouchBench are valid. Open-model and LOUO entries report one deterministic run per arm, and proprietary entries report one sample per item. 

Fine-tuning All fine-tuning experiments use Qwen3.5-9B. Both tactile interfaces fine-tune it on the same training split, prompt scaffold and schedule. The backbone is frozen and LoRA ( r=32 , α=64 , dropout 0.05 ) is applied to the attention and MLP projections. AdamW uses a peak learn-ing rate of 2 × 10 −4 with a linear schedule, 3% warm-up, bfloat16 and an effective batch size of 16. 15 Learned-token encoder The learned interface is a1.79M-parameter temporal encoder: four stride-2 1-D con-volution blocks map the 137 channels to 256 hidden chan-nels, linear interpolation produces k=32 time-aligned to-kens, and a linear projection, layer normalization and learned gate map them to the 4,096-dimensional language space. It is initialized by symmetric frame-level InfoNCE against frozen visual features, with an additional mean-pooled clip term, on the 50-minute free-form set and the 20-hour public corpus (Fig. D5). The 21 × 21 pressure grid of the public corpus is mapped onto a shared hand can-vas, assigned to the 132 target pressure positions by near-est neighbor with collision averaging and augmented with five bend channels. Each arm adapts the language model with 58,195,968 LoRA parameters. The learned-token arm additionally updates a 1,792,129-parameter encoder and a 1,048,833-parameter conditional gate (61,036,930 trainable parameters in total), whereas TaP adds none. Our learned-token experiments varied fusion, gating, normalization and pre-training, so the reported configuration is a reference rather than a tuned optimum. 

Unseen-object split The 225 unseen-object clips were recorded by one of the five participants with object in-stances that do not appear in the benchmark. The model is trained on the benchmark clips of the other four partici-pants and evaluated on this held-out set, so both the objects and the user are unseen. The relative set contains 906 pairs (95 Tier A, 653 Tier B and 158 mixed-tier) over nine scor-ing dimensions, since no touch-target contrast is available. The absolute set contains 225 clips over ten scoring dimen-sions, excluding cap tightness and pillow filling. Seeds 42, 43 and 44 change model initialization and training-example order, while evaluation is deterministic. The V and V+T re-sults average all three seeds, and the T results average the first two.

# E. Web-scale contact QA 

Fig. E1 gives the full web-scale results, including the zero-shot reasoning-style runs on the 600-item subsample, and Tab. E1 re-scores every arm on subsets that remove the con-struction shortcuts described below. 

Question construction A proprietary model of the Claude Opus family segments each recording into events and captions each event. The caption files record the model family but not a dated snapshot. A question compares two captioned events within a five-second clip on one of five contact properties computed from the raw tactile sig-nal (mean force, peak force, mean contact area, peak con-tact area or contact duration). “About the same” is the cor-rect third option when the two values differ by less than 

8% of the larger one, or, for duration, by at most one of 32 frames. The split is by recording, with 1,149 training and 206 evaluation recordings. The direct prompts were fixed in advance, but the answer-extraction rule and generation limits of the reasoning-style runs, as well as the reasoning prompt of the proprietary model, were set after inspecting the results on the first 120 items of the 600-item subsample. On the 480 items added afterward, the strongest arm scores 

60 .2% . An example asks, “Which action was performed with the greater average grip force? If an action uses both hands, average the two hands.” Its options are “A) grasp the edge of the grey foam floor mat and rotate it on the blue ta-ble”, “B) About the same” and “C) rest the hand on the rim of the blue table”. The measured answer is A. The recorded actions use different hands and overlap in time, illustrating the construction limitations below. 

The handedness shortcut An earlier construction of the web-scale split paired clips without constraining handed-ness. Because that construction aggregated force and con-tact area over both hands, two-handed events almost always exceeded one-handed ones. Mixed-handedness pairs made up 35 .6% of the evaluation set, and the two-handed clip was the correct answer 81 .9% of the time, so always choosing the two-handed option would have scored 82% on a third of the benchmark. Moreover, 49% of the corresponding cap-tions contained the word “both”. Accuracy split by pair type confirmed the effect, with 83 .6% on mixed pairs against

Tactile controls and the caption prior On a 4,000-item evaluation subset, the fine-tuned tactile-text model scores 

75 .6% with its own tactile text, 49 .7% when each clip’s tac-tile text is replaced by that of another clip, and 58 .9% when it is withheld. The learned-token model scores 67 .4% in all three conditions, indicating that it does not use its tac-tile input. Its encoder was trained from scratch on the raw taxels of both hands, without a gate and without the pre-training used on TouchBench. Over 200 evaluation clips its tactile tokens have a mean L2 norm of 57 .5, about 67 times the mean norm of the rows of the language model’s input-embedding matrix ( 0.85 ), a likely reason the model ignores them. On this corpus the tactile text is a 32-frame table of left- and right-hand grip force, contact area and con-tact flag. The caption-only prior is a three-class softmax on bag-of-words differences between the two captions, trained separately for each property on the 104,210 training ques-tions (minimum token count 20, three epochs, learning rate 0.05, seed 0).
