## Abstract 

Gratitude writing can support well-being, but independent practice may lack guidance. Generative AI could help while shaping reflec-tion, agency, and ownership. We investigated how gratitude-writing format and AI-assistance mode influence well-being and writing experiences. In a 2 ×2 between-subjects study ( 𝑁 = 401), partici-pants completed a gratitude reflection or a gratitude letter using two AI-assistance modes: scaffolding , which provided reflective ques-tions after inactivity, or co-writing , which offered inline text sugges-tions. Participants’ well-being improved after using our AI-assisted gratitude-writing system, consistent with the activity’s intended benefits. However, participants’ experiences revealed a trade-off between the AI-support types: co-writing received higher usability ratings, yet its immediacy could disrupt writing, and greater sug-gestion acceptance was associated with weaker perceived agency. Scaffolding supported stronger perceived human ownership but was sometimes overlooked. These findings extend human–AI writ-ing research beyond productivity and text quality by showing how AI involvement shapes psychological outcomes and the experience of self-guided, emotionally meaningful writing.

## 1 Introduction 

Generative language models have expanded the role of AI in writing from offering feedback and suggestions to generating ideas, sen-tences, and longer passages alongside writers [25]. Although these systems can help writers initiate and develop text, their involvement can also shape what people express and how they perceive their contribution to the resulting text [14, 20]. These implications may be especially consequential when writing is undertaken not only to produce an artifact but also to support psychological well-being. AI assistance could help people identify and articulate personally meaningful experiences; however, directly generating their words might alter the reflection, effort, and sense of authorship that struc-tured writing activities produce [16]. This tension is particularly relevant to expressive writing interventions [16, 32], which use the structured articulation and reflection of personal experiences to support mental health and psychological well-being. Within the broader landscape of well-being-oriented writing, gratitude writing directs attention toward positive experiences, meaningful relationships, and sources of appreciation [8, 13, 42]. By helping individuals notice, elaborate on, and assign meaning to positive aspects of their lives, gratitude writing interventions can foster positive affect, strengthen feelings of appreciation, and improve subjective well-being [8, 15, 42]. These interventions can structure gratitude in different ways: some ask individuals to re-flect privately on valued experiences, whereas others ask them to formulate and communicate their appreciation through a grati-tude letter [8, 13]. Despite their general benefits, gratitude writing interventions are not uniformly effective; their outcomes may de-pend on factors such as intervention format, duration, motivation, and effort [27, 37]. How an intervention structures reflection and expression may therefore shape both its well-being benefits and the writer’s experience, particularly as many people nowadays use generative AI to generate ideas or language. In writing tasks, generative AI can assist people through differ-ent levels of involvement. In a scaffolding role, AI can help writ-ers generate and explore topics, questions, or possible directions while leaving the composition of the text to the writer. In a co-writing role, AI can contribute language directly and take a more active role in determining how ideas are articulated in the final text. Prior research demonstrates that language models can support both ideation and text production [25]. However, AI assistance can also shape what writers express and create a divergence between their perceived ownership of AI-generated text and their claims of authorship [14, 20]. These distinctions may be especially con-sequential in well-being interventions that involve writing tasks, where the process of reflection may matter as much as, or more than, the production of polished text. Scaffolding assistance could facilitate reflection while preserving the writer’s direct contribution, whereas co-writing could reduce barriers to expression while also altering effort, agency, or perceived authorship. ## 2 Related Works 2.1 Writing and Gratitude Interventions for Well-being 

Structured writing has long been studied as a means of supporting psychological and physical well-being. Early expressive writing research asked individuals to write about stressful, traumatic, or emotionally significant experiences, often across several brief ses-sions [32]. Rather than focusing on the quality of the resulting text, these interventions treat writing as a process through which people articulate emotions, organize experiences, and construct meaning. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that expressive writing can produce small but reliable benefits across psychological, behav-ioral, and physical-health outcomes, although these effects vary across studies and participants [16]. This variability suggests that the benefits of writing depend not simply on whether people write, but on what they write about and how they engage in the activity. Subsequent research expanded writing interventions beyond the disclosure of difficult experiences to include reflection on posi-tive experiences and possible futures. For instance, writing about intensely positive experiences has been associated with improve-ments in health and well-being [5]. Other positive writing activities ask participants to imagine their best possible selves [22], identify positive daily events [36], or reflect on sources of meaning and appreciation [15]. Although these interventions vary in content and structure, they share the premise that directing and elaborating attention through writing can influence psychological well-being. Positive activity models [27, 28] further propose that their effec-tiveness depends on characteristics of both the activity and the participant, including dosage, variety, motivation, effort, and per-son–activity fit. Gratitude writing is one widely studied form of positive writing intervention. Emmons and McCullough [15] found that partici-pants who regularly recorded things for which they were grateful reported greater well-being and optimism—and, in some studies, fewer physical symptoms—than participants who recorded hassles or neutral events. Seligman et al. [36] similarly found that gratitude-focused activities could increase happiness and reduce depressive symptoms. Meta-analyses have since identified reliable benefits of gratitude interventions while documenting variation across out-comes, comparison conditions, intervention durations, and activity formats [8, 10, 13]. Gratitude writing may produce these benefits by directing attention toward positive experiences and meaningful relationships and encouraging people to elaborate on why they matter. Gratitude interventions structure reflection and expression in different ways. Individually oriented exercises, often described as 

gratitude listing or counting blessings , ask participants to identify and elaborate on positive experiences, people, or circumstances for which they feel grateful [15, 17]. Interpersonally oriented exercises instead ask participants to compose a gratitude letter expressing appreciation to a specific benefactor [36, 38]. Whereas the former centers private reflection, the latter requires writers to formulate their gratitude in relation to another person and a potential audi-ence. Meta-analytic findings support a small but reliable overall ef-fect of gratitude interventions on well-being while also indicating variation across intervention formats and study designs [8, 10]. Specifically, both reflective and letter-writing activities were found effective in promoting positive outcomes, but their effects may dif-fer. Watkins et al. [41] found that thinking about, writing about, and composing a letter to a person for whom one felt grateful all increased immediate positive affect relative to a neutral task, while repeated gratitude-letter writing has been associated with cumu-lative improvements in happiness and life satisfaction [38]. Prior work additionally suggests that gratitude intervention formats can differ in their perceived difficulty and likelihood of completion [21]. These differences motivate our comparison of gratitude reflection and gratitude-letter writing. 

## 2.2 Generative AI Assistance for Writing 

In conventional gratitude interventions, instructions structure the topic and form of reflection, but participants remain responsible for generating and articulating their responses. However, recent advances in generative AI can potentially change this by allowing an interactive AI system to collaborate with users in generating ideas, composing language, or both. This raises new questions about how AI involvement interacts with the distinct reflective and interpersonal demands of gratitude-writing activities. A review of 115 intelligent writing-assistant studies identified five dimensions along which such systems vary: task, user, technol-ogy, interaction, and ecosystem [24]. Of particular relevance to our work is the nature of generative AI’s contribution. Some systems support idea generation and exploration by offering conceptual prompts, questions, potential topics, or alternative directions dur-ing prewriting [18, 19, 40], whereas others generate continuations, rewrites, elaborations, or complete passages that writers can incor-porate directly into their text [7, 25, 43]. These roles distribute the reflective and compositional work dif-ferently between the writer and the system. In direct co-writing systems, writers and AI alternate or combine their contributions to produce a text. Systems, such as Wordcraft [43] and CoAuthor [25], Thu Bui*, Shashi Salavath*, Hillary Merzdorf, Louis Tay, and Sooyeon Jeong 

allow writers to request continuations, elaborations, and rewrites at different points in the composition process. Wordcraft [43] partici-pants used language-model assistance to overcome blocks and de-velop story details, although the system sometimes struggled to pre-serve writers’ intentions and authorial voices. CoAuthor [25] cap-tured 1,445 creative and argumentative writing sessions in which participants could request, accept, edit, or reject GPT-3 suggestions. Writers accepted 72.3% of the suggestions they requested, yet hu-mans still produced 72.6% of the final text, which illustrated that writers selectively incorporated AI-generated language rather than simply delegating the entire task. Other systems position AI primarily as a source of ideas. Sparks [18], for example, generated conceptually related prompts intended to inspire science writers rather than provide polished prose for di-rect insertion. Participants used these prompts to find inspiration, translate technical concepts, and consider alternative perspectives. Related research on predictive and parallel suggestions demon-strates that AI-generated options can influence both what writers consider and what they ultimately produce [3, 6]. Idea-oriented as-sistance may therefore expand the possibilities available to writers while preserving their responsibility for selecting, interpreting, and articulating those ideas. In contrast, direct co-writing shifts part of the compositional work to the system. Greater AI involvement can make writing easier, but it may also affect agency, ownership, and the diversity of written expression. Predictive suggestions can steer writers toward language made salient by the system [3], while AI-generated contributions can create discrepancies between users’ perceived ownership of a text and their attribution of authorship [14]. Other studies suggest that reliance on language-model suggestions can reduce linguistic or content diversity and shift writers from generating ideas toward evaluating AI-provided options [1, 4, 31]. The degree of assistance also appears consequential: different levels of generated support can produce tradeoffs among writing quality, cognitive investment, and perceived ownership [9]. Together, these findings show that AI assistance changes not only the efficiency or quality of writing but also how writers participate in producing the text. These consequences may be particularly important in well-being-oriented writing, where reflection and personal articulation are part of the intended activity rather than merely steps toward a finished artifact. Scaffolding assistance may help people identify and develop meaningful experiences while leaving them responsible for expressing those experiences. Co-writing may reduce barriers to expression by contributing language directly, but it may also shift effort, agency, or ownership away from the writer. Prior work has rarely examined whether these modes of assistance affect the psychological outcomes of writing interventions or whether their effects depend on the reflective and interpersonal demands of the intervention itself. 

## 3 Method 3.1 Study Design 

We conducted an online 2 × 2 between-subjects study to examine how gratitude intervention format and AI-assistance mode 

jointly influence the experience and outcomes of gratitude writ-ing. Gratitude intervention format consisted of gratitude reflection 

and gratitude letter writing , while AI-assistance mode consisted of 

scaffolding support and co-writing support . Upon informed consent, participants were randomly assigned to one of four conditions: (1) gratitude reflection with scaffolding (RS), (2) gratitude reflection with co-writing (RC), (3) gratitude letter with scaffolding (LS), and (4) gratitude letter with co-writing (LC). All conditions followed the same study procedure and used a shared interface, condition-specific writing instruction and AI-assistance features described below. 

3.1.1 Gratitude-Writing Format. The gratitude-writing activities differed in their focus and intended audience. In the gratitude re-flection condition, participants were asked to write about positive experiences, meaningful relationships, or other aspects of their lives for which they felt grateful [15, 33]. In the gratitude letter 

condition, participants addressed their writing to a specific person and explained why they appreciated that person [33, 36]. Gratitude reflection therefore allowed participants to explore different sources of gratitude, whereas gratitude letter writing focused expression on a particular recipient. The complete writing instructions are provided in Section A.7. 

3.1.2 AI-assistance Modes. Both AI-assistance modes used GPT-4o with condition-specific prompts that incorporated the assigned writing task and the participants’ current written draft. The modes differed in the content, presentation, and timing of the AI assistance. In scaffolding, the AI agent, referred to as “Ami” in the web inter-face, supported idea development through open-ended questions, topics, and possible directions. For example, it could ask “How has your friendship influenced or enhanced your experience with your hobby?” Scaffolding appeared when requested by participants or au-tomatically after 20 seconds of writing inactivity. Participants could provide feedback on each question using thumbs-up or thumbs-down buttons and remained responsible for composing the text in their own words. In co-writing, the AI-support feature functioned as an auto-complete tool that suggested words or sentences to continue the participant’s written draft based on the text before the cursor. For example, after a participant typed “ Dear Baba, I just wanted to start off by saying ”, the system suggested “ thank you for always being there for me. ” Inline suggestions appeared after approximately one second of writing inactivity. Participants accept a suggestion by pressing the Tab key or reject/ignore the suggestion and continue writing their own text. Accepted text could be edited after insertion. Co-writing also provided side-panel guidance through the Ask Ami 

button. This assistance appeared only when requested and provided a direct writing suggestion. For example, when the writing con-tained “ Dear Mom, ” the system provided direct writing guidance rather than a reflective question: “Include Specific Memories or Ac-tions: To make your letter more heartfelt, recall specific moments or actions that made you grateful for your mom. For example, you might write about a time she supported you during a challenging situation or a cherished family tradition she started. These details can make your gratitude more tangible and personal.” The comparison therefore examined two configurations of AI assistance that differed in both their contribution to the text and their interaction design. The complete model prompts are provided in Sections B.1 and B.2. Figures 3 and 4 illustrate the co-writing Supporting Without Taking Over: AI Support in Gratitude Writing

## 3.2 Participants 

We recruited participants through the Purdue’s research partici-pant pool and Prolific 1. To be eligible from the university’s research pool, student participants were required to be fluent in English and enrolled as a sophomore, junior, senior, or graduate student. They received two research credits for participating in the study. Prolific participants were required to be at least 18 years old, flu-ent in English, and have access to a computer. They received $6 for completing the study. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided informed consent before beginning the study. A total of 612 individuals began the study according to our online database. Of these, 496 could be linked to a writing-session record in our study database. Before conducting the primary analyses, we excluded 89 participants who submitted no written response, 3 who did not comply with the study procedure (e.g., copying/pasting text generated outside the assigned study interface), and 3 whose interaction records were incomplete because of technical errors. The final analytic sample consisted of 401 participants: 372 recruited through Prolific and 29 through the Purdue University’s online student participant pool. The analytic sample includes 401 participants: 104 in gratitude-letter /co-writing (LC), 107 in gratitude-letter /scaffolding (LS), 98 in gratitude reflection /co-writing (RC), and 92 in gratitude reflec-tion /scaffolding (RS). Because survey responses were optional, miss-ing responses resulted in different sample sizes across measures. The number of participants included in each measure analysis is reported in Section A.6. 

## 3.3 Writing Interface 

3.3.1 Interface Layout and Features. Participants completed the gratitude-writing task through a custom web interface embedded in Qualtrics. As shown in Figure 2, the interface used the same overall layout across conditions: the assigned writing instructions appeared above a large text editor on the left, while an AI-support panel featuring the agent, Ami, appeared on the right. The side panel explained the available AI interaction, displayed generated guidance, and included a countdown indicating the remaining re-quired writing time. Participants’ drafts were saved automatically, a word count was displayed below the editor, and the submission button became available after 15 minutes. The primary differences across conditions were the writing instructions and the way partic-ipants interacted with Ami. In co-writing, Ami displayed candidate continuations as faded inline text after participants paused for approximately one second. Participants could press Tab to accept a completion, press Esc to dismiss it, or continue typing to ignore it. They could also click the Ask Ami button to request an additional suggestion in the side panel. In scaffolding, Ami did not insert language into the document. Instead, after 20 seconds of inactivity, the side panel automatically displayed a reflective question intended to help participants develop their ideas. Participants could rate each question as helpful or not

The longer delay in scaffolding treated sustained inactivity as a possible in-dication that the writer needed guidance, allowing the system to intervene without repeatedly interrupting active writing. 

3.3.2 Interaction Logging. The system captured a recording of the study browser tab and logged participants’ writing and interactions with Ami. For co-writing, the logs recorded each inline suggestion displayed and whether it was accepted, dismissed, or ignored, as well as requests submitted through Ask Ami . A suggestion was considered accepted when the participant inserted it using Tab ;suggestions dismissed using Esc or superseded by continued typing were treated as rejected. We calculated the acceptance rate as the number of accepted suggestions divided by the number displayed, and the resolved acceptance rate as the number accepted divided by the total number accepted or rejected. For scaffolding, the logs recorded each question displayed and participants’ helpful or not-helpful ratings. The like rate was calcu-lated as the number of helpful ratings divided by the total number of helpful and not-helpful ratings among participants who rated at least one prompt. The system also saved participants’ final submit-ted texts for subsequent analysis. 

## 3.4 Study Procedure 

Recruited participants were directed to a Qualtrics survey, where they reviewed the study information, provided informed consent, and completed baseline measures of life satisfaction, trait gratitude, positive and negative affect, and moral elevation (see Sections A and 3.5). Qualtrics then presented the embedded writing interface, which delivered the writing task and AI support corresponding to each participant’s assigned condition. Participants completed the writing activity for at least 15 minutes, during which their writing and system interactions were recorded as described above. After submitting their writing, participants returned to Qualtrics and completed the same well-being measures, followed by measures of writing self-efficacy, perceived ownership, control, leadership, text match, user experience, and usability. They also answered four open-ended questions about the system and their writing expe-rience. Upon completion, Prolific participants received $6, while participants recruited through the university’s research participant pool received two research credits.

## 3.6 Behavioral Measures 

3.6.1 Writing Interaction Behavior. We derive behavioral engage-ment measures from the writing-interface logs. For co-writing, we record the number of autocomplete suggestions shown, ac-cepted, and rejected, and calculate the resolved acceptance rate as accepted /( accepted + rejected ). For scaffolding, we record partic-ipants’ likes and dislikes of reflective prompts and calculate the like rate as likes /( likes + dislikes ). The acceptance rate is com-puted for co-writing participants who receive at least one autocom-plete suggestion, whereas the like rate is computed for scaffolding participants who rate at least one prompt. We also record each participant’s final submitted word count.

Feelings range from matter-of-fact descriptions without affective content (score 1) to the disclosure of deep emotions (score 3). Information ranges from general statements without personal details (score 1) to highly per-sonal revelations about the writer or close others (score 3). Thoughts

## 4 Results 

In our analysis, we first examined changes in well-being and whether these changes differed by writing format and AI assistance mode (RQ1), followed by writing experience (RQ2), and self-disclosure in the final texts (RQ3). We then report exploratory associations between participants’ interactions with AI and their experiences. We did not detect an interaction between writing format and AI-assistance mode in pre–post well-being changes, although each factor was associated with differences in particular outcomes.

P00409 (letter/scaffolding) connected this to ownership: “I felt like it en-hanced my writing abilities and served as an aid but I was fully in control of the process.” 

4.5.3 Mode-Specific Limitations: Intrusive Output vs. Absent Sup-port. The most common complaint overall was generic, repetitive, or off-target output (22%), reported more often in co-writing (27% vs. 17%). In co-writing, this usually meant completions that lost the thread of the draft: “It didn’t keep up with the context of what I was writing. It just kept throwing in random sentences, and in the end, sentence fragments.” (P00083, letter/co-writing). In scaffolding, it meant repetitive questions: “The tips sometimes were just reworded instead of making new ones for the user” (P00043, reflection/scaf-folding). Co-writing participants were also more likely to report that suggestions interrupted their train of thought (13% vs. 6%; “naturally I had something on my mind, then boom, I am reading what it is writing,” P00348, letter/co-writing) and that completions visually covered their own text (15% vs. 3%). Scaffolding’s problems were instead about presence: 16% of scaf-folding participants (vs. 11% in co-writing) reported that the assis-tant was unavailable, showed error messages, or never appeared (“It said it was ‘offline’ for my whole writing session,” P00140, reflec-tion/scaffolding), and 15% (vs. 8%) said they did not need or barely used it: “I got so into writing it from my own past experiences and heart, that the words flowed without anything else needed” (P00057, letter/scaffolding). Disruption was not unique to co-writing, how-ever; P00470 (reflection/scaffolding) described how reading each new question triggered another pause and another question until “it would snowball,” adding, “Oftentimes I would rather think on me own if I am stuck.” 

4.5.4 Authenticity and Voice in Emotional Writing. A smaller group of participants (8%; 10% in co-writing, 5% in scaffolding) raised concerns about authenticity and voice that were specific to the emotional nature of the task. P00231 (letter/co-writing) found the text suggestions “more fake and inauthentic. I feel that the writing needed to be genuine and sincerely written in my own words,” and P00459 (reflection/co-writing) doubted that prediction could work “for these kind’ve emotional topics . . . especially since it’s coming from the heart.” P00094 (letter/scaffolding) declined to use the tool because “AI tends to erase personality and authenticity from writing so if it’s something potentially meaningful, I just would prefer not to use AI tools,” and P00019 (letter/scaffolding) noticed a drift in the other direction: “I also got less involved as the process went on and it just felt like I was completely relying on the ai.” 

4.5.5 Design Requests. Consistent with these concerns, partic-ipants’ design requests centered on control and quality rather than on more generated text: better and less repetitive sugges-tions (15%; “If something is rejected, it shouldn’t reappear,” P00053, reflection/co-writing), control over when the AI appears (7%; “I wish I could not see it until I knew I needed it and could then click a button to open it,” P00315, letter/scaffolding), a choice among alternatives (“Instead of providing one example sentence, provide Supporting Without Taking Over: AI Support in Gratitude Writing 

three to choose between,” P00250, letter/co-writing), adaptation to the writer’s own style (“giving users more control over the tone, level of detail, and writing style,” P00220, letter/co-writing), and, for a few, a conversational mode (“a chatbot option would be nice. So users can ask questions and recommendations directly,” P00448, reflection/scaffolding). 

## 5 Discussion 

Our study examined how gratitude writing format and AI-assistance mode shape well-being outcomes (RQ1), the writing experience and perceived agency (RQ2), and self-disclosure (RQ3). Prior work has shown that gratitude writing interventions can improve well-being, but that their effects vary depending on intervention format, effort, and person-activity fit [8, 10, 13, 27, 28]. At the same time, research on AI-assisted writing has demonstrated that generative AI assis-tance can facilitate ideation and text production while also shaping writers’ expression, cognitive investment, agency, and ownership [9, 14, 18, 25]. Our findings bring these two bodies of work together and show that the role that AI takes in gratitude writing matters differently depending on whether we consider psychological out-comes, the experience of writing, or the content writers disclose. 

## 5.1 RQ1: Gratitude Writing Format and AI-assistance Mode Shape Different Aspects of Well-being 

For RQ1, most well-being measures improved from pre- to post-writing across conditions, but the pattern of change differed by writing and AI-assistance type. Gratitude reflection produced larger gains in life satisfaction, whereas co-writing produced larger gains in positive affect and affect balance. These findings are consistent with prior gratitude research that showed both reflective and in-terpersonal gratitude activities can promote positive outcomes, while their effects vary across intervention formats and outcomes [8, 10, 33, 38]. Our results extend this work by suggesting that the way AI participates and collaborates in the writing process may also shape some immediate affective outcomes. At the same time, greater engagement with AI was more consis-tently associated with interface experience than with well-being change. Thus, interacting more with AI, or accepting more of its sug-gestions, should not necessarily be interpreted as greater psycholog-ical benefit. This distinction is particularly important for well-being writing since generating, selecting, and articulating meaningful experiences may themselves be part of the intervention process. However, we cannot conclude whether AI assistance itself pro-duced the overall pre-to-post improvements in well-being since our study did not include a condition without AI assistance. Rather, our findings suggest that both AI-assistance modes were compatible with the short-term outcomes of gratitude writing while shaping particular outcomes differently. 

## 5.2 RQ2: AI Writing Assistance May Change Reflective Processes 

For RQ2, our findings reveal a trade-off between the usability of AI assistance and writers’ sense of agency. Co-writing received higher usability and user-experience ratings, whereas scaffolding resulted in stronger human-ownership attribution. Moreover, within co-writing, greater suggestion uptake was associated with lower per-ceived ownership, control, and human contribution. This finding extends prior research that showed direct AI contributions can create discrepancies between authorship and ownership [14], steer writers toward AI-salient language [3, 20], and shift writers from generating ideas toward evaluating AI-generated alternatives [4]. Prior work has similarly found trade-offs among the level of AI assistance, cognitive investment, and perceived ownership [9]. Our study results extend this work by showing why this trade-off may be particularly consequential in gratitude writing. Unlike many productivity-oriented writing tasks, gratitude writ-ing is not solely concerned with the quality or efficiency of the resulting text. The act of identifying a meaningful experience, con-sidering why it matters, and finding one’s own words to express it is itself part of the intended activity. Prior work on expressive and gratitude writing similarly conceptualizes writing as a pro-cess through which people organize experiences, elaborate on their meaning, and direct attention toward personally significant events and relationships [15, 16, 32]. Direct text generation may therefore alter not only authorship of the final artifact, but also who performs the reflective work leading to that artifact. Participants’ qualitative feedback illustrates this distinction. Co-writing could make expressions easier and help participants con-tinue when they were unsure what to write, but some participants also described suggestions as interrupting their own thought pro-cess or making emotionally meaningful writing feel less genuine. Scaffolding, by contrast, left participants responsible for translating reflection into language, although its prompts could be overlooked or interrupt moments of quiet thought. Thus, for well-being writing, the relevant design question may be less about maximizing perceived usability or minimizing effort and more about determining which parts of the activity should remain effortful and self-generated because they contribute to reflection itself. 

## 5.3 RQ3: Writing Format Matters More Than AI Support for Self-Disclosure 

For RQ3, writing format shaped the nature of self-disclosure, whereas AI-assistance mode did not significantly affect disclosure depth. Gratitude letters contained deeper feelings-based disclosure, while gratitude reflections contained deeper disclosure of personal thoughts. This pattern aligns with the different structure of these established gratitude interventions. Gratitude reflections center private consid-eration of positive experiences and circumstances, while gratitude letters require writers to formulate appreciation in relation to a particular person and potential audience [15, 33, 36, 38]. This finding is notable given prior work showing that AI sugges-tions can influence what writers consider and ultimately produce [3, 6, 20]. In our study, however, whether AI provided reflective di-rections or directly generated language did not significantly change the measured depth of participants’ disclosure. Instead, the grat-itude writing format more strongly shaped the type of personal content participants disclosed: letters elicited deeper feelings-based disclosure, while reflections elicited deeper thought-based disclo-sure. This suggests that the type of gratitude writing task may play Thu Bui*, Shashi Salavath*, Hillary Merzdorf, Louis Tay, and Sooyeon Jeong 

a larger role than the type of AI assistance in shaping what people disclose. 

## 5.4 Designing AI to Support Without Taking Over 

Across the three RQs, our findings suggest that effective AI support for well-being writing should not be defined simply by maximizing AI engagement or reducing writing effort. Rather than treating scaf-folding and co-writing as fixed alternatives, future systems could allow users to regulate the timing and level of AI involvement. For example, a system could allow users to choose between receiving reflective questions, getting help generating ideas, or receiving suggested wording, depending on the type of support they need. Such designs could preserve the benefits identified in prior work (e.g., overcoming writing blocks and supporting idea development [18, 25, 40]), while reducing the risk that AI-generated content re-places participants’ own process of reflecting on and expressing meaningful experiences. More broadly, our results highlight the importance of matching the form and timing of AI assistance to the goals of the writing activity and the writer’s needs. In addition, designers should be cautious about optimizing for suggestion acceptance or the amount of AI-generated text incor-porated into a response. In our study, greater uptake of co-writing suggestions was associated with weaker perceived agency, while AI engagement was more closely related to interface experience than to well-being outcomes. For well-being writing, the goal may therefore be to provide the minimum assistance needed to sustain users’ own reflection, rather than maximizing AI contribution. Several limitations motivate future work. Our study did not include a no-AI condition, and thus we cannot isolate the contri-bution of AI to the observed well-being changes. The scaffolding and co-writing conditions also differed in timing, presentation, and type of assistance, which makes it difficult to attribute differences to a single design feature. Finally, our study examined a single short writing session and used an LLM-based proxy measure of self-disclosure. Future studies should explore repeated use, validate disclosure measures with human coding, and investigate adaptive forms of AI support that balance assistance with users’ agency, authenticity, and ownership over time. 

## 6 Conclusion 

In this study, we investigated how different forms of AI assistance and gratitude writing activities shape people’s well-being, writing experience, and self-disclosure. Our findings suggest that neither scaffolding nor co-writing is universally preferable. Co-writing provided more immediate and usable support and was associated with larger gains in positive affect and affect balance, but greater reliance on its suggestions was also associated with weaker per-ceived agency. Scaffolding better preserved human ownership by leaving participants responsible for articulating their experiences, although its support was sometimes less noticeable. Meanwhile, the gratitude writing format, rather than the AI-assistance mode, more strongly shaped the nature of writers’ self-disclosure, with letters eliciting deeper feelings and reflections eliciting deeper thoughts. Together, these findings highlight an important consideration for AI-supported well-being writing: AI assistance should support the process of reflection and expression without unnecessarily perform-ing that work for the user. Rather than maximizing AI contribution, future systems could provide context-aware support that infers users’ needs during writing and dynamically adjusts when and how support is provided. Such systems could distinguish between mo-ments when users are productively reflecting and moments when they need help generating ideas or expressing their thoughts, and provide only the level of assistance needed to sustain the writing process. In doing so, AI writing tools may help people engage in personally meaningful writing while preserving their active role in the reflective process. 

## Ethics and Privacy Statement 

AI-supported gratitude writing may make reflective guidance more accessible, but it also introduces privacy and autonomy risks be-cause users may disclose sensitive personal and relational infor-mation, and our findings associate greater engagement with co-writing suggestions with weaker perceived agency. Systems should therefore clearly disclose how users’ writing is processed, stored, and reused; minimize the collection and secondary use of emo-tionally sensitive data; and preserve users’ control over whether AI-generated content enters or remains in their writing. These find-ings should not be used to treat suggestion uptake as evidence of psychological benefit, infer users’ mental-health status for conse-quential decisions, or position AI-supported writing as a substitute for professional care.

## A Measures 

We administer measures of life satisfaction, gratitude, affect, and moral elevation immediately before and after the writing activity. Participants also post-task measures of writing self-efficacy, user experience, usability, ownership, control, leadership, and perceived text match. We additionally derive behavioral engagement measures from system logs and use LLM-generated ratings to characterize self-disclosure in participants’ final texts. 

## A.1 Well-Being Outcomes 

Satisfaction With Life. We measure life satisfaction using the five-item Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWLS) [11]. Participants rate each statement on a seven-point scale from 1 ( strongly disagree ) to 7 ( strongly agree ). We sum the five items to obtain scores ranging from 5 to 35, with higher scores indicating greater life satisfaction. A score is computed only when all five items are available. 

Trait Gratitude. We assess dispositional gratitude using the six-item Gratitude Questionnaire (GQ-6) [29]. Participants respond on a seven-point scale from 1 ( strongly disagree ) to 7 ( strongly agree ). We reverse-score Items 3 and 6 using 8 −𝑥 and sum all items, producing scores from 6 to 42; higher scores indicate a stronger tendency to experience gratitude. A score is computed only when all six items are available. 

Positive and Negative Affect. We assess affect using the 12-item Scale of Positive and Negative Experience (SPANE) [12]. Partici-pants rate six positive and six negative feelings on a five-point scale. Responses use intensity anchors from 1 ( not at all ) to 5 ( very much ). We map anchor formats onto the same 1–5 metric and treat them as comparable in the primary analyses. We sum Items 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 12 to calculate SPANE-P and Items 2, 4, 6, 8, 9, and 11 to calculate SPANE-N, each ranging from 6 to 30. We then subtract SPANE-N from SPANE-P to calculate affect balance (SPANE-B), ranging from 

−24 to 24; higher scores indicate a more positive affective balance. Each component is computed only when all six constituent items are available. 

Moral Elevation. We assess state moral elevation using six items adapted from Schnall et al. [34]. The items capture feeling moved, uplifted, optimistic about humanity, warmth in the chest, motivated to help others, and motivated to become a better person. Partici-pants rate each experience from 1 ( not at all ) to 9 ( very strongly ). We average Items 1–6, with higher scores indicating stronger moral elevation; the separately administered seventh item ( happy ) is not included. A score is computed only when all six scored items are available. 

## A.2 Writing Experience 

Writing Self-Efficacy. We assess participants’ confidence in writ-ing using nine items adapted from the Nutrition and Physical Ex-ercise Self-Efficacy Scales [35]. We replace references to nutrition and exercise with writing-specific language. Participants respond on a four-point certainty scale from 1 ( very uncertain ) to 4 ( very certain ). Four writing-process items assess participants’ confidence in developing ideas, revising repeatedly, rethinking their approach, and writing without extensive support. Five writing-barrier items assess confidence in carrying out writing intentions while worried, depressed, tense, tired, or busy. We compute complete-item sums for writing-process efficacy (range 4–16), writing-barrier efficacy (range 5–20), and overall writing self-efficacy across all nine items (range 9–36); higher scores indicate greater writing self-efficacy. The adapted version is presented in Table 7. 

User Experience and Usability. We measure overall user experi-ence using a 21-item version of the User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ) [23]. The UEQ uses seven-point semantic-differential items covering attractiveness, perspicuity, efficiency, dependability, stim-ulation, and novelty. Because some items place the positive pole on the left, we reverse-score Items 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 11, 16, 17, 18, and 20 us-ing 8 − 𝑥 so that higher values consistently indicate a more positive evaluation. We then average the complete set of 21 items on the original 1–7 scale; a score is computed only when all 21 items are available. We assess perceived usability using the 10-item System Usability Scale (SUS) [26]. Participants respond on five-point agree-ment scales. Following the standard SUS procedure, we subtract 1 from each odd-numbered item, subtract each even-numbered item from 5, sum the ten contributions, and multiply by 2.5 to obtain a total score from 0 to 100; higher scores indicate greater perceived usability. A SUS score is computed only when all ten items are available. 

Ownership, Control, and Text Match. We adapt measures of per-ceived authorship, ownership, and control from [14], replacing references to postcards with the more general term text and remov-ing items that do not apply to the present writing task. Participants rate all non-slider items on seven-point agreement scales. The five-item ownership measure assesses whether participants view themselves as the primary contributor, drafter, approver, and author of the text. No ownership items are reverse-scored. The seven-item control measure assesses intentionality, responsibility for the resulting text, control over the writing process, perceptions of the AI as a ghostwriter or controllable tool, and freedom to determine the writing approach and organization, with reversing the items as in [14]. We then average each complete scale so that higher scores indicate greater perceived ownership or control. The four-item text-match measure assesses whether participants would send the text to friends or family, are satisfied and happy with the resulting text, and are pleased with its flow. We average the items, with higher scores indicating a stronger perceived match between the participant and the resulting text. Ownership, control, and text-match scores are each computed only when all of their constituent items are available. Participants separately indicate ownership, control, and lead-ership using three continuous Me–AI sliders adapted from [14]: “To whom should this text belong?”, “Who was in control of the content?”, and “Who took the lead in writing during this interaction method?” Raw slider values range from +100 ( Me ) to −100 ( AI ). For inferential analyses, we multiply each raw score by +1 so that higher values indicate greater attribution to the participant and lower values indicate greater attribution to the AI. The three sliders are analyzed as standalone outcomes and are not averaged into the Likert ownership or control composites. Full adapted measures we used are shown in Tables 10 to 13. ## A.3 Behavioral Engagement 

We derive engagement measures from the writing interface logs. For co-writing, we record the number of autocomplete suggestions shown, accepted, and rejected. The primary co-writing engagement measure is the resolved acceptance rate, accepted accepted + rejected ,

computed among participants who received at least one auto-complete suggestion. For scaffolding, we record like and dislike responses to AI prompts. The primary scaffolding engagement measure is the like rate, likes likes + dislikes ,

computed among participants who rated at least one scaffolding prompt. We also record the final submitted word count for each participant. 

## A.4 LLM-Rated Self-Disclosure 

To characterize the content of participants’ writing, we score each final text on three self-disclosure dimensions using GPT-5.2-chat with prompts adapted from prior self-disclosure coding schemes: feelings, information, and thoughts. Each dimension is rated on a three-point depth scale from 1 to 3, where higher scores indicate deeper disclosure. Feelings capture the emotional depth of the writ-ing, information captures the depth of personal factual disclosure, and thoughts capture the depth of personal ideas or reflections. When all three dimension scores are available, we compute an overall disclosure mean as their average. These LLM ratings are treated as proxy annotations of disclosure content rather than as human-coded writing-quality scores. 

## A.5 Open-Ended Feedback 

Following the quantitative measures, participants respond to four open-ended questions about their experience: (1) “What did you like about the system?” (2) “What did you not like about the system?” (3) “Please share anything that can be improved about the system.” (4) “Please share any other thoughts or feedback you have about the writing experience.” We use these responses to contextualize participants’ quanti-tative ratings and identify perceived benefits, limitations, and op-portunities for improving the AI-supported writing experience. For exploratory qualitative analyses, we apply dictionary-assisted, multi-label thematic coding to the open-ended responses and report theme frequencies among respondents to each question.

## B LLM Prompts for AI Support 

The system used three LLM request types: reflective questions in scaffolding, on-demand suggestions in co-writing, and inline completions in co-writing. Requests were sent to an Azure Ope-nAI deployment ( gpt-4o ) through the Chat Completions API using API version 2024-02-15-preview . Scaffolding questions and on-demand co-writing suggestions used a temperature of 0 .8 and a maximum of 280 output tokens. Inline completions used a tempera-ture of 0 .4 and a maximum of 60 output tokens. Before being inserted into a prompt, each draft was sanitized by removing empty lines; lines beginning with system: , assistant: ,or user: ; and lines containing selected prompt-injection terms, including ignore all previous , override , forget , and bypass .The resulting draft was truncated to 2,000 characters. Empty drafts were represented as No text yet. . In the templates below, angle-bracketed terms denote values inserted at runtime rather than literal prompt text.

## C LLM Self-Disclosure Scoring Prompts 

Each final writing text is scored independently on three self-disclosure dimensions with GPT-5.2-chat 5 times. The dimension-specific in-structions below are sent as the system message. The user message is identical across dimensions: 

Score this text. Reply with ONLY the integer 1, 2, or 3. TEXT: <participant final writing> 

The model is instructed to return only the integer score 1, 2, or 3. Texts longer than 12 ,000 characters are truncated to the first and last 6 ,000 characters with a truncation marker. 

Feelings. 

As a social scientist, your task is to annotate the depth of this self-disclosure statements. Evaluate the feelings of the self-disclosure statement: - Score 1: No expressing of feelings at all. The speech may include a prosaic description of facts or personal ideas, without expressing any emotions or affective relevance - Score 2: Expressing of some mild feelings - Score 3: Expressions of deep feelings Only give the score (number) without any explanation 

Information. 

As a social scientist, your task is to annotate the depth of this self-disclosure statements. Evaluate the information of the self-disclosure statement: - Score 1: Statements that provide general or routine information only, without any personal reference - Score 2: Statements providing general information about the user - Score 3: Statements revealing personal information that exposes self or people close to the user, such as descriptions of physical appearance and behavior Only give the score (number) without any explanation 

Thoughts. 

As a social scientist, your task is to annotate the depth of this self-disclosure statements. Evaluate the thoughts of the self-disclosure statement: - Score 1: No indication of any thoughts or ideas on any subject that refer to the user personally; expressing of general ideas only - Score 2: Statements expressing the user's personal thoughts on past events or future plans - Score 3: Statements expressing thoughts relating to the user' s personal characteristics, physical appearance, health, or intimate and wishful ideas Only give the score (number) without any explanation
