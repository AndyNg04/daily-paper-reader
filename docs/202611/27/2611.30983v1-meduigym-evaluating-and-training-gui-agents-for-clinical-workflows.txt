## Abstract 

Automating clinical workflows requires vision-language agents that can navigate dense graphical user interfaces (GUIs) in electronic health records (EHRs) and execute precise actions, yet many medical agents still rely on text or structured tool calls. In this work, we introduce 

MedUIGym , a scalable interactive environment compris-ing 15,739 tasks from 17 benchmark sources across six clinical task families for training and evaluating clini-cal GUI agents. In MedUIGym , agents perceive EHR states from screenshots and execute mouse-and-keyboard actions, as well as a paired native-tool interface over identical clinical data. The environment verifies both final answers and required clinical actions, and supports parallel trajectory collection for training. Benchmarking twelve vision-language models shows that current agents struggle across diverse clinical workflows, and simply increasing reasoning effort fails to yield consistent gains. Furthermore, MedUIGym supports offline learning from GUI trajectories and online reinforcement learning from task rewards. After training, both Qwen3.5 models perform better on in-domain clinical tasks and generalize to tasks from external sources. By unifying tasks from existing benchmarks, dual-interface evaluation, and GUI trajectory collection, MedUIGym provides a testbed for studying how vision-language agents complete clinical tasks through the EHR’s graphical interface. Our project page is available at 

https://github.com/yitong-qiao/MedUIGym .

## 3. MedUIGym 

3.1. Problem Formulation 

We formulate clinical GUI interaction as a structured problem-solving task: given a clinical instruction x ∈ X ,the agent generates a trajectory c ∈ C that produces a task output y ∈ Y . EHR screenshots and action feedback guide the agent’s mouse and keyboard actions. For example, it can scroll through a patient’s chart to review a prior CT, se-lect a follow-up order, type its clinical indication, and check that the order appears in the chart after saving. The out-put y comprises the final answer and, when required, saved CSV files, orders, or clinical notes. The task-specific eval-uator Ex(c, y ; y∗) scores the outcome against the reference answer or clinical requirements y∗, with partial credit where the source allows it. Existing EHR benchmarks provide task descriptions, references, and evaluation procedures, but generally do not supply GUI trajectories. For a given task,

3.2. Data Construction: From Individual Datasets to Unified Benchmark 

Task and Data Identification. MedUIGym focuses on ver-ifiable clinical tasks that require interaction with electronic health records. Clinically , we prioritize tasks grounded in patient records and established EHR benchmarks, includ-ing EHRSQL [12], EHR-Complex [21], and MedAgent-Bench [7]. Computationally , we organize these tasks into six families: information retrieval, clinical decision mak-ing, cohort queries, clinical actions, clinical documentation, and outcome prediction. These tasks range from retrieving a laboratory result and identifying a patient cohort to re-viewing longitudinal records and entering a clinical order. For outcome prediction, the agent uses the available clini-cal records to predict the outcome for an individual patient. 

Task Instructions and Reference Outcomes. To stan-dardize tasks across sources, each instance in MedUIGym 

includes (1) a problem description, (2) a reference answer or clinical requirements, and (3) the corresponding EHR data and evaluation procedure. For query tasks, the eval-uator compares the submitted answer with the result of a database query or the answer supplied by the source. For clinical actions, the evaluator checks whether the requested orders, notes, or other changes appear in the patient record. We correct task instructions and evaluators where needed, retaining the source requirements and scoring criteria for most tasks, including numerical tolerances, answer equiva-lence, and clinical checkpoints. For the 34 MedAgentBoard EHR extraction tasks [40], originally assessed by hand, the agent saves the requested records as a CSV file. Our evalua-tor compares its contents with the complete reference table. 

Data Statistics. Table 2 summarizes 150,505 tasks from 17 benchmark sources. The leaderboard includes 15,739 tasks across 102 of the 104 source-specific cate-gories. We retain up to 1,000 training and 500 test tasks per source. Eight sources supply training and test tasks and nine supply external tasks. EHR-Complex (internal) and EHR-RobustGym (external) use the same MIMIC-IV v3.1 database from PhysioNet to answer different clinical ques-tions [8, 10, 18]. 

3.3. GUI Environment: From Native Tools to GUI Interaction 

Isolated and Interactive EHR Environment. The real desktop applications in OSWorld [35] and the clinical work-flows in PhysicianUIBench [2] inspired our EHR inter-face (Figure 2). AI researchers with medical backgrounds helped design and refine the interface to reflect clinical prac-tice, including patient search, chart review, cohort queries, order entry, and documentation. Patient records, encoun-ters, laboratory results, and medications remain linked to their source data, providing the clinical context for task completion. Each task begins in an independent EHR en-vironment. Orders and notes created during one interaction are confined to that task, while the underlying clinical data remain unchanged. The GUI agent works from screenshots and action feedback, using the mouse and keyboard. Na-tive tools expose the same clinical records through database queries or FHIR requests. Both interfaces use the same clin-ical objectives and evaluators (Section 4.3). In Figure 1(b), the reference query selects patients with right upper ex-tremity cellulitis at their first admission and links their next admission to a mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) test. Each patient counts once, even if several tests are recorded during that admission. 

Interactive Feedback. MedUIGym connects agents to the interface through the standard OpenAI tools format. The system prompt specifies task rules, tool schemas define ac-tion names and parameters, and the task instruction speci-fies the required final answer. Actions issued in one model response are executed in sequence. The agent receives feed-back for each executed action and a single screenshot after the last one. ## 4. Evaluations with MedUIGym 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Agent Scaffolds. We use a common scaffold to evaluate clinical GUI agents. Interactions within MedUIGym are modeled as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Pro-cess (POMDP) [11], focusing on clinical tasks p ∈ P . The agent selects GUI actions at+1 ∈ A under policy πt us-ing the latest model-visible screenshot ot ∈ O and retained interaction history. A single response may contain several mouse or keyboard actions. The agent has up to 100 model turns and 100 actions per task, including submission. 

Tasks and Datasets. The internal split comprises 6,367 training tasks and 3,406 test tasks across eight datasets: (1) MIMIC-III [9], (2) eICU [19] from EHRSQL [12], (3) MIMICSQL/TREQS [31], (4) EHR-Complex [21], (5) EHRStruct Synthea, (6) FHIR-AgentBench, (7) MedIn-sider, and (8) Synthetic Hospital. We conduct out-of-distribution evaluation on 2,607 test tasks from EHR-SeqSQL, EHR-RobustGym, MedAgentBench v1 and v2, PhysicianBench, MEMOIR, AgentRx, and the prediction and workflow tasks of MedAgentBoard. We train on tasks drawn from the eight internal benchmark sources (Table 2). 

Baselines. We evaluate twelve models, including ten baselines and two fine-tuning backbones. Seven base-lines share the GUI scaffold and limits on all 6,013 test tasks. We vary reasoning effort for GPT-5.4 Mini (low, high), Kimi K3 (low, high, max), GLM-5.3-Flash 

(high, max), and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 (high, max). We test Qwen3.5-397B-A17B , Qwen3.6-27B ,and Qwen3.8-27B with thinking enabled and disabled. We evaluate the three remaining baselines ( †) on a fixed stratified subset of 340 ID and 261 OOD tasks. Given the high API costs of these commercial frontier models, we sample about 10% per source across all test categories. 

Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate one attempt per task and adopt success rate (SR) as the primary metric, using the source-specific evaluators for final answers and completed clinical actions. AgentRx and MedAgentBoard prediction use accuracy (Acc) . MedInsider uses Aligned Task Comple-tion (ATC) [29], the fraction of required targets completed. 4.2. Evaluation Results 

Mortality Prediction Distinguishes the ID Lead-ers on External Tasks. Among the fifteen configurations evaluated on both full test sets, 

DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 and Kimi K3 rank first and second on ID tasks at high reasoning effort (54.4 and 53.5). Their order reverses on OOD tasks (41.4 and 47.8, Tables 3 and 5). The largest difference is on AgentRx, where the models predict in-hospital mortality from the first 48 hours of ICU records. Accuracy on AgentRx is 31.1% for DeepSeek and 68.3% for Kimi, accounting for 4.1 of the 6.4 points between their average OOD scores. 

Completing All Parts of a Clinical Workflow Remains Difficult. On the full test sets, the best SR reaches 87.0% on MedAgentBench v1 and 66.8% on FHIR-AgentBench, compared with 15.5% on EHR-RobustGym and 3.0% on PhysicianBench. The same contrast appears for GPT-6.1 Terra on the shared subset: SR is 100% on MedAgentBench v1 and 10% on PhysicianBench. MedAgentBench v1 in-cludes tasks such as retrieving the latest magnesium level. PhysicianBench requires all parts of a workflow to succeed. In a case of drug-resistant urinary tract infection, the agent must assess renal function, select an antibiotic, and docu-ment its safety before the patient receives chemotherapy. 

Higher Reasoning Effort Can Improve Queries While Lowering Prediction Accuracy. Raising 

GLM-5.3-Flash from high to max effort increases SR on TREQS from 46.4% to 60.2%, while accuracy on AgentRx falls from 61.7% to 31.7%. Its ID average rises from 47.1 to 53.0 and its OOD average falls from 44.3 to 42.7. All three Qwen baselines improve with thinking enabled. For Qwen3.5-397B-A17B , the ID and OOD averages rise from 20.3 to 23.4 and from 20.1 to 27.5, respectively. For Kimi K3 , high effort gives the highest average score on both the internal and external sources. 

Scaffold Choice Can Improve Success Rates and Re-duce GUI Actions. Claude Sonnet 5.5 (max) operates the same EHR controls through Claude Code’s tool-use loop [3]. SR rises from 55.3% to 64.6% on 559 tasks with binary evaluation, with the largest gains in outcome pre-diction and longitudinal analysis (Figure 4). On the 289 tasks both scaffolds solve, the median falls from 23 GUI actions to 17. In one EHR-SeqSQL case, both correctly compared mean arterial pressure across an ICU stay. With Claude Code, one search of the ICU flowsheet retrieved all four readings. Sonnet then verified the stay dates and re-ported both changes because two readings shared the final timestamp. It completed the task in 11 actions, compared with 20 under the common scaffold. 

4.3. Native-Tool and GUI Task Completion 

On the 601 tasks evaluated on both interfaces using the same source criteria, GLM-5.3-Flash (high) solves 173 only with native tools and 81 only through the GUI (ATC 

= 1 for MedInsider). Moving from native tools to the GUI lowers the source-averaged score from 64.7 to 44.7 on ID tasks and from 69.3 to 43.5 on OOD tasks. Native tools score higher on fourteen sources. EHR-Complex SR falls from 64.7% to 5.9%, while MedAgentBench v1 SR rises from 53.3% to 80.0%. For the 396 tasks with identical in-structions, the average score across the 13 sources is 63.8 for native tools and 42.8 for the GUI. An agent can specify search conditions directly in a native query. In MedUIGym ,it must also check which chart filters remain active when it starts a new search. On a MEMOIR task, a native query returned contraceptive implant records dating back to 2012. The GUI agent also sought the full contraceptive history, but its search for mestranol retained an earlier 2018–2020 filter. It later cleared the dates but did not repeat the search. The two answers earned the same score although the records retrieved from the chart differed.

5.3. Generalization, Efficiency, and Error Analysis 

Transfer after Supervised Fine-Tuning Depends on Model Size. On external sources, SFT raises the average score for Qwen3.5-9B from 13.1 to 18.7, while the 4B av-erage falls from 13.1 to 12.2 (Table 5). Averaged across external sources, the share of tasks with higher scores after SFT is 10.8% for 9B and 6.9% for 4B. Scores decline on 5.2% and 7.8%, respectively. GRPO achieves the highest external averages for both backbones (18.6 and 22.2 for 4B and 9B). On MedAgentBoard prediction, 9B accuracy rises from 41.3% after SFT to 57.8% after GRPO. PPO yields smaller gains over SFT on external tasks for both back-bones, while DPO improves mainly on the internal sources. 

Similar GUI Action Counts Can Yield Different Task Scores. Figure 5 compares seven models on the 601-task subset. We give each of the 17 sources equal weight in the score and report the mean number of GUI actions per task. A click and a text entry count as separate actions even when the model issues both in one response. GPT-6.1 Terra achieves the highest score (75.3) with the fewest actions per task (38.1 on average). Kimi K3 and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B average a similar number of actions (63.7 and 64.7), but Kimi’s score is 23.6 points higher on the same clinical tasks. 

Agents Misread Values and Mistarget Actions. AMedAgentBoard chart recorded 30 breaths per minute. GLM-5.3-Flash reported the upper reference limit of 18 as the patient’s respiratory rate. In other tasks, the agent searched ICD procedure records for a CPT billing event and recorded a daily maximum of 50 mg for a regimen allow-ing at most 45 mg. GPT-6.1 Terra reviewed the screenshots, actions, and EHR feedback for the 601 tasks attempted by GLM-5.3-Flash (high). GUI errors appeared on 156 tasks. Figure 6 counts each error type once per task, giving 183 ob-servations. The most frequent errors were actions directed at the wrong control or record (98 tasks) and incorrectly configured queries or forms (46 tasks). The agent earned full credit on 53 of the 156 tasks. In one case, it clicked the clinical notes field while trying to save a vaccine booster or-der. The form stayed open until the agent clicked Save. The 8EHR then confirmed the order. 

## 6. Conclusion 

Clinical GUI agents must find relevant information on screen and act on it in the patient’s chart. We introduced 

MedUIGym to evaluate and train agents on this work in a simulated hospital EHR, with 15,739 tasks from 17 sources across six task families. Agents read screenshots and use a mouse and keyboard. The paired native tools use the same records and scoring criteria for answers and com-pleted clinical actions. Our evaluation of twelve vision-language models shows that workflows combining chart re-view, treatment decisions, and documentation remain dif-ficult, and gains from greater reasoning effort vary across tasks. GUI trajectories support offline learning, and task scores provide rewards for online reinforcement learning. After training, both Qwen3.5 models achieve higher aver-age scores on clinical GUI tasks from internal and external sources.
