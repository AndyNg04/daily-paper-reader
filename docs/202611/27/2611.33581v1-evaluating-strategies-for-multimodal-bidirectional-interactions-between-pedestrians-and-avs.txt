ABSTRACT
Autonomous Vehicles (AVs) share spaces with Vulnerable Road Users (VRUs) in encounters where
driver cues are absent. This can leave people uncertain whether the vehicle has noticed them and
understood their request. Existing research has studied how AVs communicate with pedestrians
through external human–machine interfaces. This work investigates bidirectional multimodal VRU–
AV interactions combining Augmented Reality (AR), speech, and gestures. The study follows four
stages: literature synthesis of interactions, a survey with 124 participants, interaction loop imple-
mentation, and feasibility evaluation. Participants preferred AR cues that made vehicle motion
or warnings explicit, high-level voice commands, and conventional gestures. Prototype measure-
ments revealed a trade-off between faster local processing and more accurate infrastructure-assisted
processing. The demonstration shows bidirectional interaction using AR wearables and vehicular
communications, while leaving decision-making to the AV. Together, the results support treating
AV–VRU interaction as a closed loop of intent expression, recognition, vehicle decision, and feed-
back.
Keywords Augmented reality, vulnerable road users, pedestrian safety, autonomous vehicles, multimodal interaction,
speech-to-text, gestures, human-centered interaction design

Introduction
The future of urban mobility is trending towards mixed autonomous traffic, with many cities already operating mixed
traffic (i.e., traditional, connected, semi-autonomous, and fully Autonomous Vehicles (AVs)). This transition is re-
flected in public-road testing and deployment programs documented by transport authorities, including the National
Highway Traffic Safety Administration AV TEST initiative1 in the US and European policy on cooperative, connected,
and automated mobility [27]. In the US, commercial robotaxi services such as Waymo already operate fully driverless,
although still constrained to operate in a limited number of cities [44]2.
This transition changes the safety problem from vehicle automation alone to mutual intelligibility in mixed traffic. An
AV must detect, classify, and plan around a Vulnerable Road User (VRU); surrounding road users must also understand
its state and intended behavior, while the vehicle infers human intent in ambiguous, negotiated interactions [57, 61].
This is particularly relevant for pedestrians, cyclists, and other VRUs, whose interactions with vehicles often rely on
cues such as yielding behavior, driver eye contact, hand gestures, head movements, and speech [61, 67].
Most research has addressed explicit AV-to-VRUs communication. External Human-Machine Interfaces (eHMIs),
projections, light bands, displays, and symbolic cues can communicate whether an AV has detected a pedestrian, is
yielding, or intends to continue moving [9, 52, 73]. These supplement implicit communication through vehicle speed,
deceleration, and trajectory, which remains the most common way for road interaction. Explicit bidirectional commu-
nication becomes relevant when those cues are ambiguous or when either party needs to express a request or warning.

Recent work shows that people may expect their own signals to be recognized and that pedestrian communication
includes more than a binary crossing request [8, 15, 78]. A future AV interaction system should, therefore, support
VRU-initiated input and report whether it was recognized and accepted.
This paper addresses this gap by studying bidirectional multimodal interaction between Augmented Reality (AR)-
equipped VRUs and AVs. Wearable AR presents spatially situated, egocentric, and user-specific feed in the VRUs’s
Field of View (FOV) [1, 10, 75], while the wearable system captures speech and hand movement. Voice and gesture
allow the VRU to express high-level intents, such as asking the AV to stop, slow down, or proceed. After recognition,
a request can reach the vehicle through a direct vehicular link or through cellular or roadside infrastructure, with
processing placed locally or in a nearby computing service; this placement may change feedback delay and recognition
accuracy. This setup is deliberately interchangeable, working towards the human-facing requirement, which is the
explicit feedback to the user that closes the interaction loop.
Figure 1 illustrates the complete bidirectional multimodal interaction sequence. As the AV approaches, the AR headset
presents vehicle state or intent; the VRU responds through speech, gesture, or both; the recognized request is relayed
to the vehicle; and the AV validates it before returning an explicit acknowledgment through AR. In the illustrated
example, the vehicle accepts the pedestrian’s stopping request and communicates that it is yielding, thereby closing
the interaction loop. Vehicle motion remains visible throughout and should remain consistent with vehicle response.
This paper makes the following contributions:
• We formulate a closed-loop, bidirectional multimodal interaction that restores a channel for human agency
while preserving the AV’s responsibility, and synthesize candidate vocabularies for AR, speech, and gesture
interactions.
• Through an online survey with 124 participants, we provide empirical evidence about which AR cues, voice
commands, and gestures people perceive as clear and applicable, while identifying ambiguity, semantic over-
lap, and cultural bias.
• We implement the complete interaction loop across a wearable device, speech and gesture recognition, vehic-
ular communication, and an AV, and demonstrate it in a real outdoor environment. Additionally, we evaluate
its technical feasibility through key performance indicators.
• We evaluate response delay and speech-recognition error against human-interaction and safety reference
points and previous studies.
• We derive design implications for closed-loop multimodal interaction between AVs and VRUs, including
intent-level commands, optional modalities, uncertainty handling, and fail-safe behavior.

Related Work
Recent literature on VRU–AV interactions has focused primarily on how an AV communicates explicitly with pedestri-
ans, alongside work on implicit communication through vehicle behavior. Comparatively fewer studies examine how
pedestrians or other road users can intentionally communicate back and receive explicit acknowledgments. System-
atic reviews show that this body of work remains methodologically focused on Virtual Reality (VR) and Wizard-of-Oz
experiments [73, 74]. Tran et al. [73], for example, reviewed 31 VR studies on AV–pedestrian interaction and showed
that most relied on controlled simulations.
This type of interaction approach can be categorized as system-initiated from the VRU’s perspective because it is
initiated by the AV that detects the pedestrian, assesses the situation, and communicates its state or intent. External
Human-Machine Interfaces (HMIs), projected cues, light bands, textual displays, and symbolic interfaces have been
explored to communicate yielding, awareness, or right-of-way negotiation [9, 49, 52]. These interfaces differ in visual
form and placement, but they share a common assumption that the pedestrian receives and interprets information
produced by the vehicle correctly. This assumption becomes problematic, as these interactions are challenged by
perception and social factors, as pedestrians must infer whether the communicated intention applies to them or to a
nearby person [41, 58, 61]. In contrast, this work explores explicit participant-initiated interaction, where VRUs send
a request, acknowledgment, or warning, and receive feedback about whether it was understood.
The current state-of-the-art has shifted from asking whether an eHMI should exist to asking what it should commu-
nicate and whether the message remains understandable in complex traffic. Both messages that describe a vehicle’s
state and messages that describe what it plans to do next can also mislead if they are inconsistent with vehicle mo-
tion [24, 39]. Holländer et al. [38], for example, showed that highly legible or reassuring interfaces can also create
overtrust if pedestrians rely on the display without checking whether vehicle behavior is consistent with the message.
Scalability further complicates this problem. Single-vehicle crossing concepts may become ambiguous with multiple
vehicles, pedestrians, lanes, or shared-space trajectories. Additionally, such scenarios might create information over-
load in users [16, 74, 77]. Richer forms of spatial information, including intended paths, vehicle connectivity, and
shared space design, have also been explored in [76, 82, 83].
Compared with this mature body of AV-to-VRU signaling, VRU-to-AV communication remains less developed. This
gap matters because pedestrians and cyclists repeatedly signal urgency, hesitation, permission, warnings, and negoti-
ation through gestures, gaze, posture, movement, and speech. Colley et al. [15] showed that pedestrians may expect
acknowledgment when they initiate interaction with an AV, suggesting that bidirectional systems should detect signals
and communicate whether they were understood. Gesture-mapping studies further show that pedestrian-to-driver com-
munication includes stopping, proceeding, urgency, warnings, obstacle avoidance, and help-seeking, rather than only
crossing intent [78]. The challenge for bidirectional systems is, therefore, to select a small set of inputs that are mean-
ingful, socially plausible, recognizable in real time, and safe enough to be acknowledged by an AV. Epke et al. [25]
showed that pedestrians could signal through gestures, the vehicle responded through an eHMI, and participants pre-
ferred the two-way condition. The present work extends that direction by implementing a wearable, multimodal loop
and by testing it with an AV.
Gesture and voice are promising and under-explored candidates for this active direction. VR and Mixed Reality (MR)
studies suggest that familiar gestures can improve interaction efficiency and usability, while poorly chosen gestures
can create ambiguity about meaning, target, or authority [8, 36]. Gesture recognition techniques can support rapid
prototyping, but they do not remove social and cultural variability in public gestures [22, 79]. Speech can state intents
more explicitly than a gesture, yet it is affected by language accents, privacy concerns, noise from road traffic, and
overlapping speakers. There is also a higher degree of complexity to encompass open-ended commands that may
imply more control than the system can grant [84]. Therefore, multimodal work should add multiple and optional
interaction channels to support safer degradation and accessibility of the most viable channel [20]. Interaction inside
AVs has likewise combined voice and pointing gestures to support spontaneous requests [64]. Although that system
addressed vehicle occupants rather than external road users, these challenges directly reinforce the closed-loop design
adopted here.
Wearables and AR devices can support vehicle-initiated feedback and VRU-initiated input. In the first direction, they
can present vehicle state, collision warnings, crossing advice, or spatially registered hazards directly in the VRU’s FOV
[10]. In the second, they can capture explicit requests or confirmations from the VRU. Prior work on wearable AR

found that such interfaces can reduce cognitive load in multi-vehicle scenarios and that participants may prefer sending
a crossing request to waiting for detection [75]. An outdoor study by Aleva et al. [1] showed that head-locked cues
maximize visibility over vehicle- or world-locked cues, but may require additional confirmation against the real traffic
scene. SafeARCross demonstrated AR-based collision warnings and virtual traffic lights using connected vehicles in a
real setting; its 20-participant study reported improved perceived safety and reduced cognitive workload compared to
an unassisted crossing [10]. Related work on cyclists and pedestrians further shows that AR can also support hazard
awareness, crossing decisions, and safety-oriented feedback in dynamic road environments [50, 68, 80]. Prior work
also used a Microsoft HoloLens 2 to reveal occluded connected vehicles [12]. The reported delivery latency fell below
the 300 ms timing reference for safety-oriented road applications in ETSI TS 101 539-3 [26].
The literature contains more information on how an AV should signal to a VRU than about how a VRU should initiate
and convey an interaction. It also knows more about isolated modalities than about closed, bidirectional systems in
which AVs and VRU can communicate back and forth, expressing intents and receiving acknowledgments. However,
these strands are usually evaluated separately. Unlike prior work that evaluates isolated AV-to-pedestrian signals or
isolated input modalities, this paper jointly evaluates combined multimodal interaction channels, while examining how
people want to communicate with AVs, how the system should make recognition and responsibility visible, and what
implementation constraints may interrupt that loop.
3
Interaction Design
The proposed interaction design treats VRU–AV communication as a closed interaction loop. Road users normally
rely on implicit cues, including vehicle kinematics and pedestrian movement, while occasionally using explicit cues
when acknowledgment or negotiation is needed [45]. Removing the driver leaves vehicle motion intact, but removes
person-specific acknowledgment and the ability to signal back. The proposed loop supplements those implicit cues
with reciprocal input and feedback.
3.1
Interaction Goals
The design goal is mutual intelligibility: the VRU should understand the vehicle’s current state and intended behavior,
while the vehicle-side system should receive the VRU’s expressed intent. This restores limited human agency, while
the AV retains authority and responsibility for safe navigation. This framing follows prior work, showing that AV–
pedestrian interaction should not be one-directional [15]. Building on this view, we define a bidirectional interaction
as three actions. First, the vehicle communicates state or intended motion. Second, the VRU expresses a high-level
intent, such as asking the vehicle to stop, proceed, slow down, wait for additional pedestrians, or avoid an obstacle.
Third, the system reports what it recognized and whether the AV accepted or rejected the request.
The three modalities address different parts of this loop. Wearable AR is the primary AV-to-VRU channel, while
vehicle kinematics remain the implicit cue against which its messages should be checked. Unlike a vehicle-mounted
display, wearable AR can show egocentric, user-specific, and spatially situated information in the VRU’s own FOV,
which is useful when several road users or vehicles are present [1, 10, 75]. Its role is to clarify vehicle state, warnings,
and acknowledgments.
Voice interaction is used as an explicit VRU-to-AV intent channel. Speech allows the VRU to express short, high-
level requests; therefore, candidate vocabulary focuses on negotiation acts that are plausible in crossing encounters,
including stopping, proceeding, slowing down, waiting for pedestrians, and obstacle avoidance. These commands are
intentionally framed as semantic intents rather than trajectory instructions, because external road users should not be
responsible for detailed vehicle path planning. Additionally, these commands must be interpreted and validated by the
AV before any vehicle action is taken.
Gestures provide a complementary VRU-to-AV input channel. They are particularly relevant when the environment
is noisy, when speaking is socially inappropriate, when the user cannot or does not want to speak, or when a physical
gesture can reinforce the intended meaning of a spoken command. However, gestures are also culturally situated and
can be ambiguous when multiple vehicles or pedestrians are present [22, 78]. For this reason, our gesture design
emphasizes simple and conventional safety-relevant signals, while treating gestures primarily as an optional channel
or disambiguating evidence rather than mandatory or independent commands. Nevertheless, we included multiple
gesture candidates for the same interaction intent and gestures that do not pair directly with any voice command. This
enabled us to compare alternative gesture mappings for the same intent.
Taken together, the loop proceeds as follows: the vehicle communicates state or intent to a VRU; the VRU makes a
request through voice, gesture, or both; the system recognizes and transmits the intent directly or via intelligent infras-
tructure; the AV validates whether the request is safe and feasible; and AR feedback confirms the vehicle response.

3.2
Multimodal Interactions
Six candidates were selected for AV–VRU feedback interfaces to cover both directions of interaction. Figure 2 il-
lustrates them. These are designed to assist pedestrians at crossings using visual notifications, conventional road
symbolism, and spatial overlays.
Derived from concept 8 [68], Yielding to Cross (I1) displayed a textual yielding cue “YIELDING TO CROSS” in blue
tones, featuring a pedestrian crossing a crosswalk symbol on the left. Likewise, Vehicle Approaching (I2) presented a
red warning overlay and a textual alert “VEHICLE APPROACHING” with a stop sign on the left side. The stop sign
was chosen due to its widespread use; the crosswalk sign, on the other hand, is a bit more ambiguous, but still widely
recognizable in its ability to be interpreted as a pedestrian-related task/zone. Forty European countries are part of this
convention, making it a recognizable candidate in many European contexts [14]. The Heads-up Display (HUD) is
designed in a notification style in the top center of the user’s FOV, following guidelines for text positions in complex
environments, preserving visibility of the walking area [42]. The white text contrasts with the colored background
plate, offering readability [33, 59].
Interfaces I4 and I5 adapt the virtual traffic light cue family implemented in Clérigo et al.’s work [10]. A green or
red 3D traffic light model that appears at the user’s leftmost side FOV to communicate advice through familiar traffic
conventions. The traffic light did not include a pedestrian symbol, as it was envisioned to be used also for connected
cyclists.
Directional Arrow (I3) was inspired by concept 4, which is meant to represent a field of safe travel [35]. The field
at any given moment represents the paths that a car may take unimpeded, and it would look like a “sort of tongue
protruding forward along the road” [43]. While Tabone et al.’s approach presents a highlighted road region colored
red or green to indicate whether the denoted zones could be safely crossed [37], our designs projected an arrow onto
the road scene to visualize the vehicle’s intended direction of motion. This follows prior work that shows the value of
explicit spatial intent visualization [83], giving additional information to users to decide whether to cross or not.
Based on concept 1, Projected Crosswalk (I6) displays a virtual crosswalk-like overlay aligned with the road surface,
representing a spatially anchored safe-crossing cue inspired by previous projected and context-aware crossing inter-
faces. Instead of being anchored to the road, it is instead anchored to the vehicle, just like concept 1. Additionally,
it contains a small billboard-like notification attached to the vehicle, just like concept 2, with a text cue “SAFE TO
CROSS” in gray tones, just like the crosswalk projected pattern.
Some of these concepts have already been validated in real-world scenarios. A previous study has integrated Inter-
faces I2, I4, and I5 on an AR safety application [10] (see Section 2).

plausible in pedestrian–vehicle encounters. The use of the word “car” acts similarly to a lightweight wake word or
directed-speech marker, helping to separate target-directed commands from unrelated speech [71, 81].
The gestures, depicted in Figure 3, were presented as nine short looping videos for VRU–AV commands. These were

Interaction System Prototype
Following the interaction design in Section 3, the prototype is described to show how human-facing interactions can
be connected and where delays or recognition failures can enter the loop. At the interaction level, the system requires
the capabilities to capture a person’s speech and gestures, recognize and convey the intent to the vehicle, and return an
explicit acknowledgment or vehicle response. We have chosen the Microsoft HoloLens 2 as the AR headset, primarily
due to its popularity in research and availability.

The communication can be direct between the road users or assisted by cellular or roadside infrastructure, reflecting
heterogeneous data transmission between road users within smart cities [60, 69]. The system uses standardized ve-
hicular communication. Cooperative Awareness Messages (CAMs) [30] and VRU Awareness Messages (VAMs) [28]
represent road user state, while event-oriented Decentralized Environmental Notification Messages (DENMs) [29]
carry interaction-related requests and responses. These standards provide an interoperable implementation enabling
different radio technologies and computing locations to preserve the timing and reliability of the interaction.
The implemented interaction loop contains five steps. First, the AV communicates state or intent through its motion
and vehicular messages. Second, wearable AR presents the corresponding information to the person. Third, the
person may express a request using speech, gestures, or both. Fourth, the system recognizes, classifies, and transmits
the input. Fifth, the vehicle determines whether the request is safe and feasible. Its motion remains the primary
observable evidence of execution, while AR interface explicitly reports whether the request was accepted or rejected.

The interaction’s flow starts with a VRU speaking voice commands while wearing an AR headset that captures the
audio and relays it to the VRU compute device. For this purpose, we rely on the HoloLens 2 Sensor Streaming frame-
work [21, 65]3. Transcription may then run on the person-carried device or on a nearby remote service. The prototype
uses 5G connectivity as a conceptual offloading strategy to place processing wherever the delay and recognition quality
is best for user experience.
Finally, the Natural Language Understanding (NLU) unit derives the VRU’s intention and triggers an interaction event
that the processing service translates to the vehicular communication layer. In the current prototype, this event is
carried using a DENM. This design draws from previous HoloLens-based assistive systems that combine perception
and audio feedback for object localization [23].
Several tools were tested for ASR. Vosk [2] offers live transcription, but has limitations in model robustness and
accuracy. Moreover, it has less support for multilingual variants than other tools. Whisper [55] is OpenAI’s state-
of-the-art ASR offering multiple models with different accuracy and latency. However, its original implementation
only processes discrete audio, which limits its use in real-time applications. Finally, Kaldi [54], which is widely used
in research, offers a customizable framework. However, for the purposes of this work, its complexity was deemed
unnecessary. Considering this, we explored the use of WhisperLive [13, 72], an ASR tool that combines the accuracy
of Whisper and continuous transcription of Vosk. WhisperLive leverages Faster Whisper [66] based on CTranslate2,
and integrates voice activity detection through Silero VAD [72], reducing unnecessary processing during silence and
improving Whisper’s abilities for real-time recognition. Sherpa-ONNX [40] is also an alternative for more self-
contained speech processing, mainly keyword detections. This makes it a relevant option for future versions that are
dependent on embedded systems and do not require the recognition of complex phrases.
For intent recognition, we chose Rasa [5], which provides the NLU layer responsible for mapping natural-language
utterances into predefined intents. It was configured with tokenization and lexical features, followed by the Dual Intent
and Entity Transformer classifier [6] with 400 epochs. A fallback classifier has a confidence threshold of 0.8, to prevent
uncertain utterances from becoming vehicle-directed messages. This threshold operates on the human-centered safety
principle that it is preferable to ask for clarification or to take no action than to make an unintended request.
Given the scenarios described in Section 5, we defined Rasa’s NLU with extensive examples for the five intents:
proceed, stop, slow down, wait for more pedestrians, and avoid obstacles. Each intent contained around 20-30 ex-
ample phrases, encompassing various natural speech variations. The use of “car” in the command examples acts as
an addressing cue. This follows the same principle as wake word or keyword-triggered interaction to reduce false
activations [71, 81]. Once an intent is recognized with sufficient confidence, the system publishes the corresponding
event to the communication layer, allowing the VRU’s intention to be encoded and transmitted toward the AV.

Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) is used for gesture matching. The method computes a nonlinear temporal alignment
between the live sequence and each stored template, allowing users to perform the same mid-air gesture at different
speeds [62]. Previous literature has also used DTW for skeleton-based gesture recognition using joint positions [7].
Multiple templates can be associated with a target gesture, with each template containing a normalized frame sequence
and a configurable DTW threshold. This allows for new gestures to be created and then tuned empirically. At run-
time, the recognizer compares the most recent live motion window against each stored template. After a gesture is
recognized, the window buffer is cleared, so that the same movement does not get repeatedly triggered. This design
supports real-time interaction with high recognition behavior, which is important because ambiguous or incorrectly
recognized gestures could otherwise lead to unsafe or confusing VRU–AV communication [8, 36].

Online Survey
The following section discusses the online survey conducted, outlining participant demographics, methodology, and
data collected to evaluate user preference on multimodal interactions with AVs. We structure the evaluation around
four research questions:
RQ1. Which AR interfaces are perceived as clearest for conveying an AV’s intent to a VRU?
RQ2. Which voice commands and gestures are perceived as most applicable for VRUs to communicate intent to
AVs?
RQ3. When voice and gesture are combined, do participants naturally select semantically corresponding gestures for
safety-relevant voice commands?
RQ4. What do these preferences imply for the design of a bidirectional multimodal interaction loop between AR-
equipped VRUs and AVs?
These questions evaluate which of these alternatives were perceived as clearest and most applicable before prioritizing
them in the implementation and technical validation.
5.1
Participants
We recruited one hundred and twenty-four participants (N=124), including thirty female and ninety-four male, primar-
ily using social contacts and social media posts. Most participants (76%) were 20–24 years old. This group is relevant
to the likely early adoption of AR technologies [48], and should not be treated as a representative broader popula-
tion. Because recruitment occurred mainly through Portuguese networks, only seven participants used the English
questionnaire; therefore, results should be interpreted as predominantly situated in a Portuguese cultural and linguistic
context.
In order to classify the user’s affinity with technology, we used the Affinity for Technology Interaction (ATI) scale
[31]. The sample had a medium-high affinity for technology, with an average ATI score of 4.23 (SD = 0.75), and
acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.80).
5.2
Design
The survey was conducted entirely online using LimeSurvey [47], which contained videos and audio samples. All
participants conducted the survey independently.
Participants were asked to imagine themselves as VRUs approaching or negotiating a road crossing with an AV, where
no human driver was available for eye contact or informal negotiation. The survey contained three sections, one for
each modality: AR interfaces, voice commands, and gestures. The scenario framed the AR interfaces as information
received by the VRU, and the voice and gesture alternatives as possible ways for the VRU to communicate intent
back to the vehicle. Section 3 describes the voice commands and illustrates the interfaces and gestures presented to
participants.

5.3
Measurements and Analysis
The questionnaire combined quantitative and qualitative measurements. For each interaction family, participants rated
individual alternatives using 7-point Likert scales. AR interfaces were rated for perceived clarity, while voice com-
mands and gestures were rated for perceived applicability. At the end of each modality block, participants also ranked
the alternatives to explicitly extract a preference value [3]. In the voice–gesture pairing questions, participants selected
which gesture they considered most appropriate to accompany each voice command. Finally, optional open-ended
questions collected free-text feedback about each interaction family.
For each interaction family, omnibus differences across alternatives were tested using Friedman tests. Kendall’s W is
reported as the repeated-measures effect size. Where omnibus tests were significant, pairwise post-hoc comparisons
were conducted using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction. Participant rankings were reconstructed into
preference scores and analyzed with the same Friedman/Wilcoxon procedure.
Gesture selections in the voice–gesture pairing task were analyzed using chi-square goodness-of-fit tests against a
uniform distribution, followed by binomial tests for the expected semantic match against chance (p = 1/9). To assess
consistency between ratings and rankings, we computed participant-level Spearman correlations between Likert ratings
and the Borda-style ranking preferences.
Open-ended responses were analyzed as exploratory design feedback. We performed preliminary single-coder open
coding to identify recurring concerns and suggestions that could help interpret the quantitative trends.
As an exploratory check, we also examined whether modality scores varied with participant characteristics. Gen-
der and questionnaire-language differences were tested using Mann–Whitney U tests, age-group differences using
Kruskal–Wallis tests, and associations with ATI scores using Spearman correlations. These analyses were treated
as exploratory because the sample was unevenly distributed across demographic groups. The rank-based tests were
selected because the outcomes were ordinal and the demographic groups were uneven; they do not require normally
distributed scores. The analyses were treated as exploratory, and small groups were excluded from age comparisons as
reported in subsection A.2, because the recruitment distribution does not support strong population-level demographic
claims.

Results
6.1
Online Survey Results
We report the results in three stages. First, we summarize preferences for individual AR, voice, and gesture alterna-
tives. Second, we examine whether participants paired voice commands with semantically corresponding gestures.
Third, we synthesize the statistical and open-ended findings.
Full omnibus and selected post-hoc statistical details are reported in subsection A.1.
6.1.1
Preferences for Individual Modalities
The perceived clarity of the AR interfaces differed significantly across designs (Friedman χ2(5, N = 124) = 76.08,
p < .001, Kendall’s W = .12). As shown in Figure 7, the Directional Arrow (I3) (M = 5.73, median = 6) and Vehicle
Approaching (I2) (M = 5.70, median = 6) were the clearest interfaces. They were also the two strongest ranked
options, with the Directional Arrow selected as the first option 41% of times, while the Vehicle Approaching was
chosen 25.8% of times. This indicates that participants generally ranked highly AR interfaces that they rated highly.
The pairwise post-hoc comparison between these two leading interfaces was not significant after correction. Post-
hoc group letters in Figure 7 summarize Holm-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank comparisons within each interaction
family; items sharing a letter did not differ significantly.
Revisiting RQ1, results suggest that Directional Arrow (I3) and Vehicle Approaching (I2) are the clearest AR inter-
faces for conveying an AV’s intention to a VRU. Nevertheless, all AR designs, even the lower-rated AR designs, were
rated above the neutral midpoint after Holm correction. This pattern suggests that participants understood the gen-
eral purpose of all visual interfaces, but preferred designs that directly communicated motion direction or imminent
warnings.
Voice command applicability also differed significantly across alternatives (Friedman χ2(4, N = 124) = 97.47,
p < .001, Kendall’s W = .20). The highest mean ratings were observed for Slow Down (C4) (M = 5.27, median
= 6) and Stop (C1) (M = 5.20, median = 6) voice commands, and their pairwise difference was not significant after
Holm correction. However, the ranking results revealed a clearer preference, with Stop (C1) ranked first by 67.7% of
participants, while Slow Down (C4) was ranked first by only 10.5%.

6.1.2
Voice–Gesture Pairing
The voice-gesture pairing task focused on evaluating the best gesture fit to accompany a voice command. Figure 8
shows the selected gestures to accompany three representative voice commands: Stop (C1), Proceed (C2), and Slow
Down (C4). These commands were selected because they cover distinct intents. Limiting the task to these representa-
tive cases also reduced the participant burden after rating and ranking tasks.
Gesture selections were non-uniform for all three tested voice commands: Stop (C1) (χ2(8, N = 124) = 715.03,
p < .001, Cohen’s w = 2.40), Proceed (C2) (χ2(8, N = 124) = 759.16, p < .001, w = 2.47), and Slow Down
(C4) (χ2(8, N = 124) = 597.74, p < .001, w = 2.20). The direct semantic match was selected by 86.3% of
participants for Stop (C1), 88.7% for Proceed (C2), and 79.8% for Slow Down (C4). Exact binomial tests showed
that all three direct matches were above chance (p < .001). Addressing RQ3, results show that participants showed a
strong tendency to pair voice commands with semantically corresponding gestures (Figure 8).
These results support the multimodal agreement as a safety mechanism rather than redundancy. For example, a spoken
Stop accompanied by a raised-palm gesture can provide converged evidence about intent, whereas a spoken mis-
matched pair should trigger a fail-safe state or no vehicle action.
For each participant, we computed Spearman correlations between item ratings and ranking preference scores within
each interaction family. Median participant-level correlations were high for AR interfaces (ρ = .824), voice commands
(ρ = .718), and gestures (ρ = .766), with all one-sample Wilcoxon tests against zero yielding p < .001. This indicates
that participants generally ranked highly the same items that they rated highly. Exploratory participant-characteristic
checks are summarized in subsection A.2.
Across the three commands, 316 of 372 selections (84.9%) corresponded to the expected semantic gesture, while 56
selections (15.1%) used another gesture. Most of these mismatches were not strongly counter-semantic; therefore,
this variation reflects semantically adjacent gestures that could be used for the same command. A small number of
counter-semantic pairings were manually inspected; most occurred only once.

The Slow Down (C4) voice command showed the most variability in gesture pairing, with 25 participants not selecting
the expected gesture. However, many of these alternatives can still be interpreted as a slowing down cue: Go Back
(G4) was selected by 10 participants, Stop (G8) and Emergency Stop (G2) by 4 participants each, and T-Pose (G5)
by 2 participants. All of these imply reducing or preventing forward vehicle motion, but do not express “slow down”
explicitly. This interpretation could be viewed as participants not requiring the granularity between stopping and
slowing down, since stopping-related gestures already imply slowing down. The remaining mismatches were rare:
Follow Me (G3) was selected twice, while Proceed (G6), Wait for Pedestrians (G9), and Avoid Obstacle (G1) were
each selected once. These mismatches are supported by open-ended comments noting that movement-based gestures
could be confused.
The Stop command (C1) produced 17 non-matching selections, but the majority were still semantically close. Emer-
gency Stop (G2) accounted for 10 of these selections, which is semantically consistent with stopping but uses both
hands. T-Pose (G5) and Go Back (G4) were each selected twice, and can also be read as hold-position or retreat
signals. Slow Down (G7) was selected once, and it too can be viewed as a way to reflect a halting cue.
The Proceed command (C2) produced 14 non-matching selections. The most common was Follow Me (G3), selected
by 7 participants, which is plausible because it invites forward movement. The most frequent anomalous case was
the pairing with Stop (G8) by 4 participants. One participant explicitly noted in the open-ended questions that the
proceed command could be interpreted as signaling a pedestrian to cross, creating a safety risk if the vehicle interprets
it; therefore, the users meant to use the gesture for the vehicle to stop so another pedestrian could proceed. The
remaining Proceed mismatches, T-Pose (G5), Wait for Pedestrians (G9), and Go Back (G4), each occurred once.
One methodological factor may also have contributed to these mismatches. The matching screen reused numbered
labels for gestures, but the display order differed from the earlier gesture-rating section. Therefore, participants who
relied on remembered gesture numbers rather than the displayed video could select the unintended gesture. For
example, one participant matched Follow Me (G3) with Stop (C1), which corresponded to Gesture 7, which had
denoted Stop in the previous gesture-rating section.
These results point to two sources of gesture ambiguity: (1) semantic overlap between safety-related gestures, and (2)
uncertainty about the target of a gesture. Even though most participants paired the expected gesture with the voice
command, these findings reinforce the need to treat gestures as confirmation input rather than isolated commands. This
agreement can increase confidence, while mismatched signals should make uncertainty visible and trigger confirmation
or a fail-safe response.
6.1.3
Open-ended Feedback
In total, 29 participants commented on the AR interfaces, 13 commented on voice commands, and 16 commented on
gestures.
The open-ended AR feedback clarifies why some familiar-looking interfaces did not dominate the ratings. A recurring
concern was that traffic light symbols could be misinterpreted. There were 18 participants who explicitly suggested re-
placing generic traffic lights with pedestrian traffic-light symbols. Eight participants noted that the projected crosswalk
could be misread if its orientation did not align with the pedestrian’s crossing direction. Six participants suggested
combining or layering cues, with one suggesting pairing a directional arrow with an approaching vehicle warning, so
that the VRU sees both the vehicle’s intended motion and the required pedestrian action.
As described in Section 3, the traffic lights’ interfaces had unmarked bulbs because the design was intended to remain
applicable beyond pedestrians, including connected cyclists and other VRUs. The open-ended responses showed the
trade-off of that choice, since the improvement in cross-VRU compatibility reduced pedestrian-specific clarity in a
crossing task. A practical solution would be to make the symbol changes according to the detected or self-declared
road-user type. This would preserve the broader VRU framing without forcing pedestrians to interpret a symbol that
they associate primarily with vehicle traffic.
Regarding voice commands, 4 participants stressed that commands could be too short and not acoustically distinct.
Detailed commands, such as obstacle avoidance, were criticized as ambiguous or as placing the pedestrian in the role
of coordinating traffic, appearing in 4 responses. This supports the quantitative results and indicates that some users
may not want the burden nor the capability to coordinate traffic. Broader concerns about voice reliability, misuse, or
conflicting commands from multiple VRUs appeared in 6 responses.
Gesture comments emphasized ambiguity, as 8 participants noted that gestures that share an initial posture or overall
momentum could be difficult to remember. Three participants suggested favoring the use of simpler, faster, or one-
handed gestures. Together with the quantitative results, these comments indicate that gestures should only be used

when they are simple and clear, and that the AR system should always confirm whether the intended command was
recognized.
No participant explicitly mentioned false activations. Because the questionnaire presented fixed examples rather than
a live recognizer, this absence should not be interpreted as evidence that unintended activation is unimportant.
6.2
Prototype Feasibility Results
The prototype evaluation asks whether the candidate modalities can work as a closed loop with timing and recognition
characteristics that justify a subsequent human evaluation of the situation.
6.2.1
Latency and Recognition
Vehicle-initiated AR feedback was evaluated in prior work and was not rebenchmarked here. In a peer-to-peer config-
uration using the European wireless vehicular communication standard ITS-G5, delivery to the AR display required
81.53 ± 8.36 ms E2E [11, 12]. This prior result falls below the 300 ms reference for safety-related applications [26].
In the controlled setup, retrieving the HoloLens microphone stream introduced approximately 166 ms under both wired
and Wi-Fi conditions. The infrastructure-assisted processed speech pipeline reached 440.04 ms. The representative
local configuration reached an E2E intent-recognition delay of 365.63 ms. Figure 9 decomposes these delays for two
representative configurations: infrastructure-assisted ASR using the Turbo model from Whisper and local ASR using
the Base model from Whisper. These configurations differ in both computing placement and model.

The DTW gesture templates contain varying numbers of frames with 11 joints per frame. At the configured 20 ms
fixed-update interval, their required motion-sampling time depends on the gesture. Once the final sample is available,
matching and event dispatch occur on that update, with at most 20 ms of scheduling delay.
The communication delay has different meanings for the two representative configurations. For local processing, it
corresponds to the microphone latency. For Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) offloading, it includes 5G transport
and the overhead introduced by adapting the service to support offloading. Communication delay also encompasses
all inter-service transport latency, ITS-G5 broadcast, and vehicle-side command interpretation; vehicle actuation has
already been explored in previous work [11, 56]. Rasa intent recognition remained approximately constant, around
15 ms.
The findings do not identify one preferred computing placement. In the representative configurations presented, local
processing produced lower E2E latency but higher transcription error, whereas the infrastructure-assisted configuration
reduced transcription error with an additional communication overhead. From a human-centered perspective, this
shifts risk between waiting longer without feedback and being misunderstood more often. An infrastructure-neutral
system should adapt when possible and, regardless of placement, use confidence gating, agreement across modalities

6.2.2
Integrated Demonstration
A demonstration was conducted on the Aveiro Tech City Living Lab (ATCLL) platform [60] in Portugal. The demon-
stration video5 shows a VRU wearing a Microsoft HoloLens 2 and a Raspberry Pi 5 compute device interacting with
a PIXKIT 2.06 Autonomous Driving Development Kit. Speech, gesture, vehicular communication, vehicle-side han-
dling, and AR acknowledgment completed the implemented loop outdoors. This closes an important feasibility gap
left by survey-only and simulation-only work.
7
Discussion and Limitations
The survey and prototype provide complementary evidence about candidate interactions and their implementability.
This work extends evidence that pedestrians value feedback after initiating communication [15] by adding an at-
tributable wearable AR and a multimodal path to a physical autonomous platform. Representative systems addressed
complementary parts of the loop: VoGe [64] combined speech and pointing for vehicle occupants; I See Your Ges-
ture [25] paired pedestrian gestures with a vehicle response in VR; and SafeARCross [10] provided wearable AR
feedback without a pedestrian-to-vehicle input. This present prototype combines external speech and gesture requests
with wearable feedback and vehicle-side validation in one outdoor demonstration.
7.1
Design Implications
Addressing RQ4, the combined questionnaire and prototype results lead to the following design implications.
Prioritize explicit AV motion intent in AR.
The strongest AR interfaces represented future vehicle behavior
through a projected Directional Arrow (I3) and a Vehicle Approaching (I2) warning. Visual feedback should therefore
avoid symbolic cues when a clear motion cue or explicit warning can be shown.
Adapt traffic light symbols to the road-user role.
Eighteen respondents requested pedestrian-specific traffic light
symbols. At pedestrian crossings, pedestrian iconography should be used rather than an unmarked traffic light. A
broader VRU system should adapt the symbol to a detected or self-declared road user role.
Keep voice commands at the intent level.
Participants favored Stop (C1), Slow Down (C4), and Proceed (C2) over
more detailed commands. Voice should express a negotiation intent rather than prescribe a vehicle trajectory.
Keep Gestures simple and conventional.
Stop (G8), Emergency Stop (G2), and T-Pose (G5) were rated higher
because they resemble familiar road traffic movements. These are also simpler gestures that require less motion than
the other alternatives shown to the user. Gesture vocabularies should therefore remain small, safety-relevant, and easy
to perform and recognize.
Treat gestures as disambiguation signals.
Participants usually paired speech with semantically corresponding ges-
tures. The technical system can exploit this by increasing confidence when modalities agree and entering a fail-safe
when they conflict. Gestures should remain optional because traffic noise, occlusion, lighting, and social context affect
which modality is viable.
Make recognition, uncertainty, and responsibility visible.
The interface should distinguish at least four states:
input received, intent recognized, request accepted or rejected, and vehicle response in progress. This prevents silence
misinterpretation. The AV, not the VRU, remains responsible for deciding whether and how to act.
Explicit feedback must remain consistent with vehicle kinematics
Because the timings of messages can alter
crossing decisions and create overtrust [18, 38], explicit feedback should progress through the interaction stages and
match the vehicle’s real-life actions.

7.2
Human-facing Interpretation of Prototype Performance
There is no single latency requirement that applies to every stage of the proposed interaction. Human conversation,
an automotive voice assistant, a negotiated road request, and a collision warning operate under different expectations
and consequences. Likewise, the literature does not establish a universal WER or CER threshold at which an external
road user command becomes safe. Table 1 presents reference points for these requirements, and compares them to the
prototype’s results.
The comparison yields two additional design implications. First, the system should expose an immediate “input
received” state, because speech intent recognition arrives later than the timing of an ordinary conversational agent.
Second, the speech interaction must remain a request-and-acknowledgment channel. It cannot replace the vehicle’s
independent perception and collision-prediction functions, because it exceeds the 300 ms threshold [26].
7.3
Limitations
This work has several limitations that should guide the interpretation of the results. First, the online survey evaluated
perceived clarity and applicability rather than situated crossing behavior. Participants did not experience risk, time
pressure, vehicle motion, road noise, social presence, or a real street crossing task. The results therefore identify
promising candidates and principles but do not demonstrate effects on behavior, trust, safety, or workload
Second, the participant sample was was young, mostly Portuguese, male-skewed, and pedestrian-oriented. The ex-
ploratory age effects reported in subsection A.2 further suggest that demographic differences should not be treated as
negligible.
Third, the interaction vocabulary is culturally and contextually situated. Apart from the speech commands being
language-sensitive, some signs, traffic light conventions, and gestures may be familiar in Portugal or broader European
contexts, but may not transfer directly to other regions or road cultures.
Fourth, the speech-recognition evaluation used scripted, Text-To-Speech (TTS)-generated command audio and it did
not capture spontaneous speech, accents, hesitations, overlapping speakers, or wind. The reported WER, CER, and
wake word metrics in Appendix B should therefore be interpreted as controlled pipeline indicators rather than as field
performance estimates.

Fifth, the current work evaluates one person interacting with one focal vehicle. Real streets contain multiple road users
whose requests may overlap or conflict; therefore, a scalable deployment must determine who is addressing whom and
prevent intents being attributed to the wrong road user.
Finally, the current hardware setup may influence both usability and measured performance. A HoloLens 2 paired
with an external compute device is suitable for research prototyping, but it is heavier and more intrusive than a future
consumer-scale system. The hardware burden may affect walking comfort, field of view, social acceptability, and
willingness to use gestures or speech near traffic. Since most AR interfaces do not rely on 3D mapping, a consumer-
ready alternative could leverage smart glasses with a HUD, paired with a smartphone with cellular connectivity.
8
Conclusions and Future Work
This paper investigated how AR-equipped VRUs can participate in bidirectional multimodal interactions between
VRUs and AVs, rather than relying only on vehicle-side cues. We combined candidate design synthesis, an online
survey with 124 participants, a prototype spanning wearable AR, speech and gesture recognition, and vehicular com-
munication, controlled technical benchmarks, and an outdoor demonstration. Together, these validate the interaction
from human preference through technical feasibility.
The online survey results favored preferred AR cues that made vehicle motion or warnings explicit, high-level speech
requests such as stopping or slowing down, and simple, conventional gestures. Participants showed a strong tendency
to pair semantically corresponding gestures with spoken commands, supporting multimodal redundancy as a design
principle. The prototype completed the intended path from VRU input, through recognition and vehicular communi-
cation, to vehicle-side handling and AR acknowledgment. Controlled speech benchmarks further exposed a trade-off
between the lower latency of local processing and the lower transcription error of infrastructure-assisted processing.
Taken together, these findings support that VRU–AV communication should be designed as a closed interaction loop.
A VRU can express an intent, the system reports what it recognized, the vehicle independently determines whether
that request can be acted upon safely, and the outcome is communicated back to the VRU. This framing preserves
human agency without transferring responsibility for safe vehicle behavior to the external road user.
The paradigm shift is that automated mobility becomes capable of reciprocal, attributable, and inclusive interaction
with the people whose safety and freedom of movement depend on it.
Future work should evaluate a complete interaction loop with participants in a controlled outdoor experiment using
AR and an AV. Such studies should measure crossing behavior, trust in automation, self-perceived workload, response
time, and time-to-cross. They should also distinguish whether feedback was noticed correctly, understood, and be-
lieved, examining the consequences of delayed, rejected, or incorrect recognition. Moreover, future work should test
broader participant groups, including cyclists, older adults, children, people with impairments, and with different lev-
els of technology familiarity. Beyond cultural variation, future work should examine accessibility, social acceptability
of speaking and gesturing in public, and wearable heterogeneity. Additionally, user applications should calibrate these
cues when first encountering or registering. Users could be prompted to speak their preferred command variants and
perform key gestures several times; this way, the system could be tailored to the users’ preferences, improving gesture-
and voice-recognition accuracy for outdoor environments.
Future technical work should move from component feasibility toward robust in-the-wild recognition. The interaction
should also be extended beyond the current one-VRU, one-AV scenario to investigate target attribution and conflicting
requests when multiple road users and vehicles are present.

To evaluate how computing placement changes the time before a person can transmit intents and receive acknowl-
edgments, we explored two computational scenarios: (1) offloading ASR processing to a MEC platform (described in
subsection 4.2); and (2) no offloading with processing done on a powerful VRU compute device. MEC offloading is
recognized as a key enabler for latency-sensitive 5G services within smart cities [34, 70], but it is treated here as one
infrastructure-assisted implementation rather than a required architecture.
The MEC platform hosted a machine with an AMD EPYC 8534P Processor (8 cores) with 16GB of RAM, and an
NVIDIA RTX 4000 Ada, operating on Ubuntu 22.04.5 LTS and kernel 6.8.0-52-generic, paired with a Raspberry Pi
5 8GB8 as the VRU compute device. However, when using local processing, tests employed a powerful Jetson Orin
Nano Developer Kit9, featuring a Cortex-A78AE (6 cores) with 8GB of RAM (shared with the GPU), operating on
Ubuntu 22.04.5 LTS, kernel 5.15.136-tegra and JetPack 6.0.
Different Whisper ASR models (Tiny, Base, Small, Medium, and Turbo) were evaluated across these configurations
to quantify their impact on system latency. A TTS tool10 converted all voice commands input into Rasa’s NLU.
Figure 11 presents the transcription latency of different Whisper ASR models across local and offload com-
puting to the MEC.
The Turbo model on the Virtual Machine (VM) configuration achieved a latency (M =
123.74 ms;SD = 76.55). On the Jetson Orin Nano the Tiny model achieved (M = 135.75 ms;SD = 95.10), Base
(M = 184.26 ms;SD = 125.21), Small (M = 290.50 ms;SD = 214.91), Medium (M = 311.69 ms;SD = 216.96),
and Turbo (M = 329.08 ms;SD = 220.42). The Turbo model latency was unexpectedly high due to VRAM limita-
tions on the Jetson.
We computed the WER and CER on different configurations with three audio conditions: the NLU audio file, an
NLU audio file overlapped with urban background noise [63], and the Few-shot Learning Evaluation of Universal
Representations of Speech (FLEURS) test dataset [17]. The urban background noise track audio clips were normalized
in a round-robin manner for signal-to-noise ratio values of 15, 10, 5, 0, and -5 dB. Voice activation detection [72] was
enabled in Whisper.
We followed a similar methodology and the same physical setup as the microphone latency experiment. Table 6
presents these accuracy measurements.
The Turbo model in the VM configuration achieved the lowest error rates across all scenarios. On the command-
oriented NLU audio, it obtained WER of 2.9% and CER of 1.6%, while also detecting the wake word “car” in 96.77%
of cases with no false positives.
Wake word detection was included because the system should not treat every nearby utterance as a command to the
vehicle. In our command grammar, the wake word acted as an activation gate before intent classification. We therefore
report both wake word detection, i.e., the percentage of command utterances in which the wake word was correctly
detected, and wake word false positives (FP), i.e., the percentage of non-wake or incorrectly segmented utterances in
which the system incorrectly detected the wake word.

The Turbo model for the VM configuration remained accurate under added urban noise, increasing WER to 3.4% and
CER to 2.0%. In contrast, the Base model on the Jetson reached a WER of 8.0% and a CER of 6.7%. The FLEURS
benchmark produced higher error rates for both configurations, which is expected because it contains unconstrained
speech rather than the short command grammar.
These accuracy results come from controlled dataset- and NLU-oriented tests rather than from speech collected dur-
ing situated road-crossing interactions. Nevertheless, a deployed system could improve over time by retraining the
NLU examples and wake word using consented user-generated data. Such adaptation should increase robustness for
recurring users.
