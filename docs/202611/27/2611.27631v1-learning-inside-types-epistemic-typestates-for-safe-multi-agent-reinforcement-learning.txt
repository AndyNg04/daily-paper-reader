# Learning Inside Types: Epistemic Typestates for Safe Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning 

Lorenzo Bacchiani ∗, Mario Bravetti, Giovanni Pau, Roberto Girau 

> University of Bologna, Bologna, Italy

Abstract 

Type disciplines make programs safe by construction: components checked against each other’s types compose into programs that cannot go wrong in the ways the types exclude. Types suit reinforcement learning: a type constrains what an agent may choose, not how it chooses, so the guarantee holds for every policy a learner produces. We introduce epistemic typestates, a type discipline for safe multi-agent reinforcement learning. An agent’s type is a protocol whose states record its beliefs, the worlds it cannot rule out from its own observations and actions, and permits only actions that are safe in every world of the belief, whatever the others do within their public types. Agents learn inside their types, which are built and checked one agent at a time and run without communication. We prove type soundness and team safety, give a synthesis that is sound and complete, build types from guards when worlds are infinite, and show that shields are a special case. All proofs are mechanised in Rocq. On nine benchmarks, no typed learner causes a safety violation, and task performance does not differ significantly from that of two recent multi-agent shielding methods. Where a contract-based method fails at five and ten agents, types for ten agents are built in under a second. In the two continuous benchmarks, Lagrangian learners cause thousands of violations in every training run. 

Keywords: Safe reinforcement learning, Multi-agent reinforcement learning, Typestates, Behavioural types, Shielding, Partial observability

1. Introduction 

Type disciplines make programs safe by construction. A type states what a component may do, a checker verifies each component against the types of the others, and in a sound discipline the components that pass the check compose into a program that cannot go wrong in the ways the types ex-clude. Typestates [1] add protocols, so that the operations a component may perform depend on its state, and typestate checkers verify such proto-cols statically [2]. Reinforcement learning (RL) agents that act in a shared space need this kind of guarantee: every action that a learner tries, in training as in execution, must be safe. A type discipline offers it in a form suited to learning. A type constrains what an agent may choose, not how it chooses, so the guarantee holds for every policy that the learner produces. Agents rely on each other’s types, which act as interfaces [3], rather than on each other’s policies, which keep changing during training. Each agent is checked separately, before learning starts, so the checks remain per agent as teams grow. With several agents, whether an action is safe also depends on what the other agents do and on parts of the world that the acting agent cannot sense: an agent’s safety depends on another that it cannot see. Shields [4] are an established way to keep learners safe. With several agents, they handle such dependencies through a model of the whole team: they are synthesised over joint states or their projections [5, 6], decomposed from a centralised shield [7], projected from a global machine [8], or certified together over a global model [9]. These constructions require a finite joint model or abstrac-tion, and whether an agent can then run its shield from its own information, without communication, is a property of the derived shield. We start instead from what each agent knows. An epistemic typestate 

is a protocol for one agent whose states record its beliefs: the worlds that the agent cannot rule out from its own observations and actions. Like a classic typestate [1, 2, 10], it states which actions are permitted in each state, and permits only actions that are safe in every world of the belief and leave a continuation in the protocol. The agent reasons about the other agents only through their public types, which state what they may do and the safety requirements they guarantee, while declarations let an agent commit to actions that others can rely on, as obligations to take an action or restrictions to a set of actions. Each type is built and checked for its own agent against the public types of the others. The learner itself is not checked but confined to the actions its type permits, so every policy it learns conforms by construction. 2Each agent runs its type on its own observations, so execution needs no communication. We implement epistemic typestates in an engine that builds, analyses and runs types. Its input is the model of each agent, e.g., its worlds, their dynamics, its observations, together with the public types of the other agents. The engine synthesises a type when the agent’s reachable beliefs are finite, or builds a type from a guard, i.e., a rule that gives the permitted actions from the agent’s observation. This construction also applies when the worlds are infinite, as with continuous positions. Its static analyses report, for instance, which observations a type needs and what it loses against an exact-knowledge reference, and the finite types it builds are certified by an independent checker proved sound in Rocq 1. Finally, the engine runs each type to supply its learner’s action mask. We evaluate learning inside epistemic typestates on nine benchmarks: six in the configurations of Adalat et al. [9], with Car Platoon also in the version of Brorholt et al. [6], Traffic Junction [11], and two with continuous worlds, MPE [12] and VMAS [13]. Learners trained inside the types are compared with those protected by the shields of Adalat et al. and Brorholt et al., with unshielded learners in Traffic Junction, and with Lagrangian learners [14] in the continuous benchmarks. We measure safety in training and evaluation, task performance, construction time and the freedom left to the learner. Our contributions are the following. • Epistemic typestates, an expressive and scalable type discipline that makes multi-agent RL systems safe by construction (Section 5). We prove type soundness and team safety, show that shields are a special case, build types from guards when the worlds are infinite, and extend knowledge-based synthesis for games with imperfect information [15, 16] to declarations, keeping it sound, complete and, outside obligations, maximally permissive. • The engine, and a mechanisation in Rocq of all our formal results, including the soundness of the independent checker (Sections 5 and 6). • An evaluation on nine benchmarks (Section 6). 3. Background 

3.1. Multi-agent reinforcement learning 

In multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), several agents learn by trial and error to act in a shared environment. When each agent senses only part of the environment, the team is commonly modelled as a decentralised partially observable Markov decision process (Dec-POMDP) [17]. At each time step, every agent i receives an observation oi of the current state s of the environment and chooses an action ai from its set of actions Ai. The joint action (a1, . . . , a n) then moves the environment to a next state, drawn from a probability distribution, and every agent receives a reward. A policy 

πi maps the history of the agent’s observations to a probability distribution over Ai, and the agents seek policies that maximise the expected discounted sum of the rewards, the return. During training, the agents explore actions and update their policies from the rewards they receive. During execution, they follow the learned policies. Execution is decentralised when every agent acts on its own observations, without communicating. Independent learners, such as independent Q-learning (IQL) [18] and independent proximal policy optimisation (IPPO) [19], train each agent as if it were alone, treating the other agents as part of the environment. 

3.2. Shielding 

Shields were introduced to enforce safety specifications at run time on reactive systems [20, 21] and then on learning agents [4]. A shield is com-puted before learning from the safety specification and from a model of the environment, typically an abstraction, that records which states may follow each state and action, but not their probabilities. Computing it amounts to solving a safety game [22] between the agent, which chooses the actions, and the environment, which chooses among the possible next states. The winning region is the set of states from which the agent can keep the sys-tem safe forever, whatever the environment does, and the shield permits, in each winning state, only actions whose possible next states are all winning. 5A maximally permissive shield permits all of them: it restricts the agent as little as possible and forbids an action only if the action could endanger safety. A shield can be placed in two ways [4]. A pre-emptive shield gives the agent, at each step, the set of safe actions to choose from. A post-posed shield monitors the action that the agent chooses and replaces it only if it is unsafe. Either way, no unsafe action is executed, during training as dur-ing execution and whatever the agent’s policy, as long as the environment behaves as the model says. Probabilistic shields bound the probability of a violation instead [23]. With several agents, a centralised shield monitors the joint action of all agents [5], whereas per-agent shields each restrict the actions of one agent. 

3.3. Typestates 

Type systems ensure that programs do not perform invalid operations, such as calling a method that an object does not define, but not that opera-tions are performed in a valid order: a file, for instance, must be opened be-fore it is read or written, and cannot be used after it is closed. The operations available on such objects depend on their internal state. Typestates [1, 24], which are behavioural types [25], make this dependence explicit. A typestate is a protocol that describes, as a state machine, which operations an object permits in each of its states and the state that each operation leads to. The next state may depend on the result of the operation, for example on whether a read reached the end of the file. A typestate checker verifies statically that a program uses every object according to its protocol, as Mungo [26] and JaTyC [2, 10] do for Java. The check is sound: a program that passes it never performs an operation that the current state of the object does not permit, so protocol violations are ruled out before the program runs. 

4. Related work 

Beyond the shielding and typestates of Section 3, epistemic typestates draw on safe RL and knowledge-based control. We review these connections, focusing on the information agents need to act safely and on how local guar-antees compose. We close with the multi-agent shielding methods closest to ours and compare them with epistemic typestates. 

Safe RL. Safe RL methods either change the optimisation criterion, for in-stance to bound an expected cost, or change the exploration process using 6external knowledge [27]. Brunke et al. [28] survey safe learning in robotics, from learning-based control to safe RL. Provably safe RL guarantees safety at every step of training and deployment. Krasowski et al. [29] classify its methods by what happens to an unsafe action of the learner: it is replaced, projected onto a safe action, or masked, so that the learner cannot choose it. Learning inside a type is action masking: at each step, the actions permitted by the agent’s typestate form its action set. Policy-gradient updates remain valid when computed for the masked policy [30]. Fulton and Platzer [31] combine verified hybrid-system models with runtime monitoring to constrain learning while the observed behaviour conforms to the model. 

Shielding under partial observation. Single-agent shielding under partial ob-servation already reasons about sets of possible states. Junges et al. [32] synthesise winning regions over belief supports for POMDPs, and Carr et al. [33] integrate such shields with deep RL. Their support estimator updates possible states from action and observation histories without needing tran-sition probabilities. Their avoidance guarantee tolerates conservative transi-tion models. Their almost-sure reachability result assumes exact transition support and fairness of the permitted choices. Mazzi et al. [34] shield online POMCP planning using rules fitted from belief–action traces and expert tem-plates, including bounds on modelled risk. Our contribution concerns how these beliefs form protocols with declarations, how protocols are synthesised, and how their safety guarantees compose through public types. 

Safety filters for continuous multi-robot control. Control barrier functions certify invariance of a safe region. Safety filters can use them to minimally modify a nominal control input. Wang et al. [35] derive decentralised safety barrier certificates for collision avoidance among robots modelled as dou-ble integrators: each robot corrects its own input using the relative positions and velocities of nearby robots. Qin et al. [36] learn decentralised neural bar-rier certificates jointly with multi-agent policies. Reciprocal collision avoid-ance [37, 38] lets each robot take half of the responsibility for avoiding every neighbour. ORCA [38] turns this into half-planes of admissible velocities computed from the neighbours’ positions and velocities. These approaches use nearby robots’ states. Our guard construction (Proposition 4) is para-metric in the dynamics and safety requirement: an observation-based guard must offer an action throughout a safe region containing the possible starts, and every offered action must keep all possible successors there. This permits 7safety to depend on unsensed quantities, such as other agents’ velocities. The formalism allows continuous worlds but retains a finite action set. 

Constrained multi-agent RL. Constrained RL expresses safety through cost budgets. Constrained policy optimisation [39] controls expected cumulative costs during policy improvement. Gu et al. [14] formulate constrained Markov games and develop multi-agent constrained policy optimisation and MAPPO-Lagrangian. Their theoretical policy-update procedure guarantees constraint satisfaction and monotonic reward improvement under its assumptions. An expected-cost budget does not in general exclude every unsafe transition. Our safety guarantee applies throughout training and execution to every policy that selects permitted actions, independently of its reward optimisation. 

Cooperation without communication. For Dec-POMDPs (Section 3.1), op-timal decentralised planning is NEXP-hard already for finite horizons [40]. Epistemic typestates leave optimisation to the learner while constraining its choices through safety requirements and declarations. Much work in multi-agent RL lets agents learn to communicate [41, 42, 11]. We assume no com-munication channel at all. Social laws [43, 44] coordinate agents without runtime communication: restrictions on actions, designed offline, such that agents that obey them avoid undesirable states. Declarations play a similar role for learning agents, with commitments local to each agent: they are part of its own typestate, and other agents rely on them through its public type. Commitments between agents are also central to agent communication [45], where commitment-based protocols create and discharge them through mes-sages [46]. Declarations need no messages. 

Knowledge and supervisory control. Reasoning about knowledge in multi-agent systems rests on possible worlds [47]. Our beliefs are sets of possible worlds in this sense. Knowledge-based programs [48] make actions depend on what an agent knows. Engesser et al. [49] integrate epistemic logic with RL and use epistemic action conditions to filter choices through model checking. Alpturer et al. [50] derive knowledge-based vehicle protocols for intersec-tions, establishing safety, liveness and conditional optimality. These works provide direct precedents for epistemic action selection and traffic safety. Supervisory control under partial observation [51] enables or disables events using observed histories. Decentralised supervisory control [52] studies how local controllers jointly enforce global behavioural constraints. Ricker and Rudie [53] explicitly formalise decentralised supervisory decisions through 8possible-worlds knowledge and knowledge-based protocols. We extend this connection between information and permitted behaviour to learning agents, using declarations and public types to state their commitments and compose their guarantees. Our results guarantee safety and continued availability of an action: they impose no liveness requirement on the learner’s choices. 

Shielded multi-agent RL. For several agents, individually permitted choices must also be safe when combined. Bharadwaj et al. [54] synthesise shields that correct the agents so that a global specification always holds, minimising the cost of the corrections. ElSayed-Aly et al. [5] develop centralised shields over joint actions and factored shields with runtime coordination between re-gions. Xiao et al. [55] let shields split and merge as the agents’ states change, with a world model learned during early exploration and high-probability guarantees. Melcer et al. [56] decompose a centralised shield into per-agent shields that need no communication, for agents that all compute the same abstract label of the state. They later extend the decomposition to agents with different partial observations [7]. This construction uses a finite joint abstraction and also supports bounded observation histories, and the under-lying environment can be infinite. Yang and Yoshida [8] compile a shield process algebra through a global Mealy machine into local machines whose states represent beliefs about the global machine, and compute best- and worst-case safety probabilities over all policies by probabilistic model check-ing. These approaches establish that both history-dependent local shielding and communication-free execution are possible. Brorholt et al. [6] construct one shield per agent over projections of a finite state space, using ordered assume-guarantee reasoning: each agent’s guaran-tee may rely only on the guarantees of the agents before it, which avoids circular reasoning. Their shields are memoryless, and execution requires no communication. However, continuous systems are future work. Adalat et al. [9] use local temporal contracts for a shared global safety objective and certify them together over a finite global model, as profiles that assign a contract to each agent. Their simultaneous certification sup-ports circular assumptions. Decentralised execution requires that each local mask be determined by the active contract and the agent’s local observation history. Contract monitors can supply memory. General distributed controller synthesis under differing observations is undecidable [57], and all the shields in Table 1 are instead derived from a model of the whole team. Whether an agent can run its part from its own information is then a property of the derived shield. An epistemic typestate is built for one agent, from its own world dy-namics, its observations and the public types of the other agents. No joint state space is required. Public types act as interfaces: an agent relies only on what the others’ public types permit and guarantee. The agent runs its 10 typestate on its own actions and observations, so execution without commu-nication holds by construction. Declarations let an agent commit to actions that others can rely on. Unlike the contracts of Adalat et al. [9], they are checked with the agent’s own typestate. Rather than a model of the whole team, a typestate requires finitely many reachable beliefs of its agent, with the computability conditions of Section 5.2, or a guard supplied by the designer, which also covers infinite world spaces. A typestate uses only what its agent can know: where a shield reads state that the agent cannot sense, the typestate may permit fewer actions. 5.4. Shields as a special case 

Shields that guarantee safety in every run differ in what they read and in who chooses the action, but they share a formal core: a set of worlds from which safety can be maintained, and in each of them a nonempty set of actions that keep the system there. We express this core in our notation and show that it corresponds to epistemic typestates with full observation and no declarations. Full observation here is observation of the world represented by the shield. That world may itself be an abstraction or a local projection. 

Definition 20 (Shield) . A shield is a pair (Z, σ ) of a set Z ⊆ W of worlds and a function σ : Z → 2A such that, for every z ∈ Z, safe (z), σ(z)̸ = ∅, and

Here a world represents the state the shield reads: an abstract environmental state [20, 4], an observed projection of the global state [6], or the global state itself [9]. Requirements given by safety automata fit by adding the automaton’s current state to each world, and 

safe then checks that no violation has been recorded. The nondeterminism of Post accounts for the choices of the environment, including other agents. 21 This view also covers shields that correct chosen actions: σ describes their possible outputs (for a centralised shield, these are joint actions).

6. Evaluation 

We evaluate epistemic typestates on nine benchmarks, measuring safety, task performance and the choices left to the learner. 

Benchmarks. The nine benchmarks in Table 2 cover a range of conditions. Most have discrete worlds, but in MPE and VMAS positions and velocities are continuous, so the worlds are infinitely many and synthesis cannot apply: there, as in three of the discrete benchmarks, types are built from guards, while the remaining four are synthesised. Partial observation is the rule: in every benchmark except Connector and LBF, where agents see the whole board, each agent senses only part of the world, and some of what it cannot sense matters for its safety. The benchmarks also differ in how agents depend on each other. In Car Platoon the dependence runs along a chain, each follower depending on the cars ahead, and in LBF it rests on declarations, as a player next to the food commits to loading it whenever another is too, whereas in Traffic Junction, MPE and VMAS every agent can endanger every other. Teams range from two to ten agents, and Car Platoon and LBF are run with two, five and ten agents to study scaling. Car Platoon appears in both of its published versions, by Adalat et al. Assurance. We check the types used in the experiments independently of the engine that builds them. Every type of the discrete benchmarks is exported with its model and verified by two checkers that share no code with the engine, one of them proved sound in Rocq: a typestate passes only if it is well formed, and a guard only if it satisfies the hypothesis of Proposition 4. All 34 exported types pass. Except in Traffic Junction, the exported typestates also carry a certificate, verified by both checkers, that at every reachable state they permit exactly the actions of the synthesised typestate. To test the checkers themselves, we build 194 corrupted copies of the exports, e.g., by permitting an excluded action, dropping a transition or adding an unsafe successor, and both checkers reject every one. Theorem 2 also requires each agent’s model to cover what the simulator does while the others act within their public types. We test this hypothesis exhaustively on the unchanged simulators of Car Platoon with two followers, Connector, Flatland, LBF with two agents and Pressure Plate. From the public reset, which is the same for every seed of the experiments, the check visits every reachable team state and executes there every joint action the types permit, under every outcome of the simulator’s random draws: in 104 (Flatland) to 81267 (Connector) team states, the types predict every 25 observation and no step is unsafe. Theorem 2 involves no check of the whole team: its hypotheses concern each agent’s type, its model and the public types it relies on, and the acyclicity of reliance on safety after the step. Adding agents therefore adds no obligation as long as it changes none of these. It changes none in Car Platoon and LBF, whose models see the others only through the cars ahead and through whether two players are next to the food, and whose five- and ten-agent teams run the very types of the checked two-agent instances. In every run of these teams, each typed controller also checks at every step that its agent’s observation is one its type predicts, and stops the run otherwise (no run stopped). In Traffic Junction, a similar check is exhaustive on the easy map with three to five cars, and on the medium map the model agrees with the simulator on all 40000 transitions of 1000 sampled episodes. For MPE and VMAS, the hypothesis of Proposition 4 rests on a written argument, an invariant on pairs of agents that permitted actions preserve. Its numerical inputs include the dynamics model, checked against the simulator on sampled steps, and, in VMAS, a braking-distance bound by interval arithmetic and a reduction to finitely many ray directions. These types are thus argued and tested rather than certified, so we try to refute them. Every agent runs its type on its own observations, while an adversarial search chooses the initial positions and, among each agent’s permitted actions, the one that most reduces its distance to another agent, using the true state. In 8000 MPE and 7845 VMAS episodes, with 1.2 and 4.7 million decisions, the search finds no agent-caused collision. Its closest approaches, 0.3005 and 0.2143 against collision distances of 0.3 and 0.205, show that it brings agents close to colliding.

7. Discussion and limitations 

Epistemic typestates give learners the guarantee of a shield while lifting restrictions of existing shielding methods. Against Adalat et al., they scale: the same roles serve teams of two, five and ten agents, whereas at five and ten agents, Adalat et al.’s construction finds no certified profile or does not complete within 32 GiB. Against Brorholt et al., they are more expressive: theirs compose memoryless shields in a fixed order, whereas typestates let agents rely on each other’s public types in both directions and use the agent’s own history. Against both, types need no finite state space: both shielding methods assume one, and Brorholt et al. leave continuous systems to future work, while guards yield types for the infinitely many worlds of MPE and VMAS. Task performance does not differ significantly from that of either 32 shielding method in all comparisons but one, against Adalat et al.’s shield in Car Platoon, where it reads state that no agent can sense. Freedom explains why the shields do no better. No well-formed typestate permits more than a synthesised one given the same belief, except where an obligation fixes the action (Proposition 3), and the certificates of Section 6 show that every exported typestate outside Traffic Junction permits exactly what synthesis would. Outside obligations, a shield can therefore permit more than such a type only by knowing or assuming more than the type, as Adalat et al.’s shield does in Car Platoon. With the same information, Brorholt et al.’s shield permits nothing that the type withholds, and the type permits more in 91 states. In Traffic Junction, whose guard is not certified maximal, the type comes close: on the easy map, with three to five cars, it agrees with an exact reference that recalls the car’s whole observation history in all but 8 decisions, where it withholds GAS that the reference permits. Because well-formedness is checked state by state (Definition 10) and each agent separately (Theorem 2), an independent checker proved sound in Rocq can certify the finite types before they are used. Finally, a type constrains the learner only through its action mask. Each type serves IPPO and IQL alike, as well as Brorholt et al.’s cascading learner in their version of Car Platoon, and none of these learners has to cause a violation to learn to avoid it. Constrained learners, by contrast, learn from the cost of the violations they cause. 

7.1. Limitations 

Like every model-based safety method, epistemic typestates guarantee safety only as far as their models are right: Theorem 1 assumes that the environment follows the specified world dynamics, and Theorem 2 that each model covers what the others may do within their public types. Writing these models remains the designer’s task, although the effort grows with the kinds of agents rather than with their number: each benchmark declares one or two roles, which serve all its agents. The team checks of Section 6 test them against the simulators, but testing cannot prove a model sound, and the certification of exported types trusts the exporter that produces them. Synthesis requires finitely many reachable beliefs, and its cost grows with their number, which in the worst case is exponential in the number of worlds. The cost depends on each agent’s local model rather than on the joint be-haviour of the team, but it limits how much of the world a synthesised type can afford to track. When the worlds are infinite, types come from guards, 33 which do not enumerate beliefs but carry no maximality guarantee of their own, and whose hypothesis must be established for each model. For contin-uous dynamics, we currently establish it by numerically checked arguments rather than with a verified checker. The formalism also keeps a finite action set, so continuous actions remain outside its current scope. Outside obligations, a synthesised type is maximally permissive for the information it uses, and choosing that information is a design decision: track-ing more observations or more history can permit more, but may enlarge the set of beliefs that synthesis must explore. The engine’s analyses, such as the comparison with an exact reference in Section 7, measure what a coarser choice costs. Finally, agents may rely on each other’s public types in both directions, but reliance on safety after the step must be acyclic (Theorem 2), and public types must be agreed before deployment: the approach replaces communication at run time with agreement at design time. 

8. Conclusion 

We have introduced epistemic typestates, a type discipline for safe multi-agent RL. Agents learn inside types that permit only the actions whose safety follows from what each agent knows, so safety holds for every learner, in training as in execution, without communication. Well-formed types keep their agents safe and compose into team safety. Synthesis finds a well-formed type whenever one exists, as permissive as any other apart from declared obligations, and guards give types for infinitely many worlds. Shields are the special case with full observation and no declarations, and all proofs are mechanised in Rocq. On all benchmarks, no typed learner causes a safety violation, and task performance does not differ significantly from that of two recent multi-agent shielding methods in all comparisons but one, although one of them reads state that no agent can sense in three benchmarks. In Adalat et al.’s benchmarks, types for ten agents are built in under a second, whereas their construction found no shield for teams of five or ten agents. Types also express mutual commitments and history-dependent rules, which ordered memoryless shields cannot, and extend to continuous worlds, where Lagrangian learners cause thousands of violations in every training run. Several directions remain open. A refinement relation relative to sens-ing would let a type be replaced by one that permits less and commits to more, as far as the other agents can tell, without checking the team again. Constructing and verifying guards automatically would bring continuous dy-34 namics within reach of the certified checker, and continuous action sets would extend the formalism beyond finite choices. Learning models from data would reduce the main burden that the approach places on the designer.
