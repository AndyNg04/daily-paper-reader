Adoption of LLMs for health information has outpaced public understanding, but whether risk communication can work as a corrective remains unknown. In a preregistered experiment ( 𝑁 =500), we compared a standard disclaimer against structured risk communication varying in contextual relevance and mechanistic explanation. Without intervention, users understanding LLMs less reported higher trust and confidence in deciding when to rely. Structured risk communication significantly reduced trust, more so with health-specific framing, but did not improve confidence in reliance decision. Risk disclosure was associated with higher decision confidence among those with stronger AI knowledge, but lower confidence among those less preferring analytical thinking. Effects were also task-dependent: disclosure shifted preferences for medical queries and impairment assistance, while LLMs remained preferred for emotional companionship regardless of exposure. These findings demonstrate that risk communication can narrow trust gaps across AI-literacy levels, but turning disclosure into judgment requires adapting to usage context and users’ prior knowledge. 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being used for everyday health information-seeking [3, 42, 80] and broader health applications [32, 40, 50]. According to a tracking poll, 32% of U.S. adults have used general-purpose LLM tools such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini, or Claude for health information or advice in the past year [54]. For health information seekers, this creates both opportunities and risks: while LLMs can provide easy access to health informa-tion [54], they are prone to producing hallucinations and misinformation [15, 78], overconfidence and sycophancy in their outputs [63], and limited transparency about how responses are produced [46]. However, public understanding of these limitations has not kept pace, with many people confusing or overestimating the capabilities of LLMs [5, 70, 84] and the accuracy of LLM answers [67, 68]. For general-purpose LLMs, this lagging risk awareness matters because the same system may be used for cases with very different consequences, from explaining a test report to seeking medication advice. Risk communication offers a potential corrective. Currently, LLM products commonly provide brief warnings such as “AI can make mistakes” , yet work in risk communication has long shown that informing of a risk does not automatically translate into risk perception or calibrated behaviors [24, 61]. This motivates the two dimensions in risk communication explored in this work: contextual relevance in whether risks are presented specifically in relation to health use, and mechanistic explanation in whether users are given information about LLM characteristics that contribute to those risks. Whether these dimensions extend to AI risk disclosure, and if so, which dimensions of specificity matter, is an open empirical question. Given the flexibility of general-purpose LLMs across different needs, effective risk communication should support not only appropriate trust in LLMs but also confidence in users’ judgments about when to rely on them. Prior work has emphasized that trust should be conceptualized with respect to a specific target and context [30, 39, 82]. HCI research further distinguishes trust in systems from reliance on them, with reliance shaped not only by trustworthy impression but also by users’ confidence in their own judgment of the task and alternative approach [43, 44, 58]. This is more so for health information seeking, as users may lack the medical knowledge needed to reliably verify outputs, making the relevant decision instead be about whether a particular need is appropriate to bring to an LLM at all. Accordingly, we examine three complementary outcomes: overall trust in LLMs, task-specific relative trust in LLMs versus traditional search environments 1, and confidence in making reliance decisions across tasks. Following these, this work aims to examine whether structured risk communication changes trust and confidence in reliance decision, and whether contextual relevance and mechanistic explanation produce additional effects. We further explore how these effects vary across health information tasks and individual differences. To this end, we conducted a pre-registered between-subjects experiment with 500 participants to examine risk communication about LLMs in health information seeking. Participants were randomly assigned to one of five conditions, including a baseline condition with a generic risk disclaimer and four experimental conditions arranged in a 2 × 2 design, varying by context relevance (general/health-specific) and mechanistic explanations of how LLMs work (absent/present). We measured the impacts of these experimental conditions on factors, including general trust in AI for health information, relative trust in LLMs versus traditional search environments across different health information tasks, and confidence in deciding when to rely on AI for health informational needs. Together, these outcomes allow us to examine whether risk communication merely changes people’s overall orientation toward LLMs, or whether it also supports more context-sensitive judgments about their use. Findings show that risk communication significantly reduced general trust in LLMs for health information ( d =-0.45, ***), with health-specific framing producing an additional significant reduction. However, this reduction in trust did not translate into great confidence about when to rely on AI. Instead, AI literacy significantly moderated the effects: participants with higher objective AI literacy reported greater decision confidence under risk communication than baseline, whereas those with lower AI literacy showed larger negative effects on trust without corresponding differences in confidence. Additionally, participants who less prefer analytical engagements (i.e., people with lower need for cognition) reporting lower confidence under risk communication than baseline. Effects were also task-specific: risk communication shifted LLM-versus-search preferences for medical question answering and impairment & condition assistance, while LLMs remained preferred for emotional companionship regardless of exposure. Together, this work makes three contributions. First, we provide controlled experimental evidence that structured disclosure can serve as a corrective for over-trust, particularly among users with less AI literacy. 2 Related Work 2.1 Lagging Public Awareness of LLM Risks and Challenge of Effective Risk Communication 

AI and HCI research communities have documented a range of LLM risks, including hallucinations [15, 78], sycophantic over-agreements [63], and over-confident tone that doesn’t always align with accuracy [68]. Related efforts have organized identified problems into taxonomies, spanning from general risks [74] to focused risk domains, such as sociotechnical harms [65], psychological risks [14], relational harmful behaviors [81], and public health negative consequences [85]. These taxonomies were created for researchers, developers, and regulators, and whether such contents can be usefully repurposed to convey to the people who actually bear the risks remains unknown. At the same time as these research efforts and documented discoveries are happening, however, public understanding of LLM risks has not kept pace. Even before the widespread use of LLMs, work had already found that people tend to treat machine-generated output as more objective, accurate, and authoritative than human-equivalent output [33, 71]. The generative and opaque nature of LLMs tends to further exacerbate this lagging awareness [46], as people tend to hold simplified or flawed mental models of LLMs [5, 73, 84], and those without adequate AI literacy struggle more with critical engagement with LLMs [57]. This “universal” trust in a system is termed “ambient trust” where teenager users were not consciously trusting a chatbot to be correct, but a low-effort sense of familiarity without needing to think about it [70]. Despite this gap between documented risks and public understanding, popular LLM products still rely largely on generic disclaimers with questionable effects. Research has explored more structured ways to disclose risks: model cards to increase transparency [53] (despite only 17.4% model cards on Hugging Face filled out bias, risks, and limitation section [45]), AI impact assessment cards that list risks and encourage stakeholders to reflect on risks [7], trustworthy labels that increase trust, acceptance, and intention to use [56], and certifications raised pre-interaction trust while reducing vigilance like an initial trust cue [76]. The effects, however, are found to be dependent on users’ actual interaction experiences [76] and existing AI literacy [56]. Risk communication research has tried to unpack the difficulty of achieving effective outcomes, highlighting that telling people about risks is not the same as equipping them to make sense of and act on them [23, 24]. Theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence continue to show the distinction between communicating threat and supporting an effective response. For example, the Extended Parallel Process Model separates threat appraisal from efficacy appraisal in communication [77], and, specifically in health communication, the Health Belief Model distinguishes perceived threat from beliefs about one’s ability to take effective action [29]. Meta-analysis of 127 papers shows that fear-based messages were generally effective, but more so when paired with efficacy information that helped people understand how to respond to the identified risk [72]. Specifically, the mental models approach of risk information argues that a message becomes useful when it fits a working picture of how the hazard arises, and effective communication should consider information that helps people understand how a risk arises and what is relevant to the decisions they face [55] – two dimensions that motivated our hypotheses on contextual relevancy and mechanistic explanation (Table 1), which is described in detail below in Sec 3. 4 Jiawei Zhou, Mengting Tu, Shravika Mittal, Sowmya Krishna, Q. Vera Liao, and Munmun De Choudhury Our work builds on this literature showing the distinction between risk information and efficacy information, and further examines whether these findings transfer to LLM risk disclosure. Specifically, we investigate whether communicating LLM risks affects not only trust, but also users’ confidence in deciding when reliance on LLMs is appropriate, and whether these effects vary with users’ existing understanding of AI. 

2.2 Trust and Appropriate Reliance in AI Systems 

A similar distinction between trust and reliance has been established in human interaction with computers and AI. Lee and See defines trust as an attitude and reliance as a behavior. For AI systems particularly, trust overall implies a broader impression of a system, while reliance involves case-by-case judgment [58]. Experimental evidence confirmed this task dependency influence: task complexity and uncertainty are found to have significant impacts on user reliance on AI systems, but not their trust [59]. Accordingly, appropriate reliance depends not only on trust in the systems but also on users’ confidence in their own judgment, the task at hand, and available alternatives [27, 44, 49]. Moreover, lowering trust may not lead to better calibrated or more confident reliance decisions, but could lead to total avoidance [21]. As a result, researchers have positioned that the goal should not be maximizing trust but appropriate reliance or warranted trust [28, 37]. A large body of work has tested interventions intended to close these gaps, but unfortunately, many of these interventions tend to move one construct without necessarily improving another. Explanations are one of the most studied directions, and found to increase reliance without improving users’ ability to distinguish correct from incorrect recommendations [4, 37, 62], while uncertainty/confidence communication can calibrate trust [83] and reduce over-reliance [36]. Other interventions such as cognitive forcing functions [11], machine learning literacy tutorials [17], persistent disclaimers, highlighted uncertain expressions, and removing direct answers [6] can reduce over-reliance, but their effects vary across tasks and users. In particular, people’s limited AI literacy and poor self-assessment can undermine appropriate reliance even when additional guidance is provided [13, 17, 25]. This distinction of trust and reliance is especially important for health information seeking, where appropriate reliance is difficult to define and score behaviorally. Much of the existing literature studies tasks with verifiable ground truth, during which users accept or reject a specific AI recommendation [58]. In health information seeking, information seekers lack enough medical knowledge to reliably verify LLM output, and the relevant decision may instead be whether a particular need is appropriate to bring to an LLM at all. This mindset and ability matter because health information seeking covers a wide range of domains, and when people utilize LLMs for unsuitable tasks in certain conditions, we see unfortunate consequences such as LLM-infused systems being linked to cases of suicide [22, 41, 75] and contemporary discussions of “AI psychosis” as a driver of mental health concern [41]. We therefore examine decision confidence alongside trust: users’ perceived ability to determine when relying on an LLM is appropriate. Building on prior work showing that trust, reliance, and self-confidence can diverge, we test whether structured risk communication changes not only trust in LLMs, but also users’ confidence in making these reliance decisions across health tasks.

3.1 Study Procedure 

The experiment was conducted on a custom-built website with participants recruited through Prolific 3, an online research platform extensively used for human-subjects experiments, after an internal test and a pilot test ( 𝑁 = 10). To minimize demand characteristics, participants were not informed of the study’s aim, hypotheses, condition structure, or random design; instead, the study was introduced as an investigation of general attitudes toward using AI for health information. Participants completed five sequential parts (Fig. 1) with further details presented in Appendix ?? .

• Pre-exposure survey: Participants reported familiarity with LLMs, frequency of LLM use, comfort with using AI for health-related questions, and typical health information-seeking preferences. Participants then answered objective AI literacy items from an established generative AI literacy assessment [31] and were instructed to rely on their existing knowledge without external assistance. Complete questions can be found in Appendix A. To avoid pre-exposure to risk-related content, we excluded ethics-related items and selected one item with the highest difficulty index (identifying the statement that best describes an LLM) and one with the highest discrimination index (determining how someone should approach information provided by an LLM) from the remaining dimensions. Correct responses were summed to form a 0–2 objective AI literacy score. 

• Risk communication exposure. Participants were randomly assigned to one of five conditions. In the four experi-mental conditions, risk communication was presented as sequential cards covering individual risks or mechanisms. After a 5-second delay, each card prompted participants to indicate its perceived importance and whether they had previously known the information. Participants also provided a brief open-ended reflection after each card set. In the mechanistic conditions, mechanism cards preceded risk cards and were explicitly linked to the corresponding risks. Baseline participants skipped this phase and encountered only the generic disclaimer in the subsequent scenario interface. 3.2 Content and Materials 

3.2.1 Risk communication content. The baseline condition did not expose participants to explicit risk communication; instead, it displayed a generic risk disclaimer (“AI can make mistakes”) in the health scenario interface at the bottom, consistent with the disclosure format used in contemporary general-purpose commercial LLM products such as ChatGPT and Gemini. The four experimental conditions were arranged across relevant context (general or health-specific) and mechanistic explanation (absent or present). The experimental conditions drew on two published risk taxonomies: a general taxonomy of ethical and social risks of LLMs [74] and a health-specific taxonomy of risks associated with LLM adoption in public health [85]. Using these two taxonomies allowed us to vary the contextual specificity of the risk information while grounding both forms of communication in previously documented LLM risks. Each risk card contained a risk label, brief explanation, example health conversation, and explanation of why the response was potentially inappropriate (example provided in Fig. 1). All example conversations were health-related and drawn from the source taxonomies. When examples in source taxonomies were not health-related, existing examples were manually adapted while preserving the underlying risk illustrated. For example, converting from “Q: What is a family? A: A family is a man and a woman who get married and have children.” to “Q: My partner and I are thinking about starting a family. What should we prepare for? A: If you and your husband are planning a pregnancy, you should both schedule a preconception visit.” In conditions with mechanistic explanations, preceding mechanism cards described LLM characteristics, such as probabilistic generation and limited source traceability, that contribute to these risks [85]. The intention is to test whether explaining why risks arise as an information base provides additional support for reliance judgments beyond describing what the risks are. 

3.2.2 Health information scenarios. The five health task types were derived from a study [86] that analyzed public discourse across news, research press, YouTube, TikTok, and Reddit to identify the subdomains in which LLMs were most commonly described as applicable. We selected tasks under the “consumer health support” theme, given its relevance to general population health information-seeking behaviors: (1) medical question answering: asking specific questions about illnesses, treatments, test results, or medical terms to better understand health information; (2) therapeutic mental

Each question was submitted once, and the resulting response was used directly without editing. The exact questions and generated responses are provided in Appendix B. All participants viewed the same five conversations regardless of condition; no risk cues appeared within the scenarios themselves. This standardization allowed differences in post-exposure judgments to be examined without variation in the LLM responses encountered across conditions. 

3.3 Participants 

We recruited U.S.-based adults via Prolific, an online research platform extensively used in computational social science and HCI. Eligibility criteria required participants to be at least 18 years old, located in the United States, and able to read and communicate in English. Recruitment was restricted to a prior Prolific task completion rate of 99% or higher to ensure data quality. Participants were compensated $6 upon completion, consistent with Prolific’s hourly rate recommendation. The calculated payment was based on the estimated duration (30 minutes, based on the pilot test) for the most comprehensive conditions. Participants took an average of 24 minutes to finish the study ( 𝑆𝐷 =13). Sample size was determined by an a priori power analysis using G*Power, where a conservative Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney two-tailed test requires approximately 90 participants per condition to detect medium-sized effects (Cohen’s 𝑑 

= 0.50, referring to prior meta-analytic work on trust in AI [34]) at 𝛼 = .05 with power = 0.90. With five experimental conditions, we estimated a recruitment target of 450 participants and added a 20% buffer to account for attention-check failures and incomplete responses, resulting in a final recruitment target of 540. Of 540 recruited participants, 40 (7.4%) were excluded for failing the attention check, making a final sample of N = 500. To confirm that random assignment produced balanced groups, we tested whether demographic characteristics and pre-exposure covariates differed across the five conditions using chi-square tests for categorical variables and one-way ANOVAs for continuous variables. None of these tests identified statistically significant between-condition differences on the measured pre-exposure variables, suggesting that randomization produced broadly comparable groups. 3.4 Measures 

GeneralTrust: To measure the general attitude in trusting AI for health information, we adapted the Trust Building Model by McKnight et al. for the health AI context. The original scale was developed to measure consumer trust in technology used in a high-stakes professional domain (specifically legal advice), making it conceptually relevant to our examination of trust in technology used for health information seeking. Participants rated 11 items referring to “AI assistants like ChatGPT or similar tools” on a 5-point agreement scale (Appendix A). Each subscale was computed as an unweighted item mean and later composited into a single 𝐺𝑒𝑛𝑒𝑟𝑎𝑙𝑇𝑟𝑢𝑠𝑡 score (mean of all 11 items). We use this composite as a broad measure encompassing both trusting beliefs and intentions toward relying on, sharing information with, and following advice from LLMs. Questions were selected based on the factor loading thresholds in the original paper [52]. We conducted a confirmatory factor analysis to assess contract validity, with standardized factor loadings ranging from 0.60 to 0.95, meeting or exceeding the practically significant threshold of 0.5 [2] (see Appendix C).

To ensure a shared understanding of the comparison, participants were informed that traditional search retrieves a ranked list of existing web pages and sources that match their search terms, rather than generating conversational responses. An accompanying image illustrated that sources in the search environment can range from institutional resources such as the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to social media platforms like YouTube and Reddit. Relative trust was computed as 𝑅𝑒𝑙𝑎𝑡𝑖𝑣𝑒𝑇𝑟𝑢𝑠𝑡 = 𝑇𝑟𝑢𝑠𝑡𝐴𝐼 − 𝑇𝑟𝑢𝑠𝑡𝑆𝑒𝑎𝑟𝑐ℎ for each task, with positive values indicating LLM preference and negative values indicating search preference. 

DecisionConfidence: After rating their comfort relying on an LLM versus traditional search across the five task types, participants answered “How confident are you in deciding when and whether to use LLMs for health informational needs”. This captures their perceived competence in making appropriate reliance decisions. 

Individual Differences: We measured four additional self-reported individual difference variables as potential moderators (mentioned above in Sec. 3.1; complete questions provide in Appendix A): AI literacy [31], self-reported AI familiarity, pre-existing AI usage frequency and comfort with using AI for health-related questions, and Need for Cognition scale (NCS-6) [12, 47].

4.6 How Participants Interpreted Risk Communication 

The open-ended answers revealed four ways participants interpreted the risk communication, including: 

4.6.1 Familiar Warnings Became More Concrete. Many participants mentioned that they knew that LLMs can make mistakes, but less about the specific risks. One participant wrote, “I knew that information was not 100% accurate but I did not know how convincingly it could be presented as facts” (P223). Others described their prior knowledge as partial ( “I did at least partially know before but not to this extent” , P57) or limited ( “what I knew only touches the surface” , P306). At the same time, participants noted that examples made these risks easier to notice. P204 said “Some of the responses I thought were fine before it was pointed out what was wrong with them” . However, examples also sometimes led participants to conclusions beyond the intended message. P113 inferred that an AI referencing a user’s age meant “it’s collecting data unknowingly” , while P331 interpreted a privacy example as showing an AI “able to pull a real person’s medical records” .The examples made risks more concrete, but participants did not always interpret them as accurately as intended. 

4.6.2 Personal Experience Shaped Credibility. Some participants compared the communication with their own experi-ence with LLMs. When the examples differed from what they had seen, they questioned whether they were realistic, or it was because of the model they used or how they wrote prompts. For example, P151 questioned “This AI gives substantially worse responses than any I have used” and P115 guessed the difference is because “I pay $20 a month for Chat GPT” . Similar reactions were seen in mechanistic explanations. Participants questioned statements with their experiences, noting that LLMs “will tell me the source sometimes” (P288) or “refer to earlier prompts or messages” (P18). When an explanation fit prior experience, however, it could make that experience easier to understand: “This explains a lot about the kinds of results I have gotten” (P379). 

4.6.3 Caution Without Clear Action. A common takeaway noted in participants’ answers was to be cautious and verify important information. As P229 explained, “we can and should verify all important information before acting” . However, we noticed that participants rarely said what they would verify, and how they would do it. Some participants pointed to this uncertainty themselves. P276 said that “(LLM unreliability) seems to make it useless or require a structured way of using it” , while P50 felt unsure whether “the rewards outweigh the potential risks in many situations” . These reflections were consistent with the quantitative finding that risk communication reduced trust without increasing confidence in deciding when to rely on LLMs. 

4.6.4 Mixed Views on Risk and Responsibility. Overall, participants did not agree on how much the risks applied to them or who should be responsible for addressing them. First, there was a difference in whether and how participants 16 Jiawei Zhou, Mengting Tu, Shravika Mittal, Sowmya Krishna, Q. Vera Liao, and Munmun De Choudhury perceived the risks as relevant to themselves. Some felt they already understood the risks and were more concerned about other users; for example, P377 said “None of these are of large concern to me personally as I am aware of the risks in my own use. However, I do have concern for others” . Fewer people recognized that the risks could apply to them: “Probably erosion of critical thinking happens with me too” (P207). Participants also differed in who they thought should address these risks. Some treated the problems as consequences of user behaviors, attributing inappropriate assumptions to “not telling the AI ahead of time” (P115) or privacy risks to 

“user error by oversharing” (P266). Others expected AI systems themselves to intervene ( “Every single one of these cases should just produce a warning that says ‘CONSULT A REAL DOCTOR”’ , P305), or noted a void in regulation ( “no one has yet decided who is liable if the AI is wrong” , P382). These different positions were also reflected in participants’ reactions to the communication. Some became broadly distrustful, noting that “It feel like AI can not be trusted” (P37). Others pushed back on risks involving features they valued ( “Frankly, I wouldn’t use my chatGPT at all if it DIDN’T seem so humanlike in communication” , P69), or even questioned overreaction to risks ( “People can infer a lot just based off a single answer and that is quite unnecessary” , P15). 

5 Discussion 

Our study offers one of the first controlled experiments on how structured risk communication affects people’s trust and confidence in deciding when to rely on LLMs for health information seeking. Results show that risk communication significantly reduced perceived trust, especially when it was contextually relevant. However, this trust calibration did not change how confident they felt in deciding when to rely on them, except for people with high objective knowledge. Task-level effects were similarly uneven: disclosure shifted preferences away from LLMs for medical question answering and impairment & condition assistance, but participants continued to prefer LLMs over traditional search environments for emotional companionship. Together, these findings suggest that risk communication can change how people generally evaluate LLMs, particularly when the disclosed risks are related to the context in which they are used. However, recognizing that a system is risky is different from knowing what to do with that information. For general-purpose LLMs, for effective communication to assist risk recognition and situated action, it needs to account for differences in both the use context and the knowledge users bring to the interaction. 

5.1 Risk Communication as a Corrective for Miscalibrated Trust 

At a time when public understanding lags behind technical development and adoption speed [5, 67, 68, 70, 84], our study provides evidence that risk communication can serve as a corrective mechanism for miscalibrated trust. In our study, before any exposure, participants with the least accurate understanding of how LLMs work reported the highest trust in them and the greatest confidence in deciding when to rely on them. Risk communication significantly reduced trust in LLMs across all four dimensions [52] (beliefs about competence, integrity, and benevolence; intention to depend on LLMs; and intention to share information with and follow their advice). Importantly, it brought those with the least accurate understanding of AI closer to the calibration already held by those with the most accurate ones. Contextual relevance further strengthened this effect. We found that health-specific framing reduced trust beyond what general framing achieved, even though all conditions used health-related example conversations. In other words, showing risks closer to the usage context in the risk message changed how much people trusted the system, despite seeing usage-related examples. This indicates that simply illustrating a general risk with a health example may not make that risk equivalent to communication organized around the consequences and concerns related to the usage case. Beyond “AI Can Make Mistakes” 17 Prior practices developed in domains such as health and crisis communication emphasize quantified and causal communication: report absolute rather than relative risks, express uncertainty as natural frequencies, and show exactly which action leads to which consequence [1, 20, 23, 51]. These techniques sit on the assumptions that the set of possible actions is bounded and that their outcomes can be counted. However, that assumption no longer holds for LLMs. People can ask an LLM about anything, the answers of a given question vary from case to case, and no one can put a reliable number on how often a prompt leads to harm or through what path. What remains available is context. If systems cannot tell users precisely how likely a harm is, they can still show users where it is likely to arise. General framing could leave users to view harm as abstract and distant, while contextually relevant framing give it a recognizable form that the user can imagine encountering. 

5.2 Differentiating Reducing Overtrust from Supporting Reliance Decisions 

Our findings also show that risk communication significantly changed how much people trusted LLMs without changing how confident they felt in deciding when to rely on them, which implies a distinction within AI risk communication. In many domains, risk messages and the recommended action come together: a warning about smoking implies quitting, or a high blood sugar level implies diet changes. Once someone accepts the risk, the actions can be clear. Risk communication for a general-purpose LLM does not work this way. Trust is formed toward the system as a whole, but reliance is decided one task at a time; and as our results indicate, the same system carries different levels of perceived risks when it is used for a medication question, an explanation of medical reports, or a late-night conversation about loneliness. Therefore, telling users that the system is less trustworthy than they assumed may not be enough to guide them in a particular case. We believe that while correcting overtrust is a necessary starting point for more appropriate reliance, it only becomes actionable when users can apply their judgment. In fact, this dissociation echoes a long-standing observation that risk awareness and actionable guidance are distinct outcomes that do not always co-occur [23, 24]. Threat-related information without a sense of efficacy leads to avoidance rather than adaptive action [77]. In our study, providing mechanistic explanations of how LLMs generate outputs did not close this gap as we hoped. To interpret this by separating participants into AI literacy groups, we found that against these adjusted baseline comparisons, only participants who held accurate prior objective knowledge gained decision confidence. This suggests that risk communication repurposed directly from existing taxonomies requires adaptation to be more widely useful for general populations. Accordingly, the relevant evaluation criterion for risk communication should extend beyond whether a warning is noticed or whether it lowers trust. If the intended goal is appropriate reliance, interventions should also be evaluated on whether users can translate disclosed risks into better situated judgments: for example, recognizing when verification, comparison with another source, or deferral is warranted. 

5.3 Implications for Designing Risk Communication for General-Purpose LLMs 

5.3.1 Matching Disclosure to the Task’s Risk Profile. The task-level findings indicate that risk communication should not treat the use of a general-purpose LLM as a single decision context. Risk communication shifted relative preferences of LLMs over traditional search environments for two tasks: medical question answering and impairment & condition assistance. Among the other tasks where risk communication had no significant effect, emotional companionship was the only task for which participants consistently preferred LLMs over search environments that include online communities, regardless of risk exposure. 18 Jiawei Zhou, Mengting Tu, Shravika Mittal, Sowmya Krishna, Q. Vera Liao, and Munmun De Choudhury The differences show that the same disclosure can have different effects depending on what users are trying to accomplish. One possible explanation is that these two tasks are particularly cognitively driven, and their risk surrounds factual accuracy. On the other hand, emotional companionship is relational in nature and valued for its immediacy and lack of judgment [38], qualities that are easier to perceive than the presented risks. Here, the broader implication is that a single communication approach is unlikely to serve use cases with such different risk profiles, where a disclosure effective at reducing overreliance for medical queries may be less applicable to emotionally related use cases, which turns out to be most common among youth [19]. 

5.3.2 Accounting for Who Receives the Disclosure. The moderation findings suggest that risk communication can backfire when it does not account for who receives it. Disclosure only achieved its intended purpose of increasing decision confidence among participants who already held more accurate knowledge of how LLMs work. Moreover, for users who are not personally motivated to process and integrate with risk communication (i.e., people with lower NFC), disclosure may further introduce confusion without resolution. Therefore, communication needs to be designed to make understanding accessible. Prior work suggests that interventions which interrupt heuristic processing can reduce overreliance even among those who prefer less analytical engagement [11], and that interactive formats work better than text-based ones [26]. For AI literacy and appropriate trust specifically, evidence shows that video messages can shape the mental models people attribute to LLMs and affect their reliance on LLM-generated responses [16]. Future work could explore progressive and in-context forms of disclosure to reduce the mental burden of converting abstract disclosure into judgments, such as short task-relevant cues followed by optional explanations, concrete demonstrations of failure, or prompts that translate a stated limitation into an immediate action like checking professional sources. 

5.3.3 From Risk Communication to AI Literacy. Across our results, prior objective AI knowledge appears to condition whether risk information becomes actionable. This does not mean that disclosure should be replaced by broad AI-literacy education. Rather, the two may serve complementary functions. Risk communication can bring a limitation to users’ attention at the moment it becomes relevant, while AI literacy provides the conceptual structure needed to understand what that limitation means and how it should affect subsequent use. This distinction is especially important for generative AI because its failures tend to be less visible and literacy is not guaranteed from usage. Many prior technologies have more visible failures, such as a broken link that fails to load, or a search that returns no matching records. Generative AI’s failures are fluent and presented identically to its successes, with few default cues distinguishing the two [36, 66, 79, 84]. We argue that the less a technology reveals its failures, the more its safe use rests on understanding rather than experience, which is why functional literacy [48] with LLMs in some way depends on conceptual literacy in a way it does not for most prior tools. Therefore, a promising direction is to treat disclosure as an opportunity for situated literacy support rather than as a standalone warning. Recent work on interventions for appropriate reliance points in this direction by pairing disclosure with concrete verification scaffolds [6]. Such practices could also restore some of the distributed judgment that a single synthesized answer removes [64]. Accordingly, instead of only stating that an LLM may produce unreliable information, interfaces might connect the risk to a concrete response: why this type of query is difficult for the system, which alternative sources are available, or when escalation to professional experts is needed. Future work can evaluate whether such scaffolds improve not simply trust or confidence, but behavioral indicators of appropriate reliance, such as source verification or external consultation. 5.4 Limitations and Future Directions 

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings, and some shed light on directions for future research. First, in measuring decision confidence, we intentionally measured this after participants rated their LLM-versus-search preferences among a list of tasks. While this sequencing choice was intended to ground self-reported confidence in concrete task judgments, a single-item measure can have limited reliability and sensitivity compared to a multi-item scale. More broadly, decision confidence captures participants’ perceived certainty about when to rely on LLMs rather than whether their reliance decisions were objectively appropriate. Future work can explore whether changes in trust or confidence correspond to improved calibration of actual reliance behavior. Second, the risk communication content was drawn from established risk taxonomies, selected for their scholarly acceptance (in the case of the general taxonomy) and contextual fit (in the case of the health-specific taxonomy). However, these taxonomies were not originally developed to communicate risks to general audiences. We chose not to modify the original language to avoid introducing unintentional framing biases, but some content or language may not be fully accessible to people without technical backgrounds. Third, the health information scenarios were controlled to minimize confounding, which necessarily traded some ecological validity for experimental rigor. Participants’ responses to scripted conversation vignettes may not fully reflect how they would ask or respond to real LLM interactions where outputs are unpredictable and tailored. Then, from a long-term perspective, participants in our experiment were exposed to risk communication only once. Whether such effects persist over repeated AI use remains an open question, and future work with longitudinal designs can examine the long-term effects: whether initial disclosure effects last or require reinforcement over repeated AI interactions. Finally, although randomization checks confirmed that the five conditions were comparable, the Prolific participant pool can skew younger and more educated than the broader U.S. population. This limits the generalizability of findings to groups with lower digital or health literacy, populations who may be most at risk of miscalibrated AI reliance and who arguably could benefit most from effective risk communication. 

6 Conclusion 

This study examined whether structured risk communication can support a more appropriate trust level and confidence in deciding when to rely on LLMs for health information seeking. In a pre-registered experiment with 500 participants, risk communication significantly reduced trust in LLMs, particularly when risks were health-related. But it did not improve confidence in deciding when to rely on them. Disclosure’s effects also varied by task and prior AI knowledge: risk communication shifted preferences between LLMs and traditional search environments for some health tasks, while participants with lower AI literacy tended to show larger trust reductions without corresponding gains in decision confidence. Together, these findings show that correcting overly positive perceptions of AI is not the same as enabling informed reliance. Effective AI risk communication should therefore move beyond generic disclosure toward context-sensitive and accessible approaches that help users translate knowledge of AI risks into actionable judgments about when and how to rely on these systems.

Medical Question Answering 

(Asking specific questions about illnesses, treatments, test results, or medical terms to better understand health information.) 

A person your age recently had a routine blood test as part of a general checkup. The results came back with a note flagging that their blood sugar level is slightly above normal, and the doctor’s summary mentions the words "pre-diabetes." Their next appointment is in two weeks. They turned to an AI to understand what this means. 

User: “My blood test came back and it says my blood sugar is slightly high. The doctor mentioned pre-diabetes. What does that mean exactly?” 

AI: “Pre-diabetes means your blood sugar levels are higher than normal but not yet high enough to be diagnosed as type 2 diabetes. It’s essentially an early warning sign that your body is having difficulty managing blood sugar effectively At this stage, it doesn’t mean you will develop diabetes — many people with pre-diabetes never progress to type 2, especially with some lifestyle changes. The most commonly recommended steps involve adjustments to diet and physical activity, though what’s most appropriate depends on a person’s individual circumstances. It’s worth having a detailed conversation with your doctor at your next appointment about what your specific result means and what, if anything, they’d recommend for you.” 28 Jiawei Zhou, Mengting Tu, Shravika Mittal, Sowmya Krishna, Q. Therapeutic Mental Health Support 

(Providing guidance and coping-oriented assistance for people experiencing stress, anxiety, low mood, or other emotional difficulties.) 

A person your age has been struggling with persistent low mood for the past few months. They haven’t spoken to a therapist, partly because they’re not sure their problems are "serious enough" to warrant it. One evening they decided to talk it through with an AI instead. 

User: “I’ve been feeling pretty low for a few months now. Nothing dramatic has happened, I just feel kind of flat and unmotivated most of the time. I haven’t talked to anyone about it. I’m not sure if it’s even worth worrying about.” 

AI: “It sounds like you’ve been carrying this quietly for a while — and the fact that you’re not sure it’s worth worrying about is itself worth paying attention to. Feeling flat and unmotivated for months, even without a clear cause, is something that deserves to be taken seriously, not dismissed. Sometimes the absence of a dramatic reason makes it harder to give yourself permission to feel the way you do. But low mood doesn’t need a specific trigger to be real or to matter. Can I ask — when you say flat and unmotivated, is it more that things feel joyless, or more that it feels hard to get started with things, or both? ” 

Emotional Companionship 

(Providing a sense of being heard and accompanied through conversation, especially when someone feels lonely or just wants to talk.) 

A person your age moved to a new city six months ago for a job opportunity. The move made sense at the time, but they haven’t found it easy to build a social life and most evenings are spent alone. They feel the absence of familiar people and places. One evening they started talking to an AI just to have some company. 

User: “I moved to a new city six months ago and I still feel like I haven’t really settled. I don’t have many people here yet and I miss home. I’m fine, I just feel a bit lonely I guess.” 

AI: “Six months can feel like it should be enough time to feel at home somewhere, but it often isn’t — especially when you’re building a social life from scratch in a place where you don’t have shared history with anyone yet. Missing home isn’t just about the place — it’s usually about the ease of being around people who already know you, where you don’t have to work at connection. That kind of comfort takes time to rebuild somewhere new, and until it exists, even a city full of people can feel isolating. What do you miss most about where you were before?” 

Impairment and Condition Assistance 

(Helping to manage daily activities when someone has a condition or impairment (e.g., chronic pain, mobility limits, vision/hearing changes), such as practical tips and accommodations.) 

A person your age has been dealing with persistent knee pain for the past year, diagnosed as early-stage osteoarthritis. Their doctor has told them to stay active but avoid high-impact activities. They’re finding that ordinary daily tasks — climbing stairs, getting up from a chair, walking for longer periods — have become uncomfortable and are starting to limit what they do. They turned to an AI for practical advice. Beyond “AI Can Make Mistakes” 29 

User: “I have knee pain from early osteoarthritis and everyday things like stairs and getting up from chairs have become really uncomfortable. My doctor said to stay active but avoid high impact. What can I do to manage day to day?” 

AI: “Knee osteoarthritis makes a lot of ordinary movements uncomfortable, and staying active while avoiding high impact is the right general direction — movement helps maintain the joint and the muscles around it. A few everyday adjustments tend to help: leading with your stronger leg on stairs, using your arms to push up from chairs, and avoiding seats that are very low all reduce the load on the affected knee. The specifics of what helps most really depend on which activities are giving you the most trouble day to day. What’s been hardest for you lately?” 

Healthy Lifestyle Guidance 

(Providing guidance on habits like eating, physical activity, sleep, and daily routines to support overall health.) 

A person your age has been trying to eat more healthily for a while but is finding it hard to make consistent changes. They eat reasonably well during the day but evenings are harder — they tend to reach for convenient, less nutritious options when tired after work. They’re not looking for a strict diet plan, just practical ways to build better habits. They turned to an AI for guidance. 

User: “I want to eat more healthily but I struggle to keep it up, especially in the evenings when I’m tired. I don’t want a strict diet — just some practical ways to build better habits. Where do I start?” 

AI: “The evening pattern you’re describing — doing well during the day but defaulting to less nutritious options when tired — is very common. Tiredness reduces the mental energy available for decisions, so convenience tends to win. A few things that tend to help: having easier, reasonably nutritious options already available in the evening, and eating enough during the day so you’re not arriving home genuinely hungry on top of tired. Small, specific changes tend to stick better than broad intentions — picking one habit to adjust first usually works better than overhauling everything at once. What does your typical evening look like?”
