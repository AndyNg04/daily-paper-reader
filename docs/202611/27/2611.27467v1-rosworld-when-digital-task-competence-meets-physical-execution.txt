## Abstract 

Computer-use agents now solve most desktop tasks through direct software input, but a robot using the same computer must turn GUI knowledge into physical contact with a keyboard and mouse. We introduce ROSWorld , a benchmark that couples a physically simulated robot workstation to a live OSWorld desktop, so that a robot and a desktop agent pursue the same goals under the same official evaluators. The evaluated model receives observations only from the workstation camera views and operates the peripherals through either a single-arm rigid probe or dual-arm dexterous hands. Across five frontier models and 40 tasks in ten applications, models that complete 31–39 tasks on the desktop complete at most 23 with the probe and 28 with the hands. Paired trajectory analysis shows that models adapt their interaction strategies toward keyboard input, yet physical execution frequently fails through missed presses, incorrect targets, and unintended contacts. ROSWorld makes the gap between knowing what to do on a computer and physically doing it measurable. 

## 1 Introduction 

Computer-use agents [22, 44, 45] can edit documents, manage files, and configure applications through software-issued keyboard and mouse input. These familiar tasks also offer a rich setting for evaluat-ing models that control robots, where a small set of physical peripherals connects the robot to diverse ap-plication workflows, each with a concrete goal. When a model uses a robot to operate the computer, every intended input must be realized through motion and contact. Opening a menu requires locating and ac-tuating the appropriate control; editing a document requires coordinating many such interactions while tracking changes in the application. A missed press or an unintended input can change what must happen next, requiring the model to recognize the discrep-ancy and recover. Physical computer use therefore brings visual understanding, task planning, precise manipulation, and feedback-based recovery together in sustained, goal-directed behavior. It provides a concrete setting for asking how well a model can turn its competence at desktop tasks into ef-fective physical action. 

Many existing embodiment benchmarks [9, 17, 23, 35] focus on assessing physical skills with bounded ma-nipulation goals, such as grasping, placing, or actu-ating an object, while desktop benchmarks [7, 49] provide a rich set of computer-use scenarios with clearly defined, task-oriented purposes. Models need to complete various desktop applications selected from OSWorld [49] through robot controlling and contact with physical peripherals like keyboard and mouse within a simulated environment. Compared with an isolated manipulation goal, completing a com-puter task requires the model to maintain reliable physical control over a sequence of state-dependent operations. Closely spaced keys and small actua-tion travel demand precise contact; modifiers and mouse drags require coordinated holding and release; and the meaning of an input depends on the cur-rent window, focus, and editing state. The model must therefore connect the geometry and timing of its bodily actions to their changing effects in the dig-ital world, recognize ineffective or unintended inputs, and recover while preserving progress toward the task goal. 

ROSWorld evaluates end-to-end physical computer use under a fixed observation and action interface. Perception, action selection, robot control, physical contact, and feedback-based recovery are parts of a single embodied task rather than separately evaluated capabilities. This preserves the concrete challenges of physical computer use: reading the display from camera views, translating GUI intent into accurate 3D contact, and verifying state changes to recover over long horizons, while shared OSWorld tasks and evaluators allow direct comparison with software-based computer use. We couple an Isaac Sim workstation to a live OSWorld desktop. The model reads camera views and robot status, then produces bounded motion sequences. Contact with the keyboard and mouse generates desk-top input, and the updated desktop is rendered on the workstation monitor for the next observation. We evaluate five models on 40 shared tasks across ten application domains with two embodiment configu-rations. Aligning each model’s desktop and physical trajectories shows that the task route is preserved, whereas the input modality changes. Models tend to convert mouse operations into keyboard operations in both embodiment configuration, and with the probe they look for single-key substitutes for key chords. Most physical failures arise from presses that do not reach the key, presses on the wrong target, and un-intended contacts made by other parts of the robot. These unintended contacts reveal that some models fail to account for the robot’s full body during inter-action. At the task level, most failed runs exhaust their step budget, some give up when they judge the task infeasible, and only a few wrongly declare the task complete without passing the evaluator. Our contributions are: • A benchmark that couples simulation with a real system: input signals generated by robot contact with the keyboard and mouse in simulation drive a live VM, yielding long-horizon, fine-grained, and verifiable tasks. • A paired evaluation of five models on 40 OSWorld tasks under shared goals and official evaluators, comparing direct desktop input with two robot embodiments to quantify the gap between desktop competence and physical completion. • A trajectory analysis that links model plans, robot contact, device events, and application outcomes to characterize interaction strategies and identify failures at both the operation and task levels. 

## 2 Related Work 

Table 1 positions ROSWorld among related bench-marks. It is the first benchmark which bridges computer-use tasks and embodiment evaluation. The model produces desktop input through physical con-tact with a keyboard and mouse via robotic control, observes the screen only through camera views, and is scored by the same application-state evaluators as the paired digital run. 

GUI agents and computer-use benchmarks. GUI agents combine visual grounding with sequential inter-2action. Early work learns screenshot-based keyboard and mouse control [22, 44, 45], while ScreenAgent, Cradle, UFO, Agent S, and AssistGUI develop multi-step computer-control frameworks [2, 15, 38, 47, 57]. SeeAct and WebVoyager address web interaction [18, 58]. SeeClick and UGround localize interface ele-ments [10, 16]; Ferret-UI, ScreenAI, and OmniParser study screen understanding and parsing [4, 36, 55]; ScreenSpot-Pro evaluates high-resolution grounding [32]. CogAgent, ShowUI, Aguvis, and UI-TARS inte-grate visual understanding and action [19, 34, 41, 52]. OSWorld and Windows Agent Arena evaluate desk-top tasks [7, 49]; OmniACT evaluates executable task scripts [25]. Mind2Web provides web demonstrations [12]; WebArena, VisualWebArena, and WorkArena evaluate interactive web workflows [14, 27, 60]; An-droidWorld checks mobile task outcomes [42]. OS-World extensions examine complementary aspects of computer use: OSWorld-Verified improves task qual-ity and evaluator robustness [51]; OSWorld-G isolates GUI grounding, including layout understanding and precise manipulation [50]; OSWorld-Human compares agent trajectories with human references to assess interaction efficiency [1]; and OSWorld-MCP jointly evaluates GUI interaction and MCP tool invocation [24]. We select tasks from OSWorld-1 on which main-stream models already demonstrate strong desktop performance (Table 3), providing a capable digital ref-erence for studying physical execution. ROSWorld 

retains these goals and official evaluators while re-placing direct software input with robot sensing and contact, enabling a paired comparison of the same model on the same task. 

Embodied evaluation. Embodied evaluation spans simulation platforms, instruction-following tasks, and robot manipulation. AI2-THOR provides interactive indoor scenes, while Habitat supports scalable visual navigation and embodied-agent evaluation [28, 43]. ALFRED evaluates compositional household tasks from language instructions and egocentric observa-tions [46], and BEHAVIOR-1K extends evaluation to a broad range of everyday activities [31]. For robot manipulation, RLBench supplies a multi-task learning environment [23]; Meta-World studies multi-task and meta-learning across manipulation goals [56]; ManiSkill2 emphasizes skills that generalize across object geometry and manipulation settings [17]; LIBERO studies knowledge transfer in lifelong robot manipulation [35]. More recently, Embodied-Bench studies multimodal agents across semantic and low-level embodied tasks [53], while RobotWorld ex-amines general-purpose agents across diverse robot embodiments and physical goals [54]. RoboDojo eval-uates generalist manipulation policies in simulation and the real world, with simulation tasks targeting generalization, memory, precision, long-horizon ex-ecution, and open-vocabulary instruction following [9]. These works characterize embodied generaliza-tion. Our focus is a matched comparison of the same model and desktop goal under software input and robot contact, using a shared official outcome evalua-tor. Robot-video benchmarks also study fine-grained event understanding and trajectory evaluation. WGO-Bench evaluates temporal segmentation and semantic labeling of manipulation events [37], while RoboRewardBench, Robometer, and Flora study out-come assessment, progress estimation, and reward annotation [29, 30, 33]. RubricBench studies rubric-guided evaluation of text responses [59]. These works inform trajectory analysis and evaluator design; ROS-World evaluates agents acting in a closed loop, with success determined by live application state. 

Vision-language-action models. Vision-language-action (VLA) models such as RT-2, OpenVLA, and 

π0 learn robot actions from visual observations, lan-guage instructions, and manipulation demonstrations [6, 8, 26]. SayCan grounds language planning in skill affordances [3], while PaLM-E incorporates contin-uous sensory inputs into language models [13]. In-ner Monologue uses feedback for embodied planning [20], and VoxPoser grounds manipulation in spatial value maps [21]. ROSWorld evaluates general mul-timodal models with desktop competence, requiring them to execute application workflows through phys-ical keyboard and mouse interaction. 

Robot typing and paired desktop goals. Robot typ-ing includes tactile reinforcement learning on braille keycaps [11] and hierarchical reinforcement learning for dual-arm keyboard input [5]. The Isaac Lab SO-101 project trains letter presses using a fixed key-position map and provides hardware deployment [48]. ROSWorld connects camera-observed typing, mouse manipulation, and application workflows to live application-state scores. Whereas RobotWorld compares tasks and embodiments [54], ROSWorld 

pairs digital and physical execution of the same desk-top goal by the same model under a shared evaluator. 

## 3 ROSWORLD 

33.1 Shared digital goals, physical interac-tion 

A workstation contains a monitor, keyboard, mouse, and Franka robot. Isaac Sim and PhysX simulate robot and peripheral motion at 480 Hz [39, 40]. The monitor displays a live Ubuntu VM initialized by OSWorld. Physical key travel and mouse motion generate desktop input; the resulting desktop image updates the monitor texture. Figure 1 illustrates this feedback loop. We evaluate two embodiments. A single Franka with a rigid probe has a compact contact tip for arm posi-tioning, key actuation, and mouse manipulation, but it cannot hold one key while pressing another. The dual-arm embodiment uses two Franka arms, each equipped with a 6-DoF Inspire Hand, enabling key chords and button-held gestures. Both embodiments use the same desktop tasks and physical peripheral assets. 

3.2 Observations and actions 

The model receives a front view of the workstation, a top-down view of the manipulation area, fixed camera calibration, and its own joint, tool-pose, and contact-force measurements. These observations combine the digital state visible on the monitor with the physical state needed to act on it. Figure 2 shows the paired camera views. Each decision round follows the protocol in Table 2. The model issues bounded motion commands through a validated JSON interface. A generic inverse-kinematics and joint-drive controller tracks these com-mands; the model determines contact locations and release timing. Key coordinates, device poses, and direct GUI actions are not provided to the embodied policy. This design makes the input operation itself part of the task. Pressing a target key requires spatial alignment and sufficient travel, while a click requires mouse positioning and button actuation. The same physical action may also displace a device, uninten-tionally contact a neighboring key, or obscure the display, changing what the model must do next. 

ROSWorld evaluates the complete loop from cam-era observation to application outcome. Task success requires the model to coordinate perception, planning, physical input, and feedback-based recovery under the specified interface and budget; the recorded in-teractions expose where this end-to-end execution breaks.

3.3 Physical–digital loop 

Keyboard press and release edges are detected from key travel; mouse movement, buttons, and wheel motion are detected from peripheral state. Each key is a sprung prismatic joint that registers a press at 2 mm of travel and a release below 1 mm, with an 8 ms debounce. The mouse reports motion through an optical-sensor model (800 DPI) only while it rests within 3 mm of the desk and tilts less than 25°; its buttons actuate at 0.55 mm and its wheel has 24 detents per turn. A key therefore registers only when contact drives it past the actuation point, whichever part of the robot makes the contact. The bridge sends the generated inputs to the VM. Physics pauses during model inference. To preserve contact durations independently of inference latency, the bridge buffers keyboard and button-held mouse gestures until full release, then replays them using their physical timestamp offsets. ## 4 Paired Evaluation 

4.1 Shared goals and physical control 

We evaluate five models on the same 40 OSWorld tasks, with four tasks in each of ten domains: Chrome, GIMP, LibreOffice Calc, Impress, Writer, multi-application workflows, OS, Thunderbird, VLC, and VS Code. Tasks are selected through stratified sam-pling, execution-based validation, and manual review to filter out tasks with infrastructure issues or unsolv-able steps—verifying that every retained task is solv-able in our setting—while maintaining balanced do-main coverage and a mix of document editing, appli-cation settings, file operations, and cross-application workflows. Each task uses its OSWorld initialization and official evaluator. The selection also spans the input skills a desktop route needs: typing, single keys, key chords, mouse clicks, drags, and scrolling. The OSWorld condition uses desktop screenshots and PyAutoGUI. The two physical conditions use work-station cameras and either the single-arm rigid probe or the dual-arm hands to operate the keyboard and mouse. Both conditions use the same task definitions and official outcome evaluators, forming one-to-one paired model–task results. Each condition forms a complete observation–action loop: direct desktop perception and software control in the OSWorld con-dition, and camera perception and contact control in the physical condition. Both conditions allow up to 100 model decisions. This budget fixes the number of model–environment interaction rounds, not physical motion time or device events; the decisions spent on positioning, contact, and recovery are part of the evaluated embodied task. With API-served models, a 100-step physical episode takes 2–5 hours of wall-clock time, most of it model inference. With full parallelism, simulating the 400 physical episodes reported here occupied 16 RTX 5090 GPUs for about two days; open-weight model inference ran on separate services. Model requests use the maximum reasoning setting and the officially recommended sampling parameters.

Task termination. Of 304 failed episodes, 239 ex-haust the 100-step budget (Table 4). Of the budget-exhausted runs, 217 complete no new OSWorld route operation during the final 20 decisions, and 166 in-clude at least three consecutive failed attempts at the same target. 5.5 Decision budget 

Figure 9 shows the decision-budget curve for GPT-6 Astra, the strongest physical model. All 39 OSWorld completions finish within 18 decisions (median 8), whereas physical completions require a median of 33 with either embodiment. Thus, the 100-decision limit already exceeds five times its longest OSWorld run. By 50 decisions, the probe and hands solve 18 and 20 tasks; by 80, they reach 21 and 26, gaining only two more each by 100 (23 and 28). Physical execution demands far more rounds, yet gains flatten well before the limit. 

5.6 Implications for model training 

Each failure mode points to data that desktop tra-jectories do not contain. 

Effector-to-target geometry. Misses are the largest failure for every model (27–70% of attempts). Training data should pair camera views and effector poses with whether, and which, key actuated, includ-ing near misses, so that a model learns where its effector is relative to a target. 

Whole-body awareness. Up to a third of the weaker models’ key presses come from the arm, the probe shaft, or a finger other than the one pointing. Data should expose the contacts of the entire body, not only the effector, and favour plans that keep the rest of the body clear of the device. 

Embodiment-conditioned routes and verifica-tion. Models keep their desktop routes but must work around actions the effector cannot deliver, and even the strongest models stall on repeated, unver-ified retries. Learning which input modalities an embodiment can produce, and confirming each effect on screen before the next step, would turn the same desktop competence into fewer and surer physical steps. 

## 6 Conclusion 

ROSWorld evaluates physical computer use through shared desktop goals, official evaluators, and robot contact with a keyboard and mouse. Models with strong desktop scores show sharply different physical completion, and the choice of embodiment further separates them. Paired trajectories trace the gap from model plans through contact and input events to application outcomes, establishing ROS-World as a testbed for turning digital task compe-tence into embodied action.
