Abstract 

There is at present no objective, agnostic method to track levels of message authorship and agency in non-speakers who rely on assisted typing to communicate. Part of the problem is that nuances in spontaneous gestural movements of the typer transpire largely beneath awareness of both the typer and the communication-regulation-partner. Such spontaneous movements co-occur with the intentional ones producing the typed messages but are currently not considered part of the communication system. We introduce an assay that probes various levels of cognitive loads in an ecologically valid environment. Then, in collaboration with an emerging speaker-typer and his communication-regulation-partner, we empirically estimate motoric stochastic signatures during both independent and assisted typing. Under varying levels of cognitive loads, the assay and biometrics distinguish motoric patterns in spontaneous typing of phrases in response to open-ended questions from those in instructed typing of the same phrase. The non-dominant bodily motions, co-occurring with the dominant (typing) side reveal not only levels of cognitive mental effort and agency of the typer but also levels of creative thoughts through motoric preambles present only in open-ended mode. This level of semantic authorship is detectable in 

both the geodesic paths along the manifold representing the Gamma stochastic parameters of the motions’ 

fluctuations and those of the physical 3D spatial trajectories. The head and facial micro-motions capture these non-dominant-body nuances, offering a personalized motoric metric of cognitive effort, agency, authorship and a comprehensive suite of assays to open new research avenues for assisted typing with haptic support. 2

1 Introduction 

Communication is paramount to human agency. The ability to immediately transmit, at will, our feelings, emotions, and mental or physical states to others can be a life saving grace in difficult situations. In the spectrum of autism (ASD) communication is only recently being addressed and no proper body of research exists to guide the process of language acquisition when neurodevelopmental paths leading to autonomous motor control are delayed or stalled, as it seems to be the case in the type of ASD that requires high levels of support and cannot speak [1]. 

Conservative estimates by the US Centers for Disease Control (CDC) suggest that approximately 30% of autistic people are minimally verbal or non-speaking (ns-ASD) [2, 3]. There is however no reliable count or statistic informing us how many people can type or point to communicate. In advanced cases, the autistic person communicating with others can fluidly navigate from a keyboard to a tablet, to a letterboard ( Supplementary Figures 1-3 ). Most importantly, advanced users of these techniques can utter the words being typed, as the 

communication regulating partner (CRP) becomes merely a presence to help regulate the person’s body as 

needed. One way to regulate the typer is through haptic feedback in the form of light touch. Those few ns-ASD who learned to type independently and even began to vocalize the phrases they type, followed a learning trajectory that brought them to that level of independence. Yet we do not understand the mechanisms that led to this extraordinary feat. We need metrics that help us track that process in ns-ASD, particularly when we know that neurotypical language acquisition follows a very different, inverted neuromotor path first learning how to talk and then later, by school age, mastering writing/spelling to communicate. Research addressing the ns-ASD learning process is missing. 

As learning to communicate is a process that takes years, it evolves through different stages of autonomy. These 

may initially rely excessively on the CRP but as learning milestones are achieved, the CRP gradually transfers 

agency to the typer. The average ns-ASD engaging with these adopted world-wide communication techniques 

needs the regulating partner to keep the conversation flowing. Along those lines, recent advances in biometrics 

from digital data reveal not only the possibility of tracking the progression of agency while acquiring 

independence [4] but more importantly, they reveal the regulation levels badly needed in the process of learning 

to type independently (See Supplementary Figure 4 ). 

Research involving motor control metrics derived from wearables’ kinematic data demonstrated that touch may 

reduce cognitive load during assisted typing [5]. They have also demonstrated, using corpora of 20 years of 

communication involving multiple CRPs, that ns-ASD using these techniques make their own stylistic 

contributions to text written with such physical facilitation [6]. And eye-tracking studies in the US suggest that 

ns-ASD participants predictively look first at the letter that they are about to point during spelling exercises [7]. 

Studies from Israel suggest that saccades in ns-ASD may demonstrate receptive language better than pointing 

(which is more arduous) [8], despite excessive random noise in their eye motions [9]. This result concerning the 

eyes’ motions is congruent with excessive random noise found in their involuntary head motions at rest [10], 

their erratic heart rate variability in sympathetic overdrive, even at rest [11-14], their dysregulated gait [15] and 

their voluntary movements aimed at visual targets [16, 17] or when trying to orient to and/or differentiate 

auditory targets [18-20]. Supplementary Figure 4 offers compelling evidence for the need to help regulate the 

autistic system and move it towards physical comfort. 

1.1 Regulating Motor Control by Offering a Frame of Reference 

Physical independence, however, is different from authorship of communicating messages. The latter can happen even when the person is being physically supported to provide anchors across the body and help create interchangeable frames of reference . Frames of reference are necessary in sensory-to-motor coordinate 

transformations because different extrinsic and intrinsic sensory sources have disparate latencies in signals’ 

transduction and transmission across different brain-body systems. 

When a visual target (such as a letter on a keyboard or letter board) is presented to the brain, or an auditory cue is used to prompt a goal-directed action, the head must properly orient and coordinate with the trunk and the 3

hand to deliver a fluid pointing movement to the targeted letter. Several sensory signals’ transductions, 

transmissions and transformations delays occur in the process of delivering the motor commands, before the hand is launched towards the target in a controlled and well-coordinated manner. The brain must transform signals, from retinotopic to head centered coordinate frames [21]. This flow of sensory information originates in an external sensory source (light) and is first mediated by photoreceptors in the eye’s retina. To generate goal -directed action to a target external to the body, latencies linked to external sources must align with those linked to internal sensory sources , anchored in the body. As the human body has many degrees of freedom (DOF), the unfolding motions afford us great flexibility to accomplish the same final goal in an infinite number of manners 

– a problem beautifully stated by Nikolai Bernstein in the first half of the last century [22, 23]. This redundancy however, also creates complex computational problems that require time to solve and continuously update during planning and execution of such purposeful actions [24]. 

To solve these problems, disparate sensory sources must be integrated and transformed into motor coordinates representing the ongoing movements, which are also the returning stream (kinesthetic reafferent feedback [25]) 

that the brain uses to update and predict impending actions’ consequences [24]. Considering their variable latencies is key to timely aligning our actions to our thoughts and to forecasting the impending consequences of both intended and unintended movements. 

Kinesthetic reafferent signals mediated by mechanoreceptors coexist with other afferent streams mediated by nociceptive and thermal receptors. These intrinsic signals, coded relative to internal sensory streams, are facilitated in part by the brainstem, a conduit to both subcortical and cortical structures involved in the control, coordination and sequencing of autonomic, reflexive, spontaneous, involuntary, automatic and voluntary motions [26]. Because of its central role, since conception, in central patterns generation and protective reflexes, we posit that the brainstem is key to the chronometric alignment required between external (world-anchored) and internal (body-anchored) sensory sources [27]. 

Temporal alignment of disparate sensory-motor streams is thus necessary to produce gross motor movements but also to coordinate fine motor control during vocalization via shared structures in the vocal apparatus. There the power source (respiration), the sound source (phonation), and the filter and modifier system (resonance and articulation) must negotiate such temporal alignment codes from birth, to master autonomous control of breathing, swallowing and vocalization [28] -without choking or aspiring food into the lungs while feeding. During neurotypical development, these temporal alignments occur seamlessly so they are obviated in our research programs. However, the moment we interact with autism, we can see prolonged delays in sensory processing and sensory motor integration, since birth [16]. 

The auditory brainstem response (ABR) test routinely administered at birth in the US, has revealed such prolonged delays in the auditory domain [29-33]. Tracking public records in the states of New York and Florida, we were able to see that neonates with such prolonged ABR latencies and narrowed bandwidth of response ranges at birth, later received the autism diagnosis by 3-4 years of age and they were still present in autistic children between 1-6 years of age [29]. This temporal misalignment of the ABR necessarily impacts the motor code, [26, 34, 35]. The brainstem role in CPGs producing spontaneous movements is key to the maintenance of healthy motor variability necessary throughout life. Healthy motor variability is required to build steady motor percepts and to enable cognitive control of our biomechanical motions [16, 17, 36, 37]. 

The quality of spontaneous movements in early infancy denotes a healthy neuromotor control trajectory. As spontaneous motions across the body transition into antigravity intentional movements, we can quantify, very early, the proper evolution of voluntary neuromotor control [38, 39]. We can also track the spontaneous motions that are incidental to goal-directed movements [36, 40], as they contribute to our understanding of unintended consequences and help us learn to build awareness through movement [41-45]. Together, through complementary roles, spontaneous and volitional motions can help us track the development of bodily motor percepts, update body schemas and, in the words of Esther Thelen, help us ground and situate the nervous systems for embodied cognition [46-48]. 4

Embodied cognition can be tracked through spontaneous gestural movements embedded in purposeful actions, detectable through biosensors. These include pointing to communicate [16, 49] or performing sports and dancing activities [36]. Their patterns of variability are distinct from those of deliberate segments in the continuous trajectories describing our actions [36, 37, 40] . The spontaneous segments of purposeful movements’ excursions 

described by our end effectors are subject to different geometric constraints than the deliberate segments [37]. Their temporal dynamics reflect the forces that produce our motions [36, 37], unlike the deliberate segments where the geometric features of the intended movement paths tend to remain impervious to changes in the forces [36, 40, 50-52]. Once initiated, geometric features of the intended path to point to a letter on a keyboard remain steady despite changes in the patterns of acceleration and speed. One can therefore automatically identify and isolate key features of our mental intent and key elements of our physical volition in natural motions containing both deliberate and spontaneous segments, [37, 53-57]. Because spontaneous variations in our movements also increase with cognitive effort, they can signal levels of cognitive load [17, 37]. Furthermore, since spontaneous movements flow in open-ended conversations, where they transpire largely beneath the person’s awareness, we 

can use them to understand creative processes in the production of semantic phrases to communicate our thoughts. Spontaneous movements are spared in autism [16]. They thus offer a malleable, plastic signal to help the system develop the sense of action ownership and ultimately enhance the sense of agency in the person [17]. 

As such, we can use spontaneous motions to help us track levels of agency and ownership of the person’s inner 

thoughts as they unfold in assisted communication. 

In this paper, we define a suite of assays and personalized, standardized biometrics that leverage the spontaneous movements of the non-dominant side of the body to distinguish authorship in open-ended vs. instructed communication settings. As a person types or gestures to communicate with the dominant side of the body, the non-dominant side simultaneously moves largely beneath awareness of the typer and the CRP. Consequently, motions of the non-dominant side co-occurring with those of the dominant side during communication, cannot be deliberately manipulated by an external agent without being noticed. We find that the head motions are closer to the motions of the non-dominant side of the body, both in physical and stochastic spaces, thus offering the potential to serve as a proxy broadcasting levels of agency, cognitive effort and semantic authorship. Here we provide agnostic biometrics to objectively track the course of communication in a person, both when the person types independently and when the communication is assisted by haptic feedback. We offer a new avenue to research communication in the face of somatic-sensory-motor control issues demanding bodily support. 

2 Methods 

2.1 Participant 

This study took place at the Sensory Motor Integration Laboratory of Rutgers University under IRB protocol 

Pro2020000154 (“ Digital Characterizations of Nervous Systems ”) in compliance with the Helsinki Act. We 

attained consent from the adult participants to be videotaped and photographed. This is a case study involving Matthew Hayes (MH) an emergent speaker adult with a diagnosis of ASD who willingly participated and communicated with our team using various means. These included iPhone, pocket-size cardboards with letters, larger letterboards and electronic keyboards. The participant could type independently (without the keyboard being sustained by another person). As he typed, he repeated the words out loud. At the end of typing the full phrase, he said it out loud as well. In some instances of the suite of assays that we designed, the CRP provided haptic feedback by light touch of the right knee to dampen involuntary motions of the lower body. To sooth himself, the participant would spin a hanger with the typing hand when he was not typing. As he set the hanger aside to type during the experiments, he produced other wondering motions with the typing hand while thinking about the answers to open ended questions. These wondering motions were minimal during instructed typing, but returned with cognitive effort, albeit in a very different manner than during the expansive open-ended motoric preamble. We took breaks between conditions to minimize and avoid fatigue. The participant indicated when he wanted to break to drink water, snack or simply rest from the experiment. 5

2.3 Assay to Automatically Extract Motoric Signatures of Authorship 

We examined the biomechanical signatures across the upper body to interrogate fundamental differences between owning the motoric output of the body while elaborating and spontaneously communicating a thought by typing a phrase (and saying it) vs. typing the same phrase while prompted by another person. We use kinematic and stochastic analyses. Results from HRV and facial data will be reported elsewhere. Importantly, we specifically examine the non-dominant side of the body because as the dominant hand types in open ended or instructed mode, or as it types assisted by the CRP, the co-occurring motions of the non-dominant side of the body transpire out of conscious reach from the CRP and from the typer himself. The paradigm is designed to evoke such motoric differences across the following conditions, where order matters (see Figure 3 ). 

2.4 Warming up motions from Adaptation to a New Context and Environment 

Prior to the experiment, we recorded warming up motions. These are spontaneous movements while acclimating to the setup, the sensors, the physical layout of the lab and the social-emotional moods of the group, in general. These movement trajectories registered from the upper body in the physical 3D space along with their stochastic trajectories on the Γ manifold were obtained but they are not part of the experiment per se. They are part of the natural adaptation process of any social situation. 

2.5 The Flow of the Experiment 

First, one needs to probe communication performance using open-ended questions that evoke spontaneous thoughts. Throughout this process, we record the phrases typed (and spoken in cases where speech is emerging or steady). In cases where speech is absent, we can record vocalizations, echolalic phrases, self-talk or any spontaneous utterances that the person makes. Second, one needs to select a phrase (vocalization, echolalic or self-utterances) from the spontaneous responses to the open-ended questions or from any spontaneous self-utterances. Finally, probe the selected phrase / self-generated utterances under instruction by prompting it, to type it / spell it under different cognitive, memory and emotional loads. Notice here that launching the arm-hand linkage to type is a gross motor act while vocalization is a fine motor act. Both can be tested and data registered and analyzed using these same biometrics. 

Cognitive loads range from low (using a familiar keyboard or letter board); medium (using an unfamiliar letter board or keyboard but retaining the CRP) and high (combining the unfamiliar letter board or keyboard with an unfamiliar communication partner who offers no physical support). The latter evokes emotional load in ecologically valid situations, like those in real life, when one needs to communicate with different people who may have different social-emotional interaction styles. In addition to these, we included the condition with support from a familiar partner and familiar keyboard, yet requesting the participant and CRP to regress to the type of facilitated communication (FC) support that they practiced in the initial learning stages during MH childhood. Under such conditions, the hand of the partner serves as a supporting anchor to the typer and helps 

guide the participant’s hand as he types. Given the level of independence of MH, we hypothesized that the 

performance would become more variable than typing the prompted phrase without such support. This regression to childhood style would increase memory loads of latent dyadically shared motor programs. This assay also gave us the opportunity to measure levels of agency and leadership during the performance, as they dynamically change from moment to moment. Furthermore, we hypothesized that spontaneous typing responses to open-ended questions would show a fundamentally different motoric signature from instructed cases, i.e., prompting a phrase selected from those uttered and typed during the open-ended responses phase of the assay.

We posit that increasing the level of cognitive load would increase the variability of biomechanical motoric actions and be captured both in the physical movement trajectories in 3D space and in the stochastic trajectories 8

derived from the corresponding micro-variations in linear speed. Prior evidence from the lab supported this hypothesis in neurotypicals [54, 58, 59]. Key to this supposition was the monitoring of spontaneous movements occurring largely beneath awareness. In particular, we monitor the spontaneous motions of the non-dominant side of the body because these segments of the body are out of the intentional control of the participant while performing the biomechanical action [36] and away from the CRP awareness. They escape awareness of the unfamiliar partner and the prompter sitting across the table from the participant as well. Our full focus in fact was on typing by the dominant hand as this was the only typing hand. The incidental motions of the non-dominant side, which spontaneously transpired largely beneath our awareness, were used as motoric signatures indicating the level of authorship in open-ended cases above the levels of cognitive effort that we used throughout the assay. 

In a natural setting of assisted communication (e.g., FC, rapid prompting method (RPM), spell to communicate (S2C), etc.) the non-dominant side of the body moves spontaneously out of reach from the CRP/Prompter and even out of reach from the typer while typing. Given our discovery that spontaneous movements are key to our understanding of volition and motor control [16, 36], we precisely focused on the spontaneous, goal-less, incidental movements to reference the levels of cognitive load vs. creative wondering, during typing a phrase to answer open-ended questions and typing that same phrase under instruction. We also monitored it when the CRP assisted the typer by having the typer rest his hand over her hand (see Supplementary Figures 1-2-3 ). We 

documented video continuously and saved the biosensors’ data for all these conditions in Table 1 .

2.7 Kinematics Analyses 

The 3D position data from all sensors was buffered and saved every 5 minutes, to avoid data loss during the conversation flow. The Motion Monitor software provides real-time visualization of the upper body positional trajectories, as they unfold ( Supplementary Figure 5 ), as well as the ECG data from the EMG sensor, along with a plethora of kinematics parameters. Position data was then analyzed with in-house software. Using MATLAB splines toolbox, we smoothed the positional trajectories and derived the linear speed to examine the peaks and valleys of the continuous time series that they produce. We saved all data and documented the sessions using video and audio recordings. Supplementary Figure 6 shows the steps to acquire the temporal speed profiles and do detail kinematics analyses. 

2.8 Stochastic Analyses 

The speed profiles were used to examine the peaks and their latencies. The function findpeaks from MATLAB was used to obtain the peaks and locations of the peaks (latencies). Inter-peak-intervals (IPI) latencies will be featured in a different paper, due to space constraints. Linear Speed peak amplitudes were gathered into a frequency histogram and the distribution fitting toolbox in MATLAB used to determine the best fitting distribution. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) functions were also used to probe different scenarios. The continuous Gamma family of probability distributions was the best fit in an MLE sense. We used 95% confidence intervals to empirically determine the Γ shape and scale parameters of the micro-movement spikes (micro-peaks) derived from the linear speed peaks. Then we obtained the Γ moments (mean, variance, skewness and kurtosis) for each set of amplitude and latency parameters. Supplementary Figure 7 shows the pipeline of these analyses which have been previously published and patented.

2.13 Agency Index 

In prior work, we developed indexes of socio-motor agency as a ratio of motor control to motor autonomy [4]. Motor autonomy is expressed as approximate entropy, a measure of order, ranging from low as completely ordered (deterministic) to high as totally disordered (chaotic). From the standpoint of an external controlling agent, at the chaotic end, the system of the agent being controlled is uncontrollable and as such, it has full autonomy. The other extreme (deterministic) reflects, from the standpoint of the external controlling agent, that the system being controlled is fully controllable and as such, it has no autonomy. In the situation where the CRP guides the hand of the participant, we wanted to quantify the extent to which the CRP was fully surrendering her autonomy (high Approximate entropy) and as such letting the participant drive the movements. This is a situation whereby the CRP motor system guides the other without overpowering control and in the case of being instructed, the participant surrenders his full autonomy to follow instructions. 

The other term in the agency ratio refers to predictive motor control. To obtain this index, we relied on the 

scaling power law characterizing Gamma processes of MMS whereby the Г -NSR (variance to mean ratio) provides a range including at one extreme predictability and signal certainty and at the other extreme, randomness and memoryless processes characterized by high noise. 2.14 Transfer Entropy Analyses 

We used the Infodynamics-dist toolbox (version 1.6) [61] in MATLAB to compute continuous transfer entropy

We obtained pairwise measures of Transfer entropy (TE) to quantify the directed flow of information between the time series of micro-peaks in the MMS obtained from the sensors of the typer and that on the wrist of the supporting (dominant) hand of the CRP. We seek to quantify statistical causality by measuring how much knowing the past of one system (the typer set as the source) reduces uncertainty about predicting future events of the other system (CRP set as the destination), beyond what its own past already tells it. We then invert the source and destination and obtain a matrix of asymmetric entry values indicating (through the rows) which body part from which agent of this dyad, leads the interaction. We look 100ms and 200ms into the past as these are appropriate ranges of latencies for visual and motor-reafference sensory processing [62-66].

3.8 Response Latencies Distinguish Cognitive Effort from Semantic Authorship in Open-Ended conditions 

Cognitive effort is present in both open-ended questions and instructed-with-cognitive-load conditions. It is important to disentangle the motoric differences as in both cases, the cognitive load leaks into the motor code. What stands out in open-ended questions, where creative thought rather than rote repetition of a prompted phrase drives the system? 

Up to this point, we examined the role of the non-dominant side of the body, away from conscious awareness as the dominant side is simultaneously performing the typing action. Here we examine the differences in relation to the performance of the dominant hand. To that end, we look at the latencies of the right-hand motions 1 second from the moment that we asked the open-ended question or gave the instruction of typing the prompted phrase. We did the instructed case with low cognitive load (familiar environment) and mild load (covered keyboard and regression to FC). We highlight in cyan 90 seconds of the performance after the 1 second buffer and compare the motions. 4 Discussion 

This work proposes a new comprehensive assay and set of biometrics to establish motoric indexes of creative authorship vs. cognitive effort in communication through typing. The results from our case study revealed that the non-dominant part of the body, which moves largely beneath awareness of the typer and of the CRP, funnels out the differences in cognitive load when the typer spontaneously responds to open-ended questions vs. when the same phrase from that spontaneous response is used in deliberate instructed typing by prompting the phrase. These profound differences in the movement trajectories of the non-dominant part of the body consist of a dormant state during instructed biomechanical typing with low cognitive load. They however return to full engagement when we add cognitive load to the deliberate instructed typing. Through the physical movement trajectories in 3D space and through the abstract geodesics of the Γ-manifold, the non-dominant side of the body unambiguously reveals both the type of creative thinking that goes with the open-ended questions and the biomechanical thinking that goes with the typing of the same phrase, when the phrase is prompted. What is then the role of the dominant hand performing the intentional typing motion? 

Semantic Ownership vs. Cognitive Effort: The role of the dominant hand is evident during creative thinking, prior to and during engaging in the typing of a new phrase. The signatures on the Γ moments space in this condition unfold along different FR-geodesic paths (see also the head signatures in Figure 14C and 

Supplementary Figure 9 ). The spontaneous open-ended vs. deliberate instructed assays can automatically 

separate conditions using the slopes and intercepts of the scaling power law relations spanned by the Γ -parameters plane and differential entropy. Figure 15 also illustrates this point zooming into the physical 3D trajectories of the first 90 seconds of the typing motions of the same phrase in Figure 7 . Detailed geometric analyses of the 3D curves involving the Frenet-Serret moving frames quantified such semantic ownership in both the dominant (intended) and non-dominant (spontaneous motions) as they unfold simultaneously in each assay. Whereas the open-ended creative thoughts unfold in the first 90 seconds of the motion through wondering of the dominant hand, the instructed movements nearly completed typing the phrase with no wondering prelude. 28

Ownership in FC : Regressing to childhood FC-style, we examined a standardized index of motor agency as a ratio of ΓSNR for each assay on the denominator, and the sum of ΓSNR plus the level of Approximate Entropy of each assay in the denominator. As Approximate Entropy increases and the motions are unpredictable, Agency decreases. We found that during FC the CRP levels of Approximate Entropy overpowered her levels of agency. 

As her agency level decreased, she must have surrendered her control in favor of passive support to the typer’s hand. In contrast, the typer’s sensor on the forearm leading the hand during this form of assisted typing had 

higher levels of agency and more predictive value of Approximate entropy (lower values). This was also attested by the relationship between our normalized index of Agency and the Gamma differential entropy. Together with the (non-obvious) patterns of the non-dominant side of the body broadcasting the levels of spontaneity or deliberateness, or levels of cognitive loads during typing, these biometrics provide new ways to track the progression of the learning process that takes place in communication through typing. This is important as harsh criticism of supported communication has stalled or altogether halted the teachings of these methods requiring haptic support in the initial stages of learning to move and control the eye-head-trunk-arm-hand coordination that must take place for the pointing to succeed and acquire the target in a timely fashion. 

4.1 A New Path for Supported Typing 

Biometrics and assays here offer a new avenue for learning to type with support because they are agnostic and objective. They unambiguously reveal the level of agency of the typer and the CRP. The participant in this study is an advanced typer who can vocalize what he types, and, in this way, his performance offers a way to verify the message directly. However, the assays and biometrics presented here are general and work with any level of communication and any type of typing style. A novice who still needs haptic support from a communication partner, whether holding a hand or holding a board where the typer spells to communicate, or pointing to a piece of paper, etc. can also provide motoric data produced under the assays in Figure 3 . Combining the measures of the non-dominant side of the body and those of the typing arm and the supporting hand give a full package to track levels of agency, autonomy and control in the dyad. Should the CRP provide excessive support and decrease the typ er’s agency index, we can alert the CRP to back off and allow the typer to lead. Metrics of 

transfer entropy, approximate entropy, Γ differential entropy can give us this ability. As such it is OK to follow the process of learning to type with appropriate support. 

We caution that our biometrics and assays do not say that any given person owns the typed message. They merely point out the degree to which the motions are tied to cognitive effort or creative thoughts. A novice will first have to learn to own their actions, to self-regulate them, and to fluidly navigate the intrinsic and extrinsic sensory-motor sources of their own body in motion. These biometrics and assays will help the novice get there because they measure performance objectively and agnostically, in a personalized manner. They measure the 

person’s performance to protect the person from excessive control by others and to nurture motoric communication towards full autonomy. By revealing who leads with agency in this dyadic interaction we offer the possibility of accelerating the learning process in a respectful and humane manner. 

The case of MH gave us an upper bound to measure departure from and help others start this journey with concrete higher-level goals. We know the destination and can now have rigorous methodology to move in that direction. This work is an invitation to join us and work together with an unprecedented combination of rigor, transparency and kindness. As altricial mammals with a long developmental path at birth, we all started with 

assistance from our progenitors. It is nature’s way to help us grow strong but it is also, most importantly, our right because we are alive. 

4.2 A Useful Analogy Moving Forward 

The process of learning to type with support is akin to the process of Brain Computer Interfaces (BCIs) or Brain Machine Interfaces (BMIs) where the brain signals are used to learn to control an external device (e.g., a cursor on a computer screen [53] or a robotic arm [72, 73]). In those learning-with-assistance scenarios a computer algorithm plays the role of the CRP, and the brain signals represent the pointing hand of the typer. In the initial 

stages of assisted typing, the CRP heavily guides the hand of the typer to the desired letters. The typer’s 

contribution is minimal to null. This is much like the algorithm, rather than the brain signals, moving the cursor 29 

or the robotic arm, in the initial stages of learning, to calibrate the feedback control loop. In due time, the CRP 

relinquishes a bit of this control over the typer’s hand and lets the typer learn from the errors. Likewise, the 

BMI/BCI algorithm lets the brain signals do some of the control of the cursor or the robotic arm, and the brain learns from its own errors. Eventually, one has 50-50 control of the algorithm and brain signal (a flip of a coin) controlling the cursor or the robotic arm is random; then 60-40 then 70-30, etc. Eventually, the brain signals gain 100% control of the external device and learn to control it with full agency. Likewise, CRPs can let go 100% of their control and hand it over to the typer who acquires full agency, even when the letter board may still be held by the CRP or the hand may still need support. Passive support where the CRP has low agency and the typer controls the flow is not only possible (as we saw in this example), but also most importantly, it is quantifiable in multiple reliable ways that do not depend on the biased opinion or agenda of an observer. We have a new path forward for typers who inevitably need haptic support. 

4.3 Stochastic Equivalence of Physical Performance 

The stochastic changes derived from the bodily movements were appropriately measured by the FR-distance metric on the curved Γ parameters manifold and, in the 3D, physical space. There is congruence between these changes according to the distance metrics on the abstract Γ manifold and the physical spaces. In both spaces there is a tendency to increase the distance between the dominant and non-dominant hands with cognitive load and to decrease it with instruction. Yet, under instruction and higher cognitive-memory-emotional loads, the distances tend to increase again. These changes, which we also anchored to the non-dominant hand demonstrate a new and non-obvious way to quantify cognitive load using the motoric signatures in ways that cannot be intentionally controlled by an outsider. In this sense, they offer a new way to measure authorship of our cognitive thoughts, during spontaneous elaboration and under prompted instruction. 

4.4 Utility and Scalability: Head Motions as Proxy of the Body 

The utility of this finding is linked to the head movements. Head motions alone capture these differences in cognitive load and intent. As such, they provide a proxy of their motoric signatures across the body capturable with simple unobtrusive means, such as video. Because of the disparity in levels of mental awareness quantified between the dominant and non-dominant sides of the body, and because of the fidelity of trends in physical changes and trends in Γ stochastic changes, we propose that both the stochastic and physical signatures of cognitive load presented in this paper as indexes of degree of personalized authorship can be transferred and scaled to other cases. 

The suite of assays that we offer can be adapted to individuals who do not talk, such that spontaneous gestures and any spontaneous vocalization capturable through video and mics can be used to probe the system in deliberate instructed mode. These finer motor vocalization signals are nevertheless motorically quantifiable. They may give us a window into other issues with smooth and striated muscles across systems. By contrasting spontaneity and deliberateness in our gestures, voice and facial expressions, we can separate motoric signatures of cognitive loads from motoric signatures of mere biomechanical performance. This opens a new way to quantify in the non-dominant end-effector of the person, the individualized index of communication’s authorship. No one other than the typer’s brain itself could reproduce such geodesics on the Γ manifold in correspondence with those of his movements in 3D physical space. The dominant hand then adds a unique, 

personalized, unambiguous signature of ownership of the person’s spontaneous creative thoughts preluding the typing action in ways unique to the person. They are a large departure from what precedes the typing of the instructed, prompted phrases -even of those instructed with varying degrees of cognitive and memory effort. 

4.5 Space-Time Equivalence Through Geodesics 

One of the most compelling results of our analyses was the equivalence of temporal and spatial features which manifested both in the geodesics of the Γ manifold and in the arclength and time delays of the hand’s trajectories. 

As we obtained the motion trajectories across conditions within a fixed time length ( Figure 15 A-E-I ) we extract the amount of space traveled and the latencies of each case. They match those of the Γ FR-geodesics across conditions ( Figure 13, 15 and Supplementary Figure 15 ). Their differences provide a metric encompassing 30

both the variations in physical space corresponding to the variations in Γ space. The latter offering the ability to capture more general variations of micro-peaks in other biorhythms co-registered from the nervous systems (Supplementary Figure 4 ). In this sense, this outcome is what enables us to generalize these results to other end effectors and signals, such that we can use other end effectors (tongue, eyes, face, head) to acquire the spatial and temporal signatures distinguishing across cognitive effort, biomechanical effort and creative thinking preambles to the onset of typing. 

4.6 Caveats, Path to Generalization and Conclusions 

This is a case study of an advanced typer with emerging spoken language. It serves as our upper bound to track and verify performance in general. We need to scale the study and sample broadly across different skill levels of communicating through typing or pointing in the absence of spoken language. Our methods offer a way to profile the somatic-sensory-motor systems to determine where to start based on regulation level requirements (Supplementary Figure 4 ). Today we can do this unobtrusively and at scale with our Lab in the Box approach [14]. 

Although there are idiosyncratic aspects of MH performance that pertain to his personal style of typing, self-regulating, learning trajectory, and acquired skills, the assays and biometrics that we present are universal. They can be adapted to assess learning in a novice typer, who cannot talk and is learning to type with assistance. They can also evaluate more advanced, independent typers who may not need as much support but cannot talk either. They can be adapted to individuals who communicate through echolalia or self-talks/vocalization, using the same basic assay and biometrics. We acknowledge that this work nonetheless must be carried out to both reveal broad ranges across the population of non-speakers and to help accelerate their learning process. But we also provide the path forward, moving away from behaviorism into a humane approach that provides agency, while helping the person self-regulate and be comfortable. 

Sensory-motor variability and behavioral performance during communication are as heterogeneous as the ns-ASD population and as any other non-speaking group (stroke, CP, etc.) These differences are nonetheless quantifiable with the methods introduced here. These methods remain agnostic to the messaging or authorship claims. They can objectively reveal through precise spatiotemporal biometrics who leads with agency in a dyadic setting and because of the stochastic and geometric rules simplifying computational complexity through important parameter identification, they can be embedded in cell phones and broadly scaled. We underline the need to extend this study to other groups and offer the metrics to safely differentiate levels of cognitive effort during instructed typing from motions that are preamble to typing creative thoughts.
