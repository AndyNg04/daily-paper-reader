# Co-Evolving Behavioral Agents and Detectors for Adaptive Information Operations 

## Lin Tian and Marian-Andrei Rizoiu 

Behavioral Data Science, University of Technology Sydney Sydney, Australia {Lin.Tian-3, Marian-Andrei.Rizoiu}@uts.edu.au 

## Abstract 

Detectors of state-sponsored information operations (IOs) are trained on campaigns that platforms have already taken down. They lose much of their accuracy on an actor they have not seen, even though no operator in the archived data is trying to avoid them. A real operator wants two things, to reach an audience and to avoid being caught. We present IO-AI, a framework that gives simulated agents these two capabilities, engagement and evasion, and tests what each one does to detection. The agents are built from the posting behavior of real campaigns and choose the emotion, type, and tim-ing of each post. An agent and a detector then train against each other in turn. We rank the trained agents by how hard they are to detect and train one detector on them from easiest to hardest. On 20 Twitter IO campaigns, this detector reduces missed detections of adaptive agents by 65% relative to a detector trained only on the archived campaigns, at a similar false-alarm rate. On actors it has never seen it reaches an 𝐹 1 of 0 .81, against 0 .62. We find that an agent trained to seek engagement becomes easier to catch, because it learns the fast, emotional retweeting that archived campaigns were caught producing. An agent that was trained for engagement before the game keeps its engagement lead after it learns to evade. We also introduce a behavioral distance metric that compares two groups of accounts using only their unlabeled posting histories. The larger the distance between two groups, the worse a detector trained on one does on the other (Spearman 𝜌 𝑠 = −0.58), so the metric can be used to choose which new groups to test first.

## 1 Introduction 

Following the 2016 U.S. election, Twitter (now X) identified 3,841 accounts affiliated with Russia’s Internet Research Agency (IRA) in a retrospective archive spanning multiple years [17] 1. These ac-counts adopted different roles, including political commentators and local news personas [24]. 2. In December 2021, Twitter disclosed a separate 16-account network linked to the IRA that attempted to introduce pro-Russia viewpoints into political discussion in the Cen-tral African Republic [53] 3. Such archives make systematic analysis possible, but each records behavior from a particular operation and period. How can we train detectors to recognize new information operations when their operators adapt to evade detection?

Information operation (IO) detectors are built and evaluated on archived campaigns, using the text of the posts, the way the posts spread, the behavior of the accounts behind them, or some combi-nation of these [1, 15, 33, 34, 37, 42, 54]. Figure 1 shows how much of what they learn carries from one state actor to another. Trained and tested on the same actor, detectors built on text, propagation, or their fusion reach a mean 𝐹 1 of 0 .84 to 0 .92; trained on one actor and tested on another, the same detectors fall to 0 .52 to 0 .55. The drop arises on archived data, with no operator responding to the detector, so most of what each detector learned was specific to the actor it was trained on. Few-shot transfer adapts a troll detector to a new campaign from a handful of labels [49], early detectors decide from a partial history [35], and adversarial studies train bots to evade a fixed detector [30] or manipulate the engagement a post receives to fool one [57]. In each of these settings one side stays fixed, whether the detector is updated once a campaign is exposed or the operator learns against a detector that never retrains. What pursuing engagement, evasion, or both does to an operator when the detector also learns, and what a detector gains from training on the result, remain open. Operators also want reach, which comes from the people who share a post [56] and from the unwitting par-ticipants a campaign recruits to amplify it [46]. Which strategies survive when operators pursue engagement as well as evasion and the detector learns in turn, and what must a detector train against to counter them? We introduce IO-AI, a framework for training sequential IO detectors against a population of adaptive behavioral agents (Fig-ure 2). Agents choose post emotion, posting type, and timing; a frozen text model renders each action into a post. This compact ac-tion space makes policies interpretable and supports cloning from the takedown archives. We cross three starting policies (cloned, engagement-pretrained, and static-detector-pretrained) with three objectives (evasion, predicted engagement, and both), alongside a fixed cloned control. In the adaptive games, agents and detectors train in turn, while the detector decides whether to wait, flag, or pass. Cross-play ranks agents across the learned detector popula-tion, giving an easy-to-hard curriculum for a single detector. Our contributions connect behavioral analysis to detector train-ing. First, the game family exposes how initialization and payoff shape adaptation, including the difference between engagement pre-training and engagement optimization during play. Second, cross-play supplies an empirical difficulty ordering whose contribution we assess against static training, single-game training, and alter-native population schedules. ## 2 Related Work 

Information operation and bot detection. Prior work on social bot and information operation detection has built strong super-vised models from text, account metadata, network structure, and temporal behavior [16, 54]. These systems are useful when future campaigns look like past campaigns, but they are trained against fixed data distributions. We extend behavior-based IO detection to adaptive opponents by training sequential detectors against learned adversaries and evaluating the transfer across actors and eras. 

Adversarial detection and games. Adversarial machine learning studies how classifiers fail when an attacker chooses the inputs [8, 20]; GANs [19] and PGD-robust training [36] are the standard two-player learning formulations. Security games make the interaction explicit by casting attacker and defender as players in a Markov or Stackelberg game [32, 45]. We adapt this game-theoretic per-spective to the sequential behavior of IO agents: the agent controls post emotion, posting type, and timing, while the detector decides whether to wait, flag, or pass. 

Empirical game theory and game-space structure. Empirical game-theoretic analysis (EGTA) studies strategic interactions using pay-offs estimated from simulated policy matchups [51, 58, 59]. Related population-based approaches include policy-space response oracles (PSRO) and league-based self-play [29, 55]. Open-ended-learning work has analyzed the strategic geometry of single games [4, 11], while Gatchel and Wiedenbeck [18] learn payoff models across parameterized game families. We take the game theory to IO detec-tion. Instead of one game, we solve ten partially observable games between an IO agent and a sequential detector, one for each choice of starting policy and objective, and then play the solved agents against every solved detector. The resulting cross-play ordering is what our detector curriculum trains over. 

Curriculum learning. Curriculum learning orders training from easy cases to hard ones [6], and league-based self-play produces diverse adaptive opponents [55]. We build on these ideas by using cross-play to measure the difficulty of co-evolved IO strategies and construct a curriculum for training a single sequential detector. 

## 3 Methodology 

We train detectors to recognize IO accounts as their behavior adapts to detection. IO-AI first co-evolves agents and detectors across ten games, then uses the resulting agents to construct a detector curriculum ordered by cross-play miss rate (Figure 2).

Learning rule. Both players are trained with PPO [44] on an alternating schedule, as in adversarial reinforcement learning [39]: each iteration collects an IO batch, updates the agent for 𝑛 ep epochs with the detector fixed, then updates the detector on the same or a stored batch plus control episodes with the agent fixed. In BC–Real only the detector learns. Each player receives the same number of co-evolution updates per run, the detector’s 𝑁 𝑤 warm-up updates aside, so each update is a partial best response and neither gains a budget advantage (More details in Appendix B.2).

## 4.3 Detector Performance Across Tests 

A deployed detector meets operations it did not train on. Even content features that separate an archived campaign from organic activity are “quite dynamic”, transfer poorly between campaigns of one actor, and cannot be expected to catch “activity that is not yet known” [1]. Table 2 reports each detector where the actor, the period or the strategy changes, alongside its in-domain score. In-domain, all baselines sit within 9 pp of one another (0 .83–0 .92). Under shift the spread opens to 35 pp on an unseen actor’s later pe-riod and 39 pp on Adaptive-SD, and in-domain rank is a weak guide to it: text-only BERT beats the propagation-path RNN in-domain (0 .88 versus 0 .86) and scores below it in every held-out condition. The per-account detectors transfer worst. BERT falls from 0 .88 ±0.01 in-domain to 0 .52 ±0.05 on an unseen actor and 0 .44 ±0.06 on that actor’s later period, and to 0 .41 ±0.06 and 0 .26 ±0.05 against the BC and SD agents, within 2 pp of chance or below it in four of the six held-out conditions; it keeps a margin over chance only on the known actor’s later period (0 .66 ±0.04) and against EM agents (0 .59 ±0.04). The account-feature RF is lower still on each of the four (0 .44, 0 .37, 0 .31, 0 .21). The propagation RNN carries across time better than across actors: it keeps 0 .69 ±0.04 on the known actor’s later period, 17 pp below in-domain, and 0 .57 ±0.05 on an unseen actor, and it falls to 0 .43 ±0.05 and 0 .32 ±0.05 against the BC and SD agents. MetaTroll’s campaign adapters give the best cross-actor transfer among the four (0 .65 ±0.04 on an unseen actor) and reach 0.51 ±0.05 and 0 .39 ±0.05 against the same two agents. The fused co-ordination graph and IOHunter hold 0 .70 ±0.04 and 0 .79 ±0.03 on an unseen actor and 0 .63 ±0.05 and 0 .72 ±0.04 on its later period, 5–16 pp above the best per-account detector. Interestingly, Adaptive-EM is the easiest of the three adaptive conditions for all six published detectors and Adaptive-SD the hardest for five of them. IO-AI has the highest mean 𝐹 1 in all six shifted or adaptive conditions, and its in-domain score is 0 .91 against 0 .92 for IOHunter. Its margin also holds against fresh adaptation: when a new Evade agent is trained against each frozen detector for 100 PPO iterations, full IO-AI keeps the lowest final miss rate for every initialization, 0.18 to 0 .23 (Appendix C.2).

Goal-systems theory says when that is possible: goals conflict when they compete for the same means [28], and serving several goals at once narrows the means that remain acceptable [27]. 

## 4.5 How Well Do Detectors Transfer? 

Section 4.3 showed how far the published detectors fall under shift. Here we ask the same of our own: how much of IO-AI’s accuracy survives a new actor or a later period, and where it comes from. ## 6 Conclusion 

IO-AI trains sequential IO detectors against a population of adap-tive behavioral agents ordered by cross-play difficulty. It broadens coverage beyond static and single-game training, with an advantage that persists under fresh re-attacks within budget. The games show why both initialization and objective matter when constructing the opponent population. Behavioral distance complements this process by identifying cohort differences associated with weaker transfer. Together, the findings support using adaptive opponents to test and improve detector coverage for information operations. ## A Data and Evaluation Details A.1 Campaign Data and Controls 

Table 1 lists the 20 campaign releases and which of them are used for training or held out. Behavioral cloning, engagement-model fitting and detector training never see the eight held-out campaigns. In-domain evaluation uses users and trajectories from the training campaigns that no training stage saw. Controls are matched by topic and activity volume and follow the same campaign and time splits. The Adaptive-BC, Adaptive-EM and Adaptive-SD columns of Table 2 use new rollouts from the frozen late-window agents, so they test how well a detector covers the opponents the curriculum trained on. Table 5 shows the four complete archive files used in the archival analyses. Their counts refer to these files rather than to the cam-paign releases in Table 1, and the comparisons drawn from them are limited to the operations the takedown archives captured. 

## A.2 Engagement in the Archives 

Table 5 also describes the GRU file. There, 87 .3% of tweets come from Twitter’s web client or web app and 14 .0% are retweets. Activity concentrates in 2016–2020, with 15 earlier tweets. The five most-liked English tweets are all originals, and the most-liked (157 likes, November 2019) reports from southern Idlib. 

## A.3 Per-Operation Recall Protocol 

The static detector trained on 12 campaigns and the full IO-AI detec-tor score every account in each complete archive. Each detector’s threshold is set to a 10% false-positive rate on held-out benign controls, and Table 6 reports account-level recall averaged over five detector seeds. This fixed operating point is the counterpart of the balanced-test-set macro 𝐹 1 in Table 2. The within-archive gap between the two detectors is the comparison of interest; recall levels differ across archives for other reasons as well.
