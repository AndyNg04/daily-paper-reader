## Abstract 

How much interaction experience is needed to distill a ca-pable visual agent? We study learning from few tasks and recorded interaction histories. Our framework se-lects representative trajectories using skill–context cover-age and ordered visual-decision similarity. On-policy dis-tillation (OPD) learns through fresh interaction on selected tasks or fresh student responses at fixed histories. Across Navigation, Sokoban, and spatial mapping, even one on-line training task supports transfer to other tasks. Four fixed trajectories raise a 4B student’s success from 49.17% to 82.08% on standard Navigation and from 60.16% to 85.55% on Sokoban, recovering over 90% of the measured gap to a 27B teacher on these panels. On an independent Navigation panel, replay improves success from 45.83% to 76.67%, retaining 91.36% of the same-task online gain without new interaction during optimization. Training repe-titions, matched supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and a 9B ex-tension further support the value of compact experience. 

## 1. Introduction 

Visual agents interpret images and feedback to complete multi-step tasks [27, 34]. Distillation transfers a strong teacher’s behavior to a smaller agent [1, 19]. We ask: how much distinct interaction experience is needed for effective transfer beyond the training experiences? 

Few-example learning has yielded strong gains in language-model alignment and reasoning [4, 28, 36]. Vi-sual agents introduce a sequential challenge: a changed ac-tion alters subsequent observations [18]. Must distillation span many different tasks? Can a few recorded histories also support transfer? We study few-task online training alongside fixed-history replay. Both generate fresh student responses, but only online training executes them for new observations. Our framework selects experience from a training-only pool of student trajectories. An annotation model builds a skill–context inventory and describes visual decisions from screenshots, observations, and preceding actions. Acoverage-first selector combines these annotations with or-1arXiv:2611.28618v1 [cs.AI] 21 Nov 2026 dered decision similarity to choose K complete trajecto-ries. Annotations never enter student or distillation-teacher prompts. Selected tasks support online OPD; selected causal histories support prefix replay [11] and matched teacher-response SFT. Across three environments, even one online task sup-ports transfer. Four fixed trajectories recover over 90% of a 4B student’s measured teacher gap on the original stan-dard Navigation and Sokoban panels. Independent Naviga-tion evaluation retains 91.36% of the same-task online gain. Training repetitions, matched SFT, and a 9B extension fur-ther support the value of selected histories across runs, ob-jectives, and student scales. Our contributions are: (1) evidence of visual-agent trans-fer from few tasks and fixed histories through online and replay studies; (2) representative selection combining skill– context coverage with ordered visual decisions while pre-serving original causal inputs; and (3) evaluation across three environments and two student scales, covering data requirements, measured teacher-gap recovery, and behav-ioral transfer, with selection controls, matched SFT, full-pool, and online comparisons. 

## 2. Related Work 

Our study connects on-policy distillation [1, 5], pre-fix replay [11], and few-example adaptation [4, 28, 36]. We investigate whether few representative experiences support visual-agent transfer under the state-distribution shift induced by the learner’s actions [18]. We study representative-subset selection [15, 22, 31] at the level of complete trajectories, combining skill–context coverage with ordered visual decision similarity. Skills and experi-ence have also supported retrieval and planning [26, 35]; here, annotations select histories rather than augment policy prompts. Detailed comparisons with these methods, world alignment, and visual-agent training appear in the supple-mentary material.

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Models and optimization. We adapt Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B [17] with a frozen Qwen3.5-27B teacher, na-tive thinking, and Slime 0.4 [23]. Separate API model 

gpt-5.6-terra describes and reviews visual decisions; annotation stages are detailed in the supplement. Training uses 100 updates, batch 64, learning rate 10 −6, and fresh Adam [8]. We evaluate update 100, allow 32,768 response tokens, and preserve complete prefixes. Environment re-wards do not enter training. 

Data and comparisons. The pools contain 120 Naviga-tion, 128 Sokoban, and 32 SpatialGym initial-4B trajec-tories (two per training room). K4 uses 3.3%, 3.1%, and 12.5% of these histories. The 4B study compares subsets, full-pool OPD/SFT, and online OPD on 1, 4, 16, or all training tasks. The 9B extension reuses K4 histories for OPD/SFT and adds full-task online OPD. All learn 6,400 responses at the same update budget; tokens and compute differ. 

Evaluation. We evaluate each task four times: 60 stan-dard Navigation tasks and their long-horizon variants with reversed initial headings, 64 one-box Sokoban maps, and 16 training/four held-out single rooms plus 20 two-room SpatialGym scenes. Metrics are mean success for Naviga-tion/Sokoban and continuous cogmap_score for Spatial-Gym. Table 1 reports held-out and two-room SpatialGym; full environment and split definitions appear in the supple-ment. Models share evaluation tasks, repeat seeds, prompts, and budgets; scores average all four repeats. Original panels were inspected during development, including four moni-tored held-out rooms. Additional Navigation results include training repetitions and an independent panel.

## 5. Discussion 

Results across OPD, SFT, and student scales suggest that compact decision experience transfers beyond training tasks. Coverage and repeated supervision may help explain this reuse. 

Scope and limitations. Small data counts distinct tasks or histories; collection and annotation remain necessary. Core results use single training runs and development-inspected panels; additional Navigation repetitions and independent evaluation broaden the evidence.

## A. Extended Related Work 

Distillation and sequential distribution shift. Knowl-edge distillation transfers teacher predictions [6], and policy distillation transfers visual control policies [19]. Sequence-level KD learns teacher-generated targets [7]; GKD [1], MiniLLM [5], and DistiLLM [9] study student-generated sequences and teacher feedback. DAgger [18] highlights action-induced state distribution shift. ReOPD [11] re-plays teacher histories with step-decaying prefix sampling. WAM-OPD [13] distills robotic action models through prefix-weighted replay of fixed trajectories. We select initial-student visual histories for uniform trajectory/turn re-play and compare them with few-task online interaction, examining task breadth and history refresh at matched up-date/response budgets. 

Adaptation with very little data. LIMA studies small-data supervised alignment [36]; one-example RLVR [28] and OPD [4] show few-query reasoning gains. AdaptA-gent [24] adapts multimodal web agents from a few human demonstrations through in-context learning or meta-learned parameter adaptation. We study teacher-supervised visual-agent transfer from selected histories and restricted task cat-alogues, with OPD, matched SFT, and full-pool compar-isons. Few tasks or histories still require repeated response generation; replay also requires initial collection. 

Representative data selection. Core-set selection covers visual feature space [22], while CRAIG selects weighted subsets to approximate training gradients [15]. For lan-guage models, DSIR matches target distributions [32], DEITA combines complexity, quality, and diversity [14], and LESS uses gradient influence for targeted tuning [31]. Submodular summarization similarly balances coverage and diversity [12]. We select whole trajectories using skill– context opportunities and ordered decision-description sim-ilarity. Our lexicographic rule does not directly inherit clas-sical cardinality-constrained greedy guarantees [16]. 

Skills, world alignment, and visual agents. Voyager stores executable skills [26], ExpeL retrieves experiential insights [35], and WALL-EVE learns symbolic rules from trajectory–prediction conflicts to align a world model for planning without policy training [37]. EMMA transfers reflection-improved actions from a parallel text-world LLM to a visual agent through DAgger-DPO [33]. GTR coun-ters thought collapse during VLM RL through automated reasoning correction [30]; RAGEN [29] and GiGPO [3] study multi-turn agent RL, while VAGEN focuses on vi-sual world-model reasoning [27]. Our annotations instead select fixed histories for policy adaptation; neither learner nor distillation teacher receives them as prompts. We reuse VAGEN interfaces for AI2-THOR Navigation [10], Sokoban, and Theory-of-Space mapping [34], training with teacher feedback rather than environment rewards.

## F. Teacher and Online Gap Recovery 

For a score s, initial student s0, and matched teacher sT ,teacher-gap recovery is (s − s0)/(sT − s0). The online-gain ratio instead divides the K4 OPD gain by the online OPD gain. Neither ratio treats measured teacher/online per-formance as an upper bound, and ratios above one are possi-ble. Scores with small denominators require particular care.

## H. Data and Cost Accounting 

The complete candidate pools contain 120 Navigation, 128 Sokoban, and 32 SpatialGym trajectories. Selected budgets refer to complete trajectories, not decision prefixes or gra-dient steps. Loss reduction and update convention. The main-method losses describe offline adaptation. All retained current-response tokens, including native reasoning and budget-truncated responses, have unit loss mask; historical prompt tokens are excluded. The implementation averages token losses within each response, then responses within the global batch, using gradient accumulation for the sin-gle optimizer update. The OPD signal is the teacher log probability minus the pre-update student log probability, detached and unnormalized. The latter is evaluated by the training backend at the same frozen student weights; infer-ence/training numerical differences are audited separately. The native policy objective uses clipping bounds [0 .8, 1.2] ,no dual clip, and no offline importance correction. Its lo-cal gradient at the pre-update policy is the token log-ratio distillation signal; it is not presented as an exact estimator of the full autoregressive sequence-KL gradient. There is no additional reward, reference-policy KL, or entropy term. Matched SFT uses the same response-mean reduction. Ex-isting teacher labels are reused only when input and proto-col identities match; remaining labels are generated under the frozen-teacher protocol.

Visual annotation records. Decision descriptions con-tain goals, state facts, decisions, history, changes, uncer-tainty, and evidence references. Asynchronous online references. Sixteen persistent en-vironments supply 64 responses per update. ## J. Recorded Selection Cases and Pseudocode 

The main-paper Navigation case and the Sokoban example below use the frozen training-pool selections, not newly cu-rated demonstrations. We reconstructed each greedy rank-ing from its saved similarity matrix and verified the selected episode/turn identities against the corresponding formal K4 training pool. At each round the runner-up is the next eligi-ble candidate under the same lexicographic rule, including the one-per-task constraint. This is an illustration of the ranking rule, not an indepen-dent estimate of the performance effect of that particular choice. Visual decision captions are shortened for readability; complete fields and image identities accompany the figures. The frame rule is fixed to first/last pre-response observa-tions, plus the explicitly numbered causal example. Ac-tions, outcomes, and images are not corrected. Full se-quence matching uses all decision descriptions, not only displayed frames. The labels are selection metadata; adap-tation consumes original visual prefixes. The procedure retains raw historical reasoning, ex-cludes current and future responses from the prefix, and per-forms no environment interaction during offline adaptation.

It does not establish that the as-sumptions hold here, bound success, or justify any observed performance difference. Three gaps prevent treating this as a guarantee for our se-lector. First, ordered TF-IDF similarity is not established to induce the required metric or a smooth distillation loss. Sec-ond, facility-location representative weights generally dif-fer from the actual uniform-trajectory/turn sampler; small nearest-neighbor distances alone do not bound the transport cost for these fixed marginals.
