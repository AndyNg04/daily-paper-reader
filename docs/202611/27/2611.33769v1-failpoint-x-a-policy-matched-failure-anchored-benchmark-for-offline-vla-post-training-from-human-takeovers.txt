## Abstract 

Human takeovers offer corrective supervision for vision-language-action (VLA) policies, but their contribution to offline post-training must be separated from that of contin-ued optimization. We introduce FailPoint-X, a benchmark for offline VLA post-training with human takeovers. To represent uncertainty in failure onset, an annotator marks an interval for each failure, and training takeovers start at seeded frames sampled within it. From one fixed π0.5

checkpoint, which also initializes post-training, we assem-ble 629 natural failures across 10 LIBERO-X task families at up to five difficulty levels. All 2,823 takeover attempts are logged; 2,400 are accepted recoveries, of which 2,320 are used for post-training. Evaluation restarts every method from the fixed midpoints of 384 held-out failure intervals. Under matched actor-update counts, we compare behavior cloning of recoveries, value-based offline learning on recov-eries and failed rollouts, and continued training on existing demonstrations. Within 1,200 steps on the 240 L1–L3 held-out failures, cloning improves recovery by 29.4 percentage points (pp) over the demonstration-only control; the value-based learner does not establish a further gain (1.5 pp). In exploratory evaluation from L1–L5 nominal starts at the same horizon, cloning loses 30.0 pp of success relative to the base policy; mixing in demonstrations reduces the loss to 16.5 pp, a reduction that shrinks under a stricter, pre-specified horizon. Restorable failure states and a shared evaluator let all methods be compared from identical starts. 

1arXiv:2611.33769v1 [cs.RO] 25 Nov 2026 1. Introduction 

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies map visual obser-vations and language instructions to robot control actions. Embodied agents more broadly must decide and act reliably under partial observation, from judging whether a target is present to searching unseen environments for it [3, 11, 15]. Reliable manipulation, however, requires handling states in-duced by the robot’s own errors, including missed grasps, misplaced objects, and interrupted task progress. At such states, a human takeover can supply corrective actions that restore progress toward the original goal. Offline post-training seeks to make such recovery autonomous, learn-ing from human corrections or from the robot’s own mixed-quality deployment experience [26]; we study it on hu-man recoveries recorded at a fixed policy’s own failures. Training uses failed policy executions and human takeovers from their intermediate states; the resulting policy must au-tonomously complete the original task from held-out failure states of the same task configurations. The contribution of takeover data is difficult to isolate from overall task success alone. From a nominal start, a higher success rate may reflect either avoiding an error or recovering from one. Moreover, post-training adds opti-mization steps, so comparing a post-trained policy directly with the base policy conflates the effect of new data with that of continued optimization. LIBERO-RECOVER [18] reports recovery gains from fine-tuning on recoveries mixed with demonstrations, without a demonstration-only control matched in training recipe and optimizer steps. RePO-VLA [19] includes a matched-budget ablation of value conditioning on the real-robot Fold Towel task and evalu-ates recovery under injected perturbations, and SimPT [30] compares post-training methods at matched policy-update counts on held-out environment seeds; neither reports re-covery from the base policy’s own held-out failures against a matched demonstration-only control. We therefore ask two questions about using recovery data for offline post-training. Q1: At matched actor updates, does training on human recoveries improve failure recovery more than con-tinued training on existing demonstrations? Beyond the successful corrections, the preceding failed executions pro-vide an additional negative training signal. Q2: Does value-based post-training on both recoveries and failed rollouts improve upon behavior cloning of recoveries alone? Collecting recovery data also requires deciding when to intervene. Selecting a single frame, whether by a model or a human, commits data collection to a single temporal judg-ment, yet perceived event boundaries depend on physical changes, interpreted goals, and temporal granularity [14]. We therefore let an annotator bracket the failure onset with an interval [a, b ] and start training takeovers from seeded frames sampled within it. We introduce FailPoint-X, a policy-matched benchmark for offline VLA post-training with human takeovers: one checkpoint both generates the failures and initializes post-training (Fig. 1, Table 1). Because simulation can re-store exactly the same recorded physical state across poli-cies, the benchmark’s failure-anchored protocol (Sec. 4) restarts every evaluated policy (arm) from the interval midpoints of the same 384 held-out failures and, sepa-rately, from nominal starts to measure retained task per-formance. The unchanged base policy (Base-restart) pro-vides a reference. Demo-continue continues training on existing demonstrations for as many actor updates as the takeover-trained arms: Takeover-BC clones accepted recov-eries, IQL-R2 adds failed training rollouts and a learned critic, and Mix-BC mixes recoveries with demonstrations. Q1 and Q2, paired over task families, are confirmatory on the 240 held-out L1–L3 failures of all 10 families; Mix-BC, the 144 L4–L5 failures, and nominal starts are exploratory. Our collection pipeline (Sec. 3, Fig. 2) supplies recov-ery supervision from restorable failure states and logs its human cost. From rollouts of a fixed π0.5 [10] checkpoint, we assemble 629 natural failures across 10 LIBERO-X [31] task families at up to five difficulty levels, split by seed into training and held-out sets. Takeovers are protocol-timed: the protocol, not the operator, draws 10 seeded start frames per training failure, and unsuccessful attempts are retried at the same frame. We retain all 2,823 attempts, includ-ing 2,400 accepted recoveries, of which 2,320 are used for post-training. Within a 1,200-step horizon on L1–L3, Takeover-BC improves recovery by 29.4 percentage points (pp) over Demo-continue, whereas IQL-R2 does not establish a fur-ther gain over Takeover-BC (1.5 pp; Fig. 3a). In exploratory evaluations from L1–L5 nominal starts at the same horizon, Takeover-BC loses 30.0 pp of nominal success relative to the base policy; mixing in demonstrations reduces this loss to 16.5 pp but lowers recovery (Fig. 3b). This smaller loss depends on the evaluation horizon: under the stricter, pre-specified 1.1T horizon ( T : mean demonstration length), Takeover-BC and Mix-BC change nominal success by sim-ilar amounts ( −33.2 and −30.9 pp; Sec. 5). Thus, takeover data improve recovery in this study, while nominal-task evaluation reveals an accompanying retention loss. Our contributions are: 1. FailPoint-X, a policy-matched benchmark for offline VLA post-training with human takeovers , combin-ing restorable failure states from one base checkpoint (reusable across policies, policy-matched when initial-ized from it), fixed splits, matched actor updates, and joint recovery and nominal-task evaluation (Secs. 3 and 4). 2. Interval-based takeover collection and a failure-and-recovery dataset with human-annotated failure inter-vals, seeded takeover starts within each training inter-2val, and complete attempt logs of human collection cost (Sec. 3). 3. A controlled empirical study of recovery benefit and nominal-task loss for a fixed checkpoint , with pre-specified L1–L3 comparisons of behavior cloning against continued demonstration training (Q1) and value-based learning (Q2), and exploratory analyses of Mix-BC, L4–L5, nominal starts, task families, and addi-tional training and inference variants (Sec. 5). 

## 2. Related Work 

Learning from human interventions. Human inter-ventions supervise the states a learner actually visits, which independently collected demonstrations may cover poorly. DAgger aggregates expert labels at learner-visited states [27], and HG-DAgger lets the human decide when to intervene and return autonomy, yielding contiguous cor-rective segments [12]. Sirius weights original demon-strations, autonomous rollouts, human interventions, and pre-intervention segments within an iterative deployment-and-training loop [17]. Extending human corrections to VLA reinforcement learning, ConRFT combines offline initialization on demonstrations with online intervention-guided learning [4]. FailPoint-X instead fixes one round of protocol-timed human recoveries from a single base pol-icy’s failures and compares ways to use them for offline post-training. 

Offline learning from corrections and failures. While corrective trajectories supply direct action targets for imi-tation, autonomous rollouts additionally carry information about action outcomes. Prior work learns from such data with both imitation and value-based objectives: Sirius eval-uates behavior cloning (BC) and Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) on deployment logs [17], and RECAP integrates demonstra-tions, autonomous experience, and human corrections via value estimation and advantage-conditioned policy train-ing [9]. Focusing on failure recovery, RePO-VLA imi-tates corrective suffixes and adds progress-aligned value su-pervision to learn from successful, failed, and corrective trajectories; it includes a non-value control on the same data and budget and an offline RECAP baseline matched in training steps and recovery-data budget [19]. Similarly, SimPT benchmarks HG-DAgger, RECAP, and preference-based objectives on fixed datasets of policy rollouts and simulated expert corrections generated from per-task π0.5

parent policies, matching policy-training steps and evaluat-ing from held-out initial states [30]. We compare such objectives under controlled data and evaluation conditions. Our value-based arm adapts the advantage weighting of IQL [13] to the VLA’s flow-matching objective; contrasting it with recovery-only be-havior cloning evaluates the coupled choice of data compo-sition and learning objective. 

Benchmarks for failure recovery. Evaluating failure re-covery requires specifying the intermediate state from which a policy must resume to complete the original task. FailPoint-X builds on the task configurations, demonstra-tions, and difficulty levels of LIBERO-X [31]. Among re-cent benchmarks, LIBERO-RECOVER constructs scenar-ios from naturally occurring failures across multiple poli-cies, evaluates recovery after restoring and locally perturb-ing recorded states, and collects human recovery demon-strations; it also reports supervised fine-tuning on mix-tures of recoveries and original demonstrations, including nominal-start performance [18]. RoboRecover replays ac-tion prefixes to reconstruct deviation states from which di-verse policies resume, and studies state reversion with short learned corrections [16]. FailPoint-X compares post-training choices for a single fixed checkpoint: the failure-generating policy and the post-training initialization are identical, all methods resume from the same held-out failure states, a demonstration-only con-trol matches the number of actor updates, and nominal starts measure task retention (Table 1). 

Localizing failures and selecting takeover states. Re-covery starting states can be selected based on task progress, estimated recoverability, or human judgment regarding when a failure began. SimPT tracks progress with a task-stage graph on privileged simulator state and at-tempts planner-based recovery from saved snapshots [30]. Kintsugi-VLA estimates a privileged expert’s recovery probability along failed trajectories, selecting states within a window bounded by deviation onset and an expert-relative recovery frontier [28]. LIBERO-RECOVER in-stead prompts a vision-language model to pinpoint the ear-liest task-relevant failure timestamp [18]. In FailPoint-X, the annotated onset interval expresses uncertainty about when the failure began, not an expert’s recoverability window, and seeded sampling within it de-couples onset annotation from the selected takeover starts.

3.2. Natural Failures and Restorable States 

To obtain states arising from the policy’s own behavior, we execute the fixed checkpoint without human intervention, with rollouts assigned to separate training and held-out seed blocks before collection. Rollouts that have not completed the task by 1.5T count as failures, where T is the mean demonstration length for the family’s L1 task, counted in 20 Hz control steps and shared across its five levels; fail-ure is thus defined relative to this rollout cutoff. Screen-ing then excludes invalid initial states, physics faults, and near-misses, leaving 1,805 candidates (Fig. 2b). Candidates are sent to annotation largely in seed order, and the per-configuration target is five training and eight held-out fail-ures, so the curated set is a screened, quota-based subset (rules and exceptions in the supplement). We retain observations, action histories, simulator states, and fixture layouts. Restoration re-applies the recorded layout and simulator state and reconstructs the gripper-controller state from preceding actions, so human takeovers on training failures and all evaluated policies on held-out failures start from exactly the recorded states. 

3.3. Interval-Based Human Takeover Collection 

Failure onset can be ambiguous even when its consequence is clear. To retain this uncertainty, an annotator marks an in-terval [a, b ], targeting 10–40 frames, around a selected main failure event: a is the earliest frame at which the event may have begun, and by frame b it has certainly occurred. Acoordinator releases or withholds annotated candidates and finalizes the interval and failure-type label of each released failure. Held-out evaluation uses these finalized intervals. One person performed all annotation and teleoperation, so inter-annotator agreement is not estimated. For each released training failure selected for collection, the protocol samples 10 frames uniformly without replace-ment from the finalized [a, b ] with a recorded random seed; all training intervals contain at least 10 frames. The opera-tor then starts from the restored state at each sampled frame and supplies corrective actions with a SpaceMouse at 20 Hz, within 1,800 control steps per attempt. Collection targets one accepted recovery per sampled start: unsuccessful attempts and operator-discarded suc-cesses are retried at the same frame, and acceptance requires a submitted, successful trajectory that passes automatic in-tegrity checks. 3.4. Dataset Composition and Collection Cost 

The dataset (Table 2) contains 629 released failures: 245 on the training side and 384 held out. Collection is com-plete for 240 training failures in 48 configurations (2,400 accepted recoveries). Five released training failures lack a complete takeover set and are not used: 3 sources archived during collection (each replaced by the next released seed) and the 2 family-9 L4 failures, whose collection stopped after 2 attempts at one start reached the time limit (fam-ily 9 has no accepted L4–L5 recoveries). Held out are 240 L1–L3 failures in all 10 families and 144 L4–L5 fail-ures in nine. Seven failure types cover wrong placements, wrong objects, collisions, missed grasps, sustained lack of progress, dropped objects, and unclosed drawers. Eight complete training failures (the fifth in seed order in eight configurations of two families) form a develop-ment set for implementation checks, keeping each source’s takeovers together. Their 80 recoveries are excluded from post-training, which uses 232 source failures and 2,320 ac-cepted recoveries from all five levels. The full log contains 2,823 attempts, including those on archived sources; annotation took 5.4 hours and active takeover control 14.0 hours, and other collection time is not counted. ## 4. Controlled Post-Training and Evaluation 

We compare offline post-training methods initialized from the failure-generating checkpoint, using the training split and development exclusions in Sec. 3. 

4.1. Post-Training Methods and Controls 

Shared training and controls. Table 3 lists every arm with its data and results. Statistical inference. We bootstrap paired family differ-ences 10,000 times to obtain 95% bias-corrected and ac-celerated (BCa) intervals [5], with percentile intervals as a sensitivity check and fallback for degenerate BCa calcula-tions. These intervals are conditional on the fixed evaluated checkpoints and exclude training-seed variation. Q1 passes if its interval lies above zero and ∆1 ≥ 5 pp; Q2 passes if its interval excludes zero and |∆2| ≥ 5 pp. Under pre-specified fixed-sequence testing, Q2 is confirmatory only if Q1 passes. 

Exploratory analyses. ## 5. Experiments and Analysis 

All arms start from the checkpoint, splits, and recipes of Secs. 3–4, with one completed training run per trained arm; Base-restart was evaluated on an RTX 5090 and the other arms on H200 GPUs. Results use horizon H unless noted; differences are computed before rounding. 

5.1. Main Recovery Results 

Takeover data improve recovery beyond continued training (Q1). Takeover-BC raises L1–L3 recovery suc-cess to 51.9%, compared with 22.5% for Demo-continue and 22.2% for Base-restart. At matched actor updates, the Q1 difference is +29 .4 pp, with a 95% family-paired BCa interval of [+16.9, +44.7], so Q1 passes (Fig. 3a) across 600–1,800 steps (Fig. 3c); on the 154 points with 

0/3 Base-restart successes, Takeover-BC reaches 46.8% vs .3.7%. Q1 evaluates the collected dataset as a whole, without separating its corrective actions from its coverage of failure states. Demo-continue minus Base-restart is +0.3 pp [ −1.9, +2.6] across different GPUs. 

IQL-R2 does not establish a further gain (Q2). IQL-R2 attains 53.5% recovery success, a Q2 difference of +1 .5 pp over Takeover-BC with a 95% BCa interval of [ −2.2, +5.1]. This difference meets neither pre-specified criterion, so it does not establish an advantage over recovery-only cloning for this recipe, which changes both data composition and the learning objective. No exploratory variant improves on Takeover-BC with an interval excluding zero; Mix-BC is lower by 6.7 pp [3.3, 11.4] (Table 3). The supplement shows rollouts of Base-restart, Takeover-BC, and IQL-R2 from one restored held-out failure, selected after evaluation for a clear contrast.

5.3. Heterogeneity and Sensitivity 

Gains and losses are uneven across task families and failure types. Per-family Q1 differences are positive in 8 families, tied in 1, and negative in 1, ranging from −1.4 to +73.6 pp (Fig. 4). The nominal change of Takeover-BC is negative in every family but varies widely, and Takeover-BC has no nominal success at any level in 5 families (0, 5, 7, 9, 15). By failure type over all levels, Takeover-BC gains most over Base-restart and Demo-continue on col-lisions and dropped objects and falls below both only on unclosed drawers (supplement). Because failure types con-centrate in a few families, these patterns are descriptive and do not identify a cause. 

Decisions hold under horizon and family checks. Q1 passes at 600 and 1,800 steps (+26.7 and +30.7 pp) and Q2 at neither; dropping any family (+24.5 to +32.9 pp) or flagged width, late-success, and revised points (+31.4 pp on 223 points, supplement) keeps both decisions. On exploratory L4–L5 failures, the point-estimate order of IQL-R2 and Takeover-BC reverses (33.6% vs . 36.8%; Ta-ble 3, Fig. 3d); training includes L4–L5, measuring held-out recovery within observed configurations. 

The critic weighting fails its pre-specified check. The IQL-R2 critic’s mean sampling weights are 1.54 on R and 5.63 on Fwin , failing the check Mean (R) > Mean (Fwin ),yet R still supplies 94.7% of actor draws (776,507 vs .11,926 chunks; 98.6% in AWAC, which passes the check; supplement); this characterizes the weighting without iden-tifying Q2’s cause. Exploratory IQL-R0 (critic and actor on R alone) differs from Takeover-BC by −3.1 pp [ −7.6, +1.1], and IQL-R2 minus IQL-R0 is +4.6 pp [+1.0, +11.4], varying both critic experience and actor data. 86. Conclusion and Outlook 

FailPoint-X measures what human takeovers contribute to offline VLA post-training at restorable failure states. For the evaluated checkpoint, cloning the collected recoveries improves 1,200-step L1–L3 recovery success by 29.4 pp over continued demonstration training, whereas the tested IQL-R2 recipe does not establish a further gain. These re-covery gains come with substantial nominal-task losses, an exploratory result that holds under both nominal horizons and argues for evaluating recovery and retention together. This study is limited to one checkpoint, one operator, one training run per trained arm, and simulation. It does not test the effect of policy matching itself, takeover tim-ing, or gains over new demonstrations of equal human time (supplement). Next steps include restarts from several states along each held-out failure, factorial designs that separate data composition from the learning objective, and skill-preserving objectives. With restorable states and logged at-tempts, these studies need no new takeovers. New methods plug into the same recipe and policy interfaces.

A.1. Platform and base policy 

Simulator. LIBERO-X [31] (commit f528726 ) with its kitchen and study scenes, built on robosuite [32] (1.4.0) and MuJoCo [29] (2.3.0, EGL rendering, Python 3.10.16); con-trol runs at 20 Hz. 

Base policy. π0.5 [10], fully fine-tuned from the π0.5

73053111f932 ) for 30k steps (batch 256, action horizon 10; 4 ×H200 GPUs, 22.6 h). The 7-D actions are zero-padded to the model’s 32 action dimensions, and the EMA weights of the final step form the base checkpoint (SHA-256 6a6c6aa1da11176de1c0e16f662fe0f5 c24eda07dab80cd48ca1e8ac38397c11 ). 

Frames and control steps. A training chunk spans 10 con-secutive demonstration frames, and at evaluation all 10 pre-dicted actions are executed, one per control step, before the policy is queried again. One demonstration frame corre-sponds to one control step at 20 Hz, although LIBERO-X lists its demonstrations at 10 fps. Per unit of commanded translation, the end effector moves 0.0105 m per demonstra-tion frame (median over the 2,520 episodes) and 0.0116 m per control step (229 of our rollouts); a frame spanning two steps would roughly double the per-frame value. Success-ful L1 rollouts also take a median of 0.98 times the mean demonstration length in steps. A chunk therefore spans 0.5 s.

A.3. Rollout generation and failure screening 

This subsection gives the screening rules summarized in Sec. 3.2 of the main paper and the resulting counts. 

Seeds. Rollouts use pre-assigned seed blocks, starting at 2000 for training and at 4000 for held-out candidates. 

Failure. A rollout that has not succeeded by 1.5 T ; rollouts that succeed later (late successes) remain failures under this definition (Sec. C.5). 

Exclusions. Rollouts with an unstable start (the start fails the physics guard’s 2 s hold-still check) or with a physics fault during the episode (the guard fires) count as neither success nor failure. Failures are excluded as near-misses when they fail solely the 3 cm centering condition, and as invalid starts under the start-validity rule (object penetra-tion above 2 mm, or objects ejected at the start). The start-validity rule was not applied to families 0 and 9 before an-notation. 

Candidates for annotation. In each configuration, nomi-nally the first 10 training and 16 held-out candidates in seed order are sent to annotation; actual counts in Tabs. A2 and A3.

Label definitions. Each label has one fixed definition. 

Missed grasp : the gripper does not grasp the intended ob-ject. Dropped : a grasped object leaves the gripper and falls.

Interval guideline. The guideline reads: “Mark that the failure happens between these two frames: start a is the ear-liest frame at which you think the failure may already have begun; end b is the frame by which the failure has certainly happened, for example when the object leaves the gripper, is knocked over, or the gripper opens after a wrong placement. If unsure, widen the interval a little rather than forcing a single frame.” Takeovers use only the interval of the main event, which the annotator chooses and which need not be the first or last event; an interval longer than 2 s is recorded as it is, is not treated as a point-like failure and receives no takeovers. 

Flag and judgment. The no-single-event-frame flag is set when the robot is stuck or circling, with the interval placed near the start of a sustained ( ≥ 2 s) lack of progress. Judg-ments are “failure” (a failure can be confirmed), “no failure” (no clear failure observed) and “uncertain” (images or evi-dence insufficient); only a failure carries an interval and a label.

C.3. Takeover protocol and device mapping

with a recorded seed, so the operator does not choose when to take over; each start state is restored from the recorded rollout.

## D. Post-Training Implementation Details 

This section details the post-training methods of Sec. 4.1 of the main paper: what a method may use, the actor recipe shared by all trained arms, the critics of the value-based arms, the two inference-time variants and the compute spent on training and evaluation. 

D.1. Shared actor recipe 

Information access. The benchmark is instantiated once, for a single base checkpoint πbase . A method receives 

πbase , the 2,520 LIBERO-X demonstrations, the training failures (rollout prefix, final interval and failure-type label), all takeover attempts on them and the actor-update bud-get U ; held-out failure states, seeds and labels are hidden. Training failures, takeover start states and held-out failures all come from πbase , so the data are policy-matched by con-struction; no arm uses failures or takeovers collected from another policy. S11 Development set. Tuning was permitted only on a devel-opment set of 8 complete training failures with 80 takeover starts: the fifth non-archived training failure in seed order in each configuration of family 0 (L1–L5) and of family 9 (L1–L3). The recipe below was fixed without tuning, so these failures served only for implementation checks. They are excluded from all training, including the actor data, the critic data and the critic’s normalization statistics. 

Training data. The training data comprise, per configu-ration, the 5 lowest-seed non-archived training failures with 10 accepted recoveries each. Without the development fail-ures, post-training uses 232 source failures from all five levels, with 2,320 accepted recoveries and 797,385 con-trol steps. As in the main paper, D denotes the demonstra-tions, and R, F and N denote the accepted recoveries, the failed policy prefixes and the unsuccessful human attempts (ending in operator stops or time limits) of these training sources; physics-fault attempts are excluded. 

Flow-matching objective. All actors keep the flow-matching loss of the base π0.5 model as implemented in openpi [25]. As in the base checkpoint, the actor condi-tions on the camera images and the instruction but not on proprioception, which enters only the critic (Sec. D.2). For actor context x, a normalized chunk a of 10 actions (the ac-tion horizon of the base model), standard Gaussian noise ϵ

and sampled flow time λ, write

Optimization. Every trained arm starts from the base checkpoint, freezes the vision-language backbone and the base weights of the action expert, and trains rank-32 ( α =32 ) LoRA [8] adapters on the attention and feed-forward layers of the action expert together with the action and time projections. All trained arms keep the quantile normal-ization statistics of the base checkpoint and use AdamW (β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .95 , ϵ = 10 −8, weight decay 10 −10 ,no parameter EMA) with global-norm gradient clipping at 1.0, a cosine schedule with 500 warm-up steps (peak learn-ing rate 5 × 10 −5, final 5 × 10 −6), global batch 64 and 

U = 12 ,134 actor updates (checkpoint step 12,133). Com-plete 10-action chunks (starts up to 10 frames before an episode end) are sampled with replacement; each arm is trained with seed 42 and evaluated at its final checkpoint. 

Arms and actor data. Base-restart runs the base check-point without further training and serves as the untrained reference. Demo-continue trains further on D alone and is the continued-training control of Q1; Takeover-BC trains on R and enters both Q1 and Q2; the exploratory Mix-BC draws source D or R with probability 0.5 each and then samples complete chunks uniformly within the chosen source; and IQL-R2, the Q2 arm, trains on R and on failure-window chunks of F with value-based sampling weights (Sec. D.2). The other four arms are exploratory variants of IQL-R2 (IQL-R0 and AWAC; Sec. D.2) and of Takeover-BC (First-of-32 and IDQL-R2; Sec. D.3). 

D.2. Value-based arms: IQL-R2, IQL-R0 and AWAC

## E. Evaluation Protocol and Statistical Procedures 

This section defines how all nine arms are evaluated, from restored held-out failure points (Sec. E.1) and from nominal starts (Sec. E.2), and the statistical procedures behind their decisions and intervals (Secs. E.3 and E.4). 

E.1. Restore-and-continue evaluation 

Every arm resumes from the same restored held-out states under the following protocol. 

Held-out list. 384 points, fixed before the first held-out rollout: 240 confirmatory (L1–L3, 10 families) and 144 ex-ploratory (L4–L5, 9 families), with 8 points in every listed configuration. 

Failure point. The restart state is frame m = ⌊(a + b)/2⌋,the midpoint of the final interval [a, b ] rounded down, which differs from the exact midpoint for 185 of the 384 intervals. Interval bounds and m index the recorded simulator states, one per control step at 20 Hz: frame f is the state after f

environment steps from reset, counting the 10 zero-motion wait steps, so it follows base-policy step f − 10 ; each ren-dered frame shows exactly that state. S13 Restore. Restoring re-applies the recorded fixture layout, sets the recorded MuJoCo state at m and rebuilds the gripper-controller state from the first m recorded actions. Before every rollout, the restored MuJoCo state is checked against the recorded one; all 10,368 recovery rollouts pass this check (maximum absolute difference 0). 

Policy state. The policy starts with no queued actions and no observation history; the instruction is that of the original rollout, including L5 paraphrases. 

Success. Defined by the LIBERO-X task predicates. 

Horizon. Success counts within 1,200 control steps (pri-mary), a horizon that covers the length of 99.5% of accepted recoveries, and within 600 and 1,800 steps (sensitivity anal-yses); all three are read from one rollout of up to 1,800 steps. 

Samples. K = 3 policy-sampling seeds per point, shared across arms. Every started rollout is scored, so denomina-tors are fixed: restore errors, physics-guard faults and exe-cution errors count as failures. 

Metrics. SR rec (Eq. (2) of the main paper) for success rates and the family scores of Eq. (E1) for contrasts; every difference between arms, including the improvement over Base-restart, is paired over the same restored points. 

Completeness. Results come only from complete evalua-tion runs; interrupted runs were repeated in full. 

E.2. Nominal starts, validity and horizons 

Nominal evaluation measures how much task performance each arm retains when it starts from an initial state rather than from a failure; episodes follow the LIBERO-X evalua-tion procedure with seeded starts. 

Starts. 500 seeded initial states per arm, an equal number in each of the 50 configurations, with seeds shared across arms. After the zero-motion wait steps the policy runs for up to 1,200 steps; at L5 the instruction cycles through the level’s paraphrases. Physics-guard faults count as failures. 

Validity. 437 of the 500 starts are valid under a policy-independent check that was fixed before nominal policy evaluation and is shared by all arms; all nominal rates and changes use these 437 starts as their denominator. The check applies the start-validity rule of candidate screening (Sec. A.3) to the settled start after the wait steps, with each object’s settling baseline taken over the starts of its configu-ration; a start is also invalid if the physics guard fires during the wait. 

Sensitivity set. Some valid starts ended in a physics-guard fault within the first policy steps for every arm. A policy-free check that replays each start exactly and appends 20 no-op steps flags 51 of the 437 valid starts as unstable; re-moving them gives the 386-start sensitivity set. No arm suc-ceeds on a flagged start, so every numerator is unchanged on this set; the reported rates keep the 437-start denominator. 

Horizons. Nominal success is reported within 1,200 steps, the horizon of the recovery evaluation, and within 1.1 T , the pre-specified task-dependent criterion, where T is the mean demonstration length (Sec. A.2). Rates and changes refer to 1,200 steps unless noted; this horizon was adopted for nominal evaluation after nominal results of several arms had been seen. 

Change. ∆SR norm is the nominal success of an arm minus that of Base-restart, paired over the same valid starts and pooled over them. All nominal analyses are exploratory; their intervals follow Sec. E.4.

E.4. Interval conventions for nominal changes 

Nominal changes pool paired start-level differences over the 437 valid starts, so their intervals resample clusters of starts rather than the family scores of Eq. (E1). Under both conven-tions the interval of Demo-continue contains zero and those of Takeover-BC, Mix-BC and IQL-R2 lie below zero.

## H. Dataset Documentation, Limitations and Ethics 

This section documents the dataset in the form of adatasheet (Sec. H.1), states the boundaries of the study de-sign (Sec. H.2), describes the human participant (Sec. H.3) and describes the offline data browser (Sec. H.4). 

H.1. Datasheet 

The answers follow the questions of Gebru et al . [6] on motivation, composition, collection, preprocessing, uses, distribution and maintenance, and describe the dataset as frozen at the end of collection; human participants are cov-ered in Sec. H.3. 

Motivation. Purpose. To measure, under controlled conditions, what human takeovers at a policy’s own failure states add to VLA post-training beyond continued training on demon-strations, for one π0.5 checkpoint on 10 LIBERO-X task families. The data are policy-matched by construction: fail-ures, takeover starts and held-out evaluation states all come from rollouts of the same checkpoint. 

Creators. The authors, at Nanyang Technological Univer-sity, Singapore. 

Composition. Instances. (i) 629 natural failures of the base checkpoint released into the dataset by the coordinator (245 training, 384 held-out), each with an annotated interval and a failure-type label; (ii) 2,823 SpaceMouse takeover attempts from restorable simulator states at seeded frames inside train-ing intervals (2,799 on active and 24 on archived sources), among them 2,400 accepted recoveries, 10 for each of 240 complete training failures, with 828,827 control steps (11.5 h at 20 Hz); (iii) the 384 held-out failure points with their restorable states; (iv) the screening outcome, annota-tions and curation decision of each of the 1,287 candidates sent to annotation. Counts are given by level in Table 2 of the main paper and by configuration in Tab. C2. 

Sampling. The released failures are a screened, quota-based subset rather than a random sample of the policy’s failures. Candidates come from pre-assigned seed blocks, had not succeeded by 1.5 T , and passed the near-miss and physics checks and, except in families 0 and 9 (not screened before annotation), the start-validity check; they were then annotated and released under per-configuration quotas. Sec-tion A.3 gives, per configuration, the rollouts generated, those removed by each check and the resulting candidates. 

Content. Rollouts: two RGB views rendered from the recorded simulator state at every control step, a fixed third-person view and the wrist (eye-in-hand) camera, each 256 ×

256 pixels (the policy receives them padded and resized to 

224 ×224 ), proprioceptive state, actions and restorable sim-ulator states. Attempts: source failure, start frame, sam-pling seed, observations and actions, end reason, steps, con-S21 trol time, discard and review flags, and a pseudonymous op-erator ID. 

Labels. Annotations (interval, failure type, no-single-event-frame flag and judgment; Sec. C.1) come from one annotator; the coordinator decides which candidates enter the dataset and finalizes their intervals and labels. Attempt outcomes combine the simulator’s task predicate, the op-erator’s end action and the coordinator’s acceptance deci-sion, which rests on automatic success and integrity checks (Sec. C.3). 

Splits. Training and held-out failures are seed-disjoint in-stances of the same configurations; there are no held-out tasks. The 8 development failures, complete training fail-ures set aside together with their takeovers, serve for im-plementation checks (Sec. D.1); post-training uses the other 232 complete training failures (2,320 accepted recoveries). 

Missing data. Family 9 L4–L5 (Tab. C2): no accepted L4 recovery, no released L5 training failure and no released L4–L5 held-out failure. Of the candidates sent to annota-tion, 603 received no curation decision (Sec. A.3); 2 at-tempts have no acceptance decision and are not used for training or evaluation (Tab. C3). 

Errors and noise. Main recorded issues: one annotator without an agreement estimate; 13 width-flagged held-out intervals; 3 attempts at a frame outside the revised interval of their source; 15 physics-fault attempts on active sources; and late successes retained as failures (Secs. C.4 and C.5). 

Dependencies. The LIBERO-X [31] scenes, task defini-tions and demonstrations; the MuJoCo and robosuite simu-lators, which restore the recorded states; and the base check-point, fine-tuned with openpi [25] from the π0.5 weights (Sec. A.1). 

Sensitive content. None. The scenes are simulated house-hold layouts and no person appears in the data; the operator data are described in Sec. H.3. 

Collection process. Acquisition. Base-policy rollouts were generated in sim-ulation on pre-assigned seeds and screened automatically. One person annotated the candidates and performed the takeovers with a custom collection tool (Sec. H.3); every takeover starts from a restored simulator state. 

Takeover sampling. 10 frames per training failure, drawn uniformly without replacement inside the interval with a recorded seed; same-frame redo until success (Sec. C.3). 

Timeframe. Annotation and takeover collection ran from October 20 to November 6, 2026. 

Preprocessing, cleaning and labeling. Near-miss and physics-fault rollouts were excluded before annotation, as were invalid starts except in families 0 and 9, where the start-validity check was not applied before annotation (Sec. A.3). After annotation, the coordinator released or withheld candidates under the per-configuration quotas, 4 sources were archived, and sampled frames of family-0 L4 and L5 sources were redrawn after physics faults (Tab. C3). All records are kept: archived sources, discarded successes and attempts ending in an operator stop, a time limit or a physics fault are counted separately (Tab. C2). 

Uses. Intended uses. (i) Post-training from takeovers with evalu-ation at restored held-out failure states; (ii) same-state com-parison of the base policy’s continuation with the human recovery at every takeover start; (iii) learning from failed, abandoned and discarded corrections; (iv) failure localiza-tion from the interval labels; (v) budget subsampling within the balanced design of 5 failures × 10 accepted recoveries per configuration (48 of the 50 configurations complete); (vi) evaluating any new policy at the same restorable failure states. 

Not supported. Claims about human capability (frame-level success is 100% by construction; attempt-level rates reflect one operator under the redo rule); claims about the effect of policy matching (one policy), operator variability, when to take over, or sim-to-real transfer. 

Held-out use. The held-out failure states serve evaluation only; methods and hyperparameters should be selected on training data, such as the 8 development failures. 

Distribution. Planned public release. (i) Data: all 629 released fail-ures and all 2,823 takeover attempts ( R, F and N , plus archived-source and discarded records), distributed both as raw restorable MuJoCo states, fixture layouts, action his-tories and two-camera RGB streams and as LeRobot v2.1 datasets [2]; all 756 annotations, curation flags, split man-ifests and machine-readable task mappings (Tab. (ii) Code: the collection tool, the restore-and-continue and nominal evaluators, the locked openpi post-training patch, the IQL and AWAC critic and action-cache scripts, and the frozen analysis script (fpx analyze.py ). (iii) Weights: the fine-tuned π0.5

base checkpoint and the six post-trained actor and three critic checkpoints (Sec. D.4).

H.2. Design boundaries and limitations 

The study design sets the following boundaries. 

One checkpoint in simulation. With one π0.5 checkpoint in simulation, neither the effect of policy matching itself nor transfer to other architectures or real robots is tested. Recipes initialized from this πbase preserve policy matching by construction; evaluating policies trained from another base checkpoint reuses the restorable failure states without policy matching. 

One operator and annotator. One person performed all takeovers and annotations (no blind second annotation, so no agreement estimate) and collected families sequentially, confounding operator practice with family; sensitivity to recorded interval and curation flags is reported in Tab. F2. 

Takeover device and pace. Takeovers use a SpaceMouse at 20 Hz ( ±0.25 translation, ±0.08 rotation), whereas the base policy’s demonstrations were VR-teleoperated [31] and use larger commands (Secs. C.3 and G.4); post-trained policies inherit this slower command scale, coupling nom-inal completion at fixed step horizons with execution pace (Sec. F.6). 

Evaluation hardware. Base-restart was evaluated on an RTX 5090 GPU and the other eight arms on H200 GPUs, where bf16 sampling trajectories can differ (Secs. B.2 and G.3); same-hardware H200 nominal contrasts against Demo-continue yield consistent retention losses (Tab. F7). 

Curation. Same-frame redo and 71 discarded successes curate the accepted data; the dataset therefore keeps ev-ery attempt, and we report attempt-level human success (Sec. C.4). 

Power and coverage. Confirmatory tests use n = 10 fami-lies, which limits power (Sec. E); L4–L5 are exploratory, without family-9 held-out states or accepted recoveries (Tab. C2). 

One training seed. Each of the six actor-trained arms uses one seed (seed 42); the intervals condition on the evalu-ated checkpoints and resample task families for recovery and configurations for nominal performance, not training seeds. 

Restore and faults. Every restored state matched the recorded simulator state (Sec. E.1); physics-guard faults (37–72 of 1,152 rollouts per arm) count as failures, and excluding them shifts no pooled paired difference by more than 1.3 pp (Sec. F.2). 

Out of scope. Takeover timing (restarts at frames other than m), gains over new demonstrations of equal human time, online or iterative learning and new tasks are not stud-ied. 

H.3. Human participants and ethics 

The only human participant was one person who served as both takeover operator and annotator under a pseudony-mous ID, producing all 2,823 attempts and all 756 annota-tion records, with 14.0 h of active control and 5.4 h of an-notation. Curation decisions (which annotated candidates enter the dataset, frame replacements and takeover accep-tance) were made by a coordinator. The task, teleoperating a simulated arm with a 3D mouse, involves no physical robot. The operator and the coordinator are authors; they received no separate compensation and knew the study hypotheses when making curation, discard and frame-replacement de-cisions. No ethics review was sought. The collection tool records no video or audio of the operator: the only operator data are device actions and timing logs under the pseudony-mous ID, and the dataset contains no other personal infor-mation.
