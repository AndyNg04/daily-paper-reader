Abstract 

Standard evaluations assess LLM-simulated personas against an external ground truth, such as community trait annotations, but that ref-erence is unavailable when the persona is in-ferred only from a short, task-oriented chat. We ask how persona consistency can be eval-uated without such a reference, and propose a self-anchored internal-consistency diagnos-tic : the persona describes itself once and acts twice. A Likert self-report supplies a soft in-ternal anchor; two behavioral profiles come from constrained scenario choices scored with-out a judge and from open-ended responses judged by an LLM. Consistency is the corre-lation between the self-report and each behav-ioral profile, in a shared six-facet BFI-2 space. The framework compares persona-construction strategies by assessing how consistently their elicited responses reflect their self-reported traits. Across ordinary-user chats (WildChat and LMSYS) and well-documented personas with community annotations (the Personality Database, PDB), profile-enriched representa-tions agree with the anchor more closely than a raw-dialogue baseline. On PDB, this self-anchored ordering reproduces the community-annotation ordering. The pattern is stable across answering and judge models. 

1 Introduction 

A user simulator is a large language model (LLM) agent instructed to act as a particular user or persona. Such simulators are becoming core in-frastructure for building and studying LLM assis-tants (Chen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025): they provide interactive counterparts for training and evaluating assistants (Dou et al., 2025), synthesize dialogue data at scale (Wang et al., 2025), and stand in for human participants in field-style stud-ies of user preference and behavior (Li et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024b). Their usefulness depends on whether the simulator expresses persona-relevant preferences and behaviors consistently. This is more feasible to assess for well-documented public personas, whose traits can be reconstructed from abundant public material (encyclopedia entries, bi-ographies, recorded interviews and speeches). Or-dinary users create an information asymmetry : their personality cannot be reliably reconstructed from a short, task-dominated chat history (Zhao et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024), so conversation-derived personas leave no external personality reference to grade a simulation against. Evaluating persona consistency for these personas therefore remains an open problem, and it is the setting this paper studies. Existing role-play and persona evaluations typi-cally compare simulated behavior with an explicit persona specification, an external character ref-erence, or matched human behavior: the domi-nant role-play fidelity paradigm grades a simulated character against an annotated script, persona pro-file, or psychometric-interview reference (Wang et al., 2024b), while simulator-evaluation meth-ods instead compare against matched human di-alogue or behavior sequences (Dou et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). None of these is generally available for personas constructed from sparse, task-oriented ordinary-user conversations. Self-/temporal-consistency methods avoid this require-ment but only certify a stable surface profile (name, occupation, stated facts), not the deeper trait-level preferences that actually govern how a user reacts; the remaining evaluation approaches target orthogo-nal properties (fluency, helpfulness, factuality) and do not assess persona consistency at all. Without an evaluation signal, we also cannot compare persona representations—such as the raw conversation and an extracted profile—to identify which yields more consistent trait expression. Consequently, persona representations cannot be systematically evaluated or improved in this setting. question: how to evaluate persona consistency without an external reference. A simulator may reproduce persona traits in self-description with-out expressing them in situational responses. We treat agreement between the two as an internal-consistency diagnostic : a simulator that has inter-nalized its persona should let the traits it reports govern how it acts, whereas one that merely restates its prompt should not. We implement this idea as a 

self-anchored evaluation framework . A Likert self-report over a chat-relevant six-facet subset of the BFI-2 (Soto and John, 2017)—scored at the facet rather than the domain level, because facets carry behavioral information that domain averaging dis-cards (Paunonen and Ashton, 2001)—provides a 

soft internal anchor elicited from the persona itself. The same persona then acts in two structurally dif-ferent channels: a deterministically scored scenario questionnaire (SCN) and LLM-judged open-ended responses (OE). All three profiles occupy the same six-facet space. Consistency is the correlation be-tween each behavioral profile and the self-report anchor , per persona across facets or per facet across personas. Because all three profiles come from the same model, we first check that their agreement is not only a shared model or prompt effect. We then apply the framework to PDB, WildChat and LMSYS—the latter two with no external user-level reference—and test whether it distinguishes con-struction strategies. The metric compares those strategies; it does not establish human fidelity or downstream utility. Our main contributions are summarized as fol-lows: • We frame persona-consistency evaluation without external user-level labels as an internal-consistency problem for conversation-derived user simulation, and propose a self-anchored, cross-method diagnostic over a chat-relevant six-facet BFI-2 subset (§3). • We show that the diagnostic separates persona representation strategies on all three datasets, stably across answering and judge models, and that on PDB the self-anchored order-ing tracks the community-annotation ordering (§5). 

2 Related Work 

Persona and role-play evaluation. InCharac-ter grades role-playing agents via psychological interviews checked against an external charac-ter reference (Wang et al., 2024b); PersonaGym probes persona adherence through dynamically generated persona-relevant environments (Samuel et al., 2025); Eval4Sim compares generated con-versations to a human dialogue dataset along ad-herence, consistency, and naturalness (Bao et al., 2026). Each requires an explicit persona specifi-cation, an external reference, or a matched human dataset, none of which is available for conversation-derived user personas built from sparse dialogue. We instead measure how strongly elicited behav-ior correlates with an internally elicited self-report anchor. 

User simulation and its evaluation. LLM user simulators are increasingly conditioned on profiles derived from interaction history, e.g., USP’s im-plicit profiles (Wang et al., 2025), two of whose representation strategies we adopt (§4). Simula-torArena and BehaviorChain instead evaluate sim-ulators against matched human messages or behav-ior sequences (Dou et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). We address the intermediate problem of whether a conversation-derived persona is internally coherent when no such matched human reference exists. 

Self-report validity and facet-level measure-ment. Self-reported LLM traits should not au-tomatically be treated as a behavioral reference: stable self-reports do not reliably predict behav-ior (Han et al., 2025), coherence is construct- and context-dependent (Kocielnik et al., 2026), and self-reports can share variance with LLM judges that human ratings do not (Contreras, 2026). This mo-tivates our soft self-report anchor alongside two differently structured, behavior-oriented channels (§3), evaluated through their convergent/discrim-inant structure rather than treated as a reference. We further operationalize personality at the facet level, since facets carry behavioral information that domain averaging discards (Paunonen and Ashton, 2001; Soto and John, 2017; DeYoung et al., 2007), and since personality-inferability from public chat datasets is strongly interaction-dependent (Zhao et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024), motivating our chat-relevant six-facet subset (§3.1). 

3 The Self-Anchored Persona Consistency Evaluation Framework 

The framework lets the persona describe itself once and act twice, then scores consistency as the correlation between the self-report and the two behavioral channels (Figure 2). We first fix a six-facet scoring space (§3.1), then elicit the self-report (§3.2) and the two behavior-oriented pro-files (§3.3), and finally compute the consistency statistic (§3.4). Because all three profiles come from the same model, we also validate the anchor (§3.5; Appendices F, G, E.7). 

3.1 Facet Space: Six Chat-Relevant Facets 

The Big Five Inventory–2 (BFI-2; Soto and John, 2017) operationalizes the Big Five personality model and organizes personality into five domains (Extraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, Negative Emotionality, and Open-Mindedness), each split into three facets (15 total; Appendix B lists them all). We retain six facets, one per domain plus a second Open-Mindedness facet: Assertive-ness , Respectfulness , Productiveness , Emotional Volatility , Intellectual Curiosity and Creative Imag-ination . These six facets define the measurement space of the framework: every profile is scored on them, on a common 1–5 scale. We work at the facet rather than the domain level for incremental predictive validity over domain av-eraging (Paunonen and Ashton, 2001; Soto and John, 2017; DeYoung et al., 2007). The subset is a design choice, not an empirically optimized selection. Channel observability (primary): the facet should be elicitable in ordinary user–chatbot dialogue without contrived probing. Guided by topic distributions from public chat datasets (Zhao et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024), we drop facets that rarely arise there (e.g., Aesthetic Sensi-tivity, which typically needs talk of art or beauty) and keep facets expected to be more observable, such as Assertiveness, Productiveness, and Cre-ative Imagination. Within-domain redundancy :where sibling facets overlap in chat (e.g., Orga-nization versus Productiveness), we keep one rep-resentative per domain. Expected identifiability :conceptually distinct facets can still be hard to tell apart in text, so we streamline domains that would add little separable information, while re-taining both Open-Mindedness facets because they cover complementary aspects of open-mindedness. Leave-one-facet-out checks leave the SCN Pear-son strategy ranking against PDB unchanged (all Kendall τ = 1 ; Appendix E.8). That supports ranking robustness within this selected space ; it does not establish psychometric reliability or va-lidity, and coverage of personality remains limited (Limitations). 

3.2 Self-Anchored Report 

The first step produces the profile that everything else is compared against. The persona answers an 18-item Likert questionnaire derived from the BFI-2 (3 items × 6 retained facets), five times with independently shuffled item order; the facet score is the median of the five administrations. Shuf-fling and the median limit item-order effects and single-run noise. This profile occupies the role an external reference would play in a standard eval-uation, which is why we call it an anchor. §3.5 motivates that use. The instrument is documented in Appendix A.1.

3.5 Why the Self-Report Can Serve as an Anchor 

The anchor is elicited from the same model as the two behavioral profiles, so a high correlation among them could reflect a shared response style rather than a persona-specific trait profile. We therefore test the anchor before relying on it. Ex-isting checks support specificity but cannot rule out shared-model bias: we treat the metric as an internal-consistency diagnostic, not an absolute measure of fidelity. Following the Campbell–Fiske multitrait–multimethod criteria (Campbell and Fiske, 1959), each facet agrees with itself across the three channels more than dif-ferent facets do in 68% , 75% and 63% of tests on PDB, WildChat and LMSYS (Appendix F). A response style that moved all six facets together would not produce this separation. A second check confirms that it is also the persona that is sepa-rated: a persona’s behavior fits its own anchor bet-ter than any other persona’s (Appendix G). Neither MTMM nor identifiability identifies which part of the agreement is persona-specific rather than shared-model style.

Self-anchored and reference-based criteria agree on strategy ordering. The framework is useful only if the self-anchored correlation, which needs no external reference, tracks the reference-based one. Aggregated per strategy (Figure 3), the two induce the same Pearson or-dering: Trait-injected > Name-only > Wiki-based RAG ≈ Dialogue-driven > Raw Dialogue , with Trait-injected top-ranked in 71 /75 evaluated cases and Raw Dialogue consistently weakest. The self-anchored values are systematically higher, consis-tent with shared model and prompt variance. Averaging over the 75 personas first can inflate this agreement, so we also ask, per persona ×

model × strategy cell: does closer agreement with the imperfect anchor coincide with closer agree-ment with the reference? The two remain posi-tively associated ( r = 0 .29 –0.44 ), including cross-channel pairings and after excluding Trait-injected and Name-only (cross-channel r = 0 .22 , 95% CI 

[0 .14 , 0.30] ). This is criterion-related evidence that the self-anchored correlation carries information about the reference-based one, not evidence that it is a proxy for human fidelity or downstream util-ity (Appendix E.7). Exploratory splits of PDB’s 

40 fictional and 35 real personas show a larger real–fictional gap under Raw Dialogue than Trait-injected (SCN–anchor facet correlation; interac-tion 0.124 , unadjusted 95% CI [0 .056 , 0.194] ; Ap-pendix C). 

5.3 Robustness Across Answering and Judge Models 

The representation-strategy patterns of §5.1–§5.2 hold across all four answering models, al-though intermediate-strategy order remains model-dependent (Table 4; CIs in Appendix E.4). Re-scoring OE with two additional judges (Ap-pendix E.5) yields small aggregate differences (mean pairwise |∆| = 0 .019 ) and no qualitative ranking change. 

6 Discussion and Conclusion 

We presented a self-anchored, cross-method 

internal-consistency diagnostic for persona con-sistency when external user-level personality refer-ences are unavailable (§3–§5). A high correlation with the anchor indicates that a persona behaves as if it had a coherent trait profile across differently structured elicitation formats; on PDB it is also positively associated with the reference-based cor-relation (§5.2). It does not establish human fidelity or downstream utility. Profile-enriched representa-tions show higher consistency than Raw Dialogue (§5.1), with two non-exclusive readings: extrac-tion may make scattered trait-relevant information easier to use, or it may discard individual-specific detail and yield a simpler persona that is easier to follow. Because we measure consistency rather than information preservation, higher consistency does not imply greater fidelity to the source conver-sation. 

Limitations 

• Shared variance between the anchor and the behavioral channels. All three pro-files come from the same answering model, so shared response style may inflate agree-ment. MTMM (pass rates 68% /75% /63% 

on PDB/WildChat/LMSYS; Appendix F) and persona-identifiability (Appendix G) support trait and persona specificity but cannot rule out shared-model bias. We interpret the metric comparatively, as an internal-consistency di-agnostic rather than an absolute fidelity score. Eliciting the anchor from a separate model, or from the human user where available, would remove this source of shared variance. • Non-independent repeated conditions. Strate-gies, answering models and judges reuse the same personas. We treat the persona as the sampling unit for all CIs and permutation tests (§3.4). • Noisy, domain-level external reference. PDB annotations are community domain scores, not clinical or facet-level labels. Mapped values are domain-level proxies. Measure-ment error attenuates the reference-based cor-relations of §5.2. Removing either Open-Mindedness facet or pooling the two leaves Pearson strategy rankings unchanged (Ap-pendix C); that is a mapping-sensitivity check, not construct validation. • Elicited, single-conversation behavior. Wild-Chat and LMSYS personas come from a sin-gle conversation, and all channels are elicited rather than observed. The diagnostic does not measure dynamic multi-turn assistant-task utility. Human–simulator validation remains future work. • Facet, cultural and linguistic coverage. The six-facet subset is selected for expected chat observability. Leave-one-facet-out checks support ranking robustness within this space (Appendix E.8) but do not establish psycho-metric reliability or validity, nor coverage of personality in general. Datasets and prompts do not span cultural and linguistic variation in trait expression. Extending to all 15 BFI-2 facets and prospectively collected human self-reports remains future work. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work infers personality-style profiles from user conversations, which raises several concerns. 

Privacy and consent. The profiles are constructed from user–chatbot exchanges that users did not provide for the purpose of psychological assess-ment; we use WildChat and LMSYS only in their released, anonymized form. Profiling and manip-ulation. We caution against deploying this frame-work to profile, target, or manipulate individuals, or to make consequential decisions about them; it is intended as a research tool for comparing persona representation strategies at the population level. 

Not a clinical instrument. Inferred profiles can be inaccurate and should not be treated as reliable per-user assessments; the framework is not a clin-ical or diagnostic instrument, and should not be used to make claims about any individual’s actual personality (§6). Aggregate reporting. We report only aggregate, population-level statistics and do not release or discuss any individual-level inferred profile. 

Artifacts and licensing. The Software ZIP sub-mitted with this paper contains the instruments, evaluation pipeline, aggregate results, README run instructions, 75 PDB personas × 5 strategies, and WildChat/LMSYS sample identifiers. We re-lease under a permissive open-source license only the artifacts we created ourselves: the scenario and open-ended items, the judge template and rubric (Appendix A), the elicitation and scoring pipeline code, and the aggregate results reported here, to-gether with an example of raw model responses for one PDB persona across all five representation strategies, which involve no user data. Third-party material is not redistributed. We use WildChat un-der ODC-BY with attribution and LMSYS under its dataset license, which prohibits transferring the data to third parties, so for both we release only conversation identifiers, letting others rebuild our exact sample once the original terms are accepted. The PDB annotations are likewise not redistributed; we release the retrieval date (§4) and the code that maps the site’s community votes to our external reference. The BFI-2 item text is not reproduced or released, as it is distributed by the instrument’s authors (Soto and John, 2017); we release only the facet assignment and reverse-keying of the items we use.

A.1 Self-Report Likert Questionnaire 

The self-anchored report uses 18 Likert items de-rived from the BFI-2 (Soto and John, 2017), three per retained facet, presented on the instrument’s five-point strongly-disagree to strongly-agree scale. Following standard BFI-2 scoring, items keyed against their facet are recoded so that a higher value always means more of the facet, and the three items of a facet are combined into one facet score on the 1–5 scale. The questionnaire is administered five times per persona with the item order inde-pendently shuffled in each administration, and the reported facet score is the median of the five admin-istrations, which limits the influence of item-order effects and of a single anomalous run. Across the 

9,000 PDB facet scores ( 75 personas × 5 strate-gies × 4 answering models × 6 facets), the five-administration range is zero for 61% of scores and at most one-third of a scale point for 78% ; the cor-responding IQR is zero for 78% and ≤ 1/3 for 

90% (median range = 0 , mean range = 0 .22 ). Dis-persion is therefore low for most evaluated PDB facet scores, though the maximum range reaches 

2.67 , so the median does not hide variability in ev-ery cell. We do not reproduce the BFI-2 item text here, as it is distributed by the instrument’s authors. 

A.2 Scenario-Based Questionnaire (SCN) 

The scenario channel uses 30 forced-choice situ-ational items, five per facet. Each item describes an everyday situation and offers a set of mutually exclusive courses of action, and each option car-ries a score from 1 to 5 that was fixed when the item was written. A response is therefore scored by table lookup, with no judge model and no free-text interpretation, and the five item scores of a facet are combined into that facet’s score. This is what makes the channel a useful counterpart to the open-ended one: its failure modes are those of item design rather than those of a judge. 

A.3 Open-Ended Prompts (OE) 

The open-ended channel uses 18 free-form prompts, three per facet, each asking the persona to recount concrete behavior rather than to rate itself. Because responses are unconstrained text, they cannot be scored by lookup: each response is scored from 

1 to 5 by an LLM judge against an item-specific behavioral rubric, in three independent calls whose scores are averaged (§A.4–§A.5). This is the only channel whose scores depend on a judge model, so its sensitivity to that choice is checked separately in Appendix E.5.

C PDB External-Reference Construction 

PDB is the only dataset with an external person-ality reference, so it anchors the entire validation. This appendix documents how the reference is built, expanding the summary in §4. 

Source annotations. We use the community-aggregated Big Five (Five-Factor) domain scores from Personality Database (PDB), a public plat-form on which users vote on the personality of well-documented characters and public figures. For each persona these votes are aggregated into a score per Big Five domain. 

Persona selection. We take 75 of the most-voted, best-documented personas ( 40 fictional characters and 35 real public figures). Personas are chosen by documentation/vote volume rather than sampled at random, so the reference is drawn from the highest-agreement region of PDB. Main-text tables pool the two subsets; we report them separately below. 

Confidence threshold. To avoid low-agreement annotations, a Big Five domain is retained only if it is backed by at least 5 community votes; personas without enough votes on the relevant domains are excluded before selection. 

Mapping to the six BFI-2 facets. PDB scores are at the Big Five domain level, whereas our framework operates on six BFI-2 facets . We use a fixed, manually specified mapping that follows the standard BFI-2 domain–facet hierarchy: Ex-traversion → Assertiveness; Agreeableness → Re-spectfulness; Conscientiousness → Productiveness; Negative Emotionality → Emotional Volatility; and Open-Mindedness → Intellectual Curiosity and 

Creative Imagination. Mapped scores are therefore domain-level proxies , not facet-level ground truth. Because the reference is domain-level, the two Open-Mindedness facets inherit the same Open-Mindedness domain value; the external reference does not distinguish these two facets (the SCN/OE channels still elicit them separately). Removing either Open-Mindedness facet, or pooling the two into one averaged facet, leaves the Pearson strategy ranking against PDB unchanged in both behavioral channels (all Kendall τ = 1 on the ri ranking). Facet-difficulty order (Respectfulness / Emotional Volatility easiest, Assertiveness hardest) is likewise unchanged. This is a sensitivity check on dupli-cated mapping, not evidence of construct validity. 

Scale. The mapped scores are expressed on the same 1–5 facet scale as the elicited profiles (stored per persona as facet_scores_derived ) and used directly, with no further per-facet normalization. Because the reported statistic is a Pearson corre-lation across personas (invariant to location and scale), the specific linear placement onto 1–5 af-fects only the MAE/RMSE metrics (Table 14), not the correlations with the external reference.

Shared conversation-based strategies. Raw Di-alogue supplies the source conversation directly as context, following the in-context-history strategy common in role-playing language agents (Chen et al., 2024); Dialogue-driven Free Description in-stead supplies a free-form description synthesized from that same conversation by GPT-5.5 , in the spirit of dialogue-history-based persona character-ization without a predefined profile (Cheng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). On PDB, the source consists of reference dialogue transcripts (§C); on WildChat/LMSYS, it consists of one complete user– chatbot conversation (§4). On PDB, both strategies redact the persona’s name from the underlying tran-script, replacing it with a generic role placeholder (this character /this public figure ). 

Dataset-specific strategies. PDB additionally supports three strategies not available for anony-mous conversation-derived personas: Name-only 

(only the persona’s name is given, with no other material), Wiki-based RAG (retrieved encyclope-dic passages about the persona, with the persona’s name and article title likewise redacted and re-placed by the generic role placeholder, following retrieval-grounded role-profile construction (Wang et al., 2024a)), and Trait-injected (the persona’s name plus an explicit trait profile injected into the prompt, an oracle-style condition with direct trait information). G Persona-Identifiability: Per-Cell Detail and Omnibus Test 

This appendix expands the persona-identifiability analysis of §3.5. For each of the 20 PDB (construction-strategy × answering-model) cells (default judge DeepSeek-V4-Pro , n = 75 

personas per cell), we run a one-sided label-permutation test ( 10 ,000 shuffles) on (a) top-1 iden-tification accuracy and (b) the mean percentile rank of the true match. The complete per-cell statistics (accuracy, percentile rank, permutation z and p for both channels) are released with the code; Table 17 summarizes their distribution. 

Aggregation of permutation-test results. Amean of permutation p-values has no clean sam-pling interpretation: it is neither a valid combined significance level nor an effect size. We therefore report the fraction of cells that individually reject the chance null and the median [min , max] per-cell 

p, and complement these with a single omnibus test. 

Stratified omnibus permutation test. To obtain one significance statement per channel while re-specting the cell structure, we permute persona labels within each cell independently and simulta-neously , recompute the per-cell statistic, and ag-gregate across cells into a single global statistic: the total number of correct top-1 identifications summed over all 20 cells (equivalently, over all 

20 × 75 = 1500 trials) for the top-1 test, and the trial-pooled mean percentile rank for the rank test. Repeating this 10 ,000 times yields the null distri-bution of the global statistic. The observed totals (≈ 231 correct top-1 identifications for the scenario channel and ≈ 154 for the open-ended channel, ver-sus ≈ 20 expected under the stratified null) lie more than 30 null standard deviations above chance, so the omnibus test rejects chance at p < 0.001 for both channels and both statistics. This aggregation accounts for the shared-persona structure across cells (the same 75 personas recur across strategies and answering models), which a naive pooling of independent tests would ignore.
