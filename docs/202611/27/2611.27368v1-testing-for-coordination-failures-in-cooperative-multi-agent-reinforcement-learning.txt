# Testing for Coordination Failures in Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning 

Dennis Gross, Jørn Eirik Betten, Helge Spieker* Simula Research Laboratory, Oslo, Norway Corresponding author: helge@simula.no 

Abstract 

Deep parallel cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (CMARL) trains multiple agents that all act at every time step to optimize a common objective. Like single-agent RL, CMARL can produce unsafe behaviors that violate implicit safety requirements not captured by the reward, and these behaviors are hard to explain. However, testing CMARL policies for safety has received less attention than the safety testing of single-agent RL policies. In this paper, we propose DisQ , a test case selection method that identifies failure-revealing states in value-based CMARL by exploiting a signal specific to trained Q-learning agents. The signal is the Q-value gap , an explainable metric that flags a state when the agents’ estimates of the achievable return diverge by more than a calibrated threshold. DisQ selects such states for execution and skips those without a gap. Our experiments in four CMARL benchmark environments show that, in most of the evaluated configurations, DisQ improves failure detection over single-agent RL testing methods applied to CMARL, namely random testing and search-based testing with genetic algorithms. Our main contributions are the Q-value gap, an explainable metric for CMARL miscoordination, and DisQ, a test case selection method that adapts existing single-agent testing methods to CMARL. 

Keywords: Multi-agent Reinforcement Learning Testing, Explainable Multi-agent Rein-forcement Learning, Policy Testing, Test Case Selection 

# 1 Introduction 

Deep parallel cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (CMARL) extends single-agent reinforcement learning (RL) to distributed sequential decision-making problems in which multiple agents act simultaneously toward a common objective. Such problems arise in domains like manufacturing job scheduling, autonomous driving, and gaming [1, 2, 3], where CMARL scales better than single-agent RL, since per-agent policies avoid the joint action space that grows exponentially in the number of agents [4], and has been reported to reach higher returns in these settings [5, 3]. Agents act in parallel, each selects and executes an action at every time step according to its own learned policy, and all receive a joint reward afterwards [6], as opposed to turn-based settings in which the agents act one after another. We further focus on value-based CMARL, where each agent’s policy is 1arXiv:2611.27368v1 [cs.MA] 20 Nov 2026 derived from a learned Q-function that estimates the expected cumulative reward of each action in a given state [7, 8]. Like their single-agent counterparts, trained CMARL policies can exhibit unsafe behaviors [9], such as collisions between agents [10]: reward functions rarely capture all safety requirements [11], and the neural networks representing the policies hide the details that drive their decisions [12]. Testing is the established answer to this problem. For single-agent RL, a mature line of search-based software testing (SBST) methods exists that searches the space of environment configurations for failure-revealing test cases [13, 14, 15, 16, 17], in some cases guided by explainable-RL techniques [15]. For CMARL, the picture is different in two respects. First, existing CMARL testing work has largely focused on adversarial robustness , i.e., perturbing the agents’ observations or actions to degrade performance [16, 18, 19]. This addresses security-related threats, but not failures that originate in the agents’ learned coordination itself: states in which the agents, unperturbed, fail to cooperate. Second, testing is more expensive than in the single-agent case, because the joint state and action spaces grow exponentially with the number of agents [15, 9], so every executed test episode counts. Applying single-agent SBST methods to CMARL unchanged is possible, but ignores the one signal that is specific to cooperative agents: whether their learned expectations about the joint future agree. This work exploits exactly that signal. Since cooperative agents are trained on a shared reward, their Q-functions should assign approximately the same value to the same situation. We define the Q-value gap , an explainable CMARL metric that flags a state when the agents’ maximal Q-values diverge by more than a calibrated threshold. A Q-value gap means the agents hold contradictory expectations about the future, an indicator of potential miscoordination. We show that gap states are disproportionately failure-revealing under a generic reward-based oracle available in every environment we study, and identify the conditions under which they are not. Building on this metric, we introduce Disagreeing Q-values (DisQ) , a test case selection method for value-based CMARL. Given a candidate initial state from an arbitrary test case generator, DisQ computes the Q-value gap at that state and selects the test case for execution only if a gap is present. The decision requires a single forward pass per agent and no interaction with the environment. This distinguishes DisQ from adversarial CMARL testing, which perturbs the system under test, and from diversity-driven multi-agent testing such as MASTest [19], which requires executing episodes and analyzing the resulting trajectories before assessing a test case. To the best of our knowledge, DisQ is the first method to select CMARL test cases from the agents’ Q-values at the initial state alone. Because DisQ only filters candidate states, it is compatible with existing test case generators. For example, we evaluate it on top of random and genetic SBST in our experiments. DisQ rests on three assumptions: (i) agents whose policies are derived from per-agent action-value functions, as in deep Q-learning; pure policy-gradient methods expose no value estimates, and common actor-critic CMARL architectures employ shared or joint-action critics for which inter-agent disagreement is not meaningful; (ii) access to the agents’ Q-values, i.e., a white-box testing setting, as is available to developers testing their own systems before deployment; and (iii) environments in which the initial configuration (and hence the agents’ coordination at the start of an episode) influences the episode outcome, since DisQ evaluates only initial states. We discuss (i) and (ii) in Section 3.3, probe the boundary of (iii) empirically in Section 5.3, and return to the failure class in Section 6. 2Our contributions are: 

• the Q-value gap , an explainable metric for potential miscoordination in value-based CMARL, computable from a single state without executing the system; 

• DisQ , a test case selection method based on the Q-value gap that composes with existing SBST test case generators and avoids executing test cases that are unlikely to fail; 

• an evaluation in four benchmark environments under both centralized and decentral-ized training, showing that, in most configurations, DisQ-selected test cases expose more failures and lower rewards than those of vanilla random and genetic SBST, with statistically significant differences. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 introduces single-and multi-agent reinforcement learning, Q-learning, and search-based testing, including the definitions of test case and failure used throughout. Section 3 presents the Q-value gap and the DisQ selection method, together with their limitations. Section 4 describes the experimental setup, and Section 5 presents the results along four research questions. Section 6 discusses threats to validity, Section 7 reviews related work, and Section 8 concludes the paper.

2.3 Cooperative Multi-agent Reinforcement Learning 

CMARL extends the RL idea to find the near-optimal policies 𝜋 𝑖 in a Cooperative Multi-Agent Markov Decision Process (CMMDP) setting (see Figure 2 and Definition 2.4). Each agent policy 𝜋 𝑖 is represented by a neural network. A neural network is a function parameterized by weights 𝜃 𝑖 . 2.4 Centralized and Decentralized CMARL Training 

CMARL policies can be trained in two regimes, both of which we test. In centralized training, all agents share the same Q-network, which is trained across all agents simultaneously. The multi-agent environment is converted into a vectorized environment whose parallel instances correspond to the individual agents, so one set of weights is updated from every agent’s transitions. During execution, each agent 𝑖 passes its own observation 𝑜 𝑖 through these shared weights and selects an action from its own action space 𝐴𝑐𝑡 𝑖 ; the output head spans max 𝑖 ∈𝐼 | 𝐴𝑐𝑡 𝑖 | actions. Where the agents’ action spaces differ in size, the smaller ones are padded to this maximum, and an index that is out of range for the acting agent is remapped to that agent’s default action rather than being masked out before selection. Among the environments used in the experimental evaluation, this applies only to Simple Speaker Listener. In all other environments, all agents have action spaces of equal size, so no padding is triggered. Centralized training has the advantage that it is more data-efficient, but it is not specialized to each agent role in the environment. In decentralized training (independent Q-learning), each agent 𝑖 trains its own Q-network 𝑄 𝑖 (𝑜 𝑖 , 𝑎 𝑖 ) on its local observation, with no parameter sharing between agents, treating the other agents as part of the environment. Decentralized training allows for better specialization on each agent’s role, but it scales linearly with the number of agents and usually requires more training iterations to converge to a stable policy. We evaluate DisQ under both regimes because they produce structurally different Q-functions, and hence potentially different Q-value gap behavior.

# 3 Methodology 

This section introduces the test case selection method called Disagreeing Q-values (DisQ) .This method selects test cases by analyzing the difference between the agents’ maximal Q-values at given initial states without requiring additional information or execution of the actual episodes. Because the agents are trained on a shared reward, their maximal Q-values should roughly agree. Large discrepancies, i.e., Q-value gaps, therefore pinpoint test cases where coordination is likely to fail (Example 1). DisQ is independent of the source of the test case states and is compatible with many test case generators, for example, random sampling of initial states or genetic algorithms. DisQ evaluates the Q-value gap only at the initial state. This choice is deliberate. Selection becomes execution-free, i.e., it requires a single forward pass per agent, but no involvement of the environment, such that non-promising test cases can be discarded without any simulation cost, which is precisely where CMARL testing is expensive. The trade-off is that DisQ cannot detect miscoordination that emerges only later in an episode: agents whose value estimates agree at 𝑠 0 may still diverge at step 𝑡 > 0. Extending DisQ to monitor 𝐷 (𝑠 𝑡 ) during execution, e.g., as an online abort/prioritization criterion, is a natural extension, but it forfeits the execution-free property that motivates the method. We quantify how much signal this trade-off forgoes, and for which training regimes online monitoring would be selective, in Section 5.3.4. 

Example 1. To illustrate how DisQ works, consider a simple scenario with two CMARL agent policies. One policy estimates that choosing action 1 from a particular state will 

7maximize the expected cumulative reward to 100 . In contrast, another policy in the same state predicts a maximum expected cumulative reward of only 20 for its best action. This large divergence between the agents’ most optimistic outcomes is called the Q-value gap. It indicates a contradiction, as both agents were trained on a common reward function and should predict approximately the same cumulative reward from a particular state.

Both parameters are therefore budget-dependent choices rather than properties of the method. After the calibration, and at the beginning of each test case, DisQ evaluates whether a Q-value gap occurs at the initial state. When a Q-value gap is detected ( 𝐷 (𝑠 ) > 𝜆 ), the test executes the agents’ policies in the environment to further investigate the system’s behavior to potentially detect a failure per Definition 2.6. If no Q-value gap is found, the generator produces a new initial state and repeats the test case selection process. DisQ can be applied as a test case selection method to SBST methods such as random search or genetic algorithm. In the random search SBST method, we apply the test case selection at the initial state without executing the full test case. Depending on the Q-value gap, an execution of the test case could then be skipped to avoid the computational cost of a non-promising test case execution. For SBST with genetic algorithms, we give individuals the highest possible (worst) fitness when the individual’s initial state is not a Q-value gap state. 93.3 Limitations 

DisQ’s applicability is bounded by three assumptions: it requires per-agent action-value estimates (i), access to those estimates (ii), and environments in which initial-state coordination influences the episode outcome (iii). 

Per-agent Action-value Estimates. The Q-value gap compares the agents’ independent 

expectations of the achievable return, so DisQ requires each agent to expose an action-value estimate over its own action space. Deep Q-learning provides this natively. Pure policy-gradient methods such as PPO [22] do not: they output probability distributions over actions rather than return estimates, and two agents may assign similar probabilities to their preferred actions while expecting very different returns, so a gap over probabilities would not carry the same meaning. Actor-critic CMARL methods do train value functions, but their mainstream architectures do not expose the quantity DisQ needs. MAPPO [23] learns a centralized state-value critic 𝑉 (𝑠 ), which provides no per-action values; moreover, in the standard configuration the critic parameters are shared across agents, so any disagreement between the agents’ value estimates is zero by construction. COMA [24] likewise employs a single centralized critic, shared by all agents and conditioned on the joint action, again leaving no independent per-agent estimates whose divergence could signal miscoordination. MADDPG [25] is closer: each agent owns a critic 𝑄 𝑖 (𝑠, 𝑎 1, . . . , 𝑎 𝑁 ),but it is conditioned on the joint continuous action, so the per-agent maximum max 𝑎 𝑖 𝑄 𝑖 

underlying the Q-value gap is not directly computable. A DisQ variant for MADDPG-style architectures could instead compare the agents’ critics evaluated at the same greedy joint action, 𝑄 𝑖 (𝑠, 𝜇 1 (𝑜 1), . . . , 𝜇 𝑁 (𝑜 𝑁 )) ; developing and validating such a variant requires a different gap definition and calibration and is beyond the scope of this paper. 

Access to Q-values. DisQ operates in a white-box (or at least gray-box) setting. This is realistic when developers test their own systems before deployment, the setting we target: the Q-values are computed by the policies anyway, and exposing them requires no instrumentation beyond reading the network outputs. DisQ is not applicable when third-party agents expose only their selected actions. In such black-box settings, a possible direction is to train surrogate value functions from observed behavior, e.g., by distillation, and to apply DisQ to the surrogates. However, this would substantially increase the complexity and requirements for applying DisQ, making it an impractical alternative. 

Initial-state Dependence. DisQ evaluates only initial states, so it cannot select for miscoordination whose Q-value signal emerges later in an episode, or for failures that are not accompanied by a Q-value gap at any step (Section 3.2). RQ3.4 (Section 5.3.4) probes this boundary empirically: it identifies an environment class in which DisQ yields no improvement and quantifies, per environment and training regime, how many failure episodes carry an initial-state gap, develop one only during execution, or never exhibit one. 

# 4 Experiments 

In this section, we evaluate DisQ in four environments and with different numbers of agents. We structure the evaluation along four research questions: 10 RQ1 (Effectiveness) To what extent does DisQ improve the failure-detection effectiveness of existing SBST methods? We compare random and genetic SBST with and without DisQ on the average episode reward of executed test cases and on the number, rate, and enrichment (odds ratio) of failure episodes per Definition 2.6. 

RQ2 (Gap–severity relation) How does the magnitude of the Q-value gap relate to the severity of the exposed behavior? We sweep the selection quantile 𝑝 from 0 to 100 and measure the Spearman rank correlation between 𝑝 and the average episode reward of the selected test cases. 

RQ3 (Boundary conditions) Under which variations of the CMARL setting does DisQ’s advantage shrink or disappear? We vary the local-to-shared reward ratio (RQ3.1), the degree of initial-state cooperation required by the environment (RQ3.2), the training stage of the policies under test (RQ3.3), and the timing of Q-value divergence within episodes (RQ3.4), using the same reward and failure metrics as RQ1. 

RQ4 (Explainability) What information does the Q-value gap provide for explaining a selected test case and its outcome? We inspect the per-agent Q-values of gap states selected during testing and relate the predicted returns to the observed episode rewards. Before we answer each question individually, we introduce the used environments, trained CMARL policies, testing methods, and technical setup. 

4.1 Environments 

Multi-Agent Particle Environments (MPE) are a set of commonly used environments [19] for CMARL benchmarking where particle agents can move, see each other, and interact with fixed landmarks [26]. Simple Spread has 𝑁 agents, 𝑁 landmarks (default 𝑁 = 3). Agents must learn to cover all the landmarks while avoiding collisions. All agents are globally penalized (shared reward) based on how far the closest agent is to each landmark (the sum of the minimum distances). Locally, the agents are penalized if they collide with other agents (-1 for each collision). Simple Speaker Listener (Speaker Listener for short) has one agent that is the “speaker” and can speak but cannot move, while the other agent is the listener (cannot speak but must navigate to the correct landmark). The agents receive fully shared rewards. We refer to PettingZoo [26] for a detailed description of the environments. We further introduce two new environments: In Coin Collector , agents must cooperate to collect as many coins as possible in a grid world in a given amount of time. When a coin is collected, the agents get no penalty; otherwise, they get a shared penalty. Additionally, we introduce the Knight Wizard Zombies environment, which is a variant of the Knights Archers Zombies environment from PettingZoo [26]. We design this variant specifically to support the analysis of different cooperation levels in Section 5.3. This environment is a grid-based cooperative game where two agents (a knight and a wizard) work together to fend off three zombies on a grid. Each agent can move in four directions, with the knight able to attack zombies in the same position and the wizard capable of healing the knight at the cost of mana points. Zombies move randomly toward the far side of the grid and can attack the knight or wizard if they occupy the same position, with a 11 certain probability of success. The game concludes when the knight or wizard runs out of health points or if any zombie reaches the other side of the grid. Agents receive shared rewards for defeating zombies (which are then respawned) and for successful healing actions, promoting teamwork to survive the zombie onslaught. 

4.2 Trained CMARL Policies 

We trained centralized and decentralized CMARL agents using the deep Q-learning algorithm [8]. The centralized CMARL policies were trained for 100 million steps, while the decentralized CMARL policies were trained for 100,000 steps. This difference arises due to the distinct technical setups of the two CMARL algorithms. Each policy is represented by a neural network with three hidden layers, each containing 512 neurons. Across 1,000 episodes, the trained centralized policies achieve an average return of approximately −55, while the decentralized policies achieve an average return of approximately −32. As a robustness measure comparable across environments, we report each policy’s failure rate under uniformly random initial states (Definition 2.6 with the parametric threshold 

𝜃 = 𝜇 − 2𝜎 ). Over 25 ,000 sampled episodes per environment, the policies fail in 0 .0–4 .7% (centralized) and 0 .0–5 .6% (decentralized) of episodes. Coin Collector policies are the most robust ( ≤ 0.04%) and Speaker Listener policies the least. The trained agents are thus strong enough that failures are rare events worth searching for. For more details about the training process, we refer to the source code. 

4.3 Testing Methods 

We compare random SBST with and without DisQ and genetic SBST with and without DisQ. The random SBST randomly samples test cases, while the genetic SBST optimizes the CMARL agent positions to minimize the collected reward.

All statistical comparisons follow the guidelines of Arcuri and Briand [30] for assessing randomized algorithms. The unit of analysis is one repetition: each testing experiment is repeated 25 times, and each repetition contributes its own average reward, since episode rewards within a repetition are not independent samples of the testing method’s behavior. For the reward metric, we first apply a Kruskal–Wallis omnibus test across all methods per policy under test, followed by post-hoc two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests of each method against the Random Testing baseline; a comparison is only called significant if the omnibus test rejects as well. For the discrete failure counts, we compare each method against the baseline with Fisher’s exact test on the 2 × 2 table of failure versus non-failure episodes and report the odds ratio (OR) as effect size, where OR > 1 means the method’s executed episodes are more likely to be failures. Within each policy under test, the 𝑝 -values of each family of comparisons are adjusted for multiple comparisons with the Holm–Bonferroni procedure. 5.3.5 RQ3: Joint Answer 

DisQ’s advantage is bounded along all four axes: (1) It shrinks as the local reward ratio grows and vanishes in practical terms above a ratio of 0 .9 (RQ3.1); (2) it disappears entirely, without degrading performance relative to the baseline, in environments where initial-state 25 coordination has little influence on the episode outcome, with reward changes of at most 0.3% in Knight Wizard Zombies, where the initial agent positions account for only 0 .4% of the reward variance (RQ3.2); (3) it requires converged value estimates: for decentralized policies trained for fewer than 8 ,000 steps, vanilla Genetic Testing detects lower rewards than its DisQ variant, and for intermediately trained centralized policies Random DisQ even selects fewer failures than random sampling (OR down to 0 .53), whereas for the fully trained policies DisQ again enriches failures significantly (OR = 1.58, 𝑝 < 0.001) (RQ3.3); and (4) its selection signal is bounded by when and whether a gap occurs at all: in Knight Wizard Zombies, 63–85% of failures never exhibit a gap, explaining the null result of RQ3.2 at the signal level, while in decentralized Simple Spread (2) the gap accompanies 82% of failures but is present at 𝑠 0 in only 22%. During execution, threshold exceedance is failure-predictive only for decentralized policies (RQ3.4).

# 6 Threats to Validity 

A first threat to validity is that the study focuses on certain environments (i.e., Multi-Agent Particle environments, Coin Collector, and the Knight Wizard Zombies game). While we select a variety of different environments, these environments might not fully represent the diversity of all CMARL applications. A second threat is that implementation bugs of the DisQ method could introduce errors in the results, even though the DisQ logic is straightforward. A third threat concerns the operationalization of failures. Our discrete failure metric instantiates Definition 2.6 with a fixed reward threshold, the 5th percentile of the baseline reward distribution per policy under test. Unsafe events enter this criterion only through their reward penalties: failures whose safety impact is not (or is only weakly) penalized by the reward function would not be counted, which is a general limitation of reward-based oracles rather than of DisQ specifically. A different percentile would change the absolute failure counts, but the reported odds ratios measure the enrichment of failure episodes relative to the same threshold applied to the baseline and are therefore comparable across methods and environments. The more consequential limitation is which class of failure the metric captures: as noted after Definition 2.7, a reward threshold cannot separate a coordination failure from one in which a single agent acts poorly, so our claims are about reward-threshold failures rather than coordination failures specifically. The two are nonetheless closely associated empirically. We compared the reward-based label against threshold-free coordination events in two environments: a collision between agents in Simple Spread (2), and the listener ending nearest a landmark other than the speaker’s goal in Speaker Listener. Over 25 ,000 uniformly sampled episodes per configuration, collisions occur in 22 .1% of failure episodes against 15 .9% of non-failure episodes under centralized training, and in 47 .6% against 21 .7% under decentralized training. The corresponding rates for the wrong landmark are 98 .6% against 64 .5% and 99 .2% against 64 .4%. The wrong-landmark event is frequent even among non-failure episodes, so its base rate is high and its enrichment correspondingly modest. Reward-threshold failures are thus consistently enriched in observable coordination events, by factors of 1 .4 to 2 .2 across all four comparisons, without the label isolating 27 them. Beyond this, the metric does not distinguish qualitatively different failure types, so we cannot assess the diversity of the exposed failures. A fourth threat concerns the genetic baseline rather than DisQ. Its individuals encode only the initial agent positions, while the rest of the initial state is re-drawn on every reset, so each fitness value is a single-sample estimate. With those positions held fixed and the remainder re-drawn, they account for 7 .0% of the episode-reward variance in Simple Spread (2), 3–4% in Coin Collector and 0 .4% in Knight Wizard Zombies; where the share is small the search is driven largely by sampling noise, which is the most plausible explanation for the genetic results in Coin Collector (Section 5). Encoding the remaining elements of the initial state would give the search more to act on, so the reported genetic results are a lower bound on what genetic SBST could achieve in these environments. Finally, the performance of all the testing methods may also be sensitive to the specific hyperparameters used for training the agents, which were manually tuned in this study. A different set of hyperparameters might yield different outcomes, potentially leading to different conclusions about the method’s effectiveness. Because DisQ operates directly on learned Q-values, its behavior depends on training choices that shape those values. The discount factor 𝛾 compresses or stretches the scale of Q-values and therefore of 

𝐷 (𝑠 ). However, since 𝜆 is calibrated as an empirical quantile of 𝐷 (𝑠 ) for the specific policies under test, a global rescaling of Q-values leaves the selection invariant. Only the interpretability of the absolute gap magnitude changes. Non-uniform effects (e.g., 𝛾 

interacting differently with each agent’s horizon, or differing reward normalizations per agent) are not covered by this argument and could affect DisQ. RQ3.3 covers one such axis (training duration), while a systematic sweep over training hyperparameters remains future work. To mitigate threats to conclusion validity, all comparisons are performed on independent repetitions rather than pooled episodes, paired with effect sizes, and corrected for multiple comparisons (Section 4.5). 

# 7 Related Work 

7.1 Explainable CMARL 

Boggess et al. summarize policies to explain agent cooperation by abstracting the environment and policies, and provide language explanations to answer queries about agent behavior such as “When do [agents] do [actions]?”, “Why don’t [agents] do [actions] in [states]?” and “What do [agents] do in [predicates]?” [32]. In contrast, our method identifies potential miscoordination by analyzing the Q-value gap for specific states. Heuillet et al. present global model-agnostic quantitative explanations about the individual contribution of agents in a cooperative multi-agent RL environment [33]. Li et al. [34] and Chen and Tan [35] follow a similar approach. In contrast, our method offers a localized, fine-grained perspective by pinpointing state-specific inconsistencies in agent coordination rather than providing an overarching quantitative assessment. Boggess et al. [36] present an approach for generating policy-level contrastive expla-nations for MARL to answer a temporal user query, which specifies a sequence of tasks completed by agents with possible cooperation. The proposed approach encodes the temporal query as a PCTL logic formula and checks if the query is feasible under a given MARL policy via probabilistic model checking. Gross et al. [9] take a similar approach. 28 In contrast, our method does not rely on formal logic encoding. Orner et al. present a method called SICA [37]. SICA helps explain how and why agents might cooperate or compete in a given situation by analyzing over multiple simulations the correlation of their reward outcomes when they follow their own policies. It provides a high-level view of the relationships between agents, offering insights into their mutual “friendliness” or “animosity”, which is valuable for understanding and designing multi-agent systems. Instead of evaluating long-term reward correlations, our method detects localized inconsistencies in agent coordination at the decision-making level. Ho and Wang present an explainable and adaptable augmented knowledge attention network architecture for multi-agent DRL systems [38]. In contrast to our work, they investigate how the policies interpret the inputs, while we focus on investigating the output of the neural network (NN) policies. A well-known approach is action advising, where a teacher provides advice as state-action pairs to guide the student. While successful in simple environments, action advising often struggles in complex environments with large state spaces. To address this, Guo et al. propose an algorithm where the teacher provides both action advice and a path explanation, showing that incorporating these explanations of an internal decision tree policy enables learning through reflection [39]. In comparison, we use our explainable CMARL metric for testing policies instead of training them. Trajectory prediction is the task of predicting an agent’s future states, which can be seen as a kind of explanation. Wu et al. [40] propose the DTDE MARL algorithm for joint trajectory and intent prediction for autonomous vehicles in dense and heterogeneous envi-ronments. Their algorithm is fully decentralized without weight sharing, communication, or centralized critics, and can handle variable agents across episodes. While they predict intents, we predict failures. Lazy agent pathology occurs when agents receive incorrect credit assignments [41], leading them to learn suboptimal policies and not cooperate towards the team’s common goal. Detecting lazy agents can be viewed as a form of explainability. Pina et al. introduced Independent Causal Learning (ICL) and demonstrated that detecting causal relations between individual observations and rewards in MARL can detect and address this problem [42]. While lazy agents can be seen as an explainable CMARL approach in the context of failures, we focus on the discrepancy of future predictions of the individual policies to each other. 

7.2 Single- and Multi-agent RL Testing 

Single-agent RL policy testing, the closest neighbor of CMARL testing, has received extensive attention. DeepTest [43] is an automated testing framework for NNs in autonomous driving, utilizing metamorphic testing. It specifically targets models that process image inputs. Similarly, DeepXplore tests image-based NN agents but uses differential testing to identify errors [44]. In DeepXplore, multiple policies are provided, and failures are detected by comparing their outputs. 

MDPFuzz is a black box fuzz testing framework for policies tackling MDPs, testing if the target policy reaches abnormal or dangerous states [14, 45]. It features a cover model of the state sequences observed during testing to guide the fuzzing process toward unseen behaviors. Similarly to MDPFuzz , Eniser et al. [17] and Eisenhut et al. [46] employ 29 fuzzing techniques to generate test inputs, but the results are subsequently tested using metamorphic testing. Recently, Mazouni et al. [13] and Bhatt et al. [47] explore the use of Quality-Diversity optimization [48] to search for failures. On the other hand, RL itself has been used to optimize the search. For instance, DeepCollision trains an RL agent to modify the environment during the execution of the test cases to detect failures [49]. Likewise, Haq et al. [50] propose MORLOT, a framework for handling many-objective policy testing tasks. Their approach consists of training a deep RL agent [8] to find failure-triggering configurations of the environment. Other types of SBST approaches include genetic algorithm [51], depth-first search [52], and test oracle approximation through AI planning heuristics [53]. We refer the interested reader to the survey by Mazouni et al. [54] for a broader review of policy testing methods. While we do not focus on adversarial testing, adversarial testing has also been extensively applied to RL policy testing. The first line of research aims to reduce the number of times adversaries are injected (while achieving similar results to systemic attacks). For instance, Kos and Song [55] refine FGSM (Fast Gradient Sign Method) by only attacking critical states during the episodes, which they select based on the value function of the policy. On the other hand, Lin et al. [56] identify when to attack the policy by targeting states that maximize the difference between the most preferred action and the least preferred action. Sun et al. [57] opt for another strategy for planning the attacks and emphasize long-term damage impacts. They propose to achieve this goal by learning an antagonist model, which they train with the reward function to account for the agent’s end goal. Another line of work aims at the adversaries themselves. For instance, Chan et al. [58] quantify how much changing each feature composing the observations affects the rewards with a static reward impact map to only perturb the most impactful features. Similar to testing, prior work also formally verifies adversarial attacks on RL policies in both single-agent [59] and multi-agent settings [9]. 

COOL-MC enables training single-agent and multi-agent RL policies in modeled environments while formally verifying them at any stage of the training process [60]. 

MoGym is an integrated toolbox that trains and verifies RL agents using deep statistical model checking [61]. Mungojerrie is a tool used for testing reward functions, specifically for 

𝜔 -regular objectives [62]. Gu et al. introduce MCRL, merging verification with MARL for mission planning to ensure safety-critical requirements [63]. The main difference is that we focus on testing instead of verifying CMARL policies. Gimitest is a tool for testing single-and multi-agent RL policies in Farama Gymnasium, PettingZoo, and similar framework environments. It streamlines testing by extending the environment simulators and supports customizable test cases and configurations [29]. We use Gimitest to implement our testing and test case selection methods. Zhou et al. present a MARL testing method that tests if the trained agents are robust against noise state observations [16] by presenting a method called RTCA. RTCA introduces two key innovations: 1) a Differential Evolution (DE) method to select critical agents as noise victims and determine worst-case joint actions; 2) a team cooperation policy evaluation used as the DE optimization objective. Adversarial state perturbations are then applied to the critical agents based on these joint actions. In contrast, our method focuses on identifying miscoordination by analyzing Q-value gaps in specific and clean states. Ma and Li [64], Liu and Lai [65], Figura et al. [66], and Lv et al. [67] study adversarial testing in multi-agent settings. The main difference is, as previously mentioned, that we focus on 30 safety instead of security violations. Recently, Ma et al. [19] introduced a multi-agent system testing method, including multi-agent RL, that focuses on finding diverse test cases. To this end, multiple diversity measures are combined, e.g., individual and team diversity of the agents, failure degrees, and frequencies, to build a diverse set of test cases along the several diversity dimensions. New test cases are generated and identified through action perturbations and the abstract representation of states and trajectories before the diversity measures are calculated. While shown to be very effective in several case studies, the method is much more involved and complex than our proposed DisQ method. For example, DisQ’s selection only relies on the initial state and the agents’ corresponding Q-values, but does not require the actual execution of the episode from this state including a post-episode analysis of the trajectories. These two methods are, therefore, substantially different in their approach and focus. 

7.3 Combining Testing and Explainability 

The final, closely related category is a combination of testing and explainable RL methods. Biagiola et al. [15] propose an SBST approach to test agents. Their approach, implemented in a tool called Indago, trains a classifier on failure and non-failure environment (i.e., pass) configurations resulting from the DRL training process. The classifier is used during testing as a surrogate model for DRL agent execution in the environment, predicting the likelihood that a given environment configuration will induce a failure in the DRL agent under test. This failure prediction serves as a fitness function, guiding the generation towards failure-prone configurations while saving computation time by limiting DRL agent execution to those configurations most likely to expose failures. They use explainable methods to steer the evolutionary testing algorithm. On the other hand, we focus on CMARL and consider the dynamics of the CMARL policies. In the context of single-agent explainable and verified RL, existing work iteratively prunes NN policies to interpret the feature importance for safety at a global level [68]. Some work leverages large language models to identify safety-critical states and apply counterfactual reasoning to explain why the RL policy violated a safety property while also proposing alternative actions [69]. Existing work uses model checking to query multi-agent RL agent behaviors and uses this extracted behavior explicitly as an explanation [36]. 

# 8 Conclusion 

This paper introduces a test case selection method called DisQ (Disagreeing Q-values), which aims to improve failure detection in parallel CMARL systems. The method identifies Q-value gaps: states in which the agents’ maximal Q-values differ by more than a calibrated threshold. These gaps indicate that the agents are not aligned in their decision-making, suggesting potential miscoordination or failure behavior throughout the episode, and we identify the conditions under which the signal is absent. The main benefit of DisQ is that it is straightforward to apply because it relies on Q-values for an initial state, which are already calculated in many CMARL systems. It also integrates with most existing test case generators, such as random search or genetic SBST, without major changes to the testing process. By focusing on likely failure cases, it reduces unnecessary test executions and improves testing efficiency. 31 For future work, we plan to investigate the application of DisQ to policy gradient methods, which do not rely on Q-values but action probabilities [22], perhaps by measuring the divergence between agents’ action probability distributions, or, for MADDPG-style architectures, between the agents’ critics evaluated at the joint greedy action. We will consider how DisQ can be applied to turn-based cooperative MARL systems [70] or how to make use of online monitoring of the Q-value gap. To assess the practical utility of DisQ, we aim to apply it to real-world, industrial CMARL settings, such as autonomous vehicle coordination, distributed robotics, and collaborative scheduling systems. Finally, we see an opportunity to investigate alternative explainable CMARL methods to improve the testing method efficiency.
