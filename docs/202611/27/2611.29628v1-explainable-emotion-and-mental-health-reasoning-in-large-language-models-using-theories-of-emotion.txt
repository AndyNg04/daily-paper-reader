# Abstract 

Advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have enabled a wide range of applications. However, their ability to simulate human-like behavior and reasoning remains unclear. While emotion detection is a better-established Natural Language Processing (NLP) task, existing approaches primarily focus on directly classifying text into predefined emotion classes, often overlooking the appraisal process that explains why an individual experiences a particular emo-tion in a given situation. In this work, we propose Psychology-based Chain-of-Thought (PsyCoT) reasoning, a theory-driven prompting strategy grounded in the Schachter–Singer and Cognitive Appraisal theories of emotion. We propose and evaluate PsyCoT for subjective NLP tasks, including emotion analysis, emotion-intensity prediction, stress analysis, and depression-severity identification. Our evaluation covers four levels of classification complexity: binary, multiclass, multi-label, and ordinal scaling, spanning 11 languages. We assess the performance of five state-of-the-art LLMs across these selected subjective NLP tasks. Experimental results demonstrate that PsyCoT prompt variants improve the standard chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting across the evaluated tasks. Specifically, in multi-label emotion classification, PsyCoT yields F1 score gains ranging from 0.5% to 28.1%, depending on the model and language configuration. For multi-label emotion intensity prediction, the Schachter-Singer Two-Factor Theory (2FT) PsyCoT variant shows a clear advantage over both standard CoT and Cognitive Appraisal Theory (CAT) prompting, achieving overall improvements across models with gains up to +4.8%. For stress prediction, the 2FT PsyCoT variant consistently outperforms standard CoT and CAT prompting across evaluated models, achieving substantial F1 score gains (up to +14.6%, such as Gemma-3-27B rising to 76.5% F1 compared to 63.1% for CoT). Conversely, for depression severity, the CAT variant tends to show a stronger advantage, yielding the highest F1 scores across most models. These findings highlight the effectiveness of the proposed psychology-grounded reasoning in improving both explainability and performance in emotion and mental-health NLP tasks. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated significant progress in the evaluation of downstream Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks, enabling human-like language under-standing and text generation across diverse domains, including education [1, 2], healthcare applications [3], and mental health support [4]. In parallel, prompt engineering techniques have become essential for maximizing LLMs’ capabilities [5, 6]. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning has shown strong performance gains in tasks requiring multi-step inference, particularly in November 24, 2026 1/20 arXiv:2611.29628v1 [cs.CL] 22 Nov 2026 mathematical, symbolic, and commonsense reasoning [7, 8]. These developments position LLMs as promising tools for tackling complex NLP tasks. One such complex and subjective area of NLP is affective computing tasks [9]. The affective computing field has progressed beyond coarse-grained sentiment analysis (positive/negative) toward fine-grained emotion recognition [10], emotion intensity estimation [11], and mental health status assessment [12]. The current open-weight and closed-source LLM families, such as OpenAI’s GPT, Google’s Gemma and Gemini, Meta’s LLaMA, etc., have shown encouraging contextual learning (zero-shot and few-shot performance) on these subjective NLP tasks, sug-gesting that they internalize rich and affective knowledge during pretraining [13]. As a result, the research has increasingly explored prompt-based and instruction-tuned approaches for emotion analysis and mental health–related inferences [14]. However, as LLMs are deployed in high-stakes affective applications, such as detecting depressive symptoms [15], psychological distress [16], or suicide risk [17], the emphasis shifts from classification accuracy alone to the explainability of the reasoning behind the decision. In this setting, it is insufficient for a model to label a text as sad, stressed, or anxious; it should also provide a rationale for these labels that is coherent, transparent, and psychologically meaningful. Reasoning that lacks grounding in human emotion processes risks being misleading, uninformative, or clinically unusable [18]. From a cognitive perspective, subjective language refers to the speaker’s or writer’s internal states, such as emotions, attitudes, beliefs, or intentions. Cognitive and psychological studies of language indicate that understanding subjectivity often requires a theory of mind (i.e., inferring the speaker’s intent or feelings) and empathy. For instance, sarcasm and irony contents are typical subjective uses of language: the literal content diverges from the intended meaning, and the listener must infer the speaker’s true attitude [13]. To address the interpretability of subjective NLP tasks, most existing approaches rely on Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting [7], which encourages models to produce intermediate reasoning steps. While CoT is effective for mathematical and symbolic reasoning tasks governed by formal logic, it has a critical limitation when applied to psychology-oriented NLP tasks. This is because human emotion is not the result of linear logical deduction; rather, it emerges from complex interactions among situational stimuli, cognitive interpretation, and physiological mental states [19]. Consequently, the generic CoT principle of “think step-by-step” reasoning often yields explanations that are linguistically plausible but psychologically shallow. This raises a fundamental question: What does “think step-by-step” actually mean in the context of human emotion and mental health state analysis? . This is because emotion analysis is inherently subjective (varying across individuals, cultures, languages, and situations) and context-dependent [20]. Psychological research emphasizes that emotions are processes (not labels) shaped by appraisal, arousal, and interpretation [21]. The standard CoT prompting largely ignores this psychological grounding, treating affective inference as a generic reasoning problem. As a result, LLMs may simulate emotional outcomes without modeling the mechanisms that generate them. Human cognitive processes have been studied in numerous psychological theories of emotion. Schachter–Singer Two-Factor Theory (2FT) [22] and Cognitive Appraisal Theory (CAT) are among the most common and well-known emotion theories [23]. The 2FT theory posits that emotion arises from the interaction between physiological arousal and cognitive labeling of that arousal. Conversely, CAT argues that emotions are elicited by an individual’s evaluation of events in relation to personal goals, beliefs, and well-being [23]. Achieving meaningful explainability in affective computing requires that LLM reasoning be grounded in psychological theory rather than generic logical decomposition [24]. This shift moves from prompting LLMs to “think like logicians” toward prompting them to “process emotions like humans.” To this end, we propose a theory-driven prompting framework that operationalizes two established psychological theories of emotion into structured prompting schemas for affective computing NLP tasks: the Schachter–Singer Two-Factor Theory (2FT) ,which characterizes emotion as a combination of physiological arousal and cognitive labeling November 24, 2026 2/20 [22], and the Cognitive Appraisal Theory (CAT) , which posits that emotions arise from individuals’ evaluations of events in relation to their goals and well-being [23]. These theories suggest that understanding why an emotion occurs requires analyzing an-tecedent events, appraisal dimensions, and arousal-related components—elements that are not explicitly represented in standard CoT prompting. The proposed theory-based prompts guide LLMs to (1) identify situational antecedents, (2) perform cognitive appraisals, (3) infer physio-logical or arousal-related indicators, and (4) integrate these components into coherent inferences about emotion category, emotional intensity, and mental health status (e.g., stress and depression severity). This work tends to answer the following Research questions (RQs): • RQ1: Can LLMs mimic human psychological reasoning in assessing and explaining emotional and mental health states? • RQ2: How does psychologically grounded reasoning improve LLMs’ emotion understand-ing compared to chain-of-thought (COT) reasoning? • RQ3: How does the emotional intelligence of LLMs vary across different prompt variants, model architectures, parameter sizes, languages, and affective computing NLP tasks? In summary, this work introduces a Psychology-based Chain-of-Thought (PsyCoT) prompting strategy grounded in the Schachter–Singer and Cognitive Appraisal theories of emotion. PsyCoT embodies a shift from prompting LLMs to “think like logicians” toward prompting them to “pro-cess emotions like humans.” By embedding established theories of emotion into LLM prompting, we aim to bridge the gap between high-performing affective inference and psychologically grounded explainability, thereby enhancing the reliability and practical applicability of LLMs in emotion and mental health analysis. The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on prompting strategies for emotion and health-related NLP tasks. Section 3 presents the theoretical foundations of the proposed psychology-based Chain-of-Thought (PsyCoT) framework for modeling emotion. Section 4 describes the evaluation tasks and dataset details. Section 5 outlines the experimental setup, including prompts, selected large language models (LLMs), and evaluation parameters. Section 6 reports the findings, along with result analysis and discussion, and the paper concludes in Section 7. 

# 2 Related works 

Advancing the performance of LLMs for subjective NLP tasks has various applications, from personalizing content or user recommendations in social networks [25] to empathetic dialogue systems [26]. In subjective NLP tasks, no single correct answer may exist; instead, better reasoning is a key milestone toward modeling artificial general intelligence (AGI) [27]. Several approaches have been developed to enhance LLMs’ emotional capabilities, including in-context learning [28], language model fine-tuning methods [29], and prompting approaches [30]. How-ever, challenges remain, including overreliance on annotated data, difficulty handling complex emotions, and the difficulty of explaining LLMs’ decision-making in emotion cognition. More-over, processing human emotions requires not only computational methods and techniques but also a deep understanding of how humans perceive emotion (psychological theories) [31]. In the past decade, the explainability of deep learning approaches has advanced rapidly, ranging from explainable deep learning frameworks for prediction, such as DeepHINT [32], to methods for interpreting generative language models, such as Captum [33]. At the same time, general-purpose language models have demonstrated strong performance across a wide range of tasks, including simulating human-like behaviors such as emotional intelligence. As users interact with generative models via prompts or instructions, various prompting strategies have been proposed for evaluating downstream NLP tasks. In this related work section, we November 24, 2026 3/20 discuss the most common prompting approaches for assessing LLMs’ understanding of affective phenomena. 

## 2.1 Standard (regular) prompt 

Standard prompting is a simple approach in which a language model is given a direct question, with or without context examples of input–output pairs before generating a predicted answer [34]. In this setting, the model directly produces an answer, without step-by-step processing of the given tasks. For example, in a multi-label emotion identification task, the prompt could look like 

“Categorize the text’s emotional tone as the presence of none, one or more of the given emotions 

{emotion classes list here ... ”} [35]. However, the standard prompting approach is outperformed by the “think step-by-step” chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting strategies in multiple downstream NLP tasks [7]. 

## 2.2 Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompt 

To leverage LLMs’ reasoning capabilities, [7] proposed Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, which appends multiple reasoning steps before answering the input question. With this simple prompting strategy, LLMs perform substantially better on complex problems and reasoning tasks such as mathematical problems [36] and commonsense reasoning [37]. The CoT prompting approach is commonly appended with “think step-by-step” in standard prompting strategies. However, CoT does not consistently perform well across all NLP tasks, and performance varies with downstream evaluation tasks [38]. In this case, cognitive science prompting (emotional prompt) approaches aim to reduce specific failure modes in the processing of subjective tasks, such as by adding a short emotional stimulus to standard and/or CoT prompts [39]. 

## 2.3 Emotional prompts 

The work [39] shows that LLMs exhibit emotional intelligence, and the performance can be improved by combining standard prompts with emotional stimuli. Emotional stimuli are psychological phrases that come after the standard or CoT prompts, such as “This is very important to my career.” , “Are you sure?” , and other related phrases/sentences. The work [40] proposed Emotional Chain-of-Thought (ECoT), which inserts three manually crafted reasoning steps into the conversation to understand emotions before producing an answer. These steps typically include: (1) identifying the listener’s emotions and explaining why, (2) identifying the speaker’s emotions and explaining why, and (3) formulating an empathetic reply to the listener and other involved parties. ECoT prompts yield promising results for emotion recognition compared to standard and CoT prompting approaches. However, these steps are handcrafted and task-dependent, and LLMs may misinterpret emotional cues in the prompts, leading to inaccurate or inappropriate responses. Chain of Empathy (CoE) prompting [41] uses insights from psychotherapy to prompt LLMs to reason about human emotional states. The common reasoning steps involved in CoE are: (1) Iden-tify any word that represents the client’s emotion, and (2) Understand the individual/situational factors that may have led to the expression in the client’s message. It aims to understand and respond to human emotions by mimicking therapeutic reasoning, using frameworks from psy-chotherapy such as Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) [42], Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) [43], Person-Centered Therapy (PCT) [44], and Reality Therapy (RT) [45] to analyze a user’s feelings. These psychotherapy frameworks address underlying causes and context, and lead to more nuanced, relevant, and genuinely empathetic interactions [46]. # 3 Theoretical frameworks of emotion 

Emotions are fundamental to human survival and behavior, driving our actions and experiences and giving life meaning, which underscores the importance of understanding them [52, 53]. Although emotion detection is a better-established NLP task [10, 11, 35], it mostly involves classifying text directly into emotion labels, skipping the appraisal step necessary to interpret why an individual experiences an emotion in a particular event. The following are two well-known theories of emotion that explain how humans perceive emotions. 

## 3.1 Schachter-Singer (two-factor) theory 

The Schachter-Singer Two-Factor Theory (2FT) was developed by [22]. It proposes that emotion arises from the combination of physiological arousal and a cognitive evaluation of bodily responses. According to this theory, when an event causes physiological arousal, we interpret or label that arousal in light of the situation, and this interpretation leads to the emotion. The two factors are 1) Arousal - the physical reaction to a stimulus (e.g., sweating, rapid breathing, or a racing heart) and 2) Cognitive label - identifying a reason for the arousal or cognitive interpretation of this arousal (e.g., “I am scared because there is a snake”). For example, when a person encounters a snake, their body exhibits physiological arousal, such as an increased heart rate. This arousal is then cognitively interpreted as fear because the situation is perceived as dangerous. According to this theory, emotion arises from the November 24, 2026 5/20 combination of physiological arousal and its cognitive interpretation. The theory further suggests that similar physiological responses can lead to different emotions depending on the context. For instance, a racing heart and sweaty palms during an exam may be interpreted as anxiety, whereas the same responses on a date may be perceived as affection, attraction, or excitement, as illustrated in Fig. 1. 

## 3.2 Cognitive appraisal theory 

The Cognitive Appraisal Theory (CAT), pioneered by [23], proposes that cognitive evaluation precedes emotional experience. In this view of the theory, a person first encounters a stimulus, then interprets it, and simultaneously experiences both the physiological response and the emotion. Cognitive appraisal typically involves two stages. In the primary appraisal, individuals evaluate the significance of an event for their well-being (e.g., “Is the snake dangerous?”). In the secondary appraisal, they assess their ability to cope with the event (e.g., “Can I escape the snake?”). According to cognitive appraisal theories, the basic premise is that events themselves do not directly cause emotions and behaviors; rather, they do so through how those events are perceived or appraised [23]. Consequently, different interpretations of the same eliciting event can lead different people to experience different emotions. Cognitive Appraisal Theory helps explain why people can react differently to the same situation [54]. For example, encountering a snake may be immediately appraised as a serious threat, triggering fear and eliciting the fight-or-flight response, as shown in Fig 1. Both Schachter–Singer and Cognitive Appraisal theories rely on interpreting or labeling internal states. However, in the Schachter–Singer theory, emotion arises from the simultaneous interaction of physiological arousal and this interpretive process. In contrast, Cognitive Appraisal theory proposes that individuals first evaluate and label the stimulus, after which physiological and emotional responses follow together based on that appraisal. Inspired by prominent emotion theories, we proposed Psychology-based Chain-of-Thought (PsyCoT) prompts grounded in the Schachter–Singer Theory and the Cognitive Appraisal Theory to improve LLMs’ reasoning for subjective NLP tasks. Unlike approaches that aim to directly simulate human emotional experi-ences, PsyCoT employs established psychological theories as structured reasoning guidelines to encourage LLMs to consider contextual antecedents, physiological arousal, and cognitive appraisals when predicting emotional states. The resulting explanations are therefore intended to provide theory-informed rationales for model predictions, rather than validated representations of human psychological processes or the models’ internal reasoning mechanisms.

## 4.3 Mental health analysis 

Mental health conditions such as depression and suicidal ideation pose serious challenges to global health care [59]. Our experiment is based on two well-established, human-labeled datasets commonly used for mental health analysis. 

Stress prediction: Stress is a human experience, particularly in online environments. We select the Dreaddit dataset 3, a dataset of posts collected from Reddit across five domains (abuse, social, anxiety, stress, and financial) [56]. Multiple human annotators rated whether sentence segments exhibited stress or non-stress in the post, and the annotations were aggregated to produce final labels. We used this dataset to perform post-level binary stress prediction with state-of-the-art LLMs. 

Depression severity: Advances in NLP systems, particularly large language models (LLMs), have played an important role in analyzing user-generated content on social media to identify risk indicators in online behavior [60]. We use the Depression Severity ( DepSeverity ) dataset 4, which reflects the ordinal nature of depression severity levels and is grounded in clinical depression standards applied to Reddit posts [57]. The posts are categorized into four levels of depression severity—minimal, mild, moderate, and severe—representing increasing intensity. For the binary stress classification task, the input is a text instance T . The objective is to determine whether the text expresses stress by assigning a single label from the set S stress, not stress . In the depression severity classification task, the goal is to assign a single label from an ordinal set S minimal, mild, moderate, severe , representing increasing levels of depression severity expressed in the text.

## 5.1 Prompt variants 

We evaluate state-of-the-art LLMs using three prompting strategies: CoT as a baseline and two proposed Psychology-based Chain-of-Thought (PsyCoT) prompts. The prompt variants used in this work are shown in Fig 2. 

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompt: Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting [7] achieves state-of-the-art results from both open-weight and proprietary LLMs in multidisciplinary downstream NLP tasks such as emotion analysis [11]. We use CoT prompting as our baseline and compare it to our proposed PsyCoT prompts. 

Schachter-Singer theory-based prompt (2FT): Based on the Two-Factor Theory of emotion [22], this prompt instructs the LLM to decouple emotion detection into two distinct analytical steps: (1) estimating “physiological arousal” (intensity/energy) from linguistic markers such as punctuation, capitalization, and modifiers, and (2) applying a cognitive label to this arousal based on the situational context. By explicitly isolating the arousal factor, this prompt aims to prevent the model from conflating an event’s semantic severity with the emotional intensity expressed in the text, thereby improving the precision of intensity regression tasks. 

Cognitive appraisal theory-based prompt (CAT): This prompt directs the LLM to simulate the cognitive evaluation process that precedes an emotional response. Instead of relying solely on semantic associations, the model is instructed to analyze the text across specific appraisal dimensions, including goal relevance, goal conduciveness, agency, and coping potential, before assigning a label [23]. This structured reasoning framework is designed to enhance multi-label classification by enabling the model to distinguish between emotions that share similar valence but differ in their causal structure (e.g., distinguishing Anger from Fear based on the presence of control).

# 6 Results analysis and discussion 

Multi-label emotion analysis: The multi-label emotion analysis results are presented in Table 2. As the results show, Cognitive Appraisal Theory-based (CAT) prompting outperforms the baseline CoT and the Schachter-Singer theory-based (2FT) prompt. The superior performance of Cognitive Appraisal-based prompting in multi-label emotion classification can be driven by its ability to resolve emotional ambiguity through contextual evaluation. Unlike general CoT reasoning, which relies on broad semantic logic, Cognitive Appraisal theory provides a structured decision matrix that assesses specific dimensions such as control, agency, and goal conduciveness. This framework enables the model to distinguish between emotions that share similar negative sentiments but differ in their causal structures (e.g., Anger and Fear, based on the presence of a “control” thinking step).By forcing the model to evaluate the “cognitive evaluation” of the event rather than just the feeling, cognitive appraisal prompting can effectively create distinct decision boundaries between the overlapping emotion labels. A step-by-step thinking example from the Cognitive Appraisal theory prompt is shown in Fig 3. Among open-weight LLMs, Gemma-3-27B performs better than other evaluated larger LLMs in terms of parameter count, such as Mistral-large-123B and Llama-3-70B. This efficiency may be attributed to Gemma’s large (256k-token) vocabulary and greater multilingualism, which enable richer linguistic representation across diverse languages [61]. 

Emotion intensity prediction The emotion intensity prediction results are presented in Ta-ble 3. In this work, we proposed Psychology-based Chain-of-Thought (PsyCoT), a theory-driven prompting strategy grounded in Schachter–Singer Two-Factor Theory and Cognitive Appraisal Theory of emotion. We evaluated the proposed framework across four downstream subjective NLP tasks: multi-label emotion classification, emotion intensity prediction, stress prediction, and depression severity classification, covering 11 languages. PsyCoT aims to advance emotion understanding in language models through three key capabilities. First, it facilitates the identi-fication of implicit emotions by uncovering emotional states that are not explicitly expressed through emotion-related keywords. Second, it encourages psychologically informed emotion reasoning by guiding models to consider situational antecedents, physiological arousal, and cognitive appraisals before assigning an emotion label. Third, it generates theory-informed textual rationales that explicitly articulate relevant psychological constructs and appraisal factors associated with the predicted emotions. Experimental results demonstrate that PsyCoT variants offer performance advantages over standard Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, although their effectiveness varies across tasks, models, and languages. In multi-label emotion classification, PsyCoT achieves performance gains of up to 28.1%, highlighting the benefits of incorporating psychological appraisals into emotion recognition. For multi-label emotion intensity prediction, the Schachter–Singer-based (2FT) variant demonstrates a clearer advantage over both standard CoT and CAT prompting, with improvements of up to 4.8%. A similar advantage is observed in stress prediction, where 2FT achieves F1-score gains of up to 14.6% over standard CoT. In contrast, Cognitive Appraisal-based (CAT) prompting is more effective for depression severity classification, achieving the highest F1 scores across most evaluated models. These findings reveal a task-dependent distinction in the contributions of psychological theories to LLM reasoning: cognitive appraisal provides a useful foundation for interpreting contextual and evaluative aspects of emotional experiences, whereas the Schachter–Singer perspective offers advantages in tasks involving emotional intensity and stress-related responses. Furthermore, our evaluation highlights the importance of model selection in multilingual subjective NLP. In particular, Gemma-3-27B demonstrates competitive performance compared with substantially larger models, including Llama-3-70B and Mistral-123B, suggesting that model size alone does not determine effectiveness in psychologically grounded reasoning tasks. Overall, by bridging established psychological theories with computational linguistics, Psy-CoT moves beyond surface-level emotion classification toward more theory-informed and interpretable emotion reasoning. Our findings suggest that incorporating complementary psy-chological perspectives into prompting strategies can improve the performance of LLMs across diverse emotion and mental-health NLP tasks, while providing a foundation for more transparent and psychologically informed affective computing systems. 

# Limitations 

This work is not without limitations. Although the proposed psychology-grounded reasoning approach (PsyCoT) achieves promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our evaluation primarily relies on automatic performance metrics, such as F1 score and accuracy, and does not include formal statistical significance testing. Future work should incorporate appropriate significance tests to determine whether the observed improvements are statistically reliable. Second, PsyCoT is currently grounded in two well-established theories of emotion: the Schachter-Singer theory and Cognitive Appraisal theory. Although these theories provide complementary perspectives on emotional processes, they do not capture the full range of psychological theories of emotion. Future research could investigate whether incorporating additional emotion theories leads to further improvements in subjective NLP tasks. November 24, 2026 14/20 Third, we evaluate PsyCoT across four subjective NLP tasks and a limited set of datasets. Extending the evaluation to a broader range of subjective NLP tasks, larger and more diverse datasets, and additional languages would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the approach. In particular, future studies should examine PsyCoT’s effectiveness with larger sample sizes to assess its explainability and generalizability. Fourth, although PsyCoT generates psychologically structured explanations, an important distinction should be made between theory-informed explanation generation and validated explainability. By explicitly incorporating psychological constructs from Schachter–Singer Two-Factor Theory and Cognitive Appraisal Theory, PsyCoT encourages LLMs to articulate relevant contextual and appraisal-related factors underlying their predictions. This provides a more structured and theoretically grounded account of the factors considered during prediction. However, psychological experts have not independently evaluated the generated rationales to establish their psychological validity, nor have we conducted faithfulness analyses to determine whether they accurately reflect the models’ underlying decision-making processes. Consequently, these explanations should be interpreted as theory-guided textual rationales rather than verified accounts of human-like psychological reasoning or faithful representations of internal model processes. Finally, our experiments primarily use English-language prompts, including when evaluating non-English languages. This may introduce a potential source of linguistic and cultural bias. Future work should investigate language-specific prompting, where both the prompts and the reasoning instructions are provided in the target language. For example, when evaluating Amharic, the prompting and reasoning instructions could be formulated in Amharic rather than English. Such multilingual prompting could provide a more linguistically and culturally appropriate evaluation of subjective language understanding.
