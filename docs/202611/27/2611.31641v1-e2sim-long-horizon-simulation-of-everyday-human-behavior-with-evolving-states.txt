ABSTRACT 

AI agents are increasingly expected to support users over long periods, across work, and daily life. This requires modeling not only who users are, but also how their goals, interests, activities, and interactions evolve over time. A challenge in longitudinal user simulation is maintaining consistency between these evolving states and the consequences of actions in a shared environment. We introduce E2Sim, a longitudinal user simulation framework that jointly models evolving ac-tor states and a dynamic shared environment. E 2Sim connects actor-state updates with shared-world changes through a feedback loop linking immediate actions, de-layed outcomes, and actor-specific observations across work and life. This allows the consequences of earlier actions to shape later behavior, while preserving differ-ences in what each actor knows. We further propose a suite of longitudinal trajec-tory diagnostics, measuring dynamic consistency, coherent event progression and collaboration, and non-degenerate trajectory development rather than static con-sistency alone. In one 60-day simulation, E 2Sim produced more extracted event threads judged meaningful by our evaluator and fewer near-duplicate actions than four direct-generation baselines. The framework and evaluation methods provide a basis for studying longitudinal user simulation, with potential applications in training and evaluating AI agents that assist users over extended periods. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language model (LLM) agents are being developed as assistants for everyday work and life tasks (Yuan et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Meta, 2026; OpenAI, 2026). A personal assistant that sup-ports the same person over months must reason about multiple projects, stable habits, and evolving goals and interests. Events in one part of a person’s life can shape what they do or ask in another, sometimes days or weeks later. An isolated interaction may capture the resulting request while omitting the earlier events that shaped it, making these cross-context effects difficult to observe. A useful way to study these cross-context effects is to simulate an actor’s actions together with the evolving situation in which those actions occur. Task-level agent benchmarks evaluate the comple-tion of specified objectives (Chen et al., 2026b), while personalization benchmarks use extended interaction histories to evaluate responses to a user’s changing profile (Jiang et al., 2025). These settings do not directly trace how the consequences of everyday actions change an actor’s goals and knowledge, and how those changes shape later actions across work and life. Crucially, accumulated history should influence not only which action an actor takes next, but also how familiar activities are carried out. A PhD student, for example, may spend years reading papers, designing experi-ments, and writing. Yet the papers they attend to, the experiments they design, the details of their execution, and the outcomes they expect change with accumulated knowledge, goals, and interests. Longitudinal simulation should therefore capture how actors perform the same activities differently as their knowledge and goals change. This introduces a challenge that is easy to miss when evaluating isolated steps. A trajectory can remain locally plausible while gradually becoming repetitive, returning to the same routines without meaningful progression, or ceasing to generate consequential new developments. At the same time, simply adding new actions is not enough: new actions must remain consistent with long-term goals, 1arXiv:2611.31641v1 [cs.AI] 23 Nov 2026 Preprint prior outcomes, and what each actor has actually observed. In a multi-actor simulation, an outcome should update an actor’s state only when it becomes visible to that actor. For example, an inconclu-sive experiment may change a researcher’s next plan, while a collaborator can respond to the result only after learning about it. Long-horizon simulation therefore requires continuity : later behavior should reflect accumulated history, and development : the trajectory should produce changes that are consistent with earlier events and affect later behavior, rather than repeat the same routines. To examine these two requirements, we study the generation of long-horizon everyday action tra-jectories from an initial scenario that specifies people, their relationships, and their shared world. Even with the full history and explicit instructions to avoid previously observed errors, the language model’s outputs do not consistently reflect relevant past information, weakening continuity. Ex-plicitly asking the model to generate realistic and diverse outputs also fails to prevent repetition. We further find that asking it to generate goals and produce actions based on those goals reduces repetition to some extent. However, the resulting behavior is still highly limited by the quality and diversity of the new goals. Goal generation itself also has continuity problems, as the model does not reliably use past information to form high-quality new goals. We introduce E 2Sim ( Everyday Simulation with Evolving States ), a framework that centers long-horizon simulation on the states of simulated character (actors) with stable attributes and changing goals, plans, and knowledge. At each time slot, E 2Sim uses an actor’s current state and long-term goals to propose and select work and life goals. It then generates candidate action trajectory with different possible outcomes, and applies the realized outcome to both the actor’s state and the shared world. World changes are represented through facts, communication, and delayed follow-up events, and update other actors’ states only when they become visible to them. This actor–environment feedback loop allows immediate and delayed consequences to carry across time and actors while preserving differences in what each actor knows. To evaluate E 2Sim through the generated trajectories, we introduce a suite of trajectory-based lon-gitudinal evaluation metrics covering dynamic consistency, event progression, collaboration, and non-degeneracy. In a 60-day comparison with four baselines that prompt an LLM to generate trajec-tories directly, E 2Sim exhibits a lower near-duplicate rate and more meaningful events that develop through a series of related actions, with later actions building on earlier outcomes. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Simulation Environments and Frameworks LLM-based simulation frameworks model agents and their interactions with an environment. Generative Agents combines memory, reflection, and planning to simulate routines and emergent group behavior in a shared sandbox (Park et al., 2023). Concordia uses a Game Master to resolve agents’ actions and determine their effects on the environ-ment (Vezhnevets et al., 2023). Recent updates to Concordia support configurable components for both actors and Game Masters, with actors updating their states and plans from observations (Vezh-nevets et al., 2025). SOTOPIA studies goal-directed social interactions in open-ended scenarios (Zhou et al., 2024), with SOTOPIA-S4 extending the setting to configurable large-scale and multi-party simulation (Zhou et al., 2025), and LIFELONG-SOTOPIA carrying interaction histories across multiple social episodes (Goel & Zhu, 2025). Other systems scale LLM-based behavioral simulation to recommendation and social-network environments (Wang et al., 2025) or large synthetic societies (Piao et al., 2026). Together, these works establish mechanisms for persistent memory, interaction, and environment-mediated simulation. 

Our focus. Prior simulation frameworks provide environments and mechanisms for agent in-teraction and state updates. E 2Sim makes the feedback loop between evolving actor state and shared-world state the primary object of long-horizon simulation, tracing how action outcomes reshape actors’ goals and change the course of their ongoing activities. 

Human Behavior Simulation and Evaluation Another line of work evaluates human behavior simulation against reference responses and actions. A study of self-report-grounded agents tests whether agents built from interviews and surveys reproduce the same individuals’ responses on sur-veys and economic games (Park et al., 2026). Dynamic User Modeling and Longitudinal Life Simulation Related work on longitudinal user modeling studies how representations of an individual change with accumulating experience. 3 SIMULATION FRAMEWORK 

Existing agent simulation frameworks give agents persistent memory and let them interact in shared environments (Park et al., 2023; Vezhnevets et al., 2023; 2025). For long-horizon simulation, how-ever, we need to track both how actors’ goals, plans, and knowledge change and how the conse-quences of actions persist in the shared world. We therefore jointly model actor states and a shared world state, with actions changing the world and observed world changes updating actor states. Figure 1 summarizes E 2Sim. The scenario (A) initializes actor states and the shared world (B). At each time slot, the per-actor loop (C) uses an actor’s current state and world information visible to that actor to select goals and generate actions. The action outcomes then update both the actor’s state and the shared world, providing the updated states for the next time slot. 3.1 EVOLVING ACTOR STATE Actor

Back-ground describes an actor’s history and role. Personality describes traits that affect how the actor responds to different situations. Habits provide recurring routines, and 

Aspiration gives the actor a long-term direction. For example, a PhD student who prefers to work alone, runs each morning, and aspires to become a professor may go for a morning run and then revise a paper draft independently after receiving advisor comments. An actor with the same back-ground, habits, and aspiration but a personality that favors discussion may first meet the advisor to clarify the comments before revising. The dynamic state captures the actor’s changing objectives and its experience of events and inter-actions during the simulation. Stage Goals specify outcomes the actor works toward over multiple time slots, such as submitting a paper. Each actor maintains a set of active Stage Goals. At each time slot, it uses its long-term aspiration, current state, and observations to select an existing goal, propose a new one, or select none. Unselected goals remain active and retain their progress. Com-pleting a Stage Goal requires evidence for each specified completion condition. The model identifies whose records can establish that condition, including those of another actor or an external system when their confirmation is required. Plans record particular activities it intends to carry out, such as revising a section tomorrow. Interests record topics or activities that attract the actor’s attention and may later fade. Needs represent immediate work or life concerns generated for each slot. The state also contains Known Facts the actor has learned about the environment, messages received in the Inbox , Relations with other actors, and Activity recording its recent actions. We retain a history of updates to Stage Goals, plans, and interests and reconstruct their current states from this history. Action outcomes and observed events can change what an actor should do next, and these effects may last across multiple time slots. We therefore update the dynamic state to retain these effects for later decisions. The action’s action outcome may advance a Stage Goal , change a Plan , while the action itself is recorded in Activity . Observed world changes may update Known Facts or add messages to the Inbox , or lead the actor to revise its goals or plans. 4Preprint 3.2 ACTOR AND ENVIRONMENT DYNAMICS 

Having defined the actor state, we now describe how actors and the shared environment affect each other. An actor uses its state and current observation to propose an action, and the simulator then determines its outcome and updates the shared world accordingly. In turn, world changes that be-come visible to an actor update its dynamic state and shape its later actions. Because actors differ in their states and observations, the same world change may lead to different responses. 3.2.1 ACTIONS CHANGE THE ENVIRONMENT 

Actions generation begins by proposing and selecting slot goals based on the actor’s current state (Steps 1–2 in Figure 1). We derive slot goals that specify tasks suited to the time available for the current slot because, in preliminary simulations, actors acting directly on multi-day Stage Goals 

compressed months-long research projects into days. We also observed that a model given a work slot goal and prompted to consider life needs produced unrealistic life actions in every slot, with greater emphasis on those life needs than on the work goal. We therefore use separate work and life proposers to generate slot goals from the actor’s state, including its personality. The proposers exchange their proposals and rationales to debate which goals should take priority in the current situation. A selector then chooses the work goal, the life goal, or both for the current time slot. Based on this goal selection, the actor generates several candidate action paths with different possible outcomes, from positive to negative, and samples one uniformly (Step 3 in Figure 1). In preliminary runs, we observed that the actor tended to select actions with positive outcomes even when prompted to consider setbacks, and a separate model used for path selection showed the same preference. This often led to unrealistically fast completion of long-term tasks that normally require multiple rounds of feedback and revision. We found that generating multiple candidate actions and selecting one at random reduced this bias effectively so we include this randomness in the framework. Moreover, an actor’s limited view of the scenario may lead it to propose actions that fit its goals but may not occur in real life. The selected path therefore passes to a judge (Step 4 in Figure 1), which determines whether the action can occur fully, partly, or not at all under the current conditions. The judge then assesses the effects of the realized action on the actor’s Stage Goals and the shared world. The simulator then applies the judged outcome (Figure 1, Step 5). Depending on what occurred, the update can revise world facts and affected actors’ dynamic states, send messages to relevant actors, and schedule follow-up events for consequences that arise later. For example, an agreement today to travel next week can update the actors’ plans now and schedule a follow-up event for the trip. 3.2.2 ENVIRONMENT CHANGES SHAPE ACTIONS 

The environment influences later actions through each actor’s dynamic state. We distinguish two sources of environmental change: the effects of earlier actor actions and external events introduced independently of those actions. In both cases, actors update their states from the changes they observe, and the updated states guide their next actions. World facts record changes to shared objects and conditions, communication conveys information to the actors involved, and follow-up events introduce delayed effects at scheduled times (Step 5 in Figure 1). As these effects become visible to an actor, they update the relevant parts of its dynamic state, such as known facts, goals, or plans. External events can change an actor’s priorities by introducing new opportunities or constraints. We therefore introduce an external event component that generates events conditioned on the current scenario, with randomness in both their content and timing. Events are made visible either to se-lected actors or to all actors, and each recipient decides whether to act on them. Each actor assesses the event based on its own state and may update its knowledge, revise its plans, or develop a new 

Interest . For example, after a concert is announced, an actor with a background in music and art may add the concert to its Interests , seek more information later, and eventually decide to attend. 3.3 MULTI -A CTOR INTERACTION

This prevents an actor from responding to another actor’s action simply because it was generated earlier in the same slot. 3.4 SIMULATION WORKFLOW AND IMPLEMENTATION 

E2Sim initializes actor states and a shared environment from the scenario, then advances the simula-tion through successive time slots. Each slot begins by processing due follow-up events and any new external events. Actors observe available changes and update their dynamic states before generating actions from a frozen environment snapshot. Action generation and outcome judging run in parallel across actors. After all actors finish, the simulator merges their updates to actor and world states, making the resulting changes available for observation in the next slot. Figure 3 shows the execution architecture. A Python runner manages the slot calendar and shared simulation state. The same component functions are also registered in a LangGraph reference graph used for single-actor probes. After the environment phase (A), the runner freezes the state and dispatches actor turns to a thread pool, with each turn operating on an isolated copy. Actors run in parallel, while component calls within each turn remain sequential (B). Once all turns finish, the runner coordinates shared-project completion and merges their updates into the shared simulation state, recording progress before applying goal closures (C). Additional optimizations to model calls, inputs, and state management are detailed in Appendix C. 

4 LONGITUDINAL EVALUATION 

To assess whether trajectories remain coherent and continue to develop, we introduce four groups of longitudinal metrics for dynamic consistency, event progression, collaboration, and non-degeneracy. A LLM-based evaluator scores eligible actor transitions, event threads, and collaboration episodes. 6Preprint These scores characterize coherence and experiment within the simulated world, not fidelity to real individuals or effectiveness in downstream assistant tasks. 

Individual Dynamic Consistency (IDC). IDC asks whether changes in communication, behavior, goals, and interests follow from an actor’s prior experience. It also identifies unsupported changes, missed adaptations, and spillover to unrelated facets. IDC Macro averages alignment over eligible transitions within actors and then across actors on a 0–100 scale. Higher values indicate stronger support from history. Lower Missed Adaptation Rate and Spillover Rate indicate fewer correspond-ing errors. 

Emergent Event Evaluation (EEE). With no predefined event list, we extract action threads around a goal, problem, or shared activity. EEE asks whether each thread has a grounded start (Event Initiation Plausibility, EIP), progresses consistently with earlier outcomes and elapsed time (Event Progression Coherence, EPC), and has a status supported by its history (Event Status Appro-priateness, ESA). A thread may properly remain open or waiting. All three scores use a 0–100 scale over eligible threads, with higher values indicating stronger support. 

Event Collaboration Dynamics (ECD). A shared event may progress coherently overall while a participant forgets a commitment or acts before a dependency is met. ECD evaluates each decision against the participant’s role, commitments, event state, and other actors’ steps. Acting and waiting may both be appropriate. Episode Collaboration Plausibility (ECP) summarizes coordination on a 0–

100 scale, with higher scores indicating better coordination. Context-Sensitive Response Calibration (CSRC) checks whether responses vary appropriately across situations. ECD measures coordination within shared events, not general relationship quality. 

Non-degeneracy Diagnostics (NDD). High scores on eligible transitions can coexist with inactive or repetitive trajectories. NDD counts meaningful event threads per 1,000 actor-slots (one actor in one time slot) and measures near-duplicate actions. It also checks whether new threads are grounded in context, events have later consequences, and decisions use relevant history. Lower duplication is preferable, whereas event density has no universal target and must be read with the other diagnostics. 

Evaluation reliability. A post-hoc judge may reward an observed response based on a plausible explanation constructed afterward. We require trajectory evidence and test the judge on injected errors, including forgotten commitments and violated dependencies. Perturbation Win Rate (PWR) is the share of original–corrupted episode pairs for which the judge scores the original higher. Higher PWR indicates better detection of these errors, not agreement with human ratings. Appendix A details the rubrics, extraction procedure, and aggregation rules. 

5 EXPERIMENT 

We generate 60-day action trajectories for ten actors within one scenario spanning four connected settings, with six time slots per day. Each trajectory contains 3,600 actor-slot action records. We run E 2Sim and each of four baselines once from the same scenario file. All five generation runs use 

gpt-5.6-luna . Appendix E details the actor roster, initial goals, and world configuration. We compare E 2Sim with four baselines that generate action trajectories directly from the supplied context using LLM. Direct prompts the model once per day with the full scenario and the trajectory generated so far to generate that day’s actions for all actors. Guided retains this setup but adds instructions targeting errors observed in Direct, testing how far prompting alone can address them. 

Per-Actor generates each actor’s actions separately using its own profile, visible initial facts, its own history, and other actors’ entries that mention it. Summary retains joint daily generation but replaces the full history with a summary maintained by the model and action records from the most recent three days, examining the effect of a more compact history. We evaluate the five generated trajectories using the longitudinal measures in Section 4. We additionally compare E 2Sim with na-tive Concordia (Vezhnevets et al., 2023). Starting from the same source scenario, we run Concordia for seven simulated days and compare it with the first seven days of E 2Sim’s trajectory, covering 42 time slots and 420 scheduled actor actions per system. A LONGITUDINAL EVALUATION : I MPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

This appendix documents the reference implementation of the evaluation framework in Section 4. A.1 PIPELINE OVERVIEW 

The pipeline operates on a parsed simulation trajectory (per-actor, per-time-slot records) and pro-ceeds in four stages. 

Event-thread extraction. Atomic events are extracted from the raw trajectory, then linked into candidate threads using shared actors, shared objects or goals, explicit references, causal compati-bility, and temporal plausibility. Linking allows an event to be assigned to a NEW THREAD or left as 13 Preprint 

UNCERTAIN rather than forcing every event into an existing thread. Each resulting thread is cap-tured as an event card recording its actors, type, initiation time, core object, state sequence, current status, open dependencies, and supporting evidence. EEE and ECD are both computed from these cards. 

Judge interface. Scoring is abstracted behind a common Judge interface, and every run reported in this paper uses LLMJudge rather than the deterministic HeuristicJudge kept for offline unit tests. This means every semantic label – Pass A/B transition labels, ECD episode dimensions, EEE dimensions, CSRC dimensions, and the per-item NDD decisions – is produced by the judge model, with response caching, retry handling, and batched/threaded requests for throughput. It does not mean every number in the report is itself a model output: taxonomy-mode Transition Alignment Scores are a fixed deterministic lookup applied to the Pass A/B labels (Appendix A.2), and all rates, counts, and descriptive statistics are computed from judged labels in Python rather than requested from the model directly. 

Per-block scoring. IDC scoring follows the three-pass protocol of Appendix A.2. ECD scoring computes the five episode dimensions and the Perturbation Win Rate against six corruption opera-tors (unrelated response, dependency violation, commitment amnesia, premature closure, mechan-ical overreaction, mechanical inertia), each applied at the episode level. EEE scoring computes Event Initiation Plausibility, Event Progression Coherence, and Event Status Appropriateness per Event Card. A dedicated LLM diagnostics stage judges the Context-Sensitive Response Calibration dimensions and the per-item decisions underlying all five NDD diagnostics (Appendix A.2) using earlier judge outputs and retrieved downstream evidence. Deterministic lexical versions of both exist in the code as legacy scaffolding for the offline HeuristicJudge path, but every reported CSRC and NDD number aggregates the LLM-judged version. 

Abstention and reporting. Per-item judgments the pipeline cannot support with sufficient evidence (e.g. INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE Pass A/B labels, undecidable NDD windows) are quarantined rather than merged or imputed. Available eligibility denominators are reported in Table 3. Unavailable counts are marked NR. Scores remain conditional on eligibility, and differing abstention patterns can affect comparisons. One exception: IDC Macro and the three pooled IDC rates – Unsupported Drift Rate, Missed Adaptation Rate, and Spillover Rate – return 0.0 rather than N/A by convention when the relevant denominator is empty. The denominator counts eligible actor–facet transitions for IDC Macro, eligible STAY cases for Unsupported Drift Rate, eligible SHOULD CHANGE cases for Missed Adaptation Rate, and triggered transitions for Spillover Rate. The denominator is reported alongside each value, so a 0.0 next to a denominator of zero should be read as “not evaluated” rather than “no failures observed” (for the rates) or “perfectly consistent” (for IDC Macro). A final aggregation step assembles all blocks into a single report, which we render as the tables in this appendix. A.2 FORMAL METRIC DEFINITIONS

A.3 AGGREGATION AND COVERAGE ACROSS CONDITIONS 

Each condition in Table 1 contributes one trajectory. No metric is averaged across independent simulation runs. The within-trajectory aggregation procedures are: • IDC Macro: average eligible transition–facet TAS values within each actor, then average equally across eligible actors and scale to 0–100. Unsupported Drift Rate, Missed Adapta-tion Rate, and Spillover Rate instead pool their respective eligible cases across actors. • Episode Collaboration Plausibility: average applicable dimensions within each decision episode, average episode scores within each thread, then average equally across scorable threads and scale to 0–100. • Context-Sensitive Response Calibration: average applicable calibration dimensions within each actor, then average equally across eligible actors and scale to 0–100. • Perturbation Win Rate: compare original and corrupted episodes using dimensions scored in both versions. Average binary wins over eligible episodes within each thread, then equally across eligible threads for each corruption type, and finally equally across applicable corruption types. Ties are not wins. • EEE: for each of EIP, EPC, and ESA, average applicable dimensions within each Event Card, then average equally across cards eligible for that metric and scale to 0–100. • NDD: Meaningful Event Threads divides the number of confirmed meaningful threads by observed actor-slots and multiplies by 1,000. Supported Novelty and Consequence pool eligible threads. Near-Duplicate Actions pools decidable decision windows. History-Dependence pools decisions with relevant history and a decidable history-use judgment. These four proportions are expressed on 0–100 scales. Table 3 reports coverage separately from scores. Counts of actors, threads, episodes, and decision windows are within-trajectory evaluation units, not independent simulation replicates. Eligibility differs across metrics, so high scores should be interpreted alongside their coverage. The ECD episode counts are not universal denominators for Perturbation Win Rate: individual com-parisons require jointly scored dimensions, and mechanical-inertia corruptions additionally require prior context. C SYSTEM OPTIMIZATION 

The entire simulation framework is implemented using LangGraph. 1 We optimize model calls, prompt construction, parallel execution, and state management to reduce cost and generation time. 

Model calls and inputs. We reduce estimated API cost and generation time by making fewer and smaller model calls. Fewer calls reduce token usage and sequential waiting, while smaller inputs avoid repeatedly processing information unrelated to the task. In our framework, we remove calls whose outputs are unused, run daily life planning only in the final slot of each simulated day, and call the model for world updates only when the judge identifies realized action effects. Routing, identifier assignment, and trajectory recording are handled by Python. Each component receives task-specific views of the relevant actor, goal, activity, and world information. 

History compaction and caching. We reduce estimated input-token cost by limiting prompt growth and increasing cache reuse. Compacting superseded progress records reduces repeated in-put, while keeping reusable content stable across calls improves caching. We apply this to Stage Goal histories by retaining the latest complete progress update for each goal within the older por-tion of the prompt history. Recent records remain unchanged, and the full original event stream is preserved for auditing. We also separate stable context, older history, and current-slot updates. For components checked through replay, stable content is placed in instructions , with cache routing distinguished by actor, component, and prompt version. By default, the cutoff between older and recent history advances once per simulated day. 

Parallel actor execution. We reduce elapsed generation time by running actor branches concur-rently. Within a slot, actors use their own states and observations from the same frozen environment snapshot, so they do not need to wait for one another’s current-slot outputs. Their model calls can therefore overlap. The ten-actor configuration uses ten workers by default. Updates are merged after all actors reach a synchronization barrier, and preassigned identifiers determine the order of trajectory records. This optimization changes execution scheduling without removing model calls. 

State replay and audit logging. We reduce CPU and memory overhead in state replay, check-pointing, and recovery. Indexed replay avoids processing the full history for every event, while sep-arate audit storage avoids repeatedly copying and encoding accumulated call records. For goal-event replay, we maintain indexes over event identifiers, actor sequences, and goal histories and update the local replay state in place. This removes repeated full-history validation and registry copying. Actor branches do not copy accumulated LLM call records. Historical call text is serialized once and appended to a separate audit file. Full checkpoints are still written, using previously serialized data and streamed output to reduce repeated encoding and intermediate memory use. During recovery, call records are read sequentially, with only the required usage information retained in memory. 

Validation scope. Prompt compaction and changes to message placement were checked through component-level replay. Runtime changes were checked for state or byte equivalence, as applicable. These checks cover the tested components and operations. They do not establish identical end-to-end generated trajectories across all optimizations.

G EXPLORATORY QUESTION ANSWERING STUDY 

We constructed an 80-question, four-choice benchmark from the 60-day simulated trajectory to ex-plore whether its history supports questions about actors and events.
